# r3.06.11 Hexahedral element with one integration point, stabilised by the “Assumed Strain” method

**Summary:**

The standard under-integrated 8-node hexahedral element with 1 integration point introduces spurious modes associated with zero energy (hourglass modes) and can lead to a singularity of the global stiffness matrix for certain boundary conditions. The rank deficiency of the stiffness matrix, due to under-integration, must therefore be made good by adding to the elementary stiffness a so-called stabilisation matrix. This is the purpose of the ASM (Assumed Strain Method) developed here.

The main characteristic of this method is that the discretised gradient operator $B$ does not necessarily derive from the displacement field and from the classical relations linking strain to displacement. Indeed, this ASM method consists in projecting the discretised gradient operator onto an appropriate subspace in order to avoid the various types of locking.

## 1 Introduction

In finite-element analyses, the use of under-integration methods makes it possible to reduce analysis times significantly, which explains their success. The other objective of these methods is to avoid the various lockings encountered in the numerical implementation of finite elements.

However, this under-integration does not have only advantages: it unfortunately introduces spurious modes associated with zero energy, which lead to hourglass modes, which deform the mesh in an unrealistic way and end up by making the solution explode. This is due to a rank deficiency of the stiffness matrix caused by under-integration. It is remedied by adding to the elementary stiffness a so-called stabilisation matrix. The kernel of the new stiffness obtained by this means must reduce to the modes corresponding to rigid-body motions only.

In recent years, some authors have developed various elements based on the ASM (Assumed Strain Method) technique. The main characteristic of this method is that the discretised gradient operator $B$ does not necessarily derive from the displacement field and from the classical relations linking strain to displacement. Indeed, this ASM method consists in projecting the discretised gradient operator onto an appropriate subspace in order to avoid the various types of locking. This technique has been widely used recently and has led to several stabilised finite elements of the 4-node quadrilateral or 8-node hexahedron type [1], [2], [3].

It is the 8-node hexahedral element under-integrated with 1 integration point and stabilised by the ASM method, due to Belytschko and Bindeman [2], that is described in this document.

## 2 Formulation of the HEXA8 element with one integration point

### 2.1 Displacement field and discretised gradient operator

In the hexahedral element, the spatial coordinates ${x}_{i}$ are related to the nodal coordinates ${x}_{\text{iI}}$ by means of the isoparametric shape functions ${N}_{I}$ by the formulae:

> ${x}_{i}={x}_{\text{iI}}{N}_{I}(\xi ,\eta ,\zeta )=\sum_{I=1}^{8}{N}_{I}(\xi ,\eta ,\zeta ){x}_{\text{iI}}$ **eq 1.1-1**

The summation convention for repeated indices will be used in what follows. The lower-case indices $i$ vary from 1 to 3 and represent the spatial directions. The upper-case indices $I$ vary from 1 to 8 and correspond to the nodes of the element.

The same shape functions are used to define the displacement field of the element ${u}_{i}$ as a function of the nodal displacements ${u}_{\text{iI}}$ :

> ${u}_{i}={u}_{\text{iI}}{N}_{I}(\xi ,\eta ,\zeta )$

Since the same shape functions apply to the coordinates and to the displacements, their material derivative vanishes and the velocity field can be given by:

> ${v}_{i}={v}_{\text{iI}}{N}_{I}(\xi ,\eta ,\zeta )$ **eq 1.1-2**

The previous interpolation of the velocity field will make it possible to define the strain rate and to write the relations linking the strains to the nodal velocities. The gradient ${v}_{i,j}$ of the velocity field is written:

> ${v}_{i,j}={v}_{\text{iI}}{N}_{I,j}$

By convention, a comma preceding a lower-case index represents differentiation with respect to the spatial coordinates. The strain-rate tensor ${D}_{ij}$ is then given by the symmetric part of the velocity gradient: ${D}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}({v}_{i,j}+{v}_{j,i})$

Trilinear isoparametric shape functions ${N}_{I}(\xi ,\eta ,\zeta )$ are given by:

> ${N}_{I}(\xi ,\eta ,\zeta )=\frac{1}{8}(1+{\xi}_{I}\xi )(1+{\eta}_{I}\eta )(1+{\zeta}_{I}\zeta )$
> $\xi ,\eta ,\zeta \in [-1,1],I=1,....,8$      with ${\xi}_{I},{\eta}_{I},{\zeta}_{I}$ equal to 1 or –1 **eq 1.1-3**

These shape functions transform a unit cube in the space $(\xi ,\eta ,\zeta )$ into an arbitrary hexahedron in the space $({x}_{1},{x}_{2},{x}_{3})=(x,y,z)$ . Combining equations **1.1-1, 1.1-2, 1.1-3**, one arrives at the development of the velocity field as the sum of a constant term, of linear terms in ${x}_{i}$ , and of terms involving the functions ${h}_{\alpha}$ :

> ${v}_{i}={a}_{\mathrm{0i}}+{a}_{\mathrm{1i}}{x}_{1}+{a}_{\mathrm{2i}}{x}_{2}+{a}_{\mathrm{3i}}{x}_{3}+{c}_{\mathrm{1i}}{h}_{1}+{c}_{\mathrm{2i}}{h}_{2}+{c}_{\mathrm{3i}}{h}_{3}+{c}_{\mathrm{4i}}{h}_{4}$     i = 1,2,3 **eq 1.1-4**
> ${h}_{1}=\eta \zeta$     ${h}_{2}=\xi \zeta$     ${h}_{3}=\eta \xi$     ${h}_{4}=\xi \eta \zeta$

Indeed, equation **1.1-1** makes it possible to write $\xi ,\eta ,\zeta$ as a function only of the ${x}_{i}$ , of the ${h}_{\alpha}$ and of a constant parameter. Substituting these last relations into equation **1.1-2**, one recovers the sought expressions **1.1-4**.

Evaluating equation **1.1-4** at the nodes of the element, one arrives at the following 3 systems of 8 equations:

> ${\dot{d}}_{i}={a}_{\mathrm{0i}}s+{a}_{\mathrm{1i}}{x}_{1}+{a}_{\mathrm{2i}}{x}_{2}+{a}_{\mathrm{3i}}{x}_{3}+{c}_{\mathrm{1i}}{h}_{1}+{c}_{\mathrm{2i}}{h}_{2}+{c}_{\mathrm{3i}}{h}_{3}+{c}_{\mathrm{4i}}{h}_{4}$         $i=1,2,3$ **eq 1.1-5**

In the previous equation and in what follows, bold characters denote tensors of order at least 1. Thus, the vectors ${\dot{d}}_{i}$ and ${x}_{i}$ represent, respectively, the nodal velocities and coordinates and are given by:

> ${\dot{d}}_{i}^{t}=({v}_{\mathrm{i1}},{v}_{\mathrm{i2}},{v}_{\mathrm{i3}},...,{v}_{\mathrm{i8}})$
> ${x}_{i}^{t}=({x}_{\mathrm{i1}},{x}_{\mathrm{i2}},{x}_{\mathrm{i3}},...,{x}_{\mathrm{i8}})$

The vectors $s$ and ${h}_{\alpha}(\alpha =1,....,4)$ are given for their part by

> ${s}^{t}=(1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1)$
> ${h}_{1}^{t}=(1,1,-1,-1,-1,-1,1,1)$
> ${h}_{2}^{t}=(1,-1,-1,1,-1,1,1,-1)$
> ${h}_{3}^{t}=(1,-1,1,-1,1,-1,1,-1)$
> ${h}_{4}^{t}=(-1,1,-1,1,1,-1,1,-1)$

In order to arrive at an advantageous writing of the discretised gradient operator $\mathrm{B}$ , the 3 vectors ${b}_{i}$ defined by: ${b}_{i}^{T}={N}_{,i}(0)=\frac{\partial N}{\partial {x}_{i}}{\mid}_{\xi =\eta =\zeta =0}$         $i=1,2,3$ **eq 1.1-6** are introduced

where $N$ represents $({N}_{1},{N}_{2},...,{N}_{8})$ . These vectors ${b}_{i}$ represent the simplest form of the under-integrated discretised gradient operator introduced by Hallquist and which is based on the evaluation of the derivatives of the isoparametric shape functions at the origin of the reference frame $(\xi ,\eta ,\zeta )$ . They can be determined explicitly. Moreover, the following orthogonality conditions can be verified:

> ${b}_{i}^{T}.{h}_{\alpha}=0$     ,    ${b}_{i}^{T}.s=0$     ,    ${b}_{i}^{T}.{x}_{j}={\delta}_{ij}$

> ${h}_{\alpha}^{T}.s=0$     ,    ${h}_{\alpha}^{T}.{h}_{\beta}=8{\delta}_{\alpha \beta }$ **eq 1.1-7**
> $i=1à3$     ,    $\alpha ,\beta =1à4$

where $\delta$ denotes the Kronecker symbol.

At this stage, the unknown constants which appear in the writing of the velocity field (**eq 1.1-4)** can be determined by taking the scalar product of equation (**eq 1.1-5)** by ${b}_{j}^{T}$ and ${\mathrm{h}}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{T}}$ , respectively, and using the orthogonality relations above. One obtains:

> ${a}_{ij}={b}_{j}^{T}.{\dot{d}}_{i}$     ,    ${c}_{\alpha i}={\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}.{\dot{d}}_{i}$         with    ${\gamma}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{8}\left[{h}_{\alpha}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}({h}_{\alpha}^{T}.{x}_{j}){b}_{j}\right]$

The velocity field is finally put in the following very practical form:

> ${v}_{i}={a}_{\mathrm{0i}}+({x}_{1}{b}_{1}^{T}+{x}_{2}{b}_{2}^{T}+{x}_{3}{b}_{3}^{T}+{h}_{1}{\gamma}_{1}^{T}+{h}_{2}{\gamma}_{2}^{T}+{h}_{3}{\gamma}_{3}^{T}+{h}_{4}{\gamma}_{4}^{T}).{\dot{d}}_{i}$     $i=1,2,3$ **eq 1.1-8**

Differentiating the formula above with respect to ${x}_{j}$ , one obtains the velocity gradient:

> ${v}_{i,j}=({b}_{j}^{T}+\sum_{\alpha =1}^{4}{h}_{\alpha ,j}{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}).{\dot{d}}_{i}=({b}_{j}^{T}+{h}_{\alpha ,j}{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}).{\dot{d}}_{i}$

The discretised gradient operator relating the strain-rate tensor to the vector of nodal velocities by:

> ${\nabla}_{s}v=B.\dot{d}$

where:

${\nabla}_{s}v=\left[\begin{array}{c}{v}_{x,x}\\ {v}_{y,y}\\ {v}_{z,z}\\ {v}_{x,y}+{v}_{y,x}\\ {v}_{x,z}+{v}_{z,x}\\ {v}_{y,x}+{v}_{z,y}\end{array}\right]$ ,        $\dot{d}=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\dot{d}}_{x}\\ {\dot{d}}_{y}\\ {\dot{d}}_{z}\end{array}\right]$

then takes the practical matrix form:

$B=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{b}_{x}^{T}+{h}_{\alpha ,x}{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}& 0& 0\\ 0& {b}_{y}^{T}+{h}_{\alpha ,y}{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}& 0\\ 0& 0& {b}_{z}^{T}+{h}_{\alpha ,z}{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}\\ {b}_{y}^{T}+{h}_{\alpha ,y}{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}& {b}_{x}^{T}+{h}_{\alpha ,x}{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}& 0\\ {b}_{z}^{T}+{h}_{\alpha ,z}{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}& 0& {b}_{x}^{T}+{h}_{\alpha ,x}{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}\\ 0& {b}_{z}^{T}+{h}_{\alpha ,z}{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}& {b}_{y}^{T}+{h}_{\alpha ,y}{\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}\end{array}\right]$ **eq 1.1-9**

The vectors ${\gamma}_{\alpha}$ which appear in $B$ satisfy the following orthogonality conditions:

> ${\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}.{x}_{j}=0$         ,    ${\gamma}_{\alpha}^{T}{h}_{\beta}={\delta}_{\alpha \beta }$

An element based on this formulation is convergent when it is evaluated exactly. However, the evaluation of this operator $B$ at each of the integration points makes this element too costly for practical applications, and a simplified form of this element is required.

### 2.2 Variational formulation of the problem

The extension of the weak form of the Hu–Washizu variational principle to the case of the mechanics of non-linear solids is written for a single element ${\Omega}_{e}$

$\text{δπ}(v,\bar{\dot{\epsilon}},\bar{\sigma})={\int}_{{\Omega}_{e}}{\delta \bar{\dot{\epsilon}}}^{T}.\sigma d\Omega +\delta {\int}_{{\Omega}_{e}}{\bar{\sigma}}^{T}.(\nabla {}_{s}\text{}v-\bar{\dot{\epsilon}})d\Omega -\delta {\dot{d}}^{T}.{f}^{\text{ext}}=0$         eq 1.2-1

where $\delta$ represents a variation, $v$ the velocity field, $\bar{\dot{\varepsilon}}$ the assumed strain rate, $\bar{\sigma}$ the assumed stress, $\sigma$ the stress evaluated by the constitutive law, $\dot{d}$ the nodal velocities, ${f}^{\text{ext}}$ the external nodal forces and ${\nabla}_{s}v$ the symmetric part of the gradient of the velocity field.

The “Assumed strain” formulation retained in what follows to build the element is based on a simplified form of the Hu–Washizu variational principle which relies on the fact that the assumed stress is chosen orthogonal to the difference between the symmetric part of the velocity gradient and the assumed strain rate. Thus the second term of equation 1.2-1 is eliminated and one obtains:

> $\delta \pi (\bar{\dot{\varepsilon}})={\int}_{{\Omega}_{e}}{\delta \bar{\dot{\varepsilon}}}^{T}.\sigma d\Omega -\delta {\dot{d}}^{T}.{f}^{\text{ext}}=0$ **eq 1.2-2**

In this form, the variational principle is independent of the interpolation of the stress, since the assumed stress no longer appears and therefore does not need to be defined. The discretised equations therefore require only the interpolation of the velocity $v$ and of the assumed strain rate $\bar{\dot{\varepsilon}}$ in the element. If ${N}_{I}$ denotes the isoparametric shape functions of the element, one has:

$\nu (x,t)=\sum_{I=1}^{m}{N}_{I}(x){\dot{d}}_{I}(t)$ where $m$ denotes the number of nodes. One deduces:

${\nabla}_{s}v(x,t)=B(x).\dot{d}(t)$

The assumed strain rate $\bar{\dot{\varepsilon}}$ is defined for its part by: $\bar{\dot{\varepsilon}}(x,t)=\bar{B}(x).\dot{d}(t)$ **eq 1.2-3**

Substituting equation **1.2-3** into the variational principle **1.2-2**, one obtains:

$\delta {\dot{d}}^{T}.{\int}_{{\Omega}_{e}}{\bar{B}}^{T}.\sigma d\Omega -\delta {\dot{d}}^{T}.{f}^{\text{ext}}=0$

Since $\delta {\dot{d}}^{T}$ can be chosen arbitrarily, the previous equation leads to:

> ${f}^{int}={f}^{\text{ext}}$

with        ${f}^{int}={\int}_{{\Omega}_{e}}{\bar{B}}^{T}.\sigma (\bar{\dot{\varepsilon}})d\Omega$

In the equation above, it is clearly specified that the stress $\sigma$ is computed by the constitutive law from the assumed strain rate $\bar{\dot{\varepsilon}}$ . For non-linear problems, $\sigma$ may also be a function of the integral of the assumed strain rate and of other internal variables. The formulation thus obtained is valid for problems including both types of non-linearity: geometric and material.

In the case of linear problems, one has:

$\sigma =C.\bar{\varepsilon}=C.\bar{B}.d$

The internal forces of the element are written:

> ${f}^{int}={K}_{e}.d$         with         ${K}_{e}=\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}{\bar{B}}^{T}.C.\bar{B}d\Omega \stackrel{}{}$

In a standard displacement approach, the assumed strain rate is identified with the symmetric part of the velocity gradient, which amounts to replacing $\stackrel{}{\bar{B}}$ by $\stackrel{}{B}$ in the previous expressions. One obtains:

> ${K}_{e}=\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}{\stackrel{}{B}}^{T}.C.\stackrel{}{B}d\Omega \stackrel{}{}$

### 2.3 “Hourglass” modes associated with zero energy

The matrix writing **eq 1.1-9** of the discretised gradient operator will make it possible to understand the origin of the hourglass modes, or hourglass modes. As will be seen, these kinematic modes are due to under-integration and are associated with zero energy although they induce a non-zero strain. This anomaly is explained by the difference which under-integration induces between the kernel of the discretised stiffness operator and the kernel of the continuous stiffness operator.

Note first that the under-integrated discretised gradient operator (i.e. associated with a single integration point located at the centre of the element) reduces to:

> $B=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{b}_{x}^{T}& 0& 0\\ 0& {b}_{y}^{T}& 0\\ 0& 0& {b}_{z}^{T}\\ {b}_{y}^{T}& {b}_{x}^{T}& 0\\ {b}_{z}^{T}& 0& {b}_{x}^{T}\\ 0& {b}_{z}^{T}& {b}_{y}^{T}\end{array}\right]$ **eq 1.3-1**

Indeed, the terms ${h}_{\alpha ,i}$ of equation **eq 1.1-9** vanish at the integration point $\mid \xi =\eta =\zeta =0$ .

Let us now analyse the kernel of the stiffness matrix obtained by integration. In the linear case, this elementary matrix is written: ${K}_{e}=V{B}^{T}.C.B$     where $V$ denotes the volume of the element.

Examination of the kernel of the under-integrated stiffness amounts to the study of the rank of the matrix $B$ . One must therefore seek the velocity modes $\dot{d}$     with zero strain, that is to say satisfying:

> ${\nabla}_{s}v=B.\dot{d}=0$ **eq 1.3-2**

One must recover in the kernel of ${K}_{e}$ the modes associated with rigid-body motions, that is, in 3D, 3 translations and 3 rotations.

The kernel of the continuous operator is therefore of dimension 6 and reduces to the 6 vectors:

$(\begin{array}{ccc}s& 0& 0\\ 0& s& 0\\ 0& 0& s\end{array})(\begin{array}{ccc}y& z& 0\\ -x& 0& z\\ 0& -x& -y\end{array})$

The first 3 column vectors satisfy **1.3-2** because of ${b}_{i}^{T}.s=0$ , the last 3 because of ${b}_{i}^{T}.{x}_{j}={\delta}_{ij}$ .

But besides the previous rigid modes, the discretised gradient operator given in **1.3-1** annihilates the following 12 other vectors:

$(\begin{array}{ccc}{h}_{1}& 0& 0\\ 0& {h}_{1}& 0\\ 0& 0& {h}_{1}\end{array})(\begin{array}{ccc}{h}_{2}& 0& 0\\ 0& {h}_{2}& 0\\ 0& 0& {h}_{2}\end{array})$ $(\begin{array}{ccc}{h}_{3}& 0& 0\\ 0& {h}_{3}& 0\\ 0& 0& {h}_{3}\end{array})(\begin{array}{ccc}{h}_{4}& 0& 0\\ 0& {h}_{4}& 0\\ 0& 0& {h}_{4}\end{array})$

because ${b}_{i}^{T}.{h}_{\alpha}=0$

The column vectors above are called hourglass modes or hourglass modes.

The discretised gradient operator given in **1.3-1** and the exactly integrated one given in **1.1-9** correctly compute the gradients of linear fields and give identical results when they are applied to the 6 rigid-body modes. On the other hand, the exact formula for $B$ properly computes the other strain modes ${h}_{\alpha}$ , whereas the under-integrated form gives erroneous results.

The deficiency in the rank of the under-integrated operator $B$ (rank 6 only instead of 18 for exactly integrated $B$ ) results in unrealistic oscillations of the mesh leading to solutions which explode for certain boundary conditions.

These under-integrated elements therefore require so-called stabilisation techniques.

## 3 Stabilisation of “Assumed Strain Method” (ASM) type

The approach followed to stabilise the under-integrated hexahedral element is that of Belytschko and Bindeman [2]. The method developed to control the hourglass modes in an under-integrated element is called the Assumed Strain Method. It also implies the construction of an appropriate form of the matrix B making it possible to avoid numerical locking.

### 3.1 Formulae of Hallquist and of Flanagan-Belytschko

Up to now, in order to establish the formulae **1.1-9** of the operator B, the vectors ${b}_{i}$ based on the differentiation of the shape functions at the origin (equation **1.1-6**) have been used. This writing of $B$ is called the Hallquist form. A second form is now considered, based on the mean values of the derivatives of the shape functions of the element. This form is due to Flanagan and Belytschko and is written:

${\hat{b}}_{i}^{T}=\frac{1}{V}\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}{N}_{,i}(\xi ,\eta ,\zeta )d\Omega$     $i=1,2,3$

These vectors can be computed explicitly. They can also be integrated numerically exactly.

The orthogonality conditions of §1.2 remain satisfied except the first one on non-parallelepiped elements:

${\hat{b}}_{i}^{T}.{h}_{j}\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\text{=0}\text{sur les parallélipipèdes}\\ \mathrm{\ne }0\text{sinon}\end{array}$

${\hat{b}}_{i}^{T}.{h}_{4}=0$ on all the elements.

Still assuming this orthogonality relation to be true, the displacement gradient is written as previously:

> ${v}_{i,j}=({\hat{b}}_{j}^{T}+{h}_{\alpha ,j}{\hat{\gamma}}_{\alpha}^{T}).{\dot{d}}_{i}$         with    ${\hat{\gamma}}_{\alpha}=\frac{1}{8}\left[{h}_{\alpha}-\sum_{j=1}^{3}({h}_{\alpha}^{T}.{x}_{j}){\hat{b}}_{j}\right]$

One can continue to write: ${\nabla}_{s}v=\hat{B}\cdot \dot{d}$

where $\hat{B}$ appears as the sum of a constant term $\hat{{B}_{c}}$ and of a non-constant term $\hat{{B}_{n}}$ defined by:

$\hat{B}={\hat{B}}_{c}+{\hat{B}}_{n}$

> ${\hat{B}}_{c}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{\hat{b}}_{x}^{T}& 0& 0\\ 0& {\hat{b}}_{y}^{T}& 0\\ 0& 0& {\hat{b}}_{z}^{T}\\ {\hat{b}}_{y}^{T}& {\hat{b}}_{x}^{T}& 0\\ {\hat{b}}_{z}^{T}& 0& {\hat{b}}_{x}^{T}\\ 0& {\hat{b}}_{z}^{T}& {\hat{b}}_{y}^{T}\end{array}\right]$     ${\hat{B}}_{n}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{\hat{X}}_{1234}^{T}& 0& 0\\ 0& {\hat{Y}}_{1234}^{T}& 0\\ 0& 0& {\hat{Z}}_{1234}^{T}\\ {\hat{Y}}_{1234}^{T}& {X}_{1234}^{T}& 0\\ {\hat{Z}}_{1234}^{T}& 0& {X}_{1234}^{T}\\ 0& {\hat{Z}}_{1234}^{T}& {\hat{Y}}_{1234}^{T}\end{array}\right]$

with ${\hat{X}}_{1234}^{T}=\sum_{\alpha =1}^{4}{h}_{\alpha ,x}{\hat{{\gamma}_{\alpha}}}^{T}$         ${\hat{Y}}_{1234}^{T}=\sum_{\alpha =1}^{4}{h}_{\alpha ,y}{\hat{{\gamma}_{\alpha}}}^{T}$         ${\hat{Z}}_{1234}^{T}=\sum_{\alpha =1}^{4}{h}_{\alpha ,z}{\hat{{\gamma}_{\alpha}}}^{T}$

This formulation, even if it is less rigorous than that of Hallquist (since it assumes all the orthogonality relations to be true), gives better results in terms of accuracy and convergence.

### 3.2 Projection onto a strain field $\bar{B}$

A projection is applied to the discretised gradient operator $\hat{B}$ in order to deduce from it an operator $\bar{B}$ possessing certain good properties. The objective of the projection is twofold:

- It makes it possible, on the one hand, to eliminate the volumetric locking of the finite element in the incompressible case
- It avoids, on the other hand, the locking due to excessive transverse-shear terms in bending-dominated problems.

The operator $\hat{B}$ is replaced by an operator $\bar{B}$ such that:

$\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\hat{B}={\hat{B}}_{c}+{\hat{B}}_{n}\\ \bar{B}={\hat{B}}_{c}+{\bar{B}}_{n}\end{array}$

Only the non-constant part ${\hat{B}}_{n}$ is projected; the constant part ${\hat{B}}_{c}$ remains unchanged.

Two different projections lead to the following 2 finite elements:

- ASQBI element (Assumed Strain Quintessential Bending Incompressible)!

$\bar{{B}_{n}}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{\hat{X}}_{1234}^{T}& -\bar{\nu}{\hat{Y}}_{3}^{T}-\nu {\hat{Y}}_{24}^{T}& -\bar{\nu}{\hat{Z}}_{2}^{T}-\nu {\hat{Z}}_{34}^{T}\\ -\bar{\nu}{\hat{X}}_{3}^{T}-\nu {\hat{X}}_{14}^{T}& {\hat{Y}}_{1234}^{T}& -\bar{\nu}{\hat{Z}}_{1}^{T}-\nu {\hat{Z}}_{34}^{T}\\ -\bar{\nu}{\hat{X}}_{2}^{T}-\nu {\hat{X}}_{14}^{T}& -\bar{\nu}{\hat{Y}}_{1}^{T}-\nu {\hat{Y}}_{24}^{T}& {\hat{Z}}_{1234}^{T}\\ {\hat{Y}}_{12}^{T}& {\hat{X}}_{12}^{T}& 0\\ {\hat{Z}}_{13}^{T}& 0& {\hat{X}}_{13}^{T}\\ 0& {\hat{Z}}_{23}^{T}& {\hat{Y}}_{23}^{T}\end{array}\right]$ **eq 2.2-1**

Where:    $\stackrel{}{\bar{\nu}=\frac{\nu}{1-\nu }}$ ,        ${\hat{X}}_{14}^{T}={h}_{1,x}\hat{{\gamma}_{{1}^{T}}}+{h}_{4,x}\hat{{\gamma}_{{4}^{T}}}$         ${\hat{Z}}_{2}^{T}={h}_{2,z}\hat{{\gamma}_{{2}^{T}}}$

- ADS element (Assumed Deviatoric Strain):

> $\bar{{B}_{n}}=\left[\begin{array}{c}\frac{2}{3}{\hat{X}}_{1234}^{T}-\frac{1}{3}{\hat{Y}}_{1234}^{T}-\frac{1}{3}{\hat{Z}}_{1234}^{T}\\ -\frac{1}{3}{\hat{X}}_{1234}^{T}\text{}\frac{2}{3}{\hat{Y}}_{1234}^{T}-\frac{1}{3}{\hat{Z}}_{1234}^{T}\text{}\\ -\frac{1}{3}{\hat{X}}_{1234}^{T}-\frac{1}{3}{\hat{Y}}_{1234}^{T}\text{}\frac{2}{3}{\hat{Z}}_{1234}^{T}\\ \text{}{\hat{Y}}_{12}^{T}\text{}{\hat{X}}_{12}^{T}0\\ \text{}{\hat{Z}}_{13}^{T}0{\hat{X}}_{13}^{T}\\ 0{\hat{Z}}_{23}^{T}\text{}{\hat{Y}}_{23}^{T}\end{array}\right]$ **eq 2.2-2**

The projection schemes are detailed in [2]. The main lines of the derivation of their operators $\bar{{B}_{n}}$ will nevertheless be indicated.

For the ADS element, this amounts first to decomposing the operator $\hat{{B}_{n}}$ into the sum of a spherical term and a deviatoric term. Then, the spherical part is under-integrated (i.e. evaluated at the point $\xi =\eta =\zeta =0$ , which amounts to cancelling it). This procedure thus makes it possible to treat the diagonal terms of the strain, hence the volumetric locking, and one can verify in **eq 2.2-2** that the sum of the first three terms in each of the column vectors is zero. To avoid transverse-shear locking, one must this time act on the off-diagonal terms of the strain, and therefore cancel those responsible for excessive shear. The result of this step is expressed by the removal of certain terms in the last three rows of the operator $\hat{{B}_{n}}$ (compare the formulae giving $\hat{{B}_{n}}$ and $\bar{{B}_{n}}$ ).

For the ASQBI element, the treatment of shear locking is the same as for the ADS element. It follows that the last three rows of the operator $\bar{{B}_{n}}$ are identical in the two elements. On the other hand, the treatment of volumetric locking is slightly different for the ASQBI element (see **eq 2.2-1**). One can however verify that, when $\nu \to \frac{1}{2}$ , hence $\bar{\nu}\to 1$ , the sum of the first three terms in each column vector of $\bar{{B}_{n}}$ is zero.

### 3.3 Choice of the finite elements

The two finite elements ADS and ASQBI have been compared on a certain number of tests. It emerges that ASQBI gives better results than ADS in elasticity whereas ADS gives better results in plasticity, its behaviour being a little too soft in the elastic cases. The test case of the cantilever beam in bending [V6.04.196](doc:v6.04.196) is a good illustration of this. These observations are in good agreement with those of Belytschko and Bindeman [2].

The ASQBI element in elasticity and the ASD element in plasticity have therefore been chosen for implementation in Aster in order to benefit in each case from the best element. Moreover, the user is spared having to choose the finite element. It is recalled that the only difference between the two elements is the matrix $\bar{{B}_{n}}$ .

## 4 Integration of the element in Code_Aster

### 4.1 Description and use

This element relies on the 3D volume mesh cells HEXA8.

#### 4.1.1 Modelling

The modelling 3D_SI is assigned to the designated HEXA8 mesh cells. The usual face elements of the 3D modelling are assigned to the QUAD4 mesh cells.

#### 4.1.2 Material

All the material coefficients relating to the constitutive laws valid in small strains for the 3D modellings can be used.

#### 4.1.3 Boundary conditions and loading

All the loadings and boundary conditions available on the 3D elements and the face elements are available.

#### 4.1.4 Post-processing options

All the post-processing options usually available for the 3D modellings can be used.

#### 4.1.5 Linear buckling analysis

The option RIGI_MECA_GE being activated in the catalogue of the element, it is possible to carry out a classical buckling analysis after assembly of the elastic and geometric stiffness matrices.

#### 4.1.6 Non-linear analyses

All the constitutive behaviours available for the usual 3D modellings can be used, in small strains. As regards large strains, the only usable option is the approximation PETIT_REAC.

Numerical integration is carried out with one Gauss point, just as in material non-linearity.

### 4.2 Implementation

The options are activated in the catalogue meca_hexs8.cata.

### 4.3 Validation

The tests validating this element are, in version 9 of *Code_Aster*:

- SSNV196: 3D beam in bending in elasticity and in plasticity
- HSNV125G, PERFE01A, SDNV103, SSND105 use a single element to validate non-linear constitutive behaviours, thus allowing savings in analysis time (1 single Gauss point)

## 5 Conclusion

The 8-node hexahedral element under-integrated with one integration point and stabilised by the ASM method gives correct results on problems where the standard HEXA8 locks (bending, shear), as shown by the test case SSNV196. Moreover it gives significant gains in analysis time.

It nevertheless has a drawback in statics (the same as the analogous 2D element, the stabilised under-integrated QUAD4): when the elements are not parallelepiped, the results are affected, or even wrong if the elements have angles far from 90°. The recommended solution is then to resort, on these mesh cells, to the standard HEXA8 (with 8 Gauss points). In explicit dynamics, on the other hand, this drawback does not remain. But the present implementation concerns statics only.
