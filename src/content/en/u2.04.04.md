# u2.04.04 User's guide to contact in code_aster

> **Summary.**

This document describes the procedure to be followed in order to take frictional-contact conditions into account in non-linear studies. The concern is surface contact between deformable solids, or between a deformable solid and a rigid solid. First, what it means to take frictional contact into account in structural mechanics is recalled, then the broad outline of a contact problem in code_aster is drawn: pairing and solution.

Contact is defined with the command DEFI_CONTACT, while the solution is carried out with the commands STAT_NON_LINE or DYNA_NON_LINE. Recommendations are given for the parameterisation of the pairing and for the choice of the solution methods in these operators.

Finally, various methodologies are mentioned (contact with a rigid surface, recovering a contact pressure in post-processing, large strains and contact, rigid-body motions blocked by contact, …). They make it possible to overcome the difficulties frequently encountered in studies. In this section, alternative modellings of the frictional-contact phenomenon by joint elements or by discrete elements (through the constitutive law) are also discussed.

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Purpose of this document

To say that two solid bodies placed in contact do not interpenetrate, but that on the contrary a reciprocal force is exerted by one on the other and that this force disappears when the bodies are no longer touching, is common sense. It is the most succinct definition that one can give of the “contact” problem: yet enforcing these conditions in a structural analysis code such as *Code_Aster* requires a great deal of effort.

Solving the contact problem amounts, in the end, to imposing an inequality boundary condition on certain displacement degrees of freedom (negative or zero gap) and to finding an additional unknown, which is the reciprocal force exerted between the two bodies.

The difficulty comes from the strong non-linearity induced by this “pseudo-boundary condition”. Indeed, the condition to be imposed on the displacements (in order to prevent any interpenetration) itself depends on the displacements (which will determine at which point the surfaces come into contact).

The non-linearity due to taking contact into account is separated in *Code_Aster* into two points:

- contact(-friction) non-linearity: it follows from the contact(-friction) conditions, which are not differentiable. To solve the problem, two main families of solution are available: the DISCRETE formulation and the CONTINUE formulation. The first family is suited to problems with a small number of contact unknowns and allows short computation times, whereas the second is suited to problems which require the other mechanical non-linearities to be taken into account together with frictional contact (plasticity and large transformations).
- geometric non-linearity: it follows from the large relative sliding liable to occur between the surfaces in contact (the effective final contact surfaces being unknown *a priori*). Use is made here of a fixed-point or Newton algorithm coupled with a geometric search.

In *Code_Aster*, in the presence of contact, the user must *at least* identify the potential contact surfaces. The solution technique then rests on two fundamental stages:

- Pairing phase: it makes it possible to treat the geometric non-linearity as a sequence of small-sliding problems (in which the problem is geometrically linear). The technique for determining the effective contact surfaces, and advice on the parameter setting of this phase, are given in section 2.
- Solution phase: it makes it possible to solve the constrained optimisation problem linked to the contact non-linearity and, possibly, to friction. The various optimisation algorithms available are presented in section 3. A path is given there for choosing an algorithm suited to one's study.

It is essential to have understood that frictional contact is a non-linearity in its own right, on the same footing as the material non-linearities (non-linear constitutive law) and the kinematic non-linearities (large displacements, large rotations). It therefore requires both a knowledge of the bases of contact theory and an understanding of its treatment in *Code_Aster*, in order to make the right modelling choices (mesh and data setup).

This document is here to assist the user in these choices.

### 1.2 A question of vocabulary

In order to make the reading easier, some of the terms used abundantly in this document are given here.

When one speaks of contact mechanics, two characteristic quantities are used:

- the gap, often denoted $g$ or $d$ . It characterises the signed distance between two contact surfaces;
- the contact force density $p$ . This is the reciprocal force exerted by one solid on the other when the gap is closed (zero). It is carried by the normal to the contact surfaces. The term contact pressure will also be used, loosely.
- Master and slave zones: this is a boundary of a solid on which the frictional-contact conditions will be imposed. Contact is defined by a “pair of solids”. In each pair, the master–slave role is crucial for the proper running of an analysis.
- Incompatible meshes: these are the cases in which the master mesh cell does not coincide with the slave mesh cell. These cases are extremely difficult to handle. The best method for these cases is the MORTAR methods known as LAC, usable in the continuous formulation only. One will also speak of strong or weak mesh incompatibilities. There is no rigorous criterion for declaring a strong or a weak incompatibility. Engineering judgement is left to decide according to the case to be treated. An example of strong and of weak incompatibility is nevertheless illustrated in the figure below.

- Contact states: this is the pair (gap–pressure) characterising a contact element. There are 2 possible contact states: contact (zero gap, non-zero pressure) and separation (non-zero gap, zero pressure). The two situations are mutually exclusive. Each state can have variants: grazing contact (gap almost zero, non-zero but low pressure), firm contact (zero gap and high contact pressure), grazing separation (the opposite of grazing contact), firm separation (the opposite of firm contact).
- Cycling: this is a frequent case of non-convergence in contact analyses. The cycling situation is when a contact point or a contact mesh cell has difficulty in stabilising its status (contact/no contact, sticking/sliding, forward sliding/backward sliding).
- Hertz–Signorini–Moreau contact laws: as with any constitutive law, the interfaces between solids have their own mathematical formalisms. The contact laws derive from a formalism of non-smooth mechanics (just as, for example, the problems of fracture mechanics or of cohesion): the absence of a differentiable energy from which a force–displacement relation can readily be written.
- LAC or Mortar LAC: Local Average Contact, a method suited to incompatible meshes.
- Oscillating contact pressures: in the case of incompatible meshes, it can happen that the contact pressures have an oscillating character. One notices it through a strong scatter of the contact-pressure values.

These quantities are described in detail *in the reference document* [R5.03.50](doc:r5.03.50).

In the presence of friction, one introduces in addition:

- the sliding direction $\overrightarrow{t}$
- the friction force density $\tau$ , carried by $-\vec{t}$ .

In *Code_Aster*, a Coulomb friction criterion is used; the friction conditions are described in [R5.03.50](doc:r5.03.50).

### 1.3 Alternative modellings of frictional contact

Although the way of treating the frictional-contact phenomenon described in the introduction and in the bulk of this document is the most widespread, it is not the only one. *Code_Aster* thus proposes two alternative modellings of the mechanical interactions:

- (hydro-)mechanical joint elements (modellings *_JOINT*) for the representation of the opening of a crack under the pressure of a fluid and of the friction between the faces of the closed crack
- discrete shock elements (modellings *_DIS_T*) for the representation of a point contact by springs, with possible account taken of friction

These two other modellings both rely on finite elements and therefore on specific constitutive laws (JOINT_MECA_FROT for the joint elements and DIS_CHOC for the discrete elements).

Further details on these elements are given in § 4.10 and § 4.11.

Finally, it will be noted that it is possible to model contact on the edges of a crack represented with the X-FEM method. The reader is referred to the user's guide [U2.05.02](doc:u2.05.02) for further information.

## 2 Pairing

### 2.1 Notion of contact zones and contact surfaces

It is always for the user to define the **potential** contact surfaces: there is no automatic mechanism in *Code_Aster* for detecting the possible interpenetrations in a structure.

The user therefore supplies, in the command file, a list of pairs of contact surfaces. Each pair contains a surface said to be “master” and a surface said to be “slave”. Such a pair is called a “contact **zone**”.

MAIT_ESCL pairing case:

The contact conditions will be imposed zone by zone. Enforcing the contact consists in **preventing the slave nodes from penetrating inside the master surfaces** (the converse, on the other hand, is possible).

In the example below (*cf.*), the structure studied is made up of three solids; three potential contact zones have been defined, symbolised by the red ellipses. As their name indicates, these contact zones determine parts of the structure in which bodies are **liable** to come into contact. This means that the frictional-contact conditions are enforced there, the effective activation of contact depending *in the end* on the imposed loading.

There is no restriction on the number of contact zones. The zones must however be separated, that is to say that the intersection of two distinct zones must be empty 1
. Furthermore, within a zone, the master and slave surfaces of one and the same zone must also have a zero intersection: if this is not the case, the analysis is stopped. When a node is necessarily common to the master and slave surfaces, because of a meshing constraint for example, see § 2.3.4 for a solution. In the case where a continuous formulation is used (*cf.* 3.1.3), the slave surfaces must imperatively be pairwise disjoint.

One should not hesitate to describe wide contact zones in order to avoid any interpenetration. It is the number of nodes of the slave surface that is decisive in the computation cost. The master surface may, for its part, be as large as one wishes.

**It is imperative that the nodes of the contact surfaces (master and slave) all carry displacement degrees of freedom (DX, DY and possibly DZ), that is to say that they belong to mesh cells of the model**. An error message stops the user if this is not the case. See § 4.6 for the modelling of a contact with a rigid surface.

Figure 2.1-1: Definition of three contact zones

MORTAR pairing case:

When one has analyses with incompatible meshes and one needs to estimate the contact states precisely, it is recommended to use the MORTAR LAC method. This method makes it possible to carry out contact analyses on intersecting pairs, SURFACE ESCL on SURFACE MAIT, unlike the MAIT_ESCL pairing method, which associates NOEUDS with SEGMENTS.

To do this, there is a necessary stage which consists in pre-processing the mesh before the use of DEFI_CONTACT: CREA_MAILLAGE/DECOUPE_LAC. This stage prepares the slave contact surface for an integration of the contact terms of MORTAR type on the intersected mesh cells.

This method, because it uses a surface-to-surface approach, respects the master/slave and slave/master interpenetration conditions better. As for the MAIT_ESCL method, the user must define the potential contact zones pair by pair. There is no restriction on the number of contact zones.

Two distinct zones may have common nodes but not common mesh cells, because the contact condition is imposed on the mesh cells. Furthermore, within a zone, the master and slave surfaces of one and the same zone must also have a zero intersection. The kinematic contact quantities for the MORTAR method are geometric fields per element.

### 2.2 Choice of the master and slave surfaces

As has just been said, each contact zone is made up of a master surface and a slave surface. In the present state, one cannot do self-contact in *Code_Aster* (except in the rare cases where one can predict the future contact zone and thus define a slave and a master).

The need to differentiate the two surfaces comes from the technique adopted in the computation of the gap. This computation is carried out in a phase that is called **pairing**.

In the case of the MAIT_ESCL pairing, the gap is defined at every point of the slave surface (for the discrete methods these are the nodes, for the continuous methods the integration points) as the minimum distance to the master surface. This dissymmetry implies a choice which can *a priori* prove difficult (how is one to decide?). The points which must prevail in this choice are given in the following paragraphs.

In the case of the MORTAR pairing, the gap is defined by the intersected slave-surface–master-surface patch.

These surfaces are entered in the operator DEFI_CONTACT under the factor keyword ZONE.

#### 2.2.1 Case in which a surface must be chosen as master (GROUP_MA_MAIT)

When one of these conditions is met:

- one of the two surfaces is **rigid** (a);
- one of the two surfaces **covers** the other (b);
- one of the two surfaces has an apparent stiffness which is **large** compared with the other (“apparent” in the sense that one is not speaking of Young's moduli but of stiffnesses in ${\mathit{N.m}}^{-1}$ ) (c);
- one of the two surfaces is meshed much more **coarsely** than the other (d);

then this surface must be chosen as the master surface.

#### 2.2.2 Case in which a surface must be chosen as slave (GROUP_MA_ESCL)

When one of these conditions is met:

- one of the two surfaces is **curved** (a);
- one of the two surfaces is **smaller** than the other (b);
- one of the two surfaces has an apparent stiffness which is **small** compared with the other (c);
- one of the two surfaces is meshed much more **finely** than the other (d);

then this surface must be chosen as the slave surface.

#### 2.2.3 General case

When studying complex structures, it happens that the rules given in § 2.2.1 and § 2.2.2 are difficult to apply. For example, when a solid is almost rigid (with respect to the other solid) and is curved, rule (a) does not make it possible to decide: should the curved character or the rigid character be given priority?

In these situations, “engineering judgement” must prevail. In our example, if the two solids undergo small sliding, the curved character of the rigid solid will have little influence and one will therefore choose the latter as the master surface.

When convergence problems are encountered (especially in plasticity), it is very likely that the choices of the master and slave sides are not judicious. In this case, change the role of the surfaces.

#### 2.2.4 Orientation of the normals

It is essential always to orient the **normals** of the contact surfaces so that they are **outward**. This can be done with the operator MODI_MAILLAGE. According to whether the surface to be oriented is a skin mesh cell of a solid element, a shell or a beam, one will use respectively the keywords ORIE_PEAU_2D or ORIE_PEAU_3D, ORIE_NORM_COQUE, ORIE_LIGNE.

In the case of ORIE_LIGNE, the tangent is oriented, so as to be able to produce the normal systematically by a vector product.

By default (keyword VERI_NORM of DEFI_CONTACT), the correct orientation of the normals is checked and the user is stopped if need be.

#### 2.2.5 Fineness and degree of the mesh of curved surfaces

When the contact surfaces are curved, the proper continuity of the normal to the facets must be guaranteed. For this, one can either:

- mesh finely with linear elements and use the smoothing option (*cf.* § 2.3.2)
- mesh with quadratic elements

For the quadratic mesh to retain its interest, the **mid-side nodes must have been placed on the geometry** in the mesher, and the operator CREA_MAILLAGE/LINE_QUAD of *Code_Aster* must not have been used.

**Discrete formulation case:**

In the case of quadratic contact surfaces, **in the discrete formulation the contact surfaces must not be made up of 8-node quadrangular mesh cells** (QUAD8), and the 9-node mesh cells (QUAD9) will therefore be preferred. The HEXA20 will then be transformed into HEXA27 and the PENTA15 into PENTA18 (with the operator CREA_MAILLAGE). At the present time, mixed meshes made up of both HEXA20 and PENTA15 cannot be transformed by CREA_MAILLAGE.

If, however, the use of HEXA20 elements proves mandatory, the linear relations written automatically on this occasion may be liable to conflict with boundary conditions (in particular of symmetry); this is why it may be necessary to impose the boundary conditions only on the vertex nodes of the QUAD8 mesh cells concerned (the operator DEFI_GROUP can be used for the creation of the ad hoc node group).

**Continuous formulation case:**

**In the continuous formulation (ALGO_CONT='STANDARD'), for curved boundary mesh cells, the use of QUAD8 or TRIA6 elements can lead to violations of the contact law**: the latter is satisfied on average. One then observes slightly positive or slightly negative gaps in the presence of contact, which can disturb the results close to the contact zone or the analyses restarted with an initial state. For this reason it is **advisable** to use HEXA27 or PENTA18 elements (with QUAD9 faces) or else linear elements.

When, at the end of an analysis, one notices a high rate of interpenetration of the master nodes inside the slave surfaces (which is possible, unlike the converse), this generally means that the mesh of one or both surfaces is too coarse, or that there is too great a difference in fineness between the two meshes of the surfaces. One can then either refine, or swap master and slave.

If a surface is rigid (and therefore master), a coarse mesh is sufficient, except of course in the curved zones.

Finally, in the particular case of a **cylinder-on-cylinder** or **sphere-on-sphere** contact, care must be taken to **mesh each surface sufficiently** in order to avoid leaving too much void between them. Indeed, in *Code_Aster*, for the moment neither node repositioning nor projections onto splines passing through the master surface are carried out; a mesh that is too coarse will then cause a **strong oscillation of the contact pressure** (detection of contact on every other node).

In the case where there are oscillations in the contact pressures due to a strongly incompatible mesh in the contact zone, the method **ALGO_CONT='LAC'** must be preferred.

#### 2.2.6 Sharp corners

The pairing algorithms work less well in the presence of sharp corners, which is why one will avoid having them as far as possible in the mesh of the master and slave surfaces. For example, one will prefer to model a fillet rather than a sharp corner.

If a sharp corner is indispensable, the surface which carries it will be chosen as slave.

#### 2.2.7 Mesh quality

The quality of the surface elements which make up the master contact surface has a direct impact on the quality of the pairing. Indeed, distorted mesh cells, for example, can harm the accuracy of the projections despite the robustness of the algorithm: the uniqueness of the projection is no longer guaranteed.

For these reasons, it is recommended to check the quality of the meshes produced and, if necessary, to correct their defects. In *Code_Aster*, the command MACR_INFO_MAIL makes it possible to display the distribution of the elements as a function of their quality.

### 2.3 Control of the pairing

#### 2.3.1 Choice of the type of pairing

In *Code_Aster*, three types of pairing are available:

- “master–slave” (by default): this is the most generic; it makes it possible to prevent the nodes of the slave surface from penetrating the mesh cells of the master surface by means of orthogonal projections of a node onto a mesh cell. It is available for the discrete formulation and the continuous formulation (**ALGO_CONT='STANDARD'**).
- “nodal”: it makes it possible to prevent the slave nodes from penetrating the master nodes along a direction (given by the slave normal). This is a pairing reserved for compatible meshes of the contact surfaces, for analyses in small sliding. It is not available in the continuous formulation (*cf.* § 3.1.3).
- “MORTAR” (by default): this is the one of highest quality; it makes it possible to impose the contact conditions on average on the intersected mesh cells. With this method, interpenetration accuracies lower than 1.E-10% are reached. It is available only for the continuous formulation (**ALGO_CONT='LAC'**).

#### 2.3.2 Smoothing of the normals

As its name indicates, this option makes it possible to smooth the normals. It is particularly useful in the case of curved surfaces meshed with linear elements. This process is based on the average of the normals at the nodes, then on their interpolation from the shape functions and the averaged normals; it makes it possible to ensure the **continuity** of the normal at the nodes.

The normal is then no longer the geometric normal; one will therefore take the precaution (advisable in any case) of checking the results visually carefully.

A check of the faceting of the surfaces is carried out automatically at the end of the time step. It emits an information message when the faceting becomes too great, and it is then advisable to activate the smoothing.

#### 2.3.3 Choice of the normals: case of a formulation other than the LAC method

It is advisable always to leave the default values: NORMALE='MAIT', VECT_MAIT='AUTO'. That is to say that the non-penetration relation is written from the master normal, determined from the mesh.

However, there exist a few rare situations in which one may wish to impose the choice of the normal: these are essentially the treatment of beam–beam contact (in 2D only) and the case in which the master surface is a mesh cell of type POI1. The reader is referred to §3.1.6 of [U4.44.11](doc:u4.44.11) for further details.

#### 2.3.4 Exclusion of slave nodes from the pairing: case of a formulation other than the LAC method

The keyword SANS_GROUP_NO/SANS_NOEUD is used to exclude slave nodes from the pairing. There may be several reasons for this:

- the master and slave surfaces have a non-empty intersection (crack front, blocking of rigid-body motions); the common nodes do not need to be treated by the contact, and they must therefore be excluded.
- there already exist, on the slave nodes considered, linear relations (boundary conditions, blocking of rigid-body motions); if these interfere with the contact direction (respectively the friction direction), it is generally advisable to give priority to the boundary conditions and therefore not to solve the contact on these nodes.

A fatal error is emitted when there exist nodes common to the master and slave surfaces and these have not been excluded.

### 2.4 Understanding the geometric non-linearity

As has been explained, the geometric non-linearity follows from the fact that frictional-contact conditions must be applied on a geometric configuration which is not known. In this section, a short digression is made in order to explain the approach adopted to overcome this difficulty.

#### 2.4.1 Small-sliding hypothesis

The pairing phase is a phase prior to the formulation of the contact conditions to be solved. In practice, this means:

- for the discrete methods, the construction of a matrix $A$ (for Pairing) such that, multiplied by the displacement increment $\delta u$ from the paired configuration, it gives the (linearised) gap increment.
- for the continuous method, the association between a contact point and its projection in the parametric space of the paired master mesh cell. It is by updating the coordinates of the master mesh cell with the displacement $\delta u$ that one obtains the new (linearised) coordinates of the projection.

Just as for the equilibrium conditions, **the contact conditions are expressed on the deformed configuration** (or final configuration). This configuration is not known *a priori*.

The hypothesis of small relative sliding of the surfaces in contact is the analogue of the small-perturbation hypothesis (for the writing of the equilibrium relations).

It consists in saying that the final configuration of the surfaces in contact differs little from the initial configuration, which therefore makes it possible to carry out the pairing once and for all at the beginning of the analysis, on the initial configuration. Then to use the conditions established on this configuration for the whole analysis.

Such a problem is then geometrically linear: only the frictional-contact non-linearity remains; it is treated with suitable algorithms (*cf.* section 3).

#### 2.4.2 General case

To treat problems of large relative sliding of the surfaces in contact, two possibilities exist: the use of a fixed-point loop in order to come back to the small-sliding cases, or else, for the continuous formulation (§ 3.1.3), the simultaneous solution within Newton's algorithm.

##### 2.4.2.1 Fixed-point loop (ALGO_RESO_GEOM='POINT_FIXE')

The approach adopted is very similar to the solution of a non-linear problem by Newton's method. **A geometrically non-linear problem is transformed into a sequence of geometrically linear problems**. For this, a sequence of problems will be solved under the small-sliding hypothesis.

That is to say that a pairing is carried out (on a balanced initial configuration) and a Newton solution (with solution of the contact, as will be explained in section 3). This gives a new configuration; if this configuration is “close” to the initial configuration then one has converged (it was therefore the final configuration), otherwise one loops: a pairing is done again, then a solution … and so on until the final configuration is found (*cf.*).

The difficulty lies in the characterisation of the convergence of this fixed-point process. What are two “close” configurations? In *Code_Aster*, these are two configurations whose “mechanical” displacement vector for passing from one to the other (that is to say the displacement increment obtained by Newton, restricted to the degrees of freedom DX, DY, DZ) has an infinity norm which is small compared with the infinity norm of the previous displacement vector.

This implies that one therefore always performs at least two geometry iterations with this criterion (in order to give oneself an initial displacement vector). The reader is referred to paragraph 3.7 of [R5.03.50](doc:r5.03.50) for the exact expression of the infinity norm.

##### 2.4.2.2 Generalised Newton algorithm (ALGO_RESO_GEOM='NEWTON')

The continuous formulation (§ 3.1.3) offers the possibility of treating the geometric non-linearity directly within Newton's algorithm. For this, a pairing is carried out at each iteration and the geometric terms of the tangent matrix are themselves also updated.

The geometric convergence criterion thus becomes an additional criterion of Newton's algorithm: the displacement increment must tend towards zero.

#### 2.4.3 Convergence of the geometry loop

##### 2.4.3.1 Linearisation of the normal

The pairing supplies two pieces of information:

- the gap on the paired configuration,
- the coordinates of the potential contact point on the master surface.

Whether a discrete or a continuous contact method is used, the principle of virtual power must be differentiated (linearised), in particular the contact terms.

The fixed-point hypothesis leads one also to make the hypothesis of a small variation of the normal during the Newton iterations (of a time step). This hypothesis is consistent with the small-sliding hypothesis. The “gap” operator is thus linearised more easily. It remains in particular constant during a geometry iteration. This also has another implication: the geometric convergence towards the final configuration in fixed-point mode can sometimes be very slow.

Conversely, the solution by a generalised Newton method, possible only in the continuous formulation (§ 3.1.3), presents a much faster convergence but can prove less robust. This is why it is not the default setting. To activate it, the keyword ALGO_RESO_GEOM will be used.

##### 2.4.3.2 Geometric convergence criterion

It has been seen above that the convergence of the geometry loop is done on a geometric criterion: the difference of the displacement vectors between two successive geometric configurations is relatively small. The displacement vectors are evaluated only on the contact zone. For the particular case of the generalised Newton algorithm in the continuous formulation, the criterion applies directly to the Newton displacement increment.

For the solution by a fixed-point loop, “small” corresponds by default to less than 1% of the displacement since the beginning of the time step, whereas with the generalised Newton algorithm it is 0.0001% (the latter benefiting, in favourable cases, from a better convergence, it is possible to require a tighter criterion).

When, following an analysis, one observes an interpenetration of the slave nodes into the master surface, the only explanation 2
2.2.5
is a failure to satisfy the geometric criterion.

**One must then not hesitate to tighten the criterion**. For this, the keyword RESI_GEOM=0.005 is used so as to lower the threshold to around 0.5%, for example.

Sometimes the geometric convergence is simply slow; in this case it is enough to increase the maximum number of iterations tolerated: ITER_GEOM_MAXI=20, for example.

In certain situations, however much one varies the value of the criterion or the number of iterations, the analysis does not converge: it cycles. Several possibilities are then open to the user:

- the most common explanation is a poor discretisation of the contact surfaces (that is to say a mesh that is too coarse, a difference in fineness between the two surfaces, or a poor choice of the master and slave surfaces). The reader is then referred to § 2.2.
- when the contact surfaces are curved and meshed relatively coarsely, the explanation may come from too great a discontinuity of the normal (faceting). **Activating the smoothing then very often makes convergence easier** (*cf.* § 2.3.2). This must not, however, prevent the user from reviewing the mesh.
- if the case is truly pathological, one must oneself force the number of updates by using REAC_GEOM='CONTROLE' and NB_ITER_GEOM=n. After $n$ geometry iterations, the analysis will pass to the following time step whatever the value of the geometric criterion, but it will emit an alarm when the criterion is not satisfied to within less than 1% (for $n\ge 2$ ).

## 3 Solution

### 3.1 General scheme of the solution algorithm

#### 3.1.1 Definition and general remarks

What is called “solution of the contact” is the operation consisting in solving the system formed by the juxtaposition of the classical equations of mechanics and of the frictional-contact equations (the geometric aspect being treated by the pairing, there remain at this stage only the threshold non-linearity of friction and the status non-linearity of contact).

It must be noted that the two formulations available in the code differ markedly on this point. Without going into the details, these differences are explained briefly for what follows.

Although the discrete and continuous formulations do indeed amount to solving the same physical problem, as their name indicates they do not formulate it numerically in the same way. The differences between these two formulations are presented in a synthetic manner.

**Discrete formulation case.**

In the discrete formulation, the frictional-contact conditions are applied to the discretised system by means of Newton “sub-iterations”. In a typical Newton iteration there are two stages: first, **only** the solution of the linear system obtained by Newton $\mathit{Ku}=f$ is computed, with the initial contact conditions, then, second, by various methods of constrained optimisation condensed onto the contact zone, the contact inequality conditions are solved. This second stage makes it possible to recompute the “true” contact pressures. For the following Newton iteration, the right-hand side is modified so as to be able to take into account the new contact conditions coming from the second stage. For discrete penalisation, the matrix $K$ is also modified in order to limit the interpenetrations. This technique makes it possible to solve efficiently problems with few contact degrees of freedom. One can say that the discrete formulation imposes the contact conditions algebraically without building a continuous contact element.

**Continuous formulation case.**

In the continuous formulation, a mixed variational formulation is written in order to take the frictional-contact equations. The variational formulation is of classical Lagrangian type for the LAC method, of augmented Lagrangian type for the STANDARD method, and of penalised Lagrangian type for the PENALISATION method. The approach adopted to solve the non-linear system is to create **continuous** late contact elements. These late contact elements carry master–slave displacement degrees of freedom as well as Lagrange multipliers **only** on the slave side. On execution of DEFI_CONTACT, contact pairs are created transparently for the user, making it possible to describe the degrees of freedom according to the topology of the mesh cells potentially in contact. Likewise, at the moment of the solution in STAT_NON_LINE, **continuous** elements are created **per contact pair**, which make it possible to compute the elementary matrices and vectors which will be assembled into the global stiffness of the mechanical system. A typical Newton iteration therefore supplies, at each solution, displacements but also Lagrange multipliers (LAGS_C). The principal advantage of the continuous method is to offer, via the degree of freedom LAGS_C (in the field DEPL), **access to the contact pressure on the slave surface.** Attention is however drawn to the fact that this quantity is in fact only **a contact force density per unit area expressed on the reference configuration**. In particular, in large strains, it can no longer be called a pressure, because it no longer has a physical meaning. Because of the size of the system, the continuous formulation is often less efficient than the discrete formulation, but more robust and of better quality in various situations (plasticity + contact, for example).

#### 3.1.2 Discrete formulation

To illustrate the definition of the previous paragraph, the general scheme of the algorithm is given in the case of a discrete formulation. The following remarks can be made on this scheme:

- it represents only a single time step, assuming that one places oneself in small sliding (the outer loop is therefore not shown, as in the figure treating the geometric non-linearity and described in § 2.4);
- in this scheme, the three classical stages of a Newton iteration appear: assembly and solution of the linear system, integration of the constitutive law, analysis of the convergence;
- the particular feature of the discrete formulation of contact consists in **the addition** of a supplementary stage between the solution of the linear system (without contact) and the integration of the constitutive law. **This stage can be seen as a post-processing of the solution of the system without contact.**

The supplementary stage carried out by the “discrete contact” box has as its purpose the construction and then the solution of the system augmented by the contact and friction conditions. Two approaches exist for formulating the discrete frictional-contact conditions:

- writing of a Lagrangian and dualisation of the frictional-contact conditions; the size of the global system to be solved is then artificially increased and an optimisation algorithm is used to satisfy the inequality constraints. This approach is treated in § 3.2.1.
- penalisation (or regularisation) of the frictional-contact conditions; the same size is kept for the global system but the matrix is enriched; there is no specific algorithm, it is Newton's algorithm which ensures convergence. On the other hand, the contact is solved only approximately and the user must supply one or more parameters to control the algorithm. This approach is treated in § 3.2.2 and § 3.3.2.

What the “discrete contact” box produces as output is a displacement field satisfying the frictional-contact conditions, as well as frictional-contact reactions. These reactions are used in the check of equilibrium.

The discrete formulation therefore relies on the solution of a mechanical problem without contact, which has an important consequence: **one cannot simply treat the case of a structure in which contact, like friction, takes part directly in the blocking of the rigid-body motions** (*cf.* § 4.4).

#### 3.1.3 Continuous formulation: case ALGO_CONT='STANDARD'/'PENALISATION'

It gives the general algorithm for the solution of frictional contact with a continuous formulation; this differs markedly from the diagram in the discrete formulation. Whereas with the latter, frictional contact is solved by sub-iterations (in the “Discrete Contact” box), the continuous formulation relies on a decoupling of the non-linearities:

- the friction non-linearity (the Coulomb threshold depends on the contact pressure, which is itself an unknown) is treated by a fixed point on the value of the contact multiplier, or else by a generalised Newton algorithm
- the contact non-linearity relies on a status algorithm (with switching in batches), or else on a generalised Newton algorithm

When all the non-linearities are decoupled, only the classical material and kinematic non-linearities remain in Newton's algorithm.

To formulate the contact terms in a weak manner, an augmented or penalised Lagrangian is used, which makes it possible to regularise the original non-differentiable system. Each Newton iteration in the continuous formulation does not cost more in memory than in an analysis without contact of equivalent size, unlike the discrete formulation. Nevertheless, the nesting of the loops, or else the treatment by the generalised Newton algorithm, implies a greater number of (Newton) iterations.

In the continuous formulation, there exist additional degrees of freedom in the modelling, a consequence of the variational writing of the contact conditions, as explained in § 4.3.2.

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.04/Cadre3.gif)

Figure 3.1.3-1: General algorithm of a time step in the continuous formulation with a fixed point (small sliding)

In the continuous formulation, two algorithms exist for controlling the variables specific to contact (internal variables, by analogy with the constitutive laws):

- fixed-point method on the contact statuses: the state of the contact statuses is evaluated in a loop external to the Newton loop. To choose the algorithm, the global keyword ALGO_RESO_CONT='POINT_FIXE' must be used. The fixed-point method (ALGO_RESO_CONT='POINT_FIXE') is the most robust but also the most costly, since the non-linear problem (plasticity, for example) is solved at each change of the contact statuses.
- generalised Newton method: the contact statuses are evaluated at each Newton iteration (this is the default). The generalised Newton method (ALGO_RESO_CONT='NEWTON') is more efficient but sometimes poses convergence problems. The keyword ADAPTATION makes it possible to make this mode of convergence robust. In the case where one does not manage to converge on the statuses despite the keyword ADAPTATION, one must return to a fixed-point method.

#### 3.1.4 Continuous formulation: case ALGO_CONT='LAC'

This method makes it possible to solve, in an averaged manner, the pressures and the gaps on the intersected contact mesh cells. It belongs to the family of methods of MORTAR type, which are reputed for their ability to treat interface problems in mechanics. It has meaning only per contact element. The problems linked to the exclusion of redundant nodes for the standard/penalised continuous formulation with the boundary conditions are not a problem for this method, since the contact conditions are not imposed at the nodes but per element.

The LAC method (Local Average Contact) does not use a fixed-point loop. All the contact non-linearities (statuses + geometry) can vary from one Newton iteration to the next. Only the convergence criteria make it possible to control the quality of the contact solution. At the present time the LAC method does not yet solve friction.

Finally, in order to use the LAC method, it is necessary to carry out a mesh pre-processing phase (CREA_MAILLAGE/DECOUPE_LAC), which consists in preparing the slave “patches” for the contact conditions checked by macro mesh cell.

#### 3.1.5 Continuous formulation: treatment of the incompatibilities

In the case of meshes in which the incompatibility is weak, the standard/penalised continuous methods can be used. To appreciate the influence of the mesh compatibility, one can activate, under the keyword ZONE of DEFI_CONTACT, a keyword which reduces the oscillations of the contact pressures: INTEGRATION. The keyword has, however, two drawbacks: the number of active contact statuses is not available at the end of each computed increment, and in addition there is no CONT_NOEU at the end of the analysis. One must therefore fall back on post-processing with CALC_PRESSION. In the case of strong mesh incompatibilities, the LAC method makes it possible to have a good quality of the contact pressures.

On the example of the analytical test case “Taylor patch test”, ssnp170 (contact between two parallelepipedal blocks with an analytical pressure of -25 MPa), one notices that, according to the parameter setting of the integration of the contact terms, the oscillations disappear or not.

### 3.2 Solution of a problem with contact alone

#### 3.2.1 Dualisation in the discrete formulation (FORMULATION='DISCRETE')

##### 3.2.1.1 Principle

The dualisation of the discrete system consists in the introduction of a Lagrangian (see [R5.03.50](doc:r5.03.50)). The system to be solved takes the following form when it is reduced onto the active links:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}C.\delta \tilde{u}+{A}_{\text{c}}^{T}.{\mu}_{i}={F}_{i}\\ {A}_{\text{c}}.\delta \tilde{u}={d}_{i-1}\end{array}
$$

Given that the solution of the system without contact has already been carried out, the solution of the following system is known:

$$
C.\delta u={F}_{i}
$$

The solution technique then relies on the use of the Schur complement of the system in order to transform the system:

$$
{S}_{\text{schur}}=-{A}_{c}.{C}^{-1}.({A}_{c}{)}^{T}
$$

The problem thus transformed has the size of the number of slave nodes and it is full. Two algorithms, to be chosen from, are available for treating this new problem:

- an active-constraint method (ALGO_CONT='CONTRAINTE') relying on the **explicit** construction and the factorisation of the Schur complement
- a projected conjugate-gradient method (ALGO_CONT='GCP') relying on the **iterative** solution of the system formed by the Schur complement of the system

It is to be noted that dualisation requires the use of a direct linear solver: in *Code_Aster*, this means 'MULT_FRONT' or 'MUMPS'.

Each of the 2 algorithms cited above does indeed carry out sub-iterations during which it is necessary to solve the linear system with $C$ the stiffness matrix of the global system without contact (which is much faster if $C$ is already factorised).

##### 3.2.1.2 Method 'CONTRAINTE'

Relying on a factorisation (hence a direct solver) to solve the system associated with the Schur complement, the 'CONTRAINTE' method **requires no parameter setting**. Furthermore, its convergence [3]_
is proved, which explains why it is the default method in the presence of contact.

Nevertheless, the use of a direct solver presents a major drawback: **this algorithm is not suitable as soon as the number of slave nodes exceeds a few hundred (500)**. Indeed, the factorisation of a full matrix very quickly becomes prohibitive.

The construction of the Schur complement can be accelerated by using the parameter NB_RESOL (*cf.* [U4.44.11](doc:u4.44.11), default value 10) at the expense of the memory consumed (the greater the total number of degrees of freedom, the more expensive the increase of this parameter). In order to optimise an analysis with the active-constraint method, it is advisable to carry out an analysis on one time step in order to find a time/memory compromise (*cf.* [U1.03.03](doc:u1.03.03) for reading the information on the memory consumed).

##### 3.2.1.3 Method 'GCP'

When the default contact method can no longer be used because it is too expensive, an alternative is the use of the 'GCP' method. As has been said above, this method consists in the application of an iterative solver (projected conjugate gradient) to solve the dual problem.

**The principal advantage of such a method is no longer to be limited in the size** of the problem (several thousand slave nodes are perfectly attainable). The counterpart, specific to any iterative solver, is a mandatory parameter setting for the user.

This method can be used in a parallel analysis; it is, moreover, the only discrete method really to benefit from it.

Like any iterative solver, the 'GCP' method uses a convergence criterion: it is a criterion on the value of the gap. Given by the keyword RESI_ABSO, it controls the maximum interpenetration tolerated.

It is mandatory and is expressed in the same unit as that used for the mesh. It is advisable, as a first step, to use a criterion equal to ${10}^{-3}$ times the mean interpenetration when contact is not taken into account (see § 4.8).

If difficulties of convergence of the projected conjugate-gradient algorithm are observed, there exist 2 parameters on which it is advisable to act (in an additive manner, that is to say one and then the other):

- use a non-admissible line search (RECH_LINEAIRE='NON_ADMISSIBLE')
- use a Dirichlet preconditioner (PRE_COND='DIRICHLET')

The preconditioner has the advantage of being optimal and therefore appreciably reduces the number of iterations necessary for convergence. Furthermore, when one is close to the solution, it makes it possible to reduce the residual very rapidly and therefore to reach very low interpenetration criteria.

Its drawback is a significant cost, which can often prevent a gain in computation time despite the reduction in the number of iterations.

For this reason, it is possible to request its activation only when the residual has decreased sufficiently: the preconditioner then ideally makes it possible to converge in a few iterations. The difficulty lies in the quantification of “decreased sufficiently”, or in other words of the neighbourhood of the solution. This triggering is controlled by the keyword COEF_RESI, which is the coefficient (less than 1) by which the initial residual (hence the initial maximum interpenetration) must have been multiplied before applying the preconditioner. An example of the implementation of this parameter is given in the test case SSNA102E.

#### 3.2.2 Penalisation in the discrete formulation: algorithm 'PENALISATION'

Penalisation consists in regularising the contact problem: instead of seeking to solve exactly the conditions on the gap and the pressure, an approximate one-to-one relation is introduced, which implies that **an interpenetration will always be observed when contact is established**.

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.04/Cadre4.gif)

Figure 3.2.2-1: Contact condition (left) and regularisation (right)

As the figure shows, a parameter E_N is added in order to regularise the contact condition: the larger it is, the more one tends towards the exact condition; the smaller it is, the more interpenetration is tolerated.

In the discrete formulation, the notion of contact pressure does not exist, because one reasons on the nodes of the finite-element mesh: one therefore works with nodal forces (*cf.* § 4.3). The penalisation coefficient E_N therefore has the dimension of a stiffness (${\mathit{N.m}}^{-1}$ ).

The analogy is generally made between the penalisation coefficient and the stiffness of unilateral springs that one would place between the master and slave surfaces wherever interpenetration is observed.

E_N is generally chosen by successive trials:

- first, one will begin by taking a value equal to 10 times the greatest Young's modulus of the structure multiplied by a characteristic length of the structure;
- if the analysis gives a result (satisfactory or not), one will then increase the value by multiplying it by 10 each time, until a result that is stable in terms of displacements and, above all, in terms of stresses is obtained.

The advantage of the penalisation method is **not to increase the size of the system, unlike dualisation, but also not to restrict the choice of the linear solver**. The counterpart is a sensitivity to the penalisation coefficient, which implies systematically carrying out a parametric study before embarking on long analyses (*cf.* [U1.04.00](doc:u1.04.00) and [U2.08.07](doc:u2.08.07) for the launching of distributed parametric analyses).

To help calibrate the penalisation coefficient, there exists an automatic adaptation mechanism relying on the command DEFI_LIST_INST [U4.34.03](doc:u4.34.03). An example of implementation will be found in the test case SDNV103I [V5.03.103](doc:v5.03.103).

#### 3.2.3 Formulation 'CONTINUE': advice on the solvers and on parallelism

For the problem of contact alone, the continuous method has the advantage, like the (discrete) active-constraint method, of requiring no adjustment by the user.

Furthermore, as it **does not depend on a direct linear solver**, it is possible to use an iterative linear solver (such as 'GCPC' or 'PETSC') in order to gain enormously on the computation time. However, inasmuch as iterative solvers can prove less robust, it is advisable to turn to such a solver only once the analysis with frictional contact has been developed and validated. In any event, it is strongly advisable to return to a direct solver in the event of convergence difficulties.

When an iterative solver is used with the continuous formulation of frictional contact, it is advisable to activate the Newton–Krylov method (see the keyword METHODE of STAT_NON_LINE [U4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03)), which makes it possible to adapt automatically the convergence criterion of the linear solver.

#### 3.2.4 Other advice on parallelism

In the case where the user decides to use NORMALE='ESCL'/'MAIT_ESCL', parallelism is not available.

If implicit DYNA_NON_LINE + DEFI_CONTACT/continuous is used, the distribution is required to be centralised. For the discrete formulations, one is required to use DISTRIBUTION='CENTRALISE' in AFFE_MODELE.

For the discrete formulations, one is required to use DISTRIBUTION='CENTRALISE' in AFFE_MODELE.

### 3.3 Solution of a problem with friction

#### 3.3.1 Treatment of the threshold non-linearity

In *Code_Aster*, the only friction model available is that of Coulomb (see [R5.03.50](doc:r5.03.50)). An additional non-linearity must be treated in the presence of friction: it is the threshold non-linearity.

The friction threshold does indeed depend on the contact pressure, which is itself unknown.

Coulomb's law involves a coefficient $\mu$ , called the Coulomb coefficient. During the phase known as sticking, a point in contact does not move (it has a zero velocity and a tangential reaction exists). During the sliding phase, the point has a non-zero velocity and is subjected to a tangential reaction equal to $\mu$ times the normal reaction.

In general, **if the friction coefficient is very low, it is advisable to neglect friction**. Furthermore, it is advisable in studies to treat **only the contact at first**, in order to introduce the non-linearities one after another.

The discrete methods, whether they work by penalisation or by dualisation, rely on dedicated algorithms in the presence of friction (distinct from those used for contact), whereas the standard/penalised continuous method uses two different algorithms:

- fixed-point method on the friction thresholds: the threshold is updated in a loop external to the Newton loop (and to the loop on the contact statuses); ALGO_RESO_FROT='POINT_FIXE'.
- generalised Newton method: the friction non-linearity is treated in the Newton process, by explicit differentiation of all the non-linear terms. ALGO_RESO_FROT='NEWTON'.

#### 3.3.2 Discrete formulation: penalisation of friction (algorithm 'PENALISATION')

For 3D problems, or problems of large size, it is advisable to treat the friction problem by penalisation. This requires, as for the penalisation of contact, the entry of a penalisation parameter (E_T). More difficult to choose than its equivalent E_N, it requires a small parametric study to be carried out.

To draw the analogy with the case of the penalisation of contact, one will note that the sticking phase proper disappears (as soon as contact is activated there is interpenetration; in friction there is always sliding).

Convergence can also be accelerated by the use of the keyword COEF_MATR_FROT.

#### 3.3.3 Formulation 'CONTINUE': STANDARD/PENALISATION.

**This is the method of choice when a frictional-contact problem must be treated**: it is the most robust, and furthermore it tolerates large friction coefficients well (greater than $0,3$ ).

It is possible to choose between two solution algorithms for fixing the internal variables specific to frictional contact, with the keyword ALGO_RESO_XXXX (XXXX=CONT/FROT).

The fixed-point method (ALGO_RESO_XXXX='POINT_FIXE') is robust but costly. The generalised Newton method (ALGO_RESO_FROT='NEWTON', the default choice) is very efficient and offers a good level of robustness. The great advantage of this algorithm is its lesser dependence on the value of the friction coefficient, since there is no loop on the thresholds. A non-symmetric tangent matrix is produced, which represents a slight extra cost during factorisation and limits the range of usable iterative solvers.

It is preferable to use the generalised Newton method as soon as the friction coefficient is not negligible. The gains in computation time are very large (up to 80% gain compared with the fixed point).

The two algorithms 'POINT_FIXE'/'NEWTON' give identical results.

When, however, convergence difficulties appear, in particular in the presence of large sliding, the user will be able to set the coefficient COEF_FROT (which has the dimension of the inverse of a length). This parameter takes a value of 100 by default: values between ${10}^{-6}$ and ${10}^{6}$ will be tried. For studies in which sticking predominates, values of COEF_FROT lower than the default value will be favoured, whereas for cases in which sliding predominates, higher values will be chosen. There exist alternatives to the hard setting of COEF_CONT or COEF_FROT: these are the adaptive methods.

- A first fallback solution is to favour the continuous penalisation method with the adaptive methods (ALGO_CONT='PENALISATION'/ADAPTATION='TOUT'). Indeed, it is possible to activate an algorithm for the automatic control of the penalisation coefficient (via the analysis of the cycling). If one wishes only to control the penalisation coefficient without the contact statuses, then the keyword ADAPTATION='ADAPT_COEF' will be used. This method can fail in the sense that the control may not be effective, but it will act only on the speed of convergence and not on the quality of the results. Alternative options exist for getting round the convergence problems with friction:
- Activate the exact solution of the contact and the penalised solution of friction, and limit, as a first step, the number of geometric updates: ALGO_RESO_GEOM='CONTROLE', ZONE/ALGO_CONT='STANDARD'+ALGO_FROT='PENALISATION'.
- In the case where the technique above has not worked: consider using ADAPTATION='TOUT' in the keyword ZONE in order to treat both the cycling and the adaptation of the regularisation coefficients.
- There exists a mode which is activated as soon as the keyword ADAPTATION is active: it is FLIP-FLOP. It makes it possible to declare convergence on the contact status as soon as the contact pressure is stabilised in the contact zone. This pressure is an arithmetic mean of the contact pressures of all the zones.

### 3.4 Recap for the choice of the solution methods

#### 3.4.1 For frictional contact

For problems with a small number of contact degrees of freedom (fewer than 1000 degrees of freedom), a discrete formulation with the active-constraint algorithm ('CONTRAINTE') will be preferred. If friction must be activated, one will turn to a 'CONTINUE' formulation.

For problems with a large number of contact degrees of freedom (more than 1000 degrees of freedom), the iterative solution algorithm by active constraints 'GCP' is the most appropriate. If, however, friction must be taken into account, one will be able to turn once again to the 'CONTINUE' formulation.

For problems of large size (independently of the number of contact degrees of freedom), the solution of the linear system consumes a large part of the computation time; the choice of the linear solver is therefore essential. The 'CONTINUE' method is well suited in this respect, in that it leaves the user the choice of the linear solver and that it is well parallelised.

#### 3.4.2 For the linear system

If a discrete formulation is used (apart from penalisation), only the direct linear solvers are accessible. The solver 'MULT_FRONT' will therefore be chosen, unless a parallel analysis is being carried out, in which case 'MUMPS' will be selected. The 'GCP' method allied with the linear solver 'MUMPS' benefits from a good level of parallelisation in the contact algorithm.

If a continuous formulation is used, it is advisable, as soon as the global problem exceeds 100,000 degrees of freedom, to use an iterative solver associated with the preconditioner 'LDLT_SP' and with the Newton–Krylov method (see § 3.2.3). If the analysis implements friction, or else is parallel, the iterative solver 'PETSC' is the best choice.

A direct solver is indeed used to build the Schur complement, but the active-constraint method consists in activating or deactivating the contact links one by one until the global system is satisfied; it is therefore an iterative algorithm.

### 4.1 What to do when a contact analysis does not converge, or does not converge to the right solution?

Very often, convergence problems in contact have a physical origin and are not due to a robustness problem of the operator.

The advice given in what follows is only indicative. It is proposed, in practice, to follow the following list:

- Do not activate all the non-linearities at the same time. Always begin by activating contact alone in linear elasticity and see whether that converges.
- For very localised or point contacts, DEFI_CONTACT may not be the suitable operator (*cf.* AFFE_CHAR_MECA/LIAISON_MAIL, etc.).
- For incompatible meshes, consider using the LAC method, or changing the mesh if that is possible.
- For permanent contacts, the keyword GLISSIERE can be useful.
- In the event of failure of the integration of the constitutive law, check that this is not due to an inversion of a mesh cell caused by the frictional-contact condition (*cf.* Figure).

**- An analysis that has converged in the Newton sense is not necessarily a relevant analysis. It is for the person in charge of the study to take a critical look at the values obtained. From the point of view of contact, the presence of oscillations of the contact pressures must be watched, and the values of CONT_NOEU/CONT_ELEM must be checked. In the case of a penalisation algorithm, the values of maximum penetration visible in the convergence table must absolutely be looked at. A mesh-convergence study, or the comparison of the solution with a reference result, may be necessary in order to confirm the quality of the solution obtained.**

**- When an analysis has difficulty in converging on the statuses at a contact point (in ALGO_CONT='STANDARD'), the algorithm automatically proposes a switch to penalisation solution mode on the point which has difficulty in converging. The penalisation coefficient is chosen so as to limit the interpenetration, and the algorithm automatically switches back to the standard method as soon as convergence on the status is reached. It is for the user to check the quality of the result at the end of the analysis. If one wishes to switch this mechanism off, ADAPTATION='NON' must be used.**

- It happens that the analysis with contact converges in elasticity but poses a problem with a material non-linearity or with large transformations. Generally, the non-linear solution algorithm has difficulty when the solution computed at the first Newton iteration (prediction) is too far from the real solution. In this case, one solution is to start the analysis with a predictive displacement close to the final solution. To do this, the easiest consists in carrying out a first elastic analysis over one or two time steps close to the time instant of non-convergence. Then in using the computed elastic displacement as the predictor for the fully non-linear analysis: STAT_NON_LINE/NEWTON/PREDICTION = 'DEPL_CALCULE'.
- Use REAC_GEOM='CONTROLE' with a great deal of caution, and always check the result (visually, and the convergence table). The adaptive methods activated by the keyword ADAPTATION help convergence but can do nothing against a poor data setup.
- For analyses requiring the modelling of friction, begin first without friction. Activate ALGO_RESO_FROT='POINT_FIXE' in order to limit excessively strong variations of the Coulomb threshold.
- For analyses which are held only by the contact, the first piece of advice is to use springs (DIS_T or 2D_DIS_T, see § 4.4). Another solution is to carry out a first analysis piloted in displacement in order to initialise the contact, then to continue the analysis by piloting in force. - To control the quality of the solution, always check, in the STANDARD or PENALISATION method, whether the master mesh cells have not entered the slave mesh cells. Indeed, in the so-called node-to-segment method, the master mesh cell does not have the same role as the slave mesh cell. Only the slave mesh cell strictly respects the non-interpenetration law. In this case, it is generally because the mesh is too coarse. Consider refining it (*cf.* § 2.2). Another trick consists in multiplying the number of slave contact points if one does not wish to rework the mesh (INTEGRATION='NCOTES', ORDRE_INT=6).
- To control the quality of the solution, a diagnostic of the number of contact links that are really active at the end of each time step is available. This can help in understanding the mechanism studied.

### 4.3 Recovering the contact pressure

#### 4.3.1 Presentation of CALC_PRESSION

In the post-processing of a contact analysis, one generally wishes to access the frictional-contact forces. More precisely, one wishes to know the normal and tangential stress on the boundary of the solids in contact.

The continuous formulation of contact gives direct access to an estimate of the frictional-contact pressure, whereas the discrete formulations require it to be approximated by the stresses on the boundary.

An example of implementation for the two formulations exists in the test case SSNP154 [V6.03.154](doc:v6.03.154). CALC_PRESSION [U4.81.43](doc:u4.81.43) supplies a CHAM_GD of contact pressure, printable in table format for example.

The contact pressure is written:

$$
\lambda = \transpose{\normal} \tensTwo{\sigma} \normal
$$

where $\normal$ is the normal to the contact surface in the deformed configuration and $\tensTwo{\sigma}$ is the Cauchy stress tensor.

The tangential vector of the contact force is written:

$$
\vector{s} = (\tensTwo{\sigma} - \lambda \tensTwoUnit).\normal
$$

The box below shows how the nodal contact pressure can be computed using CALC_PRESSION.

```python
# calcul de la pression de contact: p_appr_F
p_appr_F=CALC_PRESSION(
    MAILLAGE=MESH,
    RESULTAT=RESU,
    GROUP_MA=('Bottom_C','Top_B',),
    INST=1.0,
)
```

For an analysis in large displacements, the normal must be computed on the deformed configuration. For this, the keyword `GEOMETRIE='DEFORMEE'` must be entered.

In the example above, the formula taken from it is used to compute the contact pressure explicitly. In the particular case where the boundary on which the pressure is extracted is parallel to the axes of the frame, the pressure is directly equal to one of the diagonal components of the Cauchy stress tensor (SIXX, SIYY or SIZZ). At the present time, CALC_PRESSION cannot be used if there are structural elements in the model.

#### 4.3.2 Case of the continuous formulation

In the continuous formulation, the field DEPL contains one or more additional unknowns:

- LAGS_C represents the surface density of the contact force expressed on the reference configuration.
- LAGS_F1 and LAGS_F2 represent the coordinates of a direction vector in the tangent plane. This vector, of norm less than or equal to 1, indicates the direction of sliding or of sticking when friction is taken into account.

These quantities are defined at every point of the slave contact surface. The contact pressure can therefore be accessed easily. It will however be noted that, in large displacements, the initial and final configurations no longer being coincident, the degree of freedom LAGS_C no longer has the meaning of a pressure.

To access the surface density of the friction force (in the sticking phase as in the sliding phase), an additional computation must be carried out: the norm of the direction vector in the tangent plane indeed gives the amplitude relative to the friction threshold.

If $\lambda$ denotes the contact pressure, then the friction force density $\tau$ is written:

$$
\tau =\mu .\lambda .\sqrt{({\mathit{LAGS}}_{\mathit{F1}}^{2}+{\mathit{LAGS}}_{\mathit{F2}}^{2})}
$$

In the penalised formulation (ALGO_CONT='PENALISATION'), the pressure degrees of freedom continue to exist; what precedes can therefore be applied.

It sometimes happens that the contact pressure obtained by this method presents oscillations, in particular for curved geometries. In this case, it is preferable to use CALC_PRESSION, which computes the contact pressure from the Cauchy stress tensor.

#### 4.3.3 Discrete formulation

In the discrete formulation, no degree of freedom is added to the principal unknowns. The contact problem being formulated on the discrete system, the possible Lagrange multipliers used do not even have the dimension of a pressure, but that of nodal forces.

This absence obliges one to compute the Cauchy stress tensor on the boundary of the surfaces in contact. There is therefore only one alternative: CALC_PRESSION.

### 4.4 Rigid-body motions blocked by contact

This paragraph applies only to studies in statics. In dynamics, rigid-body motions are allowed.

It happens in studies that contact makes it possible to block the rigid-body motions of certain solids (and to ensure that these deform). The initial failure to take this phenomenon into account will therefore lead to the singularity of the stiffness matrix (and hence to the impossibility of solving).

The discrete formulations are not suited to an initial taking into account of contact; carrying out studies with solids held only by the contact will therefore require, in this case, an enrichment of the modelling.

The continuous formulation makes it possible to take an initial contact into account naturally and, as such, is therefore well suited to the study of mechanisms.

For studies in three dimensions, there exist 6 possible rigid-body motions: 3 translations, 3 rotations. For studies in two dimensions (modellings D_PLAN, C_PLAN), there exist 3 rigid-body motions: 2 translations and one rotation. The axisymmetric modelling (AXIS) is particular: there exists only a single rigid-body motion, the translation along the axis $\mathit{Oy}$ (axis of the cylindrical symmetry).

When the existence of rigid-body motions is observed in one's modelling, **one will always begin by checking that there do not exist symmetries in the structure and its loading**. The symmetry conditions do indeed make it possible to remove a large part of the rigid-body motions.

An example of the blocking of rigid-body motions in the continuous formulation (by CONTACT_INIT) and in the discrete formulation (by springs) is available in the test case SSNA122 [V6.01.122](doc:v6.01.122).

#### 4.4.1 Continuous formulation

In the continuous formulation, the taking into account of an initial contact is ensured zone by zone with the keyword CONTACT_INIT. By default, at the beginning of an analysis, all the links with zero gap (or interpenetrated) are activated (CONTACT_INIT='INTERPENETRE'). The tolerance, for determining whether a gap is zero or interpenetrated, is fixed internally in the program at ${10}^{-6}\times {a}_{min}$ where ${a}_{min}$ represents the smallest non-zero edge of the mesh.

It is possible to deactivate this automatic activation (CONTACT_INIT='NON'). When non-linear analyses with restart are carried out (that is to say with the keyword ETAT_INIT of STAT_NON_LINE), it is indispensable to use the default value ('INTERPENETRE') in order to ensure a restart from the true contact state (and not from a blank state).

Finally, if one wishes initially to stick all the contact surfaces together independently of the initial gap, CONTACT_INIT='OUI' can be selected (this can be useful if the meshes are not perfectly in contact).

In all the cases in which an initial contact is declared, forces will be generated: **this is not a simple geometric repositioning** aimed at making the meshes coincide.

The activation of an initial contact blocks the rigid-body motions along the direction normal to the surface. If one wishes to take into account an initial sticking state in order to block the tangent direction, a non-zero initial contact threshold can be specified *via* SEUIL_INIT. This parameter gives the initial value of the contact pressure (homogeneous to a surface force density). By default, if the analysis comes from a restart, the value of the initial threshold is reconstructed automatically using the values of LAGS_C contained in ETAT_INIT/STAT_NON_LINE.

It should be noted that the use of an initial contact in the continuous formulation also makes it possible to avoid the non-convergence when a structure is subjected only to displacements. For example, when two solids initially in contact are pressed against each other by displacements (this is therefore a rigid-body motion).

#### 4.4.2 Discrete formulation

In the discrete formulation, one is obliged to block manually the rigid-body motions of the solid in question by springs of low stiffness. By “low” one means small enough to generate only nodal forces that are negligible compared with the nodal forces involved in the analysis.

The purpose of the springs is to ensure that the analysis without contact is capable of running in linear mechanics (that is to say in the operator MECA_STATIQUE, or else in STAT_NON_LINE once the contact conditions have been removed).

Two approaches exist for the addition of springs:

- add a spring of low stiffness at every point of the structure
- add springs at well-chosen points in order to block the rigid-body motions of the structure

The first approach has the advantage of being generic, but can sometimes disturb the solution too much (whatever the stiffness of the springs). Indeed, such an approach amounts to adding, on all the diagonal terms of the matrix, a positive term which makes it invertible.

The second approach adds springs only where it is necessary. When there exist points of the structure which will come to have a small displacement (hence to generate only a small nodal force in the spring), this approach is more suitable.

To apply a spring in *Code_Aster*, mesh cells of type POI1 must be created from nodes. For this, the operator CREA_MAILLAGE/CREA_POI1 is used. To use the first approach, one will choose to create this mesh-cell group on the whole structure (TOUT='OUI'), whereas for the second approach, the desired node group will be indicated. The newly created mesh-cell group will be used to assign a modelling of type 'DIS_T' or '2D_DIS_T' in AFFE_MODELE.

The definition of the characteristics of the spring is carried out in the operator AFFE_CARA_ELEM. By default, the stiffnesses are entered in the global frame. If, for example, one wishes to block a rigid-body motion in a direction parallel to the axes of the global frame, a non-zero stiffness will be defined only along this direction. Below, an example of the definition of a stiffness for a 2D analysis along the direction DY.

RESSORT=AFFE_CARA_ELEM(MODELE=model,

DISCRET_2D=_F(CARA='K_T_D_N',

GROUP_MA='SPRING',

VALE=(0.,1.0e-1,),),);

In the cases where the direction to be blocked is not parallel to the axes, two alternatives are possible:

- define a stiffness along all the directions
- define the stiffness in a local frame. The orientation of this frame must then be defined (keyword ORIENTATION of AFFE_CARA_ELEM), or else springs must be used which no longer rest on POI1 mesh cells but on SEG2.

For an example of the use of springs, the test case ZZZZ237 and its documentation [V1.01.237](doc:v1.01.237) will be consulted.

### 4.5 Large strains, large displacements and contact

The taking into account of frictional-contact conditions is totally decoupled from the taking into account of large displacements or of large strains. More generally, any non-linearity, whether of material or of geometric order, is *a priori* compatible with the use of contact.

In practice, convergence difficulties are often observed in studies mixing the three non-linearities. The procedure to be adopted in this case is given in the remainder of this section.

Examples of analyses mixing the three non-linearities are available in the test cases SSNP155 [V6.03.155](doc:v6.03.155), SSNP157 [V6.03.157](doc:v6.03.157) and SDNV103 [V5.03.103](doc:v5.03.103).

For the LAC method, it is possible to use TYPE_JACOBIEN='ACTUALISE' instead of 'INITIALE' in the case of large transformations.

#### 4.5.1 Decoupling the non-linearities

When such an analysis fails, the first step is to go back: by decoupling the non-linearities and by trying to apply good non-linear practice (*cf.* [U2.04.01](doc:u2.04.01)).

This means:

- carry out an elastic analysis in small perturbations with contact activated. If this analysis fails, apply the advice given in the first part of this document (orientation of the normals, choice of the master and slave surfaces, choice of the solution algorithm, …)
- carry out an analysis with a non-linear constitutive law but without contact. If this fails, then the problem comes from the integration of the constitutive behaviour. The reader will then refer to the documents [U2.04.02](doc:u2.04.02) and [U2.04.03](doc:u2.04.03).
- if necessary, carry out an analysis in large displacements but without contact and without material non-linearity. If this analysis does not work, try using another model among those for large displacements available in *Code_Aster* ('SIMO_MIEHE', 'GDEF_LOG', 'PETIT_REAC').

#### 4.5.2 Parameterising Newton's algorithm properly

If the complete analysis (mixing all the non-linearities) does not converge despite the application of the previous advice, then one can try acting on the parameters of Newton's algorithm. This starts from the following observation:

When contact and a material non-linearity are coupled, for example, it is possible (by the “abrupt” correction of the contact) to trigger, in the constitutive law, mechanisms (leaving the elastic domain, unloading) which ought not to be active in the final solution and which risk degrading the tangent matrix (to the point of making it non-invertible). Any convergence then becomes impossible.

The following settings in Newton's algorithm are therefore proposed (operator STAT_NON_LINE or DYNA_NON_LINE):

- updating of the tangent matrix at each iteration (REAC_ITER=1)
- use of an elastic prediction (PREDICTION='ELASTIQUE')
- in large strains (DEFORMATION='SIMO_MIEHE'), the tangent matrix is non-symmetric; care must therefore be taken to enter SYME='NON' in the keyword SOLVEUR.

When the analysis still has difficulty in converging, one must return to the modelling:

- does my analysis cause incompressibility problems? In this case, consult the documents [U2.04.01](doc:u2.04.01)[U2.04.02] and try to use suitable finite elements (under-integrated, with a mixed formulation).
- does the constitutive behaviour that I am using have a consistent tangent matrix? If this is not the case, one can try, as a last resort, to use an 'ELASTIQUE' matrix and to increase the number of Newton iterations.
- Case of plasticity + contact + large transformations: if the initial solution is far from the solution sought, then Newton fails. In certain cases, it may be necessary to carry out a first predictive analysis (contact replaced, for example, by LIAISON_MAIL) and then to inject the result coming from this predictive analysis into the analysis that one wishes to carry out.
- Favouring the fully PENALISATION mode, by setting the maximum penetration allowed, is also an alternative: PENE_MAXI.

#### 4.5.3 Solution of a quasi-static problem in slow dynamics

As a last resort, for quasi-static problems, carrying out a dynamic analysis over a long time can provide a solution. The mass matrix has the effect of stabilising the structure; one must however make sure that the inertia forces then remain small compared with the internal forces of the system.

For this type of modelling, it is advisable to assign to the structure its true density (this is in any case mandatory in the presence of a gravity loading) and to carry out the analysis using large time steps.

An example of implementation is available in the test case SSNP155 [V6.03.155](doc:v6.03.155).

### 4.6 Rigid surface and contact

One sometimes wishes, in studies, to model rigid solids which come into contact with deformable solids. In this section, it is explained how to optimise such studies.

In order not to burden the modelling, the rigid solids will not be modelled in full: **only their boundary will carry degrees of freedom**. In order to make the orientation of the normals of this rigid solid easier, the mesh will however include the complete solid.

After having oriented the normals, only boundary elements will therefore be assigned in AFFE_MODELE to the skin of the rigid solid: as the boundary elements carry no stiffness, an alarm is emitted to warn of the risk of a non-invertible stiffness matrix. **This alarm is normal in this case** and can be ignored.

To prevent the stiffness matrix from being singular, **the displacement of all the degrees of freedom carried by the rigid boundary must be imposed**. This is done with the commands:

- AFFE_CHAR_CINE/MECA_IMPO, whose advantage is to eliminate the unknowns
- AFFE_CHAR_MECA/DDL_IMPO, which adds additional unknowns to the problem.

It is therefore advisable to eliminate the unknowns (AFFE_CHAR_CINE).

The rigid surface will be declared as the **master surface** in DEFI_CONTACT, as explained in § 2.2.1.

The test case SSNV506 [V6.04.506](doc:v6.04.506) may be consulted for an example of contact with a rigid surface.

### 4.7 Redundancy between frictional-contact conditions and boundary conditions (symmetry): methods other than LAC

In the presence of symmetries in the structure studied, it is common for the friction conditions to conflict with the symmetry boundary conditions. The figure shows the example of two cubes in frictional contact; the hatched part represents the faces of the cubes subjected to a symmetry condition (DX=0).

In this example, the edge of the upper cube **in a thick line** belongs to the slave surface and also carries the symmetry condition. This condition conflicts with the friction condition written in the tangent plane (here the plane $\mathit{xOz}$ ). In practice, the analysis will stop once contact is established, because the tangent matrix will be singular.

Mechanically, one sees that the symmetry condition implies that sticking, or else sliding, will occur only along the direction DZ (green tangent vector). To eliminate the redundancy, the friction direction along DX (red tangent vector) must therefore be excluded.

For this, the keyword SANS_GROUP_NO_FR will be used to designate the list of nodes of the slave edge, then DIRE_EXCL_FROT=(1,0,0) will be entered (in the global frame), that is the direction DX to be excluded.

The test case ZZZZ292 implements the functionality SANS_GROUP_NO_FR.

### 4.8 Measuring the interpenetration without solving the contact: methods other than LAC

The solution of a contact problem sometimes being expensive, it can be advantageous to replace the enforcement of the contact conditions by a simple check of the interpenetration. This is all the more interesting in the cases where one simply wishes to check that solids are not going to come into contact.

For each of the contact zones defined in the operator DEFI_CONTACT, it is possible to choose whether one wishes the contact to be enforced there (RESOLUTION='OUI') or not (RESOLUTION='NON').

The interest of such an approach is not to burden an analysis: when an analysis carried out without solution **on the whole of the contact zones** shows that there is no interpenetration, then the modelling of the contact can be ignored.

Caution, however: if at least one of the contact zones is “solved” and another is “not solved”, then the existence of an interpenetration is not indicative of the solution of a complete analysis with contact (because of the possible interactions between contact zones).

Finally, this technique can also serve to measure the interpenetration rate at the contact zones in order to calibrate a criterion such as the penalisation coefficient or the maximum interpenetration tolerated in the 'GCP' solution method.

### 4.9 Visualising the results of a contact analysis

When the results of a frictional-contact analysis are visualised in a post-processing software package, care must be taken over several things:

- for the display of the deformed shapes, **an amplification factor different from 1 can lead to the visualisation of interpenetrations which are not real**
- for 2D analyses in the 'CONTINUE' formulation, care will be taken, when displaying deformed shapes, with post-processing software packages which consider the first three components of a field as the components along $X$ , $Y$ and $Z$ of the displacement. In 2D, the third component corresponds to LAGS_C and must therefore be ignored
- case of the standard/penalised method: when visualising the contact post-processing field (CONT_NOEU), and more particularly the component CONT which indicates the contact state, care will be taken over the interpolation of the fields at the nodes, sometimes carried out automatically. Indeed, this component takes the values 0 (no contact), 1 (sticking contact) or 2 (sliding contact). The sticking state is possible only in the presence of friction: if such a value is visualised for a contact analysis without friction, it is because there is interpolation of the field.
- LAC method case: the field CONT_ELEM is supplied in post-processing. The components are described in detail in the document U4.44.11, page 34.

### 4.10 Point contact with discrete elements (springs)

The discrete elements (or springs) 2D_DIS_T* or DIS_T* associated with the constitutive law DIS_CHOC [R5.03.17](doc:r5.03.17) make it possible to represent a point contact in a fixed direction. They are well suited to the modelling of shocks and are, as such, often used in modal-basis dynamics [U4.53.03](doc:u4.53.03) and in explicit dynamics [U4.53.01](doc:u4.53.01).

The springs can rest equally well on a point mesh cell or on a segment. In all cases, it is necessary to orient each element correctly with the command AFFE_CARA_ELEM [U4.42.01](doc:u4.42.01).

Contact, like friction, is solved by penalisation (see § 3.2.2). The penalisation stiffnesses, the friction coefficient and the initial gaps are specified in the material DIS_CONTACT (command DEFI_MATERIAU, [U4.43.01](doc:u4.43.01)).

This type of element cannot be used in large displacements, because the contact direction is fixed and given by the initial orientation of the discrete element.

The test cases SSNL130A and SDND100C implement contact springs.

### 4.11 (Hydro-)mechanical joint elements with contact and friction

The (hydro-)mechanical joint elements PLAN_JOINT(_HYME) and 3D_JOINT(_HYME) make it possible to model the opening of a crack under the pressure of a fluid and the friction on the faces of the closed crack, with the law JOINT_MECA_FROT [R7.01.25](doc:r7.01.25). It is possible to couple the opening of the crack and the propagation of the fluid with the modellings *_HYME.

The formulation of frictional contact is penalised, and the related parameters are entered under the keyword JOINT_MECA_FROT of the command DEFI_MATERIAU [U4.43.01](doc:u4.43.01).

The test cases SSNP142C and SSNP142D provide an example of the application of such elements to the modelling of a dam.

### 4.12 Use of the adaptive methods

**Case 1**: The statuses have difficulty in stabilising in the continuous formulation (all algorithms); use ADAPTATION='CYCLAGE'.

**Case 2**: ALGO_CONT='STANDARD' fails and I wish to use PENALISATION mode but I do not know how to set the penalisation coefficient; use the mode ALGO_CONT='PENALISATION' and ADAPTATION='ADAPT_COEF'. By default, a keyword is activated, PENE_MAXI, which is equal to 1.E-2 times the smallest mesh-cell edge in the contact zone. PENE_MAXI can be estimated fairly easily by the user, because it is directly related to the size of a contact mesh cell.

**Case 3**: if case 2 fails, use ADAPTATION='TOUT'.

**Other cases**: if the continuous formulation and ALGO_CONT='STANDARD'/'PENALISATION' are used, then by default the adaptation method 'CYCLAGE' is active. It is advisable, if convergence allows it, to use the automatic geometric updating REAC_GEOM='AUTOMATIQUE' (the default parameter setting).

### 4.13 Visualisation of the pairing used by `DEFI_CONT`

It is possible to visualise the various quantities involved with a mortar method in `DEFI_CONT` (keyword `APPARIEMENT="MORTAR"`).
More precisely, a visualisation aid is proposed in order to display:

- the **pairs** of paired mesh cells,
- the computed **intersections**,
- the **integration points** obtained.

Reference is made to the reference documentation for further details on the methodology making it possible to obtain these various quantities.
The visualisation tools are intended mainly for advanced users and for debugging operations.

#### 4.13.1 Prerequisites and input data

The use of the visualisation tools is possible provided that the `matplotlib` package is available.

Furthermore, in order to use these functionalities, it is necessary to have the following input data:

- an input mesh,
- the names of the mesh-cell groups associated with the master and slave interfaces concerned.

The visualisation can be carried out only between two specific interfaces, defined by a zone (`ZONE`).
When one speaks of an input mesh, there are two possible use cases:

- visualisation of the pairing on the initial mesh. In this case, it is enough to supply the initial mesh.
- visualisation of the pairing in the course of the analysis. In this case, the user can rely on the command [MODI_MAILLAGE](doc:u4.23.04), and the keyword `DEFORMEE/OPTION=’TRAN’`, in order to have the deformed mesh. This mesh can then be used by the visualisation tool.

At this stage, it is not possible to have the visualisation in the course of the Newton iterations, inasmuch as it is necessary to have the deformed mesh as input data.

#### 4.13.2 Help with use

A few points are given here on the arguments of the pairing-visualisation method. This function makes it possible to plot curves in order to visualise and debug
the pairing between meshes, as well as the construction of the quadrature weights. It has been designed to adapt to different levels of detail, according to whether one wishes
an overall view or a focus on a particular pair or mesh cell.

The logic of the function rests on three main options to be supplied:

- `optionMesh` determines which set of mesh cells is displayed (full domain — in 2D only —, interface, or a selected slave mesh cell);
- `subOptionMesh` makes it possible to filter the elements to be displayed among those selected (all the pairs, a given pair, or a given slave mesh cell);
