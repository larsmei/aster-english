# u2.01.05 Stresses, internal forces, forces and strains

> **Summary.**

This document defines the quantities which characterise the stresses, the forces and the strains inside a structure in a finite-element analysis in displacement, and how this is expressed. The expression of these quantities is given for the finite elements of mechanics: 2D or 3D continuum, shells and beams.

## 1 Statics

### 1.1 Stresses

Cauchy's postulate is that the contact forces exerted at a point by one part of a continuum on another depend only on the normal to the surface at that point which separates the parts.

In accordance with this postulate, one calls stress vector, for non-micropolar media, $text{F}left(text{n}right)$ the vector which characterises the contact forces exerted through a surface element $mathit{dS}$ of normal $text{n}$ on a part of a continuum [bib1]_.

One then shows [bib3]_ that the dependence, at a fixed point, of $text{F}$ with respect to the normal $text{n}$ is linear, and that there exists a tensor, which one calls the stress tensor $sigma$ , such that:

$text{F}left(text{n}right)=sigma text{n}$

The unit of stress in the International System is the $text{N}.{text{m}}^{-2}equiv text{Pa}$ .

For the structure as a whole, the "stress state" is characterised by a field of the stress tensor, which is more simply called the stress field.

### 1.2 Internal force

As regards beam or shell structures, unlike the continuum case, it must be noted that:

- only the normal directions $text{n}$ of the cuts along the space tangent to the manifold are possible,
- the characteristic quantities are obtained by integration, through the cross-section or the thickness, of the quantities defined for continua.

#### 1.2.1 Case of discrete elements

Discrete elements are finite elements which may have no physical dimension. They are represented by their stiffness matrix. The internal forces are obtained by multiplying this matrix by the displacement vector:

$(begin{array}{c}F\ Mend{array})=left[kright].(begin{array}{c}D\ Rend{array})$

#### 1.2.2 Case of beams

One calls internal force the reduction elements $left(F,Mright)$ at $P$ , the geometric centre of inertia of the cross-section $Sigma$ , of the resultant wrench of the contact forces exerted on the cross-section [bib2]_.

With the previous notation:

$begin{array}{ccc}F& ={int}_{Sigma}text{F}(tau )text{ds}& (N)\ {M}_{p}& ={int}_{Sigma}text{PM}wedge text{F}(tau )text{ds}& (N.m)end{array}$

![Figure](/figures/u2.01.05/100003940000149100000B5E3BE207DC060092F9.svg)

The force $F$ is decomposed into a normal force $N$ and shear forces $T$ in the plane of the cross-section, while the moment $M$ acting at the point $P$ is decomposed into a torsional moment and bending moments at the point $P$ . The fuel-assembly skeleton elements (R3.08.08) also carry the grid moments ${M}_{g}$ , of a formulation equivalent to $M$ .

For beams whose cross-section is not regarded as rigid, these reduction elements are not sufficient: for example, for beams which take the warping of the cross-sections into account, one is led to consider an additional internal-force quantity due to warping (the bimoment).

Multi-fibre beams (with a local 1D constitutive behaviour, relating stresses to strains at a certain number of points of the cross-section) and pipes (local constitutive behaviour in plane stress) supply both the reduction elements of the beams and a stress field for each fibre or sector.

#### 1.2.3 Case of shells

Let $P$ be a point of a surface medium $S$ of thickness $h$ , let $mathrm{dl}$ be an element of length on $S$ , and let $n$ be the normal orienting the shell at that point.

Let $left(F,Mright)$ be the reduction elements at that point of a wrench resulting from the forces exerted through a surface element $mathrm{dS}=hmathrm{dl}$ of normal $n$ tangent to $S$ on a part of $S$ .

With the previous notation:

$begin{array}{ccc}F(P)& =underset{-h/2}{overset{+h/2}{int}}text{F}(nu )text{dh}& (N)\ M(P)& =underset{-h/2}{overset{+h/2}{int}}text{PM}wedge text{F}(nu )text{dh}& (N.m)end{array}$

![Figure](/figures/u2.01.05/1000097C00001A90000010542B20991D02ADD3F6.svg)

It is clear that $M$ lies in the plane tangent to $S$ at $P$ .

Let $Nleft(Pright)$ be the projection of $Fleft(Pright)$ onto the plane tangent to $S$ at $P$ , and let $T(P)$ be its component normal to that tangent plane.

In the same way as for continua, one shows that there exist two symmetric tensors $text{N}$ and $text{M}$ and a vector $Q$ , defined in the plane tangent to $S$ , such that:

$begin{array}{}text{F}=Nnu \ T=Qcdot nu \ text{M}=text{n}wedge Mnu end{array}$

$(N,M,Q)$ are called the internal forces at the point $P$ :

- the tensor $N$ characterises the membrane forces,
- the tensor $M$ the bending moments,
- the vector $Q$ the shear forces.

- There are no universal conventions on the naming and the signs of these tensors. In particular, the bending-moment tensor is sometimes taken with the opposite sign in teaching and in the practice of French civil engineers. Our convention is used in the major finite-element codes and makes it possible to have the same sign for a beam and a plate such that* $tau =nu$.
- For non-linear materials, the constitutive law is evaluated at several points through the thickness, but the equilibrium equations always bear on the internal-force fields. It is not necessary to go back down to the stresses in order to define the "stress state".

> **Notes.**

**Links with the stress field**

In these conditions, let a frame whose third component is carried by $text{n}$ ; one has ($alpha ,beta =1text{ou}2$ ):

$begin{array}{ccc}{N}_{alpha beta }& ={N}_{beta alpha }& =underset{-h/2}{overset{+h/2}{int}}{sigma}_{alpha beta }text{dh}\ {M}_{alpha beta }& ={M}_{beta alpha }& =underset{-h/2}{overset{+h/2}{int}}{x}_{3}{sigma}_{alpha beta }text{dh}\ {Q}_{alpha}& & =underset{-h/2}{overset{+h/2}{int}}{sigma}_{alpha 3}text{dh}end{array}$

### 1.3 Nodal forces

One calls equivalent nodal force, or more simply nodal force, a vector $F$ which is the representative of a linear form $W$ (generally related to an energy) acting on displacement fields $u(x)$ discretised by finite elements.

The displacement fields $u(x)$ are expressed from their nodal values, which form a vector $q$, and from the shape functions ${Phi}_{i}(x)$ by:

$uleft(xright)=sum_{i}{q}_{i}{Phi}_{i}left(xright)$

In these conditions:

$wleft(uright)=sum_{i}{q}_{i}{F}_{i}$

- The notion of node here is very general and means, in fact, carrier of a degree of freedom (whether of Lagrange or of Hermite type).
- The notion of displacement is also very general, and includes the notion of generalised displacement, comprising translations and rotations.

> **Notes.**

### 1.4 Representation of the fields

There are several ways of representing the fields in a finite-element modelling:

- for fields continuous over the whole domain, the values at the nodes are used (CHAM_NO of *Code_Aster*)

$uleft(xright)=sum_{i}{u}_{i}{Phi}_{i}left(xright)$

one then speaks of displacements at the nodes, of stresses at the nodes or of internal forces at the nodes,

> **Note.**

The stress or internal-force fields are generally computed at the Gauss points; if they are represented continuously, it is solely for visualisation purposes.

For the other fields, the values at certain characteristic points of the finite elements (Gauss points or nodes) are used.

One then speaks of stresses per finite element at the nodes, or of internal forces per finite element at the nodes, or again of stresses at the Gauss points or of internal forces at the Gauss points.

> **Note.** *Axisymmetric modellings*

For axisymmetric modellings, the axis of revolution is the $Y$ axis of the mesh. The whole solid is meshed in $Xge 0$. The $mathit{Ox}$ axis therefore denotes the radial direction. The components of the computed fields will therefore be: $X$ for the radial direction, $Y$ for the axial direction, $Z$ for the orthoradial (circumferential) direction.

### 1.5 Quantities associated in Code_Aster

#### 1.5.1 SIEF_R

The quantity SIEF_R represents the "stress state" of the structure; it therefore contains, at least, the components:

- of the stress fields of continua (in the global frame):
  SIXX, SIYY, SIZZ, SIXY, SIXZ, SIYZ
- of the internal-force fields of a beam and of a discrete element (in the "user" frame attached to the beam or to the discrete element):
  N, VY, VZ, MT, MFY, MFZ, MGX, MGY, MGZ
- for beams with warping, the bimoment must be added (necessarily in the "user" frame attached to the fibre):
  BX
- of the shell internal-force fields (necessarily in the "user" frame attached to the surface):
  NXX, NYY, NXY, MXX, MYY, MXY, QX, QY

In addition, it is sometimes convenient to be able to use directly the internal-force fields of a beam and of a discrete element in the global frame:

FX, FY, FZ, MX, MY, MZ

It is also useful to represent the components of a stress field on the beam or shell finite elements in the "user" frame. To that end, the same components as in the global frame will be used, although confusion is possible.

#### 1.5.2 FORC_F and FORC_R

These quantities represent the forces applied to the structure on an interface.

For:

- a continuum, it is therefore a force vector,
- a beam, a wrench of forces,
- a shell, a wrench of forces.

This quantity must therefore have the following components:

- for a continuum:
  FX, FY, FZ
- and, in addition, for the beams and the shells:
  MX, MY, MZ

#### 1.5.3 DEPL_R

Given that:

- a field can be attached to only one quantity,
- mixed finite-element methods (mixing unknowns of displacement type and unknowns of nodal-force type) are not excluded,
- the dualisation of the boundary conditions leads to having, as unknown, a vector containing Lagrange variables which are nodal forces in the sense made precise above,
- it is necessary to be able to carry out any kind of linear combination on the nodal forces,
- the numbering of the unknowns must be the same as that of the right-hand sides,

the nodal forces (dual, in the sense of the energy $W$ , of the nodal displacements) necessarily have the same components as the displacements, namely:

DX, DY, DZ, DRX, DRY, DRZ, DRGX, DRGY, DRGZ

plus, for beams with warping, the degree of freedom associated with the bimoment: GRX.

The nodal forces (dual, in the sense of the energy $W$ , of the nodal displacements) have the same components as the displacements, namely:

DX, DY, DZ, DRX, DRY, DRZ

The option REAC_NODA of the operator CALC_CHAMP calls FORC_NODA and subtracts:

- the loading, in statics,
- the loading, the inertia forces and the viscous forces, in dynamics (in practice, the viscous contribution in dynamics is currently neglected in CALC_CHAMP).

For the solid finite elements, the FORC_NODA generally have the dimension of a force. This is a field on the nodes of the mesh, in which the value at a node is obtained from the stresses computed on the finite elements meeting at that node; their values therefore change when the mesh changes. In the absence of a distributed loading, equilibrium requires them to vanish at an interior node, whereas they correspond to the reaction on the supports where a kinematic relation is imposed (the case of an imposed displacement).

In the case of shells, the components DX, DY and DZ give the FORC_NODA (of the dimension of a force) in the global frame of the mesh. These components are built from the normal forces and the shear forces in the shell. The components DRX, DRY and DRZ give the FORC_NODA (of the dimension of a moment) in the global frame of the mesh, built from the bending moments in the shell.

## 2 Kinematics

### 2.1 Strains

#### 2.1.1 Continuum

In this case, the displacements of the structure are represented by a vector field $u$ with three components in general.

The strain (under the small-perturbation hypothesis) is defined by the strain tensor $varepsilon$ by (options EPSI_ELGA and EPSI_ELNO):

${varepsilon}_{ij}(u)=frac{1}{2}({u}_{i,j}+{u}_{j,i})$

One may wish to compute the "mechanical" strain, that is by subtracting the thermal expansions (options EPME_ELGA and EPME_ELNO):

${epsilon}_{ij}^{m}left(uright)=frac{1}{2}left({u}_{i,j}+{u}_{j,i}right)-{epsilon}^{text{th}}$

For non-linear analyses, it is sometimes useful to know the plastic strain (options EPSP_ELGA and EPSP_ELNO), denoted ${epsilon}^{mathit{pl}}$ .

In the case of large displacements, the Green-Lagrange strains are (options EPSG_ELGA and EPSG_ELNO):

${E}_{ij}(u)=frac{1}{2}({u}_{i,j}+{u}_{j,i}+{u}_{k,i}{u}_{k,j})$

From which one may wish to subtract the thermal strains (options EPMG_ELGA and EPMG_ELNO):

${E}_{ij}^{m}(u)=frac{1}{2}({u}_{i,j}+{u}_{j,i}+{u}_{k,i}{u}_{k,j})-{varepsilon}^{text{th}}$

For the plane-strain hypothesis (D_PLAN), it is important to note that the plane-strain condition is written on the *total* strain:

${epsilon}_{33}left(uright)=0$

If one writes the total strain as the sum of a purely mechanical strain and of the strain coming from the command variables (such as the thermal-expansion strain):

${epsilon}_{33}left(uright)={epsilon}_{33}^{m}+{epsilon}_{33}^{mathit{th}}=0$

Then it follows naturally that, in plane strain, the out-of-plane *mechanical* strain is not zero in the presence of a thermal expansion.

${epsilon}_{33}^{m}left(uright)ne 0$

#### 2.1.2 Case of beams

In the traditional beam theories, each point $P$ of the beam represents a cross-section. It is therefore the reduction elements of the displacement wrench $(T(s),Omega (s))$ of the cross-section, assumed rigid, which characterise the displacement of the point $P$ at the curvilinear abscissa $s$ . $T$ is the translation of the centre of inertia of the cross-section, $Omega (s)$ the rotation vector of the cross-section at that point.

Application of the theorem of virtual work (cf.[bib2]_) naturally leads to defining, as the strain, the wrench $(varepsilon ,chi )$ obtained by differentiation of $(T(s),Omega (s))$ with respect to the curvilinear abscissa $s$ :

$begin{array}{}varepsilon =frac{mathrm{dT}}{text{ds}}+tau wedge Omega \ chi =frac{dOmega }{text{ds}}end{array}$

![Figure](/figures/u2.01.05/100003960000149100000B5EED36AAA3CEB1754C.svg)

Let us then set:

$begin{array}{}varepsilon ={varepsilon}_{L}tau +{gamma}_{T}\ chi ={gamma}_{t}tau +text{K}end{array}$

${varepsilon}_{L}$ is the longitudinal strain,

${gamma}_{T}$ is the vector of the distortion strains (zero under the Navier-Bernoulli hypothesis),

${gamma}_{t}$ is the torsional strain of the cross-section,

$text{K}$ is the bending strain.

> **Note.**

For beam modellings which take warping into account, the kinematics is more complicated to describe, but they nevertheless lead to notions close to those presented above.

#### 2.1.3 Case of shells

We shall restrict ourselves here to the case of plates. Indeed, in the general case of shells:

- the spatial derivatives use mathematical notions too complicated for the scope of this document, [R3.07.04](doc:r3.07.04),
- shells are very often modelled by assembled plate finite elements.

In this case, only the material normals are assumed rigid. The displacement of these normals is therefore represented by the reduction elements of a wrench $(T,Omega )$ . $T$ is the translation of the point lying on the mid-surface, $Omega$ the rotation vector of the normal at that point.

It is clear that the normal component of $Omega$ is zero (in the case of non-micropolar media). One introduces the vector $text{I}$ in the tangent plane, defined by:

$text{I}=Omega wedge text{n}$

where $n$ is the normal vector orienting the surface.

![Figure](/figures/u2.01.05/100004C0000022D40000151688DCFC184B81DAB9.svg)

Let the decomposition be:

$T=wtext{n}+{text{u}}_{T}$

${text{u}}_{T}$ is the tangential displacement,

$w$ is the deflection.

In the same way as for the beams, application of the theorem of virtual work (cf.[bib2]_) leads to defining, as the strain, the set formed by the tensors $E$ and $K$ and the vector $gamma$ , all these quantities being defined in the tangent plane by:

$begin{array}{ccc}{E}_{alpha beta }& =& frac{1}{2}({u}_{alpha ,beta }+{u}_{beta ,alpha })\ {K}_{alpha beta }& =& frac{1}{2}({l}_{alpha ,beta }+{l}_{beta ,alpha })\ {gamma}_{alpha}& =& {l}_{alpha}+{w}_{,alpha }end{array}$

The strain is therefore defined by 7 real numbers.

${E}_{alpha beta }$ are the membrane strains,

${K}_{alpha beta }$ are the inverses of the curvatures of the deformed mid-surface,

${gamma}_{alpha}$ is the distortion-strain vector.

> **Note.**

Here again, there is no universal convention, and the disparity of the conventions is even greater than for the internal-force tensors.

**Link with the three-dimensional strain field**

In these conditions, one has:

$begin{array}{cc}{varepsilon}_{alpha beta }& ={E}_{alpha beta }+{x}_{3}{K}_{alpha beta }\ {varepsilon}_{alpha 3}& ={gamma}_{alpha}\ {varepsilon}_{33}& =0end{array}$

### 2.2 Quantities associated in Code_Aster

#### 2.2.1 DEPL_R and DEPL_C

The quantities DEPL_R and DEPL_C have as components the degrees of freedom of the finite-element modelling, and therefore do not necessarily have only the components of the displacement fields, which are:

DX, DY, DZ

to which must be added, for the beams or the shells:

DRX, DRY, DRZ

For the shells, the three components of the rotation vector are needed, because the finite-element equation can be expressed only in a global Cartesian frame.

- of the strain fields $varepsilon$ of continua (in the global frame):

  EXX, EYY, EXY, KXX, KYY, KXY, GAX, GAY
