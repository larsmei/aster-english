# r5.03.01 Quasi-static non-linear algorithm (STAT_NON_LINE)

**Summary:**

The operator STAT_NON_LINE [U4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03) makes it possible, in the case of a quasi-static loading, to integrate various types of non-linearities arising from the constitutive behaviour of the material, from large geometric transformations or from contact/friction conditions. The global solution algorithm employed is described here.

The integration of the constitutive relations proper is described in the documents [R5.03....] and [R7.01....], (for example [R5.03.02](doc:r5.03.02) for elastoplasticity), to which one may refer for more details.

For analyses in large geometric transformations, one may consult for example the document [R5.03.20](doc:r5.03.20) on non-linear elasticity in large displacements, or the documents [R5.03.21](doc:r5.03.21), [R5.03.22](doc:r5.03.22) on thermoelastoplasticity with isotropic hardening.

For frictional contact, there are three documents: [R5.03.50](doc:r5.03.50) on the discrete formulation of contact/friction, [R5.03.52](doc:r5.03.52) for the hybrid formulation by contact/friction elements.

For everything concerning piloting, one must refer to the document [R5.03.80](doc:r5.03.80).

## 1 Presentation

### 1.1 General points

STAT_NON_LINE is the operator of *Code_Aster* that makes it possible to carry out non-linear mechanical analyses when the inertia effects are neglected (if one wishes to take the inertia effects into account, one must use DYNA_NON_LINE, see [R5.05.05](doc:r5.05.05)).

The analysis concerns a priori only the mechanical variables (displacements, stresses, internal variables), excluding any coupling with other physical phenomena (thermal, ...). Consequently, the associated fields that influence the mechanical behaviour (thermal, hydraulic, metallurgical fields) are computed beforehand by other operators (THER_LINEAIRE [U4.33.01](doc:u4.33.01), THER_NON_LINE [U4.33.02](doc:u4.33.02)), or even by other codes (for example CODE_SATURNE for fluid mechanics, ...).

There is an exception concerning the thermo-hydro-mechanical modelling (modelling known as 'THM'), for which STAT_NON_LINE treats the whole of the coupled problem of the diffusion equations of heat, of the pressure of the fluid(s) and of mechanical equilibrium [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10).

It should be noted that when one speaks of a computation time instant in this document, one almost always refers to a pseudo-time, which has no physical meaning and which serves only to parameterise the incremental algorithm. However, the time instant retains a physical meaning in viscoplasticity and when the command variables depend on it.

### 1.2 Types of non-linearities

#### 1.2.1 Non-linear behaviours

The non-linear constitutive relations are described in the documents [R5.03....], for the general behaviours, and [R7.01....] for the geomaterials. The choice of the type of behaviour is made by the factor keyword COMPORTEMENT. This leads, through the equilibrium equation, to a non-linear system that can be of two forms:

- The system depends explicitly on the displacement field $\mathrm{u}$ with respect to the reference configuration, and is parameterised by the computation time instant (through, among other things, the thermal evolution).
- The system depends implicitly on the displacement field $\mathrm{u}$ through an **implicit differential equation** (for example elastoplasticity, viscoplasticity, hypoelasticity, etc.). In this case, the constitutive relation is integrated as presented for example in [R5.03.02](doc:r5.03.02): by linking a displacement increment $\Delta \mathrm{u}$ computed from a given mechanical state (the mechanical state being represented by a displacement field $\mathrm{u}$ , a stress field $\sigma$ and a field of internal variables $\alpha$ ) to the stress field at the time instant $t$ of the analysis. The equilibrium equation therefore leads to a non-linear system in $\Delta \mathrm{u}$ , but which is also parameterised by the computation time instant through the data of the problem (variation of the mechanical loading and thermal evolution, for example).

#### 1.2.2 Large transformations

In the cases where the small-perturbation hypothesis (moderate displacements and strains) is not satisfied, the method of solution of the problem must then integrate the evolution of the geometry of the problem, handle a particular kinematics and use an adequate formulation of the constitutive law.

In practice, the small-strain hypothesis can be applied as long as the square of the modulus of the strain remains smaller than the accuracy of the analyses envisaged. Likewise, the small-rotation hypothesis can be applied as long as the product of the square of the rotation angle and the modulus of the strain remains smaller than the accuracy of the analyses envisaged.

Various alternatives exist within *Code_Aster*; our aim here is not to give a detailed presentation of them and we refer to the various documents dealing specifically with each problem. One should distinguish the formulations that operate on solid isoparametric elements (2D or 3D) and the formulations used for structural elements (beams, plates and shells). For the cases of the solid isoparametric elements, one finds three main types of formulation of the kinematics for the case of large strains:

- The kinematics DEFORMATION=‘PETIT_REAC’ makes it possible to treat an arbitrary constitutive law in large strains. The law is written in small strains and the large strains are taken into account only by updating the geometry. This formulation is not incrementally objective. Moreover, one can use it only if the behaviour is isotropic, if the elastic strains are small compared with the plastic strains, if the rotations remain small (less than $10°$ ) and if one adopts a sufficiently fine discretisation in time. Moreover, the absence of the geometric contribution in the tangent matrix can sometimes make convergence difficult (see [R5.03.21 for more details).
- The kinematics DEFORMATION=‘SIMO_MIEHE’ makes it possible to treat an elastoplastic constitutive law with isotropic hardening in large strains, the ductile failure law known as “Rousselier” or the elasto(visco)plasticity with phase changes for metallurgy (see [R5.03.21](doc:r5.03.21), [R5.03.06](doc:r5.03.06) and [R4.04.03](doc:r4.04.03)). This formulation is incrementally objective, with no limitation on the level of the transformations applied, but it is available only for the three constitutive laws mentioned and makes it possible to treat only the cases where the behaviour is isotropic.
- The kinematics DEFORMATION=‘GDEF_LOG’ also makes it possible to treat any hypo-elastoplastic constitutive law. It is incrementally objective, with no limitation on the level of the transformations applied, and makes it possible to treat the case of anisotropic behaviours (see [R5.03.24](doc:r5.03.24)).

In order to treat large elastic strains, one must employ another formalism, called via DEFORMATION = 'GROT_GDEP', which can be used for non-linear hyperelastic constitutive relations in large displacements (see [R5.03.20](doc:r5.03.20) and [R5.03.22](doc:r5.03.22) for the case of small strains) or for the hyperelastic constitutive law (see [R5.03.19](doc:r5.03.19)).

Finally, for the structural elements (beam, plates or shells), specific formulations exist. One may mention:

- The beams in large displacements (see [R5.03.40](doc:r5.03.40)) or the multifibre beams in large displacements (see [R3.08.09](doc:r3.08.09)). Keyword DEFORMATION='GROT_GDEP'.
- The volumetric shell elements in geometric non-linearity (see [R3.07.05](doc:r3.07.05)).

There are no structural elements (beam, plate or shell) usable in large strains in *Code_Aster*.

#### 1.2.3 Unilateral contact and friction

For contact and friction, one will refer to three documents: [R5.03.51](doc:r5.03.51) on discrete contact with friction, [R5.03.52](doc:r5.03.52) for the hybrid formulation by contact/friction elements and [R5.03.53](doc:r5.03.53) on contact in large sliding with the XFEM method.

#### 1.2.4 Boundary conditions and loadings

It is possible to define non-linear boundary conditions, that is to say depending on the displacement of the structure. There are two main categories of non-linear boundary conditions:

- For the Neumann loadings, it is essentially a matter of pressure: the latter having to remain normal to the surface, the fact that the structure undergoes large transformations implies that the loading *depends* on the displacements. One will consult on this subject the documents [R3.03.07](doc:r3.03.07), [R3.03.04](doc:r3.03.04) and § 3.2;
- For the Dirichlet loadings, Code_Aster allows for the moment only the loading of type LIAISON_SOLIDE (see [U4.44.01](doc:u4.44.01)), which rigidifies exactly a part of the structure. One will consult on this subject [R3.02.02](doc:r3.02.02);

In both cases, it is necessary to specify *explicitly* that this loading is non-linear (one speaks of a **follower** loading) by means of the keyword TYPE_CHARGE in the factor keyword EXCIT defining the loadings (see [U4.01.03](doc:u4.01.03)).

Note: in all generality, one can liken frictional contact to a mixed boundary condition (in displacement and in pressure) that is non-linear.

### 1.3 Statement of the quasi-static non-linear problem

#### 1.3.1 General problem

As a consequence of paragraph 1.1, one sees that it is legitimate to consider that the non-linear problem has a displacement as unknown and that it is parameterised by time. Let us therefore consider the quasi-static non-linear problem by the classical expression of the principle of virtual work.

$$
{\mathrm{v}}^{T}.{\mathrm{L}}^{int}(\mathrm{u},t)={\mathrm{v}}^{T}.{\mathrm{L}}^{\text{ext}}(t)\text{}\forall \mathrm{v}=0
$$

where:

- $t$ represents the time-instant variable;
- $u$ is the displacement field taken from a reference configuration;
- $v$ is the kinematically admissible virtual displacement field;
- ${L}^{\text{ext}}$ is the external mechanical loading to which the structure is subjected (pressure, imposed force, ...);
- ${L}^{int}$ represents the internal forces of the quasi-static non-linear mechanics problem. In the linear case, one has ${L}^{int}(u,t)=K.u$ , where $K$ is the stiffness matrix of the structure.

For the moment, the Dirichlet boundary conditions are not taken into account. In fact, more precisely, ${L}^{int}(u,t)$ is related to the stress field $\sigma$ by the operator of the work of the virtual strains ${Q}^{T}$ according to the following relation:

$$
{L}^{int}(u,t)={Q}^{T}(u).\sigma
$$

In small displacements, ${Q}^{T}$ is independent of the displacements; for large displacements, this is no longer the case. One gives oneself a discretisation of the time interval to be computed:

$$
t\to \left[{t}_{0},\cdots ,{t}_{i},\cdots ,{t}_{n}\right]
$$

The stress field ${\sigma}_{i}$ at the time instant ${t}_{i}$ is written $\sigma ({u}_{i},{\beta}_{i},{t}_{i},{H}_{i-1})$ , if one denotes ${\beta}_{i}$ the fields of command variables and ${H}_{i-1}$ the past history of the structure. For elastic behaviours, the history does not intervene: the set ${H}_{i-1}$ is therefore empty. For incremental behaviours, the history is the set of the states (fields of displacements, stresses and internal variables) at the previous time instant: ${H}_{i-1}=\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1},{\sigma}_{i-1},{\alpha}_{i-1},{t}_{i-1}\right\rbrace$ .

In the general case, the dependence of the operator ${L}^{int}$ is, as we have seen in [§ 1.1], implicit with respect to time: it results from the integration of the constitutive relation in time (for elastoplasticity problems, for example). The explicit dependence with respect to time appears in particular in the case of constitutive relations taking into account a phenomenon of hardening by time (time-hardening) or in the case of ageing.

The formulation of the quasi-static problem consists in expressing the equilibrium of the structure (the internal forces are equal to the external forces) for a sequence of computation time instants ${\left\lbrace {t}_{i}\right\rbrace }_{1\le i\le I}$ that parameterise the loading; one will denote the quantities at the time instant ${t}_{i}$ by the index $i$ (for example ${\mathrm{L}}^{int}({\mathrm{u}}_{i},{t}_{i})={\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int}$ ):

$$
{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int}={\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{ext}}
$$

Which amounts to cancelling at $({\mathrm{u}}_{i},{t}_{i})$ the vector ${\mathrm{R}}_{i}({\mathrm{u}}_{i},{t}_{i})$ called the equilibrium residual vector, defined by:

$$
{\mathrm{R}}_{i}({\mathrm{u}}_{i},{t}_{i})={\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{ext}}
$$

The state of the structure at ${t}_{0}$ is assumed known. One carries out $I$ increments (or steps) of load. The unknowns are computed incrementally by the global solution algorithm (even for elastic behaviours). From ${\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}$ , a solution satisfying equilibrium at ${t}_{i-1}$ , one determines $\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}$ which will make it possible to obtain the solution at ${t}_{i}$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{t}_{i}={t}_{i-1}+\Delta {t}_{i}\\ {\mathrm{u}}_{i}={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}\end{array}
$$

The increment $\Delta {u}_{i}$ is first estimated by linearising the problem with respect to time about $({\mathrm{u}}_{i-1},{t}_{i-1})$ (phase known as prediction or Euler). Then one uses a Newton method or one of its variants to solve equation () iteratively (one computes a sequence $\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}$ where the exponent $n$ is the iteration number). In addition to these variables, for the incremental constitutive relations, one needs to know at ${t}_{i-1}$ the stress field ${\sigma}_{i-1}$ and the field of internal variables ${\alpha}_{i-1}$ (see [R5.03.02](doc:r5.03.02) for an example).

#### 1.3.2 Dirichlet boundary conditions

We have imposed that the virtual displacement field be *kinematically* admissible, that is to say that it respects the boundary conditions in displacement (or Dirichlet conditions). In a general manner, the **linear** Dirichlet conditions are written:

$$
\sum_{j=1}^{r}{\alpha}_{j}{u}_{j}=\beta (t)
$$

With ${u}_{j}$ the list of the $r$ degrees of freedom, ${\alpha}_{j}$ the coefficients and $\beta$ the right-hand side, which may depend on time.

In Code_Aster, there are two ways of taking these conditions into account:

- By a method known as “kinematic” (definition by AFFE_CHAR_CINE), which uses a method of pseudo-elimination in the resulting linear system (see [U4.44.03](doc:u4.44.03)). In this case, one can define only simple boundary conditions such that $r=1$ ;
- By a dualisation method (definition by AFFE_CHAR_MECA), which uses the technique of double Lagrange multipliers (see [R3.01.01](doc:r3.01.01)).

It is possible to eliminate dualised boundary conditions by the use of a special method that is described in [R3.03.05](doc:r3.03.05), but only by using the PETSc solver.

We now describe the way of taking the **dualised** boundary conditions into account in the solution of a non-linear problem. In a first stage, we shall assume that the boundary conditions () can be written in the following discrete form:

$$
B.u={u}^{d}(t)
$$

$\mathrm{B}$ is a *linear* operator from the space of the displacement fields onto a space of functions defined on a part of the boundary of the structure, ${u}^{d}$ is a given function on this part. The dualisation of the Dirichlet boundary conditions $B.u={u}^{d}(t)$ leads to modifying () in the following manner:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}{L}^{int}(u,t)+{B}^{T}.\lambda ={L}^{\text{ext}}(t)\\ B.u={u}^{d}(t)\end{array}
$$

The unknowns are now, at every time instant $t$ *,* the pair $(u,\lambda )$ , where $\lambda$ represents the *Lagrange multipliers* of the Dirichlet boundary conditions. The vector ${B}^{T}.\lambda$ is then interpreted as the opposite of the support reactions at the corresponding nodes.

Denoting the quantities at the time instant ${t}_{i}$ by the index $i$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}{L}_{i}^{int}+{B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}={L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}\\ B.{u}_{i}={u}_{i}^{d}\end{array}
$$

The equilibrium residual vector ${R}_{i}({u}_{i},{\lambda}_{i},{t}_{i})$ is now:

$$
{R}_{i}({u}_{i},{\lambda}_{i},{t}_{i})=(\begin{array}{c}{L}_{i}^{int}+{B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}-{L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}\\ B.{u}_{i}-{u}_{i}^{d}\end{array})
$$

The unknowns are still computed incrementally by the global solution algorithm. From $({u}_{i-1},{\lambda}_{i-1})$ , a solution satisfying equilibrium at ${t}_{i-1}$ , one determines $\Delta {u}_{i}$ and $\Delta {\lambda}_{i}$ which will make it possible to obtain the solution at ${t}_{i}$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}{t}_{i}={t}_{i-1}+\Delta {t}_{i}\\ {u}_{i}={u}_{i-1}+\Delta {u}_{i}\\ {\lambda}_{i}={\lambda}_{i-1}+\Delta {\lambda}_{i}\end{array}
$$

## 2 Newton method

### 2.1 Principle of the method

The Newton method is a classical method for solving equations of the zero-finding type. Consider a vector function $F$ non-linear in the vector $x$ . One seeks the zero of this function, that is to say:

$$
F(x)=0
$$

The Newton method consists in constructing a sequence of vectors ${x}^{n}$ converging towards the solution $x$ . In order to find the new iterate ${x}^{n+1}$ , one approximates $F({x}^{n+1})$ by a Taylor expansion to first order about ${x}^{n}$ and one expresses that $F({x}^{n+1})$ must be zero:

$$
0=F({x}^{n+1})\approx F({x}^{n})+{F}^{'}({x}^{n})({x}^{n+1}-{x}^{n})
$$

Or again:

$$
{F}^{'}({x}^{n})({x}^{n+1}-{x}^{n})=-F({x}^{n})
$$

Finally:

$$
{x}^{n+1}={x}^{n}-{\left[{F}^{'}({x}^{n})\right]}^{-1}.F({x}^{n})
$$

${F}^{'}(x)$ is the tangent linear mapping associated with the function $F$ . The derivative at the point $x$ in the direction $h$ is defined as the following directional derivative:

$$
{F}^{'}(x).h=\underset{\theta \to 0}{\lim}\frac{F(x+\theta .h)-F(x)}{\theta}
$$

The matrix of ${F}^{'}(x)$ in the bases chosen for the vector spaces concerned is called the *Jacobian* matrix of $F$ at the point $x$ . When $F$ is a function from a Euclidean vector space into the reals, ${F}^{'}(x)$ is a linear form, and one can show that there exists a (unique) vector, denoted $\nabla F(x)$ and called the gradient of $F$ , such that:

$$
{F}^{'}(x).h={h}^{T}.\nabla F(x)
$$

That is to say the scalar product of $h$ and of the gradient of $F$ .

When one is close to the solution, the convergence of the Newton method is quadratic, that is to say that the number of zeros after the decimal point in the error doubles at each iteration (0.19 – 0.036 – 0.0013 – 0.0000017, for example). But this method (using the true tangent) has several drawbacks:

- It requires the computation of the tangent at each iteration, which is all the more costly as the size of the problem is large (especially in the case where one uses a direct solver),
- If the increment is large, the tangent (known as coherent or consistent) can lead to divergences of the algorithm,
- It may not be symmetric, which obliges one to use particular solvers.

It is for this reason that one can use other matrices in place of the tangent matrix: the elastic matrix, a tangent matrix obtained previously, the symmetrised tangent matrix, ...

### 2.2 Adaptation of the Newton method to the problem posed

In a first stage, the Dirichlet boundary conditions are not taken into account. One must solve a system (non-linear because it depends on ${u}_{i}$ ) of the form:

$$
{L}_{i}^{int}({u}_{i})={L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}({u}_{i})
$$

$$
R({u}_{i},{t}_{i})={L}_{i}^{int}-{L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}
$$

The internal forces ${L}_{i}^{int}$ can **symbolically** be denoted ${Q}_{i}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i}$ , where ${Q}_{i}^{T}$ is the matrix associated with the divergence operator (expression of the work of the virtual strain field). The internal forces are then expressed:

$$
{L}_{i}^{int}={Q}_{i}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i}={\int}_{\Omega}\varepsilon ({w}_{i}):\sigma ({u}_{i}).d\Omega
$$

And the forces of the mechanical loading:

$$
{L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}={\int}_{\Omega}{f}_{i}.{w}_{i}.d\Omega +{\int}_{\Gamma}{t}_{i}.{w}_{i}.d\Gamma
$$

Where:

- ${w}_{i}$ denotes the virtual displacement field;
- ${f}_{i}$ denotes the body forces applied at the time instant ${t}_{i}$ on $\Omega$ ;
- ${t}_{i}$ denotes the surface forces applied at the time instant ${t}_{i}$ on the boundary $\Gamma$ of $\Omega$ .

The application of the Newton method leads to solving a sequence of linear problems of the type ($n$ is the number of the Newton iteration, $i$ that of the time-instant variable):

$$
{K}_{i}^{n}.\Delta {u}_{i}^{n+1}={L}_{i}^{\text{méca},n}-{L}_{i}^{int,n}
$$

One denotes $\delta {u}_{i}^{n+1}={u}_{i}^{n+1}-{u}_{i}^{n}$ the displacement increment between two successive Newton iterations. The matrix ${K}_{i}^{n}$ is the tangent stiffness matrix at ${u}_{i}^{n}$ and the vector ${L}_{i}^{int,n}$ represents the internal forces at the ${n}^{\text{ième}}$ Newton iteration of the ${i}^{\text{ème}}$ load step. The quantity ${R}_{i}^{n}=({L}_{i}^{\text{méca},n}-{L}_{i}^{int,n})$ represents the unbalanced forces, which one calls the “equilibrium residual”. The matrix ${K}_{i}^{n}$ is the matrix of the tangent linear mapping of the function ${R}_{i}^{n}$ ; it is therefore:

$$
{K}_{i}^{n}={\frac{\partial {R}_{i}^{n}}{\partial u}\mid }_{({u}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}={\frac{\partial {L}_{i}^{int,n}}{\partial u}\mid }_{({u}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}-{\frac{\partial {L}_{i}^{\text{méca},n}}{\partial u}\mid }_{({u}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}
$$

In the absence of follower forces [§ 3.2], the second term is zero. There therefore remains of the matrix ${K}_{i}^{n}$ only the derivative at the point ${u}_{i}^{n}$ of the internal forces with respect to the displacements:

$$
{K}_{i}^{n}={\frac{\partial {L}_{i}^{int,n}}{\partial u}\mid }_{({u}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}
$$

A small error in the evaluation of the internal forces can have serious consequences, because it is their exact computation that guarantees, if one converges, that it will be towards the solution sought. On the other hand, it is not always necessary to use the true tangent matrix, whose computation and factorisation are costly. For example, a variant of the method uses the elastic matrix ${K}_{\text{élas}}$ . The method using the true tangent matrix ${K}_{i}^{n}$ (also called the coherent or consistent matrix) is called the Newton method; the methods using other matrices (such as, for example, the elastic matrix ${K}_{\text{élas}}$ ) are called modified Newton methods or quasi-Newton methods. The choice between a tangent matrix (the last one obtained or a previous matrix) and an elastic matrix is made in *Code_Aster* by means of the keyword MATRICE= 'TANGENTE' or MATRICE='ELASTIQUE' of the factor keyword NEWTON. Moreover, it is possible to use an unloading matrix, that is to say a matrix with constant internal variables (the evolution of the non-linearities is therefore not taken into account in this matrix), below a certain time step, for certain constitutive laws. One will refer to the documentation [U4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03) for the use of this functionality.

One then continues with the loop of Newton iterations, which makes it possible, at convergence, to obtain the values of $\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}$ , and therefore those of ${\mathrm{u}}_{i}$ by application of equation ():

$$
{\mathrm{u}}_{i}={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}
{\mathrm{u}}_{i}={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}
$$

### 2.3 Euler prediction phase

Experience shows that the convergence of the Newton method depends strongly on a judicious choice of the initial estimate: “the closer the initial estimate is to the solution, the faster the algorithm converges”. In order to start the iterative process of the method, it is therefore useful to determine a “good” initial increment $\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}$ . For this, one linearises the continuous problem with respect to time: this is what is called the *prediction* phase.

#### 2.3.1 Linearisation

One will therefore linearise the system () with respect to time about ${\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}$ . One begins by linearising the internal forces ${L}_{i}^{int}$ :

$$
{L}_{i}^{int}\approx {L}_{i-1}^{int}+{\frac{\partial {L}^{int}}{\partial u}\mid }_{{u}_{i-1}}.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}+{\frac{\delta {L}^{int}}{\delta t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}
$$

One assumes that the mechanical loading does not depend on time (follower loads are excluded), therefore:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}={L}_{i-1}^{\text{méca}}+\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}\\ {u}_{i}^{d}={u}_{i-1}^{d}+\Delta {u}_{i}^{d}\end{array}
$$

By substituting back into the first equation of the system, one obtains for the equilibrium equation:

$$
{\mathrm{L}}_{i-1}^{int}+{\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\partial \mathrm{u}}\mid }_{{\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}}.\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}+{\frac{\delta {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\delta t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}.\Delta {t}_{i}={\mathrm{L}}_{i-1}^{\text{méca}}+\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}
$$

One has equilibrium at the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ , that is to say:

$$
{\mathrm{L}}_{i-1}^{int}={\mathrm{L}}_{i-1}^{\text{méca}}
$$

And there therefore remains:

$$
{\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\partial \mathrm{u}}\mid }_{{\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}}.\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}+{\frac{\delta {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\delta t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}.\Delta {t}_{i}=\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}
$$

One obtains the system of equations making it possible to compute predictive values $\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}$ :

$$
{\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\partial \mathrm{u}}\mid }_{{\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}}.\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}=\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{\frac{\delta {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\delta t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}.\Delta {t}_{i}
$$

#### 2.3.2 Prediction tangent matrix

The quantity ${\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\partial \mathrm{u}}\mid }_{{\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}}$ denotes the partial derivative at constant time of ${L}_{i-1}^{int}$ ; it can be expanded:

$$
{K}_{i-1}={\frac{\partial {L}^{int}}{\partial u}\mid }_{{u}_{i-1}}=({Q}_{i-1}^{T}.{\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial u}\mid }_{{u}_{i-1}}+{\frac{\partial {Q}^{T}}{\partial u}\mid }_{{u}_{i-1}}.{\sigma}_{i-1})
$$

The matrix ${K}_{i-1}$ is called the prediction tangent matrix. The dependence of the matrix $Q$ with respect to the displacements is neglected under the small-displacement hypothesis: the term ${\frac{\partial {Q}^{T}}{\partial u}\mid }_{{u}_{i-1}}$ , called the geometric stiffness term, therefore disappears from . This term is taken into account for the large transformations (see § 1.2.2). For the developers, let us specify that the computation of the tangent matrix during the prediction phase is carried out via the computation option RIGI_MECA_TANG.

#### 2.3.3 Variants of the prediction

There exist other prediction options available in STAT_NON_LINE.

- One can use an elastic matrix ${K}_{\text{élas}}$ in place of the rate tangent matrix ${K}_{i-1}$ ; this is the option PREDICTION='ELASTIQUE' (option RIGI_MECA).
- One can use a secant matrix ${K}_{\text{sécante}}$ in place of the rate tangent matrix ${K}_{i-1}$ ; this is the option PREDICTION='SECANTE' (option RIGI_MECA_ELAS). The secant matrix is an elastic matrix whose Young's modulus is used by applying the damage (see for example [R5.03.25](doc:r5.03.25) for more details)
- One can use a previously computed displacement increment in place of the estimate; this is the option PREDICTION='DEPL_CALCULE'. In this case one performs no inversion of a system and $\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}$ is given directly. See the documentation [U4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03) for its use.
- One can use a displacement increment extrapolated with respect to the previous step. One computes the estimate of the displacement increment from the total increment obtained as the solution at the previous time step (weighted by the ratio of the time steps). This is the option PREDICTION='EXTRAPOL'.

In these last two cases, in order to ensure that the initial displacement is kinematically admissible, one projects the estimate onto the set of the kinematically admissible fields (i.e. satisfying the Dirichlet boundary conditions) according to the norm given by the **elastic** matrix, which must therefore be computed.

#### 2.3.4 Right-hand side vector of the command variables

A command variable $\beta (t)$ is a scalar quantity, a function of time and of space 1
, given a priori by the user via the keyword AFFE_VARC in the operator AFFE_MATERIAU. It is a *parameter* of the problem and not an *unknown*. The quantity ${\frac{\delta {L}^{int}}{\delta t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}$ denotes the partial differential, with respect to $t$ and with $u$ constant, of ${L}^{int}=Q.\sigma (t,\beta (t))$ . This particular notation is intended to draw attention to the fact that for ${n}_{\mathrm{varc}}$ command variables, one writes the total differential:

$$
\frac{\delta \sigma }{\delta t}=\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial t}+\sum_{j=1,{n}_{\mathrm{varc}}}^{}\frac{\delta \sigma }{\delta {\beta}^{j}}.\frac{\delta {\beta}^{j}}{\delta t}
$$

If one takes as an example the command variable describing the temperature $\theta$ :

$$
\frac{\delta \sigma }{\delta t}=\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial t}+\frac{\delta \sigma }{\delta \theta }.\frac{\delta \theta }{\delta t}
$$

One assumes that the temperature varies linearly between the two time instants:

$$
\frac{\delta \theta }{\delta t}=\frac{\Delta {\theta}_{i}}{\Delta {t}_{i}}
$$

The dependence of $\sigma$ with respect to time and with respect to temperature makes it possible to write:

$$
{\frac{\delta {L}^{int}}{\delta t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}=\frac{\delta}{\delta t}({Q}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1})={Q}_{i-1}^{T}.({\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}+{\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \theta }\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}.\frac{\Delta {\theta}_{i}}{\Delta {t}_{i}})
$$

Because $Q$ does not depend on time and therefore $\frac{\delta}{\delta t}({Q}^{T})=0$ . The vector $\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{varc}}$ , whose expression is given by , is the temperature loading increment (beware of the change of sign!) that one has generalised to all the *command variables*: temperature, irradiation, metallurgical phases (see [R4.04.02](doc:r4.04.02)), ...

$$
\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{varc}}=-{\frac{\delta {L}^{int}}{\delta t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}.\Delta {t}_{i}=-{Q}_{i-1}^{T}.({\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}.\Delta {t}_{i}+\sum_{j=1,{n}_{\mathrm{varc}}}^{}({\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial {\beta}^{j}}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}.\Delta {\beta}_{i}^{j}))
$$

One does not currently take into account the explicit dependence of the stresses with respect to time and therefore the first term of is zero. And therefore finally:

$$
\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{varc}}=-{Q}_{i-1}^{T}.(\sum_{j=1,{n}_{\mathrm{varc}}}^{}({\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial {\beta}^{j}}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}.\Delta {\beta}_{i}^{j}))
$$

The loading increment *of the command variables* $\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{varc}}$ , arising from the differentiation of the internal forces with respect to the command variables, is an estimate of the effect of a variation of the command variables.

In the case of temperature, if one denotes $K$ the hydrostatic compression modulus and $\alpha$ the thermal expansion coefficient, the thermal stress is written:

$$
{\sigma}_{i}^{\text{ther}}=-3.K.\alpha .\Delta {\theta}_{i}.I+{\sigma}_{i-1}^{\text{ther}}
\textrm{si}
\Delta {\theta}_{i}={\theta}_{i}-{\theta}_{i-1}
$$

Where $I$ is the identity matrix. And therefore, by applying:

$$
\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{ther}}=-({Q}_{i-1}^{T}.{\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \theta }\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}).\Delta {\theta}_{i}=3.K.\alpha .\Delta {\theta}_{i}.({Q}_{i-1}^{T}.I)
$$

In the elastic case, these are the internal forces associated with a thermal expansion (it is not strictly speaking a loading; it is more akin to the effect of an initial strain). This estimate is used in the prediction phase and in the stopping criterion. If the thermal expansions take the structure out of the elastic domain (plasticity, for example), this estimate will be corrected during the Newton iterations.

#### 2.3.5 Right-hand side vector of the mechanical loading

The *mechanical* loading increment $\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}$ is composed of the dead loads (independent of the geometry, such as gravity) and of the follower loads. In reality, there exist cases (the first load increment, for example) where ${L}_{i-1}^{\text{méca}}$ is unknown. One recalls that the loading increment () is written:

$$
{L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}={L}_{i-1}^{\text{méca}}+\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}
$$

One has equilibrium at the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ , therefore by applying:

$$
\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}={\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i-1}^{int}
$$

The expression of the internal forces at the previous time step ${L}_{i-1}^{int}$ implies either saving this vector from the previous analysis if it exists (restart of an earlier analysis), or integrating the constitutive law from the initial state given by the user (which can be costly). For the sake of simplicity and efficiency, one chooses not to reintegrate the constitutive law and one simply expresses the internal forces as the nodal forces by taking the stresses known at this time instant, that is:

$$
{L}_{i-1}^{int}={Q}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1}
$$

Whence the new expression:

$$
\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}={\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{\mathrm{Q}}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1}
$$

The direct computation from () requires the user to take care of the consistency between the stress field, the displacement fields and the internal-variable fields (DEPL, SIGM and VARI in ETAT_INIT) with respect to the integration of the constitutive law in the case of a restart of an analysis.

By substituting back the expression of ${\frac{\partial {L}^{int}}{\partial u}\mid }_{{u}_{i-1}}$ (equation ), of ${\frac{\delta {L}^{int}}{\delta t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}$ (equation ) and $\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}$ (equation ) into , the system of equations in prediction is written:

$$
{\mathrm{K}}_{i-1}.\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}={\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{\mathrm{Q}}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{varc}}

### 2.4 Newton correction phase

At the end of the prediction phase, we find ourselves with an estimate of the displacement increment $\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}$ . If this estimate is exact (modulo the application of the convergence criteria described in § 2.5), then one obtains the **converged** solution for the time step ${t}_{i}$ :

$$
{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{\text{convergé}}={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}
$$

But if this is not the case, one must find the value $\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}$ of the displacement increment from the value ${\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}$ obtained at the previous equilibrium (time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ ):

$$
{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{\text{convergé}}={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}
$$

If the prediction phase has converged, one therefore has trivially:

$$
\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}=\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}
$$

Otherwise, one takes as initial value $\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}$ obtained at the end of the prediction phase, before correcting by the iterations $\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}$ of the Newton method. With a number ${n}_{\mathrm{CV}}$ of Newton iterations that is sufficient (always with the arbitration of the convergence criterion):

$$
{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{\text{convergé}}={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n={n}_{\mathit{CV}}}\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{j}
$$

As long as one has not converged (if the number of Newton iterations is not sufficient), one denotes:

$$
\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}=\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n<{n}_{\mathit{CV}}}\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{j}
$$

The total displacement, for the time step $i$ and the Newton iteration $n$ , will therefore be written:

$$
{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}
$$

At each iteration, one must solve a system making it possible to determine $\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}$ , the displacement increments since the result ${\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}$ of the previous iteration:

$$
{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}+{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{j}
$$

One also denotes:

$$
\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}=\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{j}
$$

Or again:

$$
{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}={\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}
$$

#### 2.4.1 Linearisation

One must linearise the system () with respect to the unknowns at ${\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}$ with ${t}_{i}$ constant. One begins by linearising the internal forces ${L}_{i}^{int,n}$ :

$$
{L}_{i}^{int,n}\approx {L}_{i}^{int,n-1}+{\frac{\partial {L}^{int}}{\partial u}\mid }_{{u}_{i}^{n-1}}.\delta {u}_{i}^{n}
$$

One assumes that the mechanical loading does not depend on time (follower loads are excluded).

#### 2.4.2 Linear system

By substituting () back into equation (), one obtains for the equilibrium equation:

$$
{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int,n-1}+{\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\partial \mathrm{u}}\mid }_{{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}}.\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}={\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}
$$

The quantity ${\frac{\partial {L}^{int}}{\partial u}\mid }_{{u}_{i}^{n-1}}$ is called the consistent tangent matrix and it is denoted ${K}_{i}^{n-1}$ :

$$
{\frac{\partial {L}^{int}}{\partial u}\mid }_{{u}_{i}^{n-1}}
$$

The system to be solved is finally written:

$$
{\mathrm{K}}_{i}^{n-1}.\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}={\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int,n-1}
$$

The internal-force vector ${L}_{i}^{int,n-1}$ is computed from the stresses ${\sigma}_{i}^{n-1}$ . These being computed from the displacements ${\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}$ by means of the constitutive relation of the material [§ 1.1]. In fact, in the case of **incremental** behaviours, ${\sigma}_{i}^{n-1}$ is computed from $({\sigma}_{i-1},{\alpha}_{i-1})$ and from the strain increment $\epsilon (\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1})$ induced by the displacement increment since the beginning of the iterative process (including the prediction phase) or by the gradient of the transformation $\mathrm{F}$ in the case of large transformations.

#### 2.4.3 Difference between the matrices in prediction and in correction

It is important to emphasise that the tangent matrix arising from the option RIGI_MECA_TANG (prediction phase) and the tangent matrix arising from the option FULL_MECA (correction phase) are in general not identical. Suppose that one has reached convergence for the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ and that one now seeks to obtain equilibrium for the following time instant ${t}_{i}$ . The matrix arising from RIGI_MECA_TANG comes from a linearisation of the equilibrium equations with respect to **time** about $({u}_{i-1},{\lambda}_{i-1})$ *i.e.* about equilibrium at the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ . It is therefore the tangent matrix of the system converged at the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ .

On the other hand, the matrix arising from FULL_MECA comes from a linearisation of the equilibrium equations with respect to the **displacement** about ${\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}$ *i.e.* about equilibrium at the time instant ${t}_{i}$ .

One can interpret the differences between RIGI_MECA_TANG and FULL_MECA in other terms. One can thus show that the matrix arising from RIGI_MECA_TANG corresponds to the tangent operator of the problem **continuous** in time, also called the rate problem (and relates the stress rate to the strain rate), whereas the matrix arising from FULL_MECA corresponds to the tangent operator of the problem **discretised** in time. The document [R5.03.02](doc:r5.03.02) gives the expression in each of the two cases for the elastoplastic relation of Von Mises with isotropic or linear kinematic hardening.

One recalls that the treatment of a constitutive relation [R5.03.02 § 5] consists in:

- Computing the stresses ${\sigma}_{i}^{n}$ and the internal variables ${\alpha}_{i}^{n}$ from the initial state $({\sigma}_{i-1},{\alpha}_{i-1})$ and from the strain increment $\varepsilon (\Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1})$ induced by the displacement increment since the beginning of the iterative process (including the prediction phase).
- Computing the internal forces ${L}_{i}^{int,n}={Q}_{i}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i}^{n}$ .
- Computing (possibly) the tangent matrix (option RIGI_MECA_TANG for the prediction phase, option FULL_MECA for the Newton iterations).

### 2.5 Convergence criteria

At the end of the prediction and at each Newton iteration, one must estimate whether the iterative process has converged (the equilibrium of the structure is reached). One places oneself at the current time step ${t}_{i}$ and at the Newton iteration $n$ (it being understood that the value $n=0$ corresponds to the prediction). There exist four convergence criteria.

The criterion RESI_GLOB_MAXI consists in checking that the infinite norm 2
of the residual is less than the value $\gamma$ specified by the user.

$$
{\parallel {\mathrm{Q}}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i}^{n}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}\parallel }_{\infty}\le \gamma
$$

It is not advisable to use this criterion alone, because one cannot easily have an idea of the admissible absolute orders of magnitude.

The criterion RESI_GLOB_RELA (chosen by default) amounts to checking that the residual is sufficiently small, as previously, and this relative to a quantity representative of the loading.

$$
\frac{{\parallel {\mathrm{Q}}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i}^{n}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}\parallel }_{\infty}}{{\parallel {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}+{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{varc}}\parallel }_{\infty}}\le \eta
$$

$\eta$ being the desired relative accuracy given by the user under the keyword RESI_GLOB_RELA (or the default value of ${10}^{\text{-6}}$ ).

One may note that, in the case of use of RESI_GLOB_RELA, the criterion can become singular if the external loading ${\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}+{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{varc}}$ becomes zero. This can happen in the event of a total unloading of the structure. If such a situation arises (i.e. a loading ${10}^{\text{‑6}}$ times smaller than the smallest loading observed up to the present increment), the code then uses the criterion RESI_GLOB_MAXI with, as value, that observed at the convergence of the previous increment. When the loading becomes non-zero again, one returns to the initial criterion.

The third criterion is the criterion RESI_REFE_RELA: the idea of this criterion is to construct a reference nodal force, which will serve to estimate, term by term, the (approximate) nullity of the residual:

$$
\forall j\in \left\lbrace \text{ddl}\right\rbrace \mid {\left({\mathrm{Q}}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i}^{n}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}\right)}_{j}\mid \le \epsilon .{\mathrm{F}}_{j}^{\text{ref}}
$$

More precisely, the reference nodal force ${\mathrm{F}}_{j}^{\text{ref}}$ is constructed from the datum of a reference amplitude ${A}^{\text{ref}}$ which can be:

- A stress;
- A pressure, a temperature in the case of THM;
- A generalised force in the case of beams or shells;
- others ...

The list is accessible in the user's documentation of the command STAT_NON_LINE [U4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03), description of the operand RESI_REFE_RELA.

If one takes a stress as an example, the reference amplitude ${\sigma}^{\text{ref}}$ being given by the user via the keyword SIGM_REFE. From this reference stress amplitude, one defines the tensor ${\sigma}^{\text{test}}$ : it is zero for all its components, except the *j*-th, which is equal to ${\sigma}^{\text{ref}}$ . One then defines, for each node of each element, the nodal force ${\tilde{R}}_{i}^{e}$ (the aim being to give an idea of the importance of a component, at a Gauss point, of the stress on the nodal force):

$$
{\tilde{R}}_{i}^{e}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha =1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mid {B}_{i,j}^{\alpha}{\sigma}_{j}^{\text{test}}\mid {\omega}^{\alpha}
$$

With $N$ the number of Gauss points of the element, $M$ the number of components of the stress tensor; the exponent $\alpha$ serving to denote the evaluation of the quantity at the Gauss point. For example ${\omega}^{\alpha}$ are the weights of the Gauss points.

**Note:** For certain elements, such as the bars, the grids or the membranes, this definition leads to zero reference residuals on certain axes. In order to remedy this, one determines the nodal reference forces via an order-of-magnitude computation based on the size of the element.

The reference nodal force is then defined by:

$$
{F}_{i}^{\text{ref}}=\underset{e\in {\Gamma}_{i}}{\min}{\tilde{R}}_{i}^{e}
$$

where ${\Gamma}_{i}$ is the set of the elements connected to the node $i$ .

The fourth criterion is the criterion RESI_COMP_RELA: the idea of this criterion is to separate the various contributions of the residual, component by component (in the sense of DX, DY, DZ, PRE1, PRE2, TEMP). Each of the vectors obtained will then be normed by the internal force corresponding to this residual. This choice of convergence criterion makes sense only for strongly evolutionary problems, typically THM problems. Moreover, this choice will be effective as soon as one has problems with strong contrasts. It is indeed the zones with a “strong gradient” that will control the convergence. One defines ${F}^{n}(c)$ the part of the residual ${\mathrm{Q}}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i}^{n}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}$ corresponding to the component $c$ and ${\mathrm{L}}^{int,0}(c)$ the vector of the internal forces in prediction corresponding to this same component $c$ . The criterion RESI_COMP_RELA then amounts to checking that this residual is sufficiently small, that is to say:

$$
\underset{c=1,\dots ,\mathit{nbcmp}}{\max}\left(\frac{\underset{d=1,\dots ,\mathit{nbddl}}{\max}|{\mathrm{F}}^{n}(c,d)|}{\underset{d=1,\dots ,\mathit{nbddl}}{\max}|{\mathrm{L}}^{int,0}(c,d)|}\right)<\epsilon
$$

Convergence is declared to have been achieved when all the criteria specified by the user are satisfied simultaneously. By default, one performs a test on the relative global residual (RESI_GLOB_RELA) and the maximum number of Newton iterations (ITER_GLOB_MAXI).

For the residuals RESI_GLOB_RELA and RESI_GLOB_MAXI, all the components of the displacement field are used in the evaluation of the norm ${\mathrm{\parallel }.\mathrm{\parallel }}_{\infty}$ , except in two cases where a particular treatment is made at the level of the choice of the components:

- For loadings of type AFFE_CHAR_CINE, the degree of freedom concerned is ignored in the evaluation of the norm of the residual, because the procedure of elimination of the unknowns does not make it possible to access the support reactions;
- For continuous contact, the components LAGR_C and LAGR_F1/LAGR_F2 are ignored in the evaluation of the norm, because the Signorini–Coulomb law is already satisfied in the algorithm (see [R5.03.52](doc:r5.03.52)) and because these terms are dimensionally inconsistent with those relating to the displacements; On the other hand, for the case of contact in XFEM, these components are retained because they serve to check the LBB condition;

### 2.6 Use of an evolving tangent-secant matrix

The method described in this paragraph applies exclusively to strongly non-linear problems, where a classical Newton method fails for any type of choice of matrix, for the prediction or the correction phase. Typically, the usual Newton method is found wanting for ill-posed problems arising from the use of softening constitutive laws (see for example R5.04.01).

In these situations, a non-convergence appears when the algorithm does not succeed in choosing between several admissible solutions, in a given increment of (pseudo)-time. This lack of convergence at the global level is most often reflected at the local level (i.e. at the integration point) by a repeated alternation between a non-dissipative state (elastic) and a dissipative state (plasticity, damage, …) in the course of consecutive Newton iterations.

One of the strategies consists in using the notion of piloting (see R5.03.80) in order to remedy the shortcomings of Newton. The other strategy consists in modifying the tangent matrix. It is this latter strategy that is detailed here.

By following the state of each integration point from one iteration to the next, one can identify the points responsible for a global non-convergence. Once these points have been identified, one decides to modify the matrix in the hope that this modification will allow a global convergence.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.01/Shape1.gif)

Figure 2‑2.6-a: Softening constitutive law, use of a mixed tangent-secant matrix

The approach named tangent-secant, activated under the keyword COMPORTEMENT with TANGENTE_SECANTE=’OUI’, is justified by the following reasoning: if the Newton method using the consistent tangent matrix does not converge, it is often because a certain (variable) number of integration points changes state (non-dissipative/dissipative) between two global Newton iterations. At the local level (see Figure 2-2.6-a), this means that one continues to alternate between domain 1 ($\varepsilon <{\varepsilon}_{\text{pic}}$ ) and domain 2 ($\varepsilon >{\varepsilon}_{\text{pic}}$ ). Because of the change of slope between 1 and 2, the use of a secant or consistent tangent matrix leads to a very poor approximation, whence the interest of modifying it. The choice that is presented consists in constructing the tangent matrix as a linear combination of the consistent tangent matrix and the secant matrix, both being determined by the constitutive laws. Currently, the approach is available only for the softening constitutive law ENDO_ISOT_BETON.

In order to manage the triggering of the tangent-secant option, one introduces an additional internal variable with respect to the existing internal variables, $\alpha =({\alpha}_{1},\dots {\alpha}_{n},{\alpha}_{n+1})$ (with $n$ the number of internal variables of the model used). This variable accounts for the possible alternation between the elastic state and the softening state of a Gauss point. One initialises it at the first Newton iteration of each new time step, then one makes it evolve in order to know, at each Gauss point, the number of successive alternations between the two states. Having this information, one can estimate that, from a certain threshold onwards (for example ${S}_{0}=3$ alternations), the Newton algorithm will no longer be able to converge for the current time increment and that it is necessary to modify the tangent matrix. In order to modify the matrix, one relies directly on the way in which the consistent tangent matrix in ENDO_ISOT_BETON is constructed (see [R7.01.04](doc:r7.01.04)). It is a matter of summing the dissipative part and the non-dissipative part.

$$
{K}_{\text{tg}}={K}_{\text{sc}}+{K}_{\text{end}}
$$

where ${K}_{\text{tg}}$ is the tangent matrix, ${K}_{\text{sc}}$ the secant matrix (non-dissipative part) and ${K}_{\text{end}}$ the damaging correction (dissipative part). For the modified matrix ${K}_{\text{t-s}}$ , one replaces the expression ${K}_{\text{tg}}$ in () by:

$$
{K}_{\text{t - s}}={K}_{\text{sc}}+\eta .{K}_{\text{end}}
$$

where $\eta$ is a function of ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ with values between 0 and 1. The function $\eta$ retained in what follows is the following:

$$
\eta =\frac{1}{{A}^{({\alpha}_{n+1}-{S}_{0})}}
$$

where $A$ is a constant and ${S}_{0}$ the threshold on the value of the number of successive alternations undergone, from which the tangent matrix is modified. For ${\alpha}_{n+1}={S}_{0}$ the matrix remains consistently tangent and for ${\alpha}_{n+1}\gg {S}_{0}$ , it becomes secant. Values judged satisfactory for these parameters are $A=1,5$ and ${S}_{0}=3$ (default values). The choice of the evolution of the value of ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ is essential for the performance of the algorithm. One chooses an increase of ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ by $G=1,0$ , ${\alpha}_{n+1}\to {\alpha}_{n+1}+G$ for each new alternation between an elastic state and a damaging state, then a decrease of ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ by $P$ , ${\alpha}_{n+1}\to {\alpha}_{n+1}-P$ , when the state remains damaging twice in succession. The aim of the use of $P$ is to allow the return to the consistent tangent matrix when a Gauss point remains damaging over several iterations, since the consistent tangent matrix makes the convergence quadratic, provided that one is close to the solution. The value of the rate of decrease $P$ relative to the rate of increase $G$ is crucial for the behaviour of the evolving algorithm. The overall idea consists in increasing ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ when one is far from the solution, in order to have an operator closer to the secant than to the consistent tangent, then, once “close” to the solution, in switching to the consistent tangent matrix (which is the best in the sense of Newton). The ratio $P/G$ (keyword TAUX_RETOUR – 0.05 by default) represents the third parameter of the algorithm, in addition to $A$ (keyword AMPLITUDE) and ${S}_{0}$ (keyword SEUIL).

### 2.7 Newton-Krylov method

#### 2.7.1 General principle

The Newton-Krylov method is one of the inexact Newton methods. It can be used only when the solver of the linear system () is iterative (as opposed to a direct solver). This approach does not modify the choice of the tangent matrix of the system as the previous methods do. It acts on the convergence criterion of the iterative solver used for the linearised system. By adapting as well as possible the convergence criterion of the iterative method to the Newton convergence, one avoids performing useless iterations (in the linear solver) and one thus gains in analysis time.

#### 2.7.2 Implementation

The general principle of the inexact Newton methods is to replace the condition that the solution increment $\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}$ be the exact solution of the system () by a weaker condition. One requires that $\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}$ satisfy the condition:

$$
∥\begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{K}}_{i}^{n-1}.\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}-\left({\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int,n-1}\right)\end{array}∥⩽{\eta}_{n}∥{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int,n-1}∥
$$

where ${\eta}_{n}$ is called the *forcing term*.

One can show that the method proposed is convergent and that when the sequence ${\eta}_{n}$ tends towards $0$ , the convergence is super-linear (see [ 8 ] p.96). The smaller this value is, the closer the solution will be to that obtained by an exact solution, but the less time one will gain in the solution of the linear system. One must therefore find a good compromise between solving the linear systems rapidly and not degrading too much the convergence of the Newton iterations.

By examining condition (), one observes that it is identical to the relative convergence criterion of the iterative solvers used to solve the linearised Newton system. In order to check this condition, it is therefore a matter of using the *forcing term* as the convergence criterion of an iterative linear solver.

As has been seen previously, one must also ensure that the sequence ${\eta}_{n}$ tends towards $0$ in order to retain the super-linear convergence of the Newton method. To this end, one will slave ${\eta}_{n}$ to the decrease of the Newton residual by the evolution law (*cf.* [ 8 ] p.105):

$$
{\eta}_{n+1}^{\mathit{Res}}=\gamma \frac{{∥{\mathrm{R}}_{i}^{n}∥}^{2}}{{∥{\mathrm{R}}_{i}^{n-1}∥}^{2}}
$$

where the constant is chosen such that $\gamma =0.1$ .

This simple formula is not sufficient in practice, because one must guarantee an adequate decrease of ${\eta}_{n}$ . For this, one determines ${\eta}_{n}$ completely by the following expression:

$$
{\eta}_{n+1}=\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{\eta}_{0}& n=-1& \\ max(min(0.4{\eta}_{n},{\eta}_{n+1}^{\mathit{Res}}),{\eta}_{min})& n⩾0,& (1-\gamma ){\eta}_{n}^{2}⩽0.2\\ min(0.4{\eta}_{n},max({\eta}_{n+1}^{\mathit{Res}},(1-\gamma ){\eta}_{n}^{2}))& n⩾0,& (1-\gamma ){\eta}_{n}^{2}>0.2\end{array}
$$

where the constant is equal to ${\eta}_{0}=0.9$ and corresponds to the convergence criterion used for the first linear solution.

${\eta}_{min}$ , for its part, is the value of the convergence criterion of the iterative linear solver supplied by the user (keyword RESI_RELA).

To be more precise, a command variable is assigned to a mesh cell. It is therefore not a function of the geometry (and therefore of the displacements), but of the topology of the mesh.

## 3 Boundary conditions and loadings

### 3.1 Classification

In the following paragraphs, we shall describe the set of the loadings possible in the operator and their classification. In the first place, one should distinguish three main categories of loadings:

- The Dirichlet loadings (boundary conditions) apply to the nodal unknowns in the broad sense: this can be a displacement, a temperature or a pressure (for THM). They can be applied by elimination (AFFE_CHAR_CINE) or by dualisation (AFFE_CHAR_MECA). When the boundary conditions are treated by elimination, they are taken into account at the moment of the solution of the linear system, internally, and they therefore do not modify in any way the equations already described previously;
- The Neumann loadings apply in general to the fluxes. They are conditions in forces, in general, defined on the mesh cells (except for the loading FORCE_NODALE).

There is another distinction to be made between the **follower** loadings and those that are not. A loading is a follower loading when it depends on the geometry of the structure. Typically, pressure is exerted in the direction opposite to the normal. Therefore, when the structure deforms with the evolution of the load, the loading, expressed in an absolute frame, is transformed. There also exist follower Dirichlet loadings. These are boundary conditions that depend on the geometry because they are non-linear. The loads that do not depend on the geometry of the structure are called dead or fixed loads (for example, gravity).

### 3.2 Follower loadings

In order to indicate that a load must be treated as a follower load in STAT_NON_LINE, one indicates TYPE_CHARGE='SUIV' under the keyword EXCIT. A mechanical loading ${L}^{\text{méca}}({u}_{i})$ comprising follower loads is therefore decomposed into two parts:

$$
{L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}({u}_{i})={L}_{i}^{\text{fixe}}+{L}_{i}^{\text{suiv}}({u}_{i})
$$

The exponent ${}^{\mathrm{fixe}}$ denotes here the dead loads, and ${}^{\text{suiv}}$ the follower loads. The system of equations to be solved then becomes:

$$
{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int}={\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{fixe}}+{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{suiv}}({\mathrm{u}}_{i})
$$

The differentiation operations making it possible to write the prediction phase and the iterations of the Newton method therefore involve the derivatives of ${L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}({u}_{i})={L}_{i}^{\text{fixe}}+{L}_{i}^{\text{suiv}}({u}_{i})$ with respect to the displacements $({u}_{i})$ . The prediction phase becomes:

$$
\left({\mathrm{K}}_{i-1}-{\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}^{\text{suiv}}}{\partial \mathrm{u}}\mid }_{{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}}\right).\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}=\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{fixe}}+\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{suiv}}+\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{varc}}
$$

And the Newton iterations consist in solving the system:

$$
\left({\mathrm{K}}_{i}^{n-1}-{\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}^{\text{suiv}}}{\partial \mathrm{u}}\mid }_{{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}}\right).\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}={\mathrm{L}}^{\text{fixe}}+{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{suiv},n-1}({\mathrm{u}}_{i})-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int,n-1}
$$

Thus, at the beginning of each load step (prediction) and at each Newton iteration, one must compute a stiffness matrix ${\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}^{\text{suiv}}}{\partial \mathrm{u}}\mid }_{\mathrm{u}}$ and a vector ${L}^{\text{suiv}}$ linked to the follower loadings.

The only Neumann loads that can be treated as follower loads in the current state of the operator STAT_NON_LINE are:

- The pressure for the modellings 3D, 3D_SI, D_PLAN, D_PLAN_SI, AXIS, AXIS_SI, C_PLAN, C_PLAN_SI [R3.03.04](doc:r3.03.04) and COQUE_3D [R3.03.07](doc:r3.03.07);
- The gravity loading for the elements CABLE_POULIE [R3.08.05](doc:r3.08.05), three-node elements comprising a pulley and two cable strands: the gravity force exerted on the element depends on the respective lengths of the two strands;
- The centrifugal force in large displacements, which for a rotation speed $\omega$ is given by: ${\int}_{\Omega}\rho .\omega \wedge \left[\omega \wedge \text{OM}\right].d\Omega ={\int}_{\Omega}\rho .\omega \wedge \left[\omega \wedge ({\text{OM}}_{0}+u)\right].d\Omega$ . Available for the modellings 3D and AXIS_FOURIER;
- The gravity loading for all the THM modellings of unsaturated porous media [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10): indeed, the density depends on the nodal variables in order to take into account the constitutive relations of the geomaterials.

### 3.3 Dualised boundary conditions – Linear case

We shall now take the dualised boundary conditions into account in the writing of the linear system to be solved. In a first stage, we shall consider only **linear** relations. One assumes the existence of a potential $J(v)$ such that the minimisation of the latter makes it possible to express the mechanical equilibrium (in the weak sense) of the structure. If $J(u)$ is this minimum then one has:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\text{Trouver}u\in V\text{tel que :}\\ \lbrace \begin{array}{c}J(u)=\underset{v\in V}{min}J(v)\equiv \lbrace {L}^{int}(u,v)-{L}^{\text{ext}}(u,v)\rbrace \\ \text{Avec :}V=\lbrace v\in {R}^{n}\text{tel que}\mathit{Bv}=0\rbrace \end{array}\end{array}
$$

- ${L}^{int}(u,v)$ denotes the sum of the discretised internal forces. It depends on the solution sought via the constitutive laws, the large-transformation kinematics, etc.
- ${L}^{\text{ext}}(u,v)$ denotes the sum of the discretised external forces. It can depend on the solution as in the case of follower forces, of contact, etc.
- $V$ denotes the set of the admissible test solutions;
- $U$ denotes the set of the admissible solutions sought;
- $B$ is a *linear* operator from the space of the displacement fields onto a space of functions defined on a part of the boundary of the structure;

**Note:** in mechanics, one can consider the presence of dissipative forces linked to the viscous character of the constitutive law or to the friction forces. In these cases the potential is replaced by the existence of a pseudo-potential of dissipation. One does not go into the details but one shows that one can come back within the framework of an optimisation problem thanks to the Legendre–Fenchel transformation. For the sake of simplicity, one keeps the general framework of the previous equation, because the rest of the developments is not affected by the existence of a pseudo-potential.

In discrete form, the dualisation of the Dirichlet boundary conditions $\mathrm{B}.\mathrm{u}={\mathrm{u}}^{d}(t)$ leads to the following problem [R3.03.01]:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}{L}^{int}(u,t)+{B}^{T}.\lambda ={L}^{\text{ext}}(t)\\ B.u={u}^{d}(t)\end{array}
$$

The unknowns are now, at every time instant $t$ *,* the pair $(u,\lambda )$ , where $\lambda$ represents the *Lagrange multipliers* of the Dirichlet boundary conditions [R3.03.01](doc:r3.03.01). The vector ${B}^{T}.\lambda$ is interpreted as the opposite of the support reactions at the corresponding nodes. Parameterising by the time instant ${t}_{i}$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}{L}_{i}^{int}+{B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}={L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}\\ B.{u}_{i}={u}_{i}^{d}\end{array}
$$

Finding equilibrium therefore consists in cancelling at $({u}_{i},{\lambda}_{i},{t}_{i})$ the equilibrium residual vector, which will be defined by:

$$
{R}_{i}({u}_{i},{\lambda}_{i},{t}_{i})=(\begin{array}{c}{L}_{i}^{int}+{B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}-{L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}\\ B.{u}_{i}-{u}_{i}^{d}\end{array})
$$

The unknowns are computed incrementally by the global solution algorithm, including the Lagrange multipliers of the dualised boundary conditions. From $({u}_{i-1},{\lambda}_{i-1})$ , a solution satisfying equilibrium at ${t}_{i-1}$ , one determines $\Delta {u}_{i}$ and $\Delta {\lambda}_{i}$ which will make it possible to obtain the solution at ${t}_{i}$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}{t}_{i}={t}_{i-1}+\Delta {t}_{i}\\ {u}_{i}={u}_{i-1}+\Delta {u}_{i}\\ {\lambda}_{i}={\lambda}_{i-1}+\Delta {\lambda}_{i}\end{array}
$$

#### 3.3.1 Euler prediction phase

For the prediction, one must always linearise the system () with respect to time but, in this case, about the two unknowns (displacements and Lagrange multipliers) $({u}_{i-1},{\lambda}_{i-1})$ . One notes first that the linearisation of the support reactions ${B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}$ is immediate because one considers linear boundary conditions and therefore the matrix $\mathrm{B}$ is constant (it does not depend on the displacements or on time). Since ${\lambda}_{i}=\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}+{\lambda}_{i-1}$ , one has immediately:

$$
{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}={\mathrm{B}}^{T}.\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i-1}
$$

One assumes that the mechanical loading does not depend on time (follower loads are excluded) and that the Dirichlet boundary conditions are also linear, therefore:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}={\mathrm{L}}_{i-1}^{\text{méca}}+\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}\\ {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{d}={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}^{d}+\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{d}\end{array}
$$

One obtains for the equilibrium equation:

$$
{\mathrm{L}}_{i-1}^{int}+{\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\partial \mathrm{u}}\mid }_{{\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}}.\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}+{\frac{\delta {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\delta t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}.\Delta {t}_{i}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i-1}={\mathrm{L}}_{i-1}^{\text{méca}}+\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}
$$

One has equilibrium at the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ , that is to say:

$$
{\mathrm{L}}_{i-1}^{int}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i-1}={\mathrm{L}}_{i-1}^{\text{méca}}
$$

And there therefore remains:

$$
{\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\partial \mathrm{u}}\mid }_{{\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}}.\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}+{\frac{\delta {\mathrm{L}}^{int}}{\delta t}\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}.\Delta {t}_{i}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}=\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}
$$

If one now looks at the second equation of the system (), one obtains for the Dirichlet boundary conditions:

$$
\mathrm{B}.({\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0})={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}^{d}+\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{d}
$$

One has equilibrium at the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ , that is to say:

$$
\mathrm{B}.{\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}={\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}^{d}
$$

There finally remains:

$$
\mathrm{B}.\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}=\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{d}
$$

One obtains the system of equations making it possible to compute predictive values $(\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0},\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0})$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}{K}_{i-1}.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}+{B}^{T}.\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}={L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{Q}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1}-{B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i-1}+\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{varc}}\\ B.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}=\Delta {u}_{i}^{d}\end{array}
$$

One will then note that this expression now involves the Lagrange multipliers at the *previous* time instant, which are sometimes unknown (at the first load increment, for example). Which means that, with this new expression, one has moved the problem of the knowledge of the internal forces at the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ towards the ignorance of the Lagrange multipliers at this same time instant! But since the boundary conditions are linear, the problem is simplified. Let us consider that the solution of () as regards the Lagrange multipliers ${\hat{\lambda}}_{i}$ is written in incremental form:

$$
{\hat{\lambda}}_{i}={\hat{\lambda}}_{i-1}+{\Delta \hat{\lambda}}_{i}
$$

This solution solves the first equation of the system:

$$
{K}_{i-1}.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}+{B}^{T}.{\Delta \hat{\lambda}}_{i}^{0}={L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{Q}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1}-{B}^{T}.{\hat{\lambda}}_{i-1}+\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{varc}}
$$

The idea is to seek this ${\hat{\lambda}}_{i}$ . Since the operator ${B}^{T}$ is constant, by applying , one has:

$$
{K}_{i-1}.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}+{B}^{T}.{\hat{\lambda}}_{i}={L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{Q}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1}+\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{varc}}
$$

One assumes that the boundary conditions are satisfied, therefore:

$$
B.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}=\Delta {u}_{i}^{d}
$$

The imposed displacements are also written in incremental form:

$$
{u}_{i}^{d}={u}_{i-1}^{d}+\Delta {u}_{i}^{d}
$$

The matrix $B$ is constant; we therefore had at the previous increment (the problem has been solved):

$$
B.{u}_{i-1}={u}_{i-1}^{d}
$$

By using and in :

$$
B.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}=\Delta {u}_{i}^{d}={u}_{i}^{d}-{u}_{i-1}^{d}={u}_{i}^{d}-B.{u}_{i-1}
$$

At equilibrium, one therefore has ${\hat{\lambda}}_{i}$ which also satisfies the boundary conditions, which one rewrites:

$$
B.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}={u}_{i}^{d}-B.{u}_{i-1}
$$

The vector of the Lagrange multipliers ${\hat{\lambda}}_{i}$ can therefore be found during the prediction phase by modifying the equation of imposition of the boundary conditions by the expression . By analogy with the displacement increment found in prediction $\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}$ one will denote $\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}={\hat{\lambda}}_{i}$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}{K}_{i-1}.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}+{B}^{T}.\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}={L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{Q}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1}+\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{varc}}\\ B.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}={u}_{i}^{d}-B.{u}_{i-1}\end{array}
$$

A particular case concerns the use of an excitation of type TYPE_CHARGE= 'DIDI', meaning differential Dirichlet, that is to say with respect to the initial state. This consists, for boundary conditions of the blocking type, in imposing, not $B.u={u}^{d}$ , but $B.(u-{u}_{\text{didi}})={u}^{d}$ In this case, the system to be solved in the prediction phase for the new load increment is:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}{K}_{i-1}.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}+{B}^{T}.\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}={L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{Q}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1}+\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{varc}}\\ B.\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}={u}_{i}^{d}-B.{u}_{i-1}+B.{u}_{\text{didi}}\end{array}
$$

#### 3.3.2 Newton correction phase

One must linearise the system with respect to the unknowns at $({u}_{i}^{n},{\lambda}_{i}^{n})$ with ${t}_{i}$ constant. One begins by linearising the internal forces ${L}_{i}^{int,n}$ :

$$
{L}_{i}^{int,n}\approx {L}_{i}^{int,n-1}+{\frac{\partial {L}^{int}}{\partial u}\mid }_{{u}_{i}^{n-1}}.\delta {u}_{i}^{n}
$$

The linearisation of the support reactions ${B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}$ is immediate (linear conditions):

$$
{B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}={B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n-1}+{B}^{T}.\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n}
$$

One assumes that the mechanical loading does not depend on time (follower loads are excluded) and that the Dirichlet boundary conditions are also linear. One obtains for the equilibrium equation:

$$
{L}_{i}^{int,n-1}+{\frac{\partial {L}^{int}}{\partial u}\mid }_{{u}_{i}^{n-1}}.\delta {u}_{i}^{n}+{B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n-1}+{B}^{T}.\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n}={L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}
$$

For the boundary conditions, the linearisation of the system () gives us, in a manner analogous to :

$$
B.\delta {u}_{i}^{n}={u}_{i}^{d}-B.{u}_{i}^{n-1}
$$

The system to be solved is finally written:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{K}}_{i}^{n-1}.\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n}={\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int,n-1}-{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n-1}\\ \mathrm{B}.\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}={\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{d}-\mathrm{B}.{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}\end{array}
$$

#### 3.3.3 Adaptation of the convergence criteria

One makes explicit here the adaptation of RESI_GLOB_MAXI and RESI_GLOB_RELA to the cases of dualised boundary conditions. The criterion RESI_GLOB_MAXI consists in checking that the infinite norm of the residual is less than the value $\gamma$ specified by the user. It is necessary to introduce the contribution of the dualised boundary conditions:

$$
{\parallel {\mathrm{Q}}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i}^{n}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}+-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}\parallel }_{\infty}\le \gamma
$$

- The criterion RESI_GLOB_RELA (chosen by default) amounts to checking that the residual is sufficiently small, as previously, and this relative to a quantity representative of the loading.

$$
\textrm{*}
\frac{{\parallel {\mathrm{Q}}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i}^{n}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}\parallel }_{\infty}}{{\parallel {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}+{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{varc}}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}\parallel }_{\infty}}\le \eta
$$

If $J(u)$ is this minimum then one has:

#### 3.4.1 Equilibrium problem under non-linear equality conditions

One assumes the existence of a discrete non-linear equality $C(u)$ from ${ℝ}^{n}$ into $ℝ$ depending on the solution $\textcolor{#000000}{u\in {R}^{n}}$ such that one has the following problem:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\text{Trouver}u\in V\text{tel que :}\\ \lbrace \begin{array}{c}J(u)=\underset{v\in V}{min}J(v)\equiv \lbrace {L}^{int}(u,v)-{L}^{\text{ext}}(u,v)\rbrace \\ \text{Avec :}V=\lbrace v\in {R}^{n}\text{tel que}\mathit{Bv}=0\text{et}C(v)=0\rbrace \end{array}\end{array}
$$

One assumes that $\textcolor{#000000}{C(.)}$ is a differentiable function
of class at least one. The previous problem () and the problem () are such that one seeks the minimum in a region of admissible solutions $\textcolor{#000000}{V\subset {R}^{n}}$ . The difficulty lies in the a priori construction of the set of the admissible solutions, which is not direct in the case of the problem () because of the unknown character (depending on the solution) of this admissible space. One will use the technique of dualisation by the Lagrange multipliers; one writes the augmented Lagrangian of this system:

$$
L(v,{\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}},{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}})=J(v)+{\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}}\mathit{Bv}+{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}}C(v)
$$

Whence the following problem, equivalent to the problem ():

$$
\begin{array}{c}\text{Trouver les extrémums locaux}u\in {ℝ}^{n},{\lambda}_{l}\in ℝ\text{et}{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}\in ℝ\\ \text{de}L(v,{\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}},{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}})\\ \forall (v,{\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}},{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}})\in {ℝ}^{n}\times ℝ\times ℝ\end{array}
$$

For this, it suffices to establish the stationarity conditions of the Lagrangian, that is:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{{\partial}_{v}L(v,{\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}},{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}})\mid }_{u,{\lambda}_{l},{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}}\delta v=0\\ {{\partial}_{{\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}}}L(v,{\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}},{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}})\mid }_{u,{\lambda}_{l},{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}}\delta {\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}}=0\\ {{\partial}_{{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}}}L(v,{\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}},{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}})\mid }_{u,{\lambda}_{l},{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}}\delta {\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}}=0\end{array}\forall (\delta v,\delta {\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}},\delta {\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}})\in {ℝ}^{n}\times ℝ\times ℝ
$$

Whence the following non-linear system to be solved:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{L}^{int}(u,\delta v)-{L}^{\text{ext}}(u,\delta v)+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}{\lambda}_{l}\delta v+\nabla {\mathrm{C}}^{T}{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}\delta v=0\\ \delta {\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{B}u=0\\ \delta {\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{C}(u)=0\end{array}\forall (\delta v,\delta {\lambda}_{l}^{\text{*}},\delta {\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{\text{*}})\in {ℝ}^{n}\times ℝ\times ℝ
$$

Which one will write in a more compact form:

$$
S(u,\lambda )=0
$$

#### 3.4.2 Linearisation of the problem

In order to solve this problem one also uses the Newton method. But one must linearise the system (). One places oneself at the time step ${t}_{i}$ (by simplification of the writings, one omits to specify the dependence of the quantities on the time step). One approximates the quantities at the Newton iteration $n$ , with respect to these same quantities at the iteration $n-1$ :

$$
S({u}^{n},{\lambda}^{n})\approx S({u}^{n-1},{\lambda}^{n-1})+\langle {\partial}_{u}S({u}^{n-1},{\lambda}^{n-1}),\Delta u\rangle +\langle {\partial}_{\lambda}S({u}^{n-1},{\lambda}^{n-1}),\Delta \lambda \rangle \approx 0
$$

One sets:

$$
\langle K({u}^{n-1}),\Delta u\rangle =\langle {\partial}_{u}S({u}^{n-1},{\lambda}^{n-1}),\Delta u\rangle +\langle {\partial}_{\lambda}S({u}^{n-1},{\lambda}^{n-1}),\Delta \lambda \rangle
$$

By expanding all the terms:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\langle K({u}^{n-1}),\Delta u\rangle =\delta v\left\lbrace {\partial}_{u}{L}^{int,n-1}\Delta u-{\partial}_{u}{L}^{\text{ext},n-1}\Delta u+{{\partial}^{2}}_{{u}^{2}}{\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{n-1}\Delta u\right\rbrace \\ \delta v\left\lbrace {\mathrm{B}}^{T}\Delta {\lambda}_{l}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\delta {\lambda}_{l}\left\lbrace \mathrm{B}\Delta u\right\rbrace \\ \delta v\left\lbrace {\partial}_{u}{\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}\Delta {\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\delta {\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}\left\lbrace {\partial}_{u}{\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}\Delta u\right\rbrace \end{array}
$$

One finally has the following system:

$$
\langle K({u}^{n-1}),\Delta u\rangle =\langle \begin{array}{ccc}\delta v& \delta {\lambda}_{l}& \delta {\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}\end{array}\rangle \left[\begin{array}{ccc}{K}^{int,n-1}+{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{n-1}\nabla \nabla {\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}& {\mathrm{B}}^{T}& {\nabla}^{T}{\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}\\ \mathrm{B}& 0& 0\\ \nabla {\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}& 0& 0\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\Delta u\\ \Delta {\lambda}_{l}\\ \Delta {\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

With:

- ${K}^{int,n-1}={\partial}_{u}{L}^{int,n-1}$ the classical tangent matrix (see 2.3.2 and 2.4.3);
- $\nabla {\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}={\partial}_{u}{\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}$ the Jacobian matrix of the non-linear relation;
- $\nabla \nabla {\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{n-1}={\partial}_{{u}^{2}}^{2}{\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{n-1}$ the Hessian matrix of the non-linear relation

One recovers the following approximate problem: find the increments $\Delta u\in {ℝ}^{n}$ , $\Delta {\lambda}_{l}\in ℝ$ and $\Delta {\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}\in ℝ$ of the system in prediction (linearisation about the time step $i$ ). In § 2.3.5, we found the expression of the *mechanical* loading increment $\Delta {L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}$ :

$$
\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}={\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{\mathrm{Q}}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1}
$$

For the linear boundary conditions, paragraph § 3.3.1 has also given its expression.

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{K}_{i-1}^{int}+{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl},i-1}\nabla {\mathrm{C}}_{i-1}& {\mathrm{B}}^{T}& {\nabla}^{T}{\mathrm{C}}_{i-1}\\ \mathrm{B}& 0& 0\\ \nabla {\mathrm{C}}_{i-1}& 0& 0\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\Delta u\\ \Delta {\lambda}_{l}\\ \Delta {\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}-{\mathrm{Q}}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1}-{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{varc}}\\ \mathrm{B}{u}_{i}\\ {\mathrm{C}}_{i}{u}_{i}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

One should note the difference between $\mathrm{B}$ , the matrix of the linear relations, and ${\mathrm{C}}_{i}$ the matrix of the non-linear relations. Indeed, the matrix $\mathrm{B}$ is constant throughout the analysis, whereas the matrix ${\mathrm{C}}_{i}$ changes at each Newton iteration. This is why the latter is indexed by $i$ , unlike the matrix $\mathrm{B}$ . Equivalently, during the Newton iterations $n$ , one must solve the following system:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{K}^{\text{int,n-1}}+{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{n-1}\nabla \nabla {\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}& {\mathrm{B}}^{T}& {\nabla}^{T}{\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}\\ \mathrm{B}& 0& 0\\ \nabla {\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}& 0& 0\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\delta {u}^{n}\\ \delta {\lambda}_{l}^{n}\\ \delta {\lambda}_{\mathit{nl}}^{n}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{L}}^{\text{méca},n-1}-{\mathrm{L}}^{int,n-1}-{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}^{n-1}\\ {\mathrm{u}}^{d}-\mathrm{B}.{\mathrm{u}}^{n-1}\\ {\mathrm{C}}^{n-1}{u}^{n-1}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

Notes:

- As in the standard case, it is possible to do quasi-Newton by ignoring the contribution of $\nabla \nabla \mathrm{C}$ to the tangent matrix. But this is a choice of the developer, because the user does not have access to the choice of updating of this term, unlike the general case;
- The problem becoming non-linear in terms of boundary conditions, it is necessary to switch to a matrix updated at every Newton iteration (REAC_ITER=1). An alarm message warns you if you do not do so;

#### 3.4.3 Adaptation of the convergence criterion

It is important to define properly the stopping criterion at the end of each Newton iteration, both after the prediction and during the correction. One recalls (see § 2.5) that in *Code_Aster, one can define it in several ways.* One makes explicit here the adaptation of RESI_GLOB_MAXI and RESI_GLOB_RELA to the non-linear cases.

The criterion RESI_GLOB_MAXI consists in checking that the infinite norm of the residual is less than the value $\gamma$ specified by the user. It is necessary to introduce the contribution of the non-linear boundary conditions:

$$
{\parallel {\mathrm{Q}}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i}^{n}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}+{\nabla {\mathrm{C}}_{i}}^{n,T}.{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl},i}^{n}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}\parallel }_{\infty}\le \gamma
$$

The criterion RESI_GLOB_RELA (chosen by default) amounts to checking that the residual is sufficiently small, as previously, and this relative to a quantity representative of the loading.

$$
\frac{{\parallel {\mathrm{Q}}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i}^{n}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}+{\nabla {\mathrm{C}}_{i}}^{n,T}.{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl},i}^{n}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}\parallel }_{\infty}}{{\parallel {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}+{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{varc}}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}+{\nabla {\mathrm{C}}_{i}}^{n,T}.{\lambda}_{\mathit{nl},i}^{n}\parallel }_{\infty}}\le \eta
$$

If $\textcolor{#000000}{C(.)}$ is non-differentiable then the general formalism does not change, with the exception of the correct definition of the generalised Jacobian of the non-differentiable function (see [R5.03.52] for example).

## 4 Line search

The line search set out here concerns the line search in the absence of piloting.

### 4.1 Principle

The introduction of the line search in the operator STAT_NON_LINE results from an observation: the Newton method with a consistent matrix does not converge in every situation, in particular when one starts too far from the solution. On the other hand, the use of matrices other than the consistent tangent matrix can, when they are too “flexible”, lead to divergence. The line search makes it possible to guard against such divergences.

It consists in considering $(\delta {u}_{i}^{n},\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n})$ , no longer as the increment of the displacements and of the Lagrange multipliers, but as a search direction in which one will seek to minimise a functional (the energy of the structure). One will find an advance step $\rho$ in this direction, and the update of the unknowns will consist in doing:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}={\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}+\rho .\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}\\ {\lambda}_{i}^{n}={\lambda}_{i}^{n-1}+\rho .\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n}\end{array}
$$

In the absence of line search (by default) the scalar $\rho$ is of course equal to 1.

### 4.2 Minimisation of a functional

In order to convince oneself better of the soundness of the line search, one can interpret the Newton method as a method of minimisation of a functional (in the case where the tangent matrices are symmetric). We insist on the fact that the equations obtained are rigorously those of the Newton method set out in [§ 2] and that only the way of arriving at them is different.

Let us “forget”, in order to simplify the presentation, the dualisation of the Dirichlet boundary conditions and let us place ourselves under the small-strain hypothesis. One considers the functional:

$$
\begin{array}{}J:V\to ℝ\\ u\to J(u)={\int}_{\Omega}\Phi (\varepsilon (u)).d\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}f.u.d\Omega -{\int}_{\Gamma}t.u.d\Gamma \end{array}
$$

where the free-energy density $\Phi$ makes it possible to relate the stress tensor $\sigma$ to the linearised strain tensor $\varepsilon$ by the relation $\varepsilon =\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial \sigma }$ in the case of hyperelasticity (one generalises this situation to the other non-linearities in the rest of the document). The functional $J$ being convex, finding the minimum of $J$ is equivalent to cancelling its gradient, that is:

$$
\nabla J(u).v=0\forall v\in V
$$

Which is exactly the Principle of Virtual Work, since:

$$
\nabla J(u).v={\int}_{\Omega}\sigma (u):\varepsilon (v).d\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}f.v.d\Omega -{\int}_{\Gamma}t.v.d\Gamma
$$

Thus, solving the equations arising from the Principle of Virtual Work (basis of the problem formulated in [§ 1.3]) is equivalent to minimising the functional $J$ which represents the energy of the structure (internal energy diminished by the work of the external forces $\mathrm{f}$ and $t$ ).

### 4.3 Minimisation method

The minimisation is done iteratively, classically in two stages at each iteration:

- Computation of a search direction $\delta$ along which one will seek the next iterate,
- Computation of the best advance step $\rho$ in this direction: ${u}^{n+1}={u}^{n}+\rho .\delta$

In a minimisation problem, the natural idea is to advance in the direction opposite to the gradient of the functional, which is locally the best descent direction since this direction is normal to the contour lines and directed in the sense of the decreasing values Figure 2-4.3-1

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.01/Shape3.gif)

**Figure** 2 **‑** 4.3-1

However, it is possible to improve the choice of the descent direction by using this gradient method in a new metric. This is what will allow us to recover the classical equations of the Newton method. Let us take the simple example of a problem with two variables $x$ and $y$ for which the functional has the form of an ellipse whose minimum is at $(\frac{\alpha}{a},\frac{\beta}{b})$ :

$$
J(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}.{\mathrm{ax}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}.{\mathrm{by}}^{2}-\alpha x-\beta y
$$

By choosing as descent direction the inverse of the gradient of $J$ , one passes from one iterate to the next (let us reason on $x$ only) by:

$$
{x}^{n+1}={x}^{n}-{\text{ax}}^{n}+\alpha
$$

which does not point towards the solution, since the normal at a point of an ellipse does not in general pass through the centre of the ellipse

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.01/10000000000001EF0000019E14CC81AB6B039A7A.png)

**Figure** **2‑** 4.3-2

On the other hand, if one carries out a change of variables so that the contours of $J$ become circles:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}\bar{x}=\sqrt{a}.x\\ \bar{y}=\sqrt{b}.y\\ \bar{J}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\frac{1}{2}.({\bar{x}}^{2}+{\bar{y}}^{2})-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{a}}.\bar{x}-\frac{\beta}{\sqrt{b}}.\bar{y}\end{array}
$$

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.01/Shape4.gif)

**Figure** 2 **‑** 4.3-3

The use of the inverse direction of the gradient of $\bar{J}$ then makes it possible to obtain the solution in one iteration:

$$
{\bar{x}}^{n+1}={\bar{x}}^{n}-({\bar{x}}^{n}-\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{a}})=\frac{\alpha}{\sqrt{a}}\mathrm{\Rightarrow }{x}^{n+1}=\frac{\alpha}{a}
$$

Thus, the use of the gradient method in the new metric allows an immediate convergence. In a more complicated case (convex functional but different from an ellipse), the convergence is not instantaneous but the change of variables makes it possible to reduce substantially the number of iterations necessary.

### 4.4 Application to the minimisation of the energy

For simplicity, one will place oneself in the discretised linear case where the functional $J$ is:

$$
J(u)=\frac{1}{2}.{u}^{T}.K.u-{u}^{T}.L
$$

One denotes $K$ the stiffness matrix of the structure, and $L$ the vector of the imposed loadings. In order to minimise $J$ *,* we shall use the same descent method as previously by first carrying out a change of variables that is quite similar. The matrix $K$ being symmetric positive definite, its eigenvalues are real and positive: one can therefore define the “square root” of $K$ which will be denoted $\sqrt{K}$ (also symmetric). One sets $\bar{u}=\sqrt{K}.u$ ; the minimisation of $J$ is then equivalent to that of:

$$
\bar{J}(\bar{\mathrm{u}})=\frac{1}{2}.{\bar{\mathrm{u}}}^{T}.\bar{\mathrm{u}}-{\bar{\mathrm{u}}}^{T}.{(\sqrt{\mathrm{K}})}^{-T}.\mathrm{L}
$$

By using a decomposition by the diagonal:

$$
K=P.D.{P}^{-1}
$$

With $\mathrm{D}$ diagonal, therefore:

$$
\sqrt{K}=P.\sqrt{D}.{P}^{-1}
$$

Which leads to:

$$
\sqrt{{K}^{T}}.\sqrt{K}=P.\sqrt{{D}^{T}}.\sqrt{D}.{P}^{-1}=K
$$

By taking as descent direction the inverse direction of the gradient of $\bar{J}$ , one obtains:

$$
{\bar{u}}^{n+1}={\bar{u}}^{n}-({\bar{u}}^{n}-\sqrt{{K}^{-1}}.L)
$$

That is, returning to the initial variables:

$$
{u}^{n+1}={u}^{n}-{K}^{-1}.(K.{u}^{n}-L)
$$

Or again:

$$
K.({u}^{n+1}-{u}^{n})=L-K.{u}^{n}
$$

One recovers the equations of the Newton method: the matrix $K$ is the Hessian of the functional $J$ (matrix of the second derivative) and the right-hand side is the difference of the loading and of the internal forces, also called the equilibrium residual. Thus the Newton method can be interpreted as resulting from the minimisation of the energy of the structure via a gradient method applied after a change of metric.

### 4.5 Determination of the advance step

Let us return to the real problem, that of the solution of ${L}_{i}^{int}({u}_{i})={L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}$ . This problem can be interpreted as the minimisation of the following functional:

$$
J=W({u}_{i})-{u}_{i}^{T}.{L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}
$$

where $W({u}_{i})$ corresponds to the discretisation, on the basis of the shape functions, of the internal energy of the structure:

$$
W={\int}_{\Omega}\Phi (\varepsilon (u)).d\Omega
$$

One has computed by the Newton method a displacement increment $\delta {u}_{i}^{n}$ which, in the minimisation problem, is interpreted as a search direction, according to what precedes. One will compute the advance step $\rho$ in this direction making it possible to minimise the value of the functional:

$$
\underset{\rho \in ℝ}{\text{Min}}\left\lbrace W({u}_{i}^{n-1}+\rho .\delta {u}_{i}^{n})-{L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}({u}_{i}^{n-1}+\rho .\delta {u}_{i}^{n})\right\rbrace
$$

In order to find the minimum of this scalar function of $\rho$ which will be denoted $f(\rho )$ , one seeks the point where its derivative vanishes (this amounts to making the final residual and the search direction orthogonal):

$$
f'(\rho )={\left[\delta {u}_{i}^{n}\right]}^{T}.\left[{Q}^{T}.\sigma ({u}_{i}^{n-1}+\rho .\delta {u}_{i}^{n})-{L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}\right]=0
$$

$f'(\rho )$ is the projection of the residual onto the search direction. With the notations of [§ 2.2] and taking into account the support reactions, the scalar equation to be solved in order to determine the advance step $\rho$ is written:

$$
f'(\rho )={\left[\delta {u}_{i}^{n}\right]}^{T}.\left[{Q}^{T}.\sigma ({u}_{i}^{n-1}+\rho .\delta {u}_{i}^{n})+{B}^{T}.({\lambda}_{i}^{n-1}+\rho .\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n})-{L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}\right]=0
$$

At the end of the line search, one updates the displacements and the Lagrange parameters by:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}={\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}+\rho .\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}\\ {\lambda}_{i}^{n}={\lambda}_{i}^{n-1}+\rho .\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n}\end{array}
$$

The convergence test bears:

- On the maximum number of line-search iterations indicated by the user under the keyword ITER_LINE_MAXI of the factor keyword NEWTON (the default value 0 inhibits the line search, and $\rho$ is then equal to 1),
- On the criterion RESI_LINE_RELA given by $f(\rho )\le \tau .f(0)$ , where $\tau$ is equal by default to 0.1.

The line search is in a way an “insurance” making it possible to guard against serious divergences of the Newton method. When the search direction is “bad” (if the tangent matrix is too flexible, for example), the line-search algorithm leads to a small value of $\rho$ , which avoids moving too far away from the solution. It is not necessary to perform many iterations in the secant method (two or three are enough to avoid disasters) because each one is rather expensive (one must recompute the internal forces) and one does not have the ambition of finding at each Newton iteration the truly optimal value of $\rho$ .

### 4.6 Computation of the line-search coefficient

There exist two methods for computing the optimal $\rho$ in the line search *.*

#### 4.6.1 Secant method (METHODE=’CORDE’)

In order that the determination of $\rho$ should not be too costly, one uses a secant method whose maximum number of iterations is fixed by the user. The secant method can be interpreted as a Newton method in which the derivative at the current point is approximated by the direction joining the current point and the previous point:

$$
{\rho}^{p+1}={\rho}^{p}-\frac{{\rho}^{p}-{\rho}^{p-1}}{{g}^{p}-{g}^{p-1}}.{g}^{p}=\frac{{\rho}^{p-1}.{g}^{p}-{\rho}^{p}.{g}^{p-1}}{{g}^{p}-{g}^{p-1}}
$$

Where one has denoted ${g}^{p}=f'({\rho}^{p})$ . One starts from ${\rho}^{0}=0$ and ${\rho}^{1}=1$ . The secant method has an order of convergence of the order of 1.6. It is represented schematically in Figure 2-4.6.1-1.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.01/Shape5.gif)

**Figure** 2 **‑** 4.6.1-1

#### 4.6.2 Mixed method (METHODE=’MIXTE’)

This method mixes several solution techniques in order to be more robust. It consists essentially in the application of a secant method (see the previous paragraph) between two predetermined bounds. It is in fact the application of the secant method with variable bounds. Here is the algorithm used:

1. One assumes that $f'(0)>0$ . If this is not the case, one changes the sense of the descent direction (by examining negative $\rho$ , which amounts to defining $f'$ as being equal to $-f'$
2. One seeks a positive ${\rho}_{\max}$ such that $f'({\rho}_{\max})<0$ . The method is simply iterative by doing ${\rho}_{n+1}=3.{\rho}_{n}$ with ${\rho}_{0}=1$ (bracketing or bounding stage)
3. One thus has the two new bounds between which the function changes sign. If one assumes that the function $f'$ is continuous, there therefore exists a solution between these bounds.
4. One applies the secant method on this interval: one starts from ${\rho}^{0}=0$ and ${\rho}^{1}={\rho}_{\max}$ .

#### 4.6.3 Particular case: the Newton-Krylov method

It has been specified above that the line search is carried out simultaneously on the unknowns $\mathrm{u}$ and $\lambda$ as shown by formula () of update of the variables. Now the functional to be minimised does not present a minimum as a function of the unknowns $(\mathrm{u},\lambda )$ ; it is indeed a Lagrangian which presents a saddle point, that is to say a minimum in $\mathrm{u}$ and a maximum in $\lambda$ (see [R3.03.01](doc:r3.03.01)). This way of proceeding is therefore not licit in the general case.

That said, one can show that in the case where the system in prediction is solved “exactly” (at least to a numerically satisfactory accuracy), this approach is licit. This is generally the case in the usual use of *Code_Aster*.

This is on the other hand not the case within the framework of the use of the Newton-Krylov method, where the linear systems are precisely solved in a deliberately inexact manner. In this situation, in order to circumvent the problem, only the unknowns $\mathrm{u}$ are affected by the line search and the formula for updating the variables therefore becomes:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}={\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}+\rho .\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}\\ {\lambda}_{i}^{n}={\lambda}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n}\end{array}
$$

Insofar as the functional to be minimised does indeed have a minimum in $\mathrm{u}$ , the line-search procedure is licit.

## 6 Viscoelastic regularisation REGU_VISC

In the presence of material instabilities, resulting for example from damage, the solution no longer depends continuously on time (or on the loading parameters) but exhibits jumps. Piloting is a technique for crossing these jumps, by allowing non-monotonic evolutions of the loading in order to recover continuity. But these techniques are not applicable when physical time intervenes, whether through a history of command variables (a thermal transient, for example) or in the constitutive behaviour of the material (viscosity).

Viscous regularisation is another approach for recovering continuity. It consists in superimposing on the stresses arising from the constitutive law $\sigma$ a stress of viscous origin ${\sigma}_{\mathit{vis}}$ so as to penalise sufficiently the very rapid evolutions (whose limit is a jump of the solution):

$$
{\sigma}_{\mathit{tot}}=\sigma +{\sigma}_{\mathit{vis}};\frac{d{\sigma}_{\mathit{tot}}}{d\epsilon }=\frac{d\sigma }{d\epsilon }+\frac{d{\sigma}_{\mathit{vis}}}{d\epsilon }
$$

This is a numerical technique, with no a priori relation to a viscous reality of the material. One describes in the document [R5.03.34](doc:r5.03.34) the viscoelastic constitutive law that governs the viscous stress ${\sigma}_{\mathit{vis}}$ .
