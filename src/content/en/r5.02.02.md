# r5.02.02 Non-linear thermal analysis

**Summary**

The operator THER_NON_LINE [U4.54.02](doc:u4.54.02) makes it possible to solve transient thermal problems in solids in the presence of non-linearities of the material properties (heat capacity and conductivity), or of the boundary conditions (heat exchange of radiation type). The formulation and the algorithm used are presented here, the latter being close to that associated with the operator STAT_NON_LINE [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01). The various analysis options required have been presented for the plane, axisymmetric and three-dimensional structural elements [U3.22.01](doc:u3.22.01), [U3.23.01](doc:u3.23.01) and [U3.24.01](doc:u3.24.01).

## 1 Expression of the heat equation in non-linear thermal analysis

### 1.1 Heat equation for a fixed solid

In this document, only the thermal analysis of solid bodies is treated, even though the liquid/solid phase change is taken into account. There is therefore no heat exchange by convection, but only by conduction.

The first principle of thermodynamics relates the time variation of the total energy ${\text{dE}}_{\text{totale}}$ of a system included in a control volume $\Omega$ to the work of the external forces $\delta W$ and to the heat $\delta Q$ received by this same system:

${\text{dE}}_{\text{totale}}=d({E}_{\text{interne}}+{E}_{\text{cinétique}})=\delta W+\delta Q$                     eq 1.1-1

By injecting the kinetic-energy theorem into this equation, the power of the internal forces is thus made to appear, a function of the velocity field [bib1]:

${\dot{E}}_{\text{interne}}=\dot{Q}-{P}_{i}(u)$                                 eq 1.1-2

For the solution of the thermal problem, the system is assumed to be without motion. The power of the internal forces ${P}_{i}(u)$ is therefore zero. Indeed, in the majority of the applications aimed at, the thermal and mechanical phenomena are uncoupled; the volumetric power dissipated by the plastic strains, ${P}_{i}={\sigma}_{c}.{\dot{ϵ}}_{\text{plastique}}$ , is neglected compared with the heat exchanged at the surface or the other volumetric heat sources.

Equation [eq 1.1-2], which expresses the variation of the heat in the volume $\Omega$ , is then written:

$\forall s\in \Omega \phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\rho \frac{d}{\text{dt}}\underset{S}{\int}ed\Omega =\dot{Q}=\underset{S}{\int}\left({r}_{\text{vol}}-divq\right)d\Omega$                         eq 1.1-3

where the following have been denoted:

| $e$ | the internal energy, |
| --- | --- |
| $\rho$ | the density, |
| ${r}_{\text{vol}}$ | the volumetric rate of external heat supply, |
| $q$ | the heat-flux vector. |

Moreover, since the solid is fixed, for any control volume $\Omega (t)=\Omega$ , the local heat-conservation equation is then obtained:

$\rho \frac{\text{de}}{\text{dt}}={r}_{\text{vol}}-divq$                                     eq 1.1-4

In the case where the whole system is animated by a rigid-body motion, an additional term appears on the left-hand side, involving the velocity of the solid and the gradient of the energy. This situation is treated by the command THER_NON_LINE_MO [R5.02.04](doc:r5.02.04).

In the case of a reversible transformation, equation [eq 1.1-4] becomes, with the help of the second principle of thermodynamics, which makes it possible to write in our case ${\text{dE}}_{\text{interne}}=\text{TdS}$ :

$\mathrm{\rho T}\dot{s}={r}_{\text{vol}}-divq$                                 eq 1.1-5

and finally the heat equation in its classical form:

${\mathrm{\rho C}}_{P}\dot{T}={r}_{\text{vol}}-divq$                             eq 1.1-6

with the heat capacity at constant pressure defined by: ${C}_{P}=T\frac{\partial s}{\partial T}{\mid}_{P}$

As explained in section 1.4, it may be advantageous to write the left-hand side of equation [eq 1.1-6] with the enthalpy $\beta$ , which then depends only on the temperature:

$\dot{\beta}={r}_{\text{vol}}-divq$                                     eq 1.1-7

where $\beta (T)={\int}_{{T}_{0}}^{T}\rho {C}_{P}\text{dT}$

### 1.2 Fourier’s law

In heat conduction, Fourier’s law provides an equation relating the heat flux to the temperature gradient (vector normal to the isothermal surface). In its most general form, this law brings in a conductivity tensor. In the case of an isotropic material, this tensor reduces to a coefficient $\lambda$ (which may depend on the temperature), the thermal conductivity:

$q(x,t)=-\lambda (T)\nabla T(x,t)$                         eq 1.2-1

### 1.3 Heat equation in the case of the non-linear transient thermal model

By combining equations [eq 1.1-5] and [eq 1.2-1], one obtains:

${r}_{\text{vol}}-div(-\lambda (T)\nabla T)=\frac{d\beta }{\text{dt}}$                     eq 1.3-1

or, if the heat capacity does not depend on the temperature:

${r}_{\text{vol}}-div(-\lambda (T)\nabla T)={\mathit{\rho C}}_{p}\frac{\text{dT}}{\text{dt}}$                     eq 1.3-2

### 1.4 Numerical advantage of the enthalpy formulation for problems with phase change.

The relation between enthalpy and heat capacity is:

$\beta (T)={\int}_{{T}_{0}}^{T}\rho {C}_{p}(u)\text{du}$

When this enthalpy function presents abrupt variations, it is more accurate to handle $\beta (T)$ than its derivative. Indeed, the characteristic shapes of these functions in the neighbourhood of the melting point are the following:

![Figure](/figures/r5.02.02/1000184A000069D50000251BE6F10BC1F051F663.svg)

During an iteration, either because the thermal transient is violent, or because the phase-change range is very small (pure substance), the two successive iterates of the temperature may lie on either side of the discontinuity. The evaluation of the slope of the enthalpy function in the neighbourhood of the melting point will be very wrong if one considers ${C}_{p}({T}_{1})$ , ${C}_{p}({T}_{2})$ or a weighted average of the two. By contrast, the slope of the dashed line is always a correct approximation of $d\beta /\text{dT}$ at the melting point.

## 2 Boundary conditions, loading and initial condition

Refer to [R5.02.01](doc:r5.02.01) for the thermal boundary conditions and the loadings leading to equations that are linear in temperature, as well as for the initial condition.

### 2.1 Non-linear normal flux

These are conditions of Neumann type, defining the flux entering the domain.

$-q(x,t).n=g(x,T)$     on the boundary $\Gamma$                 eq 2.1-1

where $g(x,T)$ is a function of the temperature and possibly of the space variable $x$ and/or of time $t$ , and $n$ denotes the outward normal to the boundary $\Gamma$ , $q$ is the heat-flux vector (directed along decreasing temperatures).

This expression makes it possible to introduce, for example, conditions of the exchange type with a convective exchange coefficient depending on the temperature:

$-q(x,t).n=g(x,T)=h(x,T)({T}_{\text{ext}}(x,t)-T)$             eq 2.1-2

### 2.2 Non-linear normal flux — radiation condition at infinity

A particular case of the previous boundary conditions is the radiation at infinity of grey bodies, which is expressed by a particular case of the function $g(x,T)$ :

$-q(x,t).n=\sigma \epsilon \left[(T(x)+273.15{)}^{4}-({T}_{\infty}+273.15{)}^{4}\right]$                         eq 2.2-1

The characteristics to be defined when defining this loading are the emissivity, the Stefan–Boltzmann constant $\sigma =5,73{.10}^{-8}\mathrm{usi}$ and the temperature at infinity.

$T(r)$ and ${T}_{\infty}$ are then expressed in degrees Celsius. $–273.15°C$ is the temperature of absolute zero.

## 3 Variational formulation of the problem

We shall confine ourselves here to presenting the problem with only the boundary conditions of imposed temperature [R5.02.01 §2.1], of imposed normal flux [R5.02.01 §2.3], of exchange [R5.02.01§2.4], of non-linear flux [§2.1] and of radiation [§2.2].

Let $\Omega$ be an open set of ${R}^{3}$ , with boundary $\Gamma ={\Gamma}_{1}\cup {\Gamma}_{2}\cup {\Gamma}_{3}\cup {\Gamma}_{4}\cup {\Gamma}_{5}$ .

Equation [eq 1.1-4] in $T$ on $\Omega \times ]0,t[$ must be solved with the boundary conditions:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{ccccc}T={T}^{d}(r,t)& & & \text{sur}& {\Gamma}_{1}\\ \lambda (T)\frac{\partial T}{\partial n}& =& f(r,t)& \text{sur}& {\Gamma}_{2}\\ \lambda (T)\frac{\partial T}{\partial n}& =& h(r,t)({T}_{\text{ext}}(r,t)-T)& \text{sur}& {\Gamma}_{3}\\ \lambda (T)\frac{\partial T}{\partial n}& =& g(r,T)& \text{sur}& {\Gamma}_{4}\\ \lambda (T)\frac{\partial T}{\partial n}& =& \sigma \epsilon \left[(T+273.15{)}^{4}-({T}_{\infty}+273.15{)}^{4}\right]& \text{sur}& {\Gamma}_{5}\end{array}$                 eq 3-1

and with, possibly, initial conditions $T(t=0)$ . If the latter are not specified, the stationary problem is solved beforehand, that is equation [eq 1.3-1] without the time-evolution term.

Let $v$ be a sufficiently regular function vanishing on ${\Gamma}_{1}$ ; noting that:

$\begin{array}{}\frac{d}{\text{dt}}(\underset{\Omega}{\int}\beta (T).\mathrm{v.d}\Omega )=\underset{\Omega}{\int}\dot{\beta}(T).\mathrm{v.d}\Omega \\ \begin{array}{cc}\underset{\Omega}{\int}\lambda (T)\nabla T.\nabla \mathrm{v.d}\Omega =& -\underset{\Omega}{\int}div(\lambda (T)\nabla T).\mathrm{v.d}\Omega +\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\lambda (T)\frac{\partial T}{\partial n}.\mathrm{v.d}\Gamma \\ & \end{array}\end{array}$         eq 3-2

the weak formulation of the heat equation may then be written:

$\frac{d}{\text{dt}}(\underset{\Omega}{\int}\beta (T).\mathrm{v.d}\Omega )+\underset{\Omega}{\int}\lambda (T)\nabla T.\nabla \mathrm{vd}\Omega -\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\lambda (T)\frac{\partial T}{\partial n}.\mathrm{v.d}\Gamma =\underset{\Omega}{\int}{r}_{\text{vol}}.\mathrm{v.d}\Omega$     eq 3-3

The variational formulation of the problem is deduced from it:

$\begin{array}{c}\underset{\Omega}{\int}\frac{d\beta (T)}{\text{dt}}.\mathit{v.d}\Omega +\underset{\Omega}{\int}\lambda (T)\nabla \mathit{T.}\nabla \mathit{v.d}\Omega +\underset{{\Gamma}_{3}}{\int}\mathit{hT.v.d}{\Gamma}_{3}=\\ \underset{\Omega}{\int}{r}_{\text{vol}}.\mathit{v.d}\Omega +\underset{{\Gamma}_{2}}{\int}\mathit{f.v.d}{\Gamma}_{2}+\underset{{\Gamma}_{3}}{\int}{\mathit{hT}}_{\text{ext}}.\mathit{vd}{\Gamma}_{3}+\\ \underset{{\Gamma}_{4}}{\int}\mathit{g.v.d}{\Gamma}_{4}+\underset{{\Gamma}_{5}}{\int}\sigma \epsilon .\left[(T+273.15{)}^{4}-({T}_{\infty}+273.15{)}^{4}\right].\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{5}\end{array}$         eq 3-4

## 4 Time discretisation of the differential equation

### 4.1 Introduction of the theta-method

A classical way of discretising a first-order differential equation is the $\Theta$ -method. Consider the following differential equation:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\dot{y}(t)=\phi (t,y(t))\\ y(0)={y}_{0}\end{array}$                                 eq 4.1-1

The $\Theta$ -method consists in discretising equation [eq 4.1-1] by a finite-difference scheme

$\frac{1}{\Delta t}({y}_{n+1}-{y}_{n})=\theta .\varphi ({t}_{n+1},{y}_{n+1})+(1-\theta ).\varphi ({t}_{n},{y}_{n})$             eq 4.1-2

where ${y}_{n+1}$ is an approximation of $y({t}_{n+1})$ , ${y}_{n}$ being assumed known and $\theta$ is the parameter of the method, $\theta \in \left[0,1\right]$ .

**Note:**

*if* $\theta =0$ *the scheme is said to be explicit,*

*if* $\theta \ne 0$ *the scheme is said to be implicit.*

### 4.2 Application to the heat equation

Let us use the $\Theta$ -method in the variational formulation of the heat equation, where one has set:

$\begin{array}{cccc}{T}^{+}=T(r,t+\Delta t)& {T}^{-}=T(r,t)& {h}^{+}=h(r,t+\Delta t)& {h}^{-}=h(r,t)\\ {f}^{+}=f(r,t+\Delta t)& {f}^{-}=f(r,t)& {T}_{\text{ext}}^{+}={T}_{\text{ext}}(r,t+\Delta t)& {T}_{\text{ext}}^{-}={T}_{\text{ext}}(r,t)\\ {r}_{\text{vol}}^{+}={r}_{\text{vol}}(r,t+\Delta t)& {r}_{\text{vol}}^{-}={r}_{\text{vol}}(r,t)& {T}^{d+}={T}^{d}(r,t+\Delta t)& {T}^{d-}={T}^{d}(r,t)\\ {g}^{+}=g(r,{T}^{+})& {g}^{-}=g(r,{T}^{-})& & \end{array}$

where ${T}^{d}(r,t)$ represents the temperature imposed on the boundary of the domain, as a function of time and of space.

Let us introduce the following function spaces:

$\begin{array}{c}{V}_{{t}^{+}}=\left\lbrace v\in {H}^{1}(\Omega ){v}_{\text{/}{\Gamma}_{1}}={T}^{d+}\right\rbrace \\ {V}_{{t}^{-}}=\left\lbrace v\in {H}^{1}(\Omega ){v}_{\text{/}{\Gamma}_{1}}={T}^{d-}\right\rbrace \\ {V}_{0}=\left\lbrace v\in {H}^{1}(\Omega ){v}_{\text{/}{\Gamma}_{1}}=0\right\rbrace \end{array}$

The field ${T}^{-}\in {V}_{{t}^{-}}$ being assumed known, one seeks ${T}^{+}\in {V}_{{t}^{+}}$ such that $\forall v\in {V}_{0}$ :

$\begin{array}{cc}\underset{\Omega}{\int}\frac{\beta ({T}^{+})-\beta ({T}^{-})}{\Delta t}\mathit{v.d}\Omega & +\underset{\Omega}{\int}(\theta \lambda ({T}^{+})\nabla {T}^{+}.\nabla v+(1-\theta )\lambda ({T}^{-})\nabla {T}^{-}.\nabla v)d\Omega \\ -\underset{{\Gamma}_{2}}{\int}(\theta {f}^{+}+(1-\theta ){f}^{-})\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{2}& -\underset{{\Gamma}_{3}}{\int}(\theta {h}^{+}{T}_{\text{ext}}^{+}+(1-\theta ){h}^{-}{T}_{\text{ext}}^{-}-\theta {h}^{+}{T}^{+}-(1-\theta ){h}^{-}{T}^{-})\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{3}\\ -\underset{{\Gamma}_{4}}{\int}(\theta {g}^{+}+(1-\theta ){g}^{-})\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{4}& =\\ \underset{\Omega}{\int}(\theta {r}_{\text{vol}}^{+}+(1-\theta ){r}_{\text{vol}}^{-})\mathit{v.d}\Omega & +\underset{\Omega}{\int}(\theta {r}_{\text{v}}({T}^{+})+(1-\theta ){r}_{\text{v}}({T}^{-}))\mathit{v.d}\Omega \end{array}$ eq 4.2-1

In order not to make the writing excessively heavy, and insofar as the procedure is identical to the other terms, the radiation term has not been included in these equations (integral over ${\Gamma}_{5}$ ).

Setting:

$\begin{array}{c}({\text{hT}}_{\text{ext}}{)}^{\theta}=\theta {h}^{+}{T}_{\text{ext}}^{+}+(1-\theta ){h}^{-}{T}_{\text{ext}}^{-}\\ {f}^{\theta}=\theta {f}^{+}+(1-\theta ){f}^{-}\\ {r}^{\theta}=\theta {r}_{\text{vol}}^{+}+(1-\theta ){r}_{\text{vol}}^{-}\end{array}$ one finally obtains:

$\begin{array}{c}\underset{\Omega}{\int}\frac{\beta ({T}^{+})}{\Delta t}\mathit{v.d}\Omega +\theta \underset{\Omega}{\int}\lambda ({T}^{+})\nabla {T}^{+}.\nabla \mathit{v.}d\Omega +\theta \underset{{\Gamma}_{3}}{\int}{h}^{+}{T}^{+}\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{3}\\ -\theta \underset{{\Gamma}_{4}}{\int}g({T}^{+}).\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{4}-\theta \underset{\Omega}{\int}{r}_{v}({T}^{+}).\mathit{v.d}\Omega ={L}_{1}(v,{T}^{-})\\ \forall v\in {V}_{0}\end{array}$     eq 4.2-2

where one has set:

$\begin{array}{c}{L}_{1}(v,{T}^{-})=\underset{\Omega}{\int}\frac{\beta ({T}^{-})}{\Delta t}\mathit{v.d}\Omega -\underset{\Omega}{\int}(1-\theta )\lambda ({T}^{-})\nabla {T}^{-}.\nabla v.d\Omega +\underset{{\Gamma}_{2}}{\int}{f}^{\theta}\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{2}\\ +\underset{{\Gamma}_{3}}{\int}(({\text{hT}}_{\text{ext}}{)}^{\theta}-(1-\theta ){h}^{-}{T}^{-})\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{3}+\underset{\Omega}{\int}{r}^{\theta}\mathit{v.d}\Omega \\ +(1-\theta )\underset{{\Gamma}_{4}}{\int}g({T}^{-})\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{4}+(1-\theta )\underset{\Omega}{\int}{r}_{v}({T}^{-})\mathit{v.d}\Omega \end{array}$     eq 4.2-3

At a given analysis time instant, this term is known. Indeed, only the temperature at the previous time instant, ${T}^{-}$ , as well as the values at the current time instant of functions *known* as a function of time, are involved.

In the case where the temperature distribution in the system at the initial time instant is not supplied, the stationary problem is solved. The evolution terms disappear, $\Theta =1$ ; the temperature field at the initial time instant is given by:

$\begin{array}{c}\underset{\Omega}{\int}\lambda ({T}^{t=0})\nabla {T}^{t=0}.\nabla \mathit{v.d}\Omega +\underset{{\Gamma}_{3}}{\int}{h}^{t=0}{T}^{t=0}\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{3}-\underset{{\Gamma}_{4}}{\int}g({T}^{t=0})\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{4}\\ =\underset{{\Gamma}_{2}}{\int}{f}^{t=0}\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{2}+\underset{{\Gamma}_{3}}{\int}{h}^{t=0}{T}_{\text{ext}}^{t=0}\mathit{v.d}{\Gamma}_{3}+\underset{\Omega}{\int}{r}^{t=0}\mathit{v.d}\Omega \\ \forall v\in {V}_{0}\end{array}$             eq 4.2-4

The problem is finally written in the condensed form:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\mathrm{Soit}{T}^{-}\in {V}_{{t}^{-}}\mathrm{connu},\mathrm{trouver}{T}^{+}\in {V}_{{t}^{+}}\mathrm{tel}\mathrm{que}\\ \forall v\in {V}_{0}:a(v,{T}^{+})={L}_{1}(v,{T}^{-})\end{array}$                 eq 4.2-5

## 5 Spatial discretisation and adaptation of Newton’s algorithm to the problem

The principle of Newton’s method is described in great detail in [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01); only the adaptations specific to the non-linear thermal algorithm will be set out here.

### 5.1 Spatial discretisation

Let ${P}_{h}$ be a partition of the space $\Omega$ , désignons par :math:`N$ the number of nodes of the mesh, ${p}_{i}$ the shape function associated with node $i$ . Let $J$ denote the set of nodes belonging to the boundary ${G}_{1}$ .

Let:

$\begin{array}{c}{V}_{{t}^{+}}^{h}=\left\lbrace v=\sum_{i=1,N}{v}_{i}{p}_{i}(x);{v}_{j}={T}^{d}({x}_{j},{t}^{+})\text{}j\in J\right\rbrace \\ {V}_{{t}^{-}}^{h}=\left\lbrace v=\sum_{i=1,N}{v}_{i}{p}_{i}(x);{v}_{j}={T}^{d}({x}_{j},{t}^{-})\text{}j\in J\right\rbrace \\ {V}_{0}^{h}=\left\lbrace v=\sum_{i=1,N}{v}_{i}{p}_{i}(x);{v}_{j}=0\text{}j\in J\right\rbrace \end{array}$                         eq 5.1-1

Problem [eq 4.2-5] may be replaced by the following discretised problem with a finite number of unknowns:

| Let ${T}^{-}\in {V}_{t}^{h}$ known, find ${T}^{+}\in {V}_{{t}^{+}}^{h}$ such that |
| --- |
| ${v}_{h}\in {V}_{0}^{h}a({v}_{h},{T}^{+})={L}_{1}({v}_{h},{T}^{-})$ |

> eq 5.1-2

which may also be written, with the same formalism as STAT_NON_LINE [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01), in vector form:

| ${v}^{T}R({T}^{+},{t}^{+})={v}^{T}L({T}^{-},{t}^{+})$ | $\forall v$ such that $Bv=0$ |
| --- | --- |
| ${\mathit{BT}}^{+}={T}^{d}({t}^{+})$ |  |

> eq 5.1-3

where the operator $B$ expresses the boundary condition of imposed temperature ${T}^{+}\in {V}_{{t}^{+}}^{h}$ . It is defined by:

$(\text{Bv}{)}_{j}=\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\mathrm{si}j\notin J\\ {v}_{j}\mathrm{si}j\in J\end{array}$                                     eq 5.1-4

The case where the mapping $R$ is linear is treated by the command THER_LINEAIRE [R5.02.01](doc:r5.02.01).

The dualisation of the boundary conditions, detailed in [R3.03.01](doc:r3.03.01), leads to the non-linear problem in ${T}^{+}$ :

$\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}R({T}^{+},{t}^{+})+{B}^{T}{\lambda}^{+}=L({T}^{-},{t}^{+})\\ {\mathit{BT}}^{+}={T}^{d}({t}^{+})\end{array}$                                 eq 5.1-5

The unknowns are the pair $({T}^{+},{\lambda}^{+})$ , where ${\lambda}^{+}$ represents the “Lagrange multipliers” of the Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Solving the system [eq 5.1-5] amounts to cancelling at $({T}_{i}^{+},{\lambda}_{i}^{+})$ the vector $F({T}^{+},{\lambda}^{+})$ , called the residual, defined by:

$F({T}^{+},{\lambda}^{+})=(\begin{array}{c}L({T}^{-},{t}^{+})-R({T}^{+},{t}^{+})-{B}^{T}{\lambda}^{+}\\ {T}^{d}({t}^{+})-{\mathit{BT}}^{+}\end{array})$                 eq 5.1-6

Newton’s method consists in constructing a sequence of vectors ${\left\lbrace {x}^{n}\right\rbrace }_{n}$ converging towards the solution of $F(x)=0$ by means of the tangent linear mapping of $F$ .

### 5.2 Stationary analysis

The variational problem is that of equation [eq 4.2-4]. Note: in a stationary analysis, the enthalpy does not appear in the mapping $R$ .

The matrix of the tangent linear mapping of the function $R({T}^{n})$ is introduced:

${K}^{n}=\frac{\partial R}{\partial T}{\mid}_{{T}_{n}}$

That of the function $F({T}^{n},{\lambda}^{n})$ is then:

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}{K}^{n}& {B}^{T}\\ B& 0\end{array}\right]$

In the case of the stationary analysis, one must iterate from an initialisation value of the temperature field, which is given by the keyword ETAT_INIT. The first iteration of the analysis, called the prediction iteration, consists in solving the following system:

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}K({T}_{0})& {B}^{T}\\ B& 0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}{T}_{1}-{T}_{0}\\ {\lambda}_{1}-{\lambda}_{0}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}L-R({T}_{0})-{B}^{T}{\lambda}_{0}\\ {T}^{d}-{\mathrm{BT}}_{0}\end{array}\right]$             eq 5.2-1

As can be seen in the equation of the stationary problem [eq 4.2-4], the temperature does not appear on the right-hand side: one writes $L$ and not $L({T}_{0})$ .

If the problem is linear, $R({T}_{0})=K({T}_{0}){T}_{0}=K.{T}_{0}$ . All the terms in ${T}_{0}$ disappear by simplification. The solution is obtained in one iteration by inversion of a system identical to that described in [R5.02.01 §6].

The following iterations are Newton iterations, with or without updating of the tangent matrix $K$ .

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}K({T}_{(i)})& {B}^{T}\\ B& 0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}{T}_{i+1}-{T}_{i}\\ {\lambda}_{i+1}-{\lambda}_{i}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}L-R({T}_{i})-{B}^{T}{\lambda}_{i}\\ 0\end{array}\right]$             eq 5.2-2

For the prediction iteration, the writing of the lower subsystem of equation [eq 5.2-1], after simplification, ensures that ${\mathrm{BT}}_{1}={T}^{d}$ . The first iterate and all the following ones therefore satisfy the Dirichlet conditions.

The parentheses around the iteration index in the expression $K({T}_{(i)})$ mean that one may or may not update the tangent matrix in the course of the iterations.

**Note:**

*The initialisation temperature* ${T}_{0}$ *has an influence only for a non-linear stationary analysis. By being of the order of magnitude of the expected temperatures, it would make it possible to “start” less far from the solution than a field that is zero everywhere, and would thus reduce the number of iterations.*

### 5.3 Transient analysis

For the first iteration of the time step, called the prediction iteration, one “acts as if” the problem described in [eq 5.1-5] were linear. This formulation must make it possible to obtain the solution directly for a linear thermal problem. But here the situation is a little different from the stationary analysis because of the enthalpy formulation. The linearisation of [eq 5.1-5] gives:

$\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}R({T}^{-},{t}^{+})+K({T}^{-},{t}^{+})({T}^{+}-{T}^{-})+{B}^{T}{\lambda}^{+}=L({T}^{-},{t}^{+})\\ {\mathit{BT}}^{+}={T}^{d}({t}^{+})\end{array}$                 eq 5.3-1

Which amounts to solving, for the problem presented in matrix form:

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}K({T}^{-})& {B}^{T}\\ B& 0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}{T}_{1}^{+}\\ {\lambda}_{1}^{+}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}L({T}^{-},{t}^{+})+K({T}^{-}){T}^{-}-R({T}^{-})\\ {T}^{d}({t}^{+})\end{array}\right]$                 eq 5.3-2

The enthalpy function is known up to an integration constant, which appears in the relation linking $R({T}^{-})$ to $K({T}^{-}){T}^{-}$ . This same constant is found in the expression of $L({T}^{-}{\text{,t}}^{+})$ . It can then be eliminated, leading to the following system of equations:

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}K({T}^{-})& {B}^{T}\\ B& 0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}{T}_{1}^{+}\\ {\lambda}_{1}^{+}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}\tilde{L}({T}^{-},{t}^{+})\\ {T}^{d}({t}^{+})\end{array}\right]$                                 eq 5.3-3

where $\tilde{L}({T}^{-}{\text{,t}}^{+})$ is the right-hand side computed with the heat capacity and not the enthalpy (option CHAR_THER_EVOLNI [§6.2]).

Finally, as for the stationary case seen in the previous section, the following iterations are Newton iterations:

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}K({T}_{(i)},{t}^{+})& {B}^{T}\\ B& 0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}{T}_{i+1}^{+}-{T}_{i}^{+}\\ {\lambda}_{i+1}^{+}-{\lambda}_{i}^{+}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}L({T}^{-},{t}^{+})-R({T}_{i},{t}^{+})-{B}^{T}{\lambda}_{i}\\ 0\end{array}\right]$             eq 5.3-4

This time, however, $L({T}^{-}{\text{,t}}^{+})$ is computed with the enthalpy and not the heat capacity, in order to be consistent with $R({T}_{i}^{+})$ .

### 5.4 Convergence

Since time appears in the expression of the tangent matrix, and likewise the time step, it is preferred to update the latter systematically at the beginning of each step so as not to degrade the convergence rate too much. On the other hand, the user is left free to control its computation frequency during a time step.

At each iteration, a search for an optimum progression step towards the solution may be carried out by a few iterations (2 or 3) of line search. This method is described in detail in [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01).

The analysis is deemed to have converged when the residual vector is zero [eq 5.1-6]:

$F({T}_{{i}^{}}^{+},{\lambda}_{{i}^{}}^{+},{t}^{+})=(\begin{array}{c}L({T}^{-},{t}^{+})-R({T}_{{i}^{}}^{+},{t}^{+})-{B}^{T}{\lambda}_{{i}^{}}^{+}\\ {T}^{d}({t}^{+})-{\mathit{BT}}_{{i}^{}}^{+}\end{array})$                 eq 5.4-1

The lower part of the vector is always zero (Dirichlet conditions). One therefore checks:

$\frac{{\mathrm{\parallel }L({T}^{-},{t}^{+})-R({T}_{{i}^{}}^{+},{t}^{+})-{B}^{T}{\lambda}_{{i}^{}}^{+}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}}{{\mathrm{\parallel }L({T}^{-},{t}^{+})-{B}^{T}{\lambda}_{{i}^{}}^{+}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}}\le \epsilon$                         eq 5.4-2

The user also has the possibility of stopping the iterations on an absolute criterion:

${\mathrm{\parallel }L({T}^{-},{t}^{+})-R({T}_{{i}^{}}^{+},{t}^{+})-{B}^{T}{\lambda}_{{i}^{}}^{+}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{\infty}\le \epsilon$                         eq 5.4-3
