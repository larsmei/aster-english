**Summary:**

The study of the dynamic stability of a damped and/or rotating structure leads to the solution of a modal problem of higher order than the traditional standard modal problems (SEP) or generalised modal problems (GEP).

To tackle them, methods are proposed **via** the operator [CALC_MODES](doc:u4.52.02): inverse powers and the Müller method, Lanczos, IRA and QZ. Each has its field of use, its advantages / disadvantages and its development history.

In the first part of the document we summarise the problem of solving a quadratic problem and its place in the general architecture of a modal analysis. We then detail the numerical, software and functional aspects of each of the approaches available in the code. The various results, algorithms or parameters discussed in this document often rely on the modal methods of lower order (SEP and GEP) described in the document [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem](doc:r5.01.01). Reading the latter is therefore a recommended prerequisite.

## 1 Introduction

A majority of studies concerning the dynamic behaviour of damped and/or rotating structures is carried out by performing a transient analysis on a modal basis. In order to extract these vibration modes, a host of algorithms have been developed over some sixty years. In order to cope with the continual increase in the size of the problems and with the degradation of the conditioning of the discretised operators, only the most efficient and the most robust in practice have been incorporated into the modal operator [CALC_MODES](doc:u4.52.02).

The optimal fields of use of the various available methods can be separated. When it is a matter of determining a few eigenvalues (typically half a dozen) or of refining a few estimates, the option `PROCHE` or `AJUSTE` of [CALC_MODES](doc:u4.52.02) is entirely appropriate. This covers heuristic algorithms and those of power type.

On the other hand, in order to capture a significant part of the spectrum, one resorts to one of the options `CENTRE`, `PLUS_PETITE` or `TOUT` of [CALC_MODES](doc:u4.52.02). The latter brings together the so-called “subspace” methods (Lanczos, IRAM) which project the working operator in order to obtain an approximate modal problem of reduced size (then treated by a global method of QR type).

This operator also makes it possible to extract, in a robust manner, the whole spectrum of the modal problem. To do this, one uses a reference global method (method `QZ`) which computes all the modes exhaustively. It is therefore to be reserved for certain uses: a problem of small size (fewer than about one thousand degrees of freedom) or an algorithm benchmark.

The two families of methods can moreover complement each other, because the methods implemented with the option `PROCHE` or `AJUSTE` are very efficient for optimising eigenmodes that have already almost converged. In one or two iterations, they can thus improve the eigenvectors previously estimated *via* one of the options `CENTRE`, `PLUS_PETITE` or `TOUT`. The projection onto the modal basis will only be the better for it.

## 2 Context

### 2.1 Problem statement

We consider the quadratic modal problem (QEP)

$$
\begin{array}{c}\text{Trouver }(\lambda, u) \text{ tel que :}\hfill \\
({\lambda}^{2}\tensTwo{B}+\lambda \tensTwo{C}+\tensTwo{A})\vector{u} = \vectorZero \text{ avec } \vector{u} \ne \vectorZero
\end{array}
$$

where $\tensTwo{A}$, $\tensTwo{B}$ and $\tensTwo{C}$ are square matrices of size $n$, with real or complex coefficients, symmetric or not. This type of problem corresponds, in mechanics, in particular to the study of the free vibrations of a damped and/or rotating structure. For this structure, one looks for the eigenvalues ${\lambda}_{i}$ (and their associated eigenvectors ${\vector{u}}_{i}$ ) closest, in the complex plane, to a given reference value (the “shift” $\sigma$ ) in order to know whether an exciting force can create a resonance. In this standard case:

- The matrix $\tensTwo{A}$ is the stiffness matrix, denoted $\tensTwo{K}$, real symmetric (possibly augmented by the complex symmetric matrix, denoted $\tensTwo{E}_{\text{hyst}}$, if the structure exhibits hysteretic damping), that is $\tensTwo{A}=\tensTwo{K}+\tensTwo{E}_{\text{hyst}}$. Thus $\tensTwo{A}$ is real or complex symmetric.
- The matrix $\tensTwo{B}$ is the mass or inertia matrix denoted $\tensTwo{M}$ (real symmetric).
- The matrix $\tensTwo{C}$ groups, for its part, the possible gyroscopic effects and those of viscous damping *via* the combination:

with $\tensTwo{E}_{\text{visq}}$, the (real symmetric) matrix of damping induced by dissipative forces, $\tensTwo{G}$ the gyroscopic matrix (real skew-symmetric) and $\xi$ a real parameter representative of the rotation speed. Thus $\tensTwo{C}$ is potentially real non-symmetric.

This type of problem is activated by the keyword `TYPE_RESU='DYNAMIQUE'`. In the general case of the QEP and exploiting only their symmetry and arithmetic properties, one can break down the field of the modal operators as follows.

In the absence of hysteretic damping, the standard QEP to be solved is therefore made up of real symmetric matrices. These eigenvalues are either real, or complex conjugate in pairs $\left( \lambda, \tilde{\lambda} \right)$. The eigenvectors then potentially have complex components.

In the presence of hysteretic damping, the QEP becomes complex and loses the previous property (To recover it, the matrices must, for example, be complex Hermitian). These modes are then real, complex in pairs or unpaired.

### 2.3 Specific features of QEPs and elements of theory

A major algebraic specific feature between QEPs and the other classes of modal problems of lower level (GEP and SEP) is the existence of twice as many eigenmodes ($2n$) as the size of the discretised problem. The eigenvectors can no longer be linearly independent; moreover, the eigenvalues may be finite or infinite and this whole “little world” generally lives in the complex plane 3. One must then define robust heuristics in order to filter the eigenvalues desired by the user and to distinguish the real ones from the complex ones, the unpaired complex ones from the coupled ones...

Another complication inherent in the QEP is of an algorithmic nature. There is no Schur decomposition (resp. generalised Schur) as for the SEPs (resp. GEPs) on which the solution algorithm can rely. For example, for the SEP $\tensTwo{A}\vector{u}=\lambda \vector{u}$, this decomposition assures us of the existence of a unitary matrix (hence well conditioned and easily invertible) $\tensTwo{U}$, making it possible to rewrite the working matrix $\tensTwo{A}$ in a form that is easier to handle 4: the upper triangular matrix $\tensTwo{T}$ .

QEPs therefore constitute a very particular and important class 5 of non-linear problems. Their solution is less routine than for the other classes of modal problems. In particular, few methods make it possible to solve the problem directly; one must often pass through a GEP ( *via*  an *ad hoc* linearisation ) then an SEP (spectral transformation), which induces a loss of spectral information and numerical instabilities (propagation of rounding errors).

In the theoretical literature, rather than the QEP (2.1-1), one is interested in the matrix polynomial (quadratic in $\lambda$ )

$$
\tensTwo{Q}(\lambda)
:=
\left(
    {\lambda}^{2}\tensTwo{B}
    +
    \lambda \tensTwo{C}
    +
    \tensTwo{A}
\right )
$$

It is also called the $\lambda$-matrix or dynamic matrix and one looks for its spectrum denoted $\spectre(\tensTwo{Q}):=\lbrace  \lambda \in C/det\tensTwo{Q}(\lambda)=0\rbrace$. When this determinant is zero whatever $\lambda$, the $\lambda$-matrix is said to be singular. Otherwise, it is said to be regular (one generally places oneself in this framework).

The characteristic polynomial of the problem is therefore written in the form $\det\tensTwo{Q}(\lambda)=\det(\tensTwo{B}){\lambda}^{2n}$ plus terms of lower order in $\lambda$. Thus, as soon as $\tensTwo{B}=\tensTwo{M}$ is singular, this polynomial admits $r<2n$ finite roots to which one must add $2n-r$ infinite ones. On the other hand, these distinct eigenvalues can share the same eigenvectors. This is the “dual situation” of the multiple eigenvalues of the SEP, where one and the same eigenvalue is attached to several eigenvectors. Thus, for the canonical QEP

### 2.4 Linearisation strategy

#### 2.4.1 Introduction

The standard approach for solving a modal problem is to transform it so as to bring out canonical matrices revealing the modes sought. For example, the Schur decomposition for the SEPs or the generalised one for the GEPs. Unfortunately, this practice cannot be generalised to modal problems of higher order. Two solutions then present themselves:

- Solve the non-linear problem directly (root of a polynomial, factorisation of a $\lambda$-matrix, methods of Newton type, ...).
- Linearise it into a GEP, then treat the latter *via* the methods adapted in [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem](doc:r5.01.01).

The first strategy is implemented in the options `PROCHE` and `AJUSTE` (Müller-Traub method) in order to capture a first estimate of the eigenvalues. One then combines it with the following strategy, because in order to refine the estimates and accelerate convergence, one calls on a method of inverse-power type (variant of Jennings) on the linearised QEP. Some authors propose complete direct approaches. They often rely on variants of Newton. But their convergence is slow (one mode at a time) and not always assured (cf. Kublanovskaya 1970, G.Peters & J.H.Wilkinson 1979 and A.Ruhe 1973).

The linearisation strategy is therefore often preferred. It doubles the size of the modal problem to be treated, changes its nature (at the risk of losing spectral information 6) and introduces numerical instabilities (sensitivities to rounding errors 7 and to numerical parameters 8). But it has the immense advantage of reusing all the “numerical artillery” already deployed for the GEPs 9.

One can thus capture simultaneously the whole spectrum (cf. QZ method usable with the options `CENTRE`, `PLUS_PETITE` and `TOUT`) or only the part centred on a zone of interest (cf. IRAM or Lanczos) by resorting to the appropriate spectral transformation.

#### 2.4.4 Strategies retained

One therefore has a very great variety of choices for adapting the linearisation of the QEP to each situation. One does not offer the user a parameter making it possible to modulate the linearisation alone. The latter is fixed for a given modal solver:

- With `OPTION` among `PROCHE` and `AJUSTE` (second phase) the QEP/GEP passage is carried out *via*  the companion matrices of ${L}_{1}$, setting $\tensTwo{N}={\tensTwoUnit}_{n}.$ The linearised system is not symmetric but this is not a prerequisite for applying a method of inverse-power type.

- With `OPTION` among `CENTRE` and `PLUS_PETITE` and `METHODE=’TRI_DIAG'` or `METHODE=’SORENSEN'`, the QEP/GEP passage is carried out *via*  the companion matrices of ${L}_{2}$ setting $\tensTwo{N}=\tensTwo{M}$. If the stiffness, mass and damping matrices are real symmetric, one falls back into the field of standard GEPs (real symmetric). The Lanczos and Sorensen methods are then accessible. If math:tensTwo{K} becomes complex or if one of them is non-symmetric, only IRAM remains in the running (with QZ, cf. following item).

However, when $\tensTwo{M}$ is not invertible (because of blocking Lagrange multipliers, for example), the inversion of the diagonal term of $\tensTwo{A}$ is no longer assured. To remedy this problem, one introduces a “regularisation” of the matrix $\tensTwo{M}$, denoted $\tensTwo{M}_{R}$ , in the diagonal term. The latter has the property of cancelling, by multiplication, all the components of the kernel of $\tensTwo{M}$: ${\tensTwo{M}}_{R}\vector{u}=0\text{ si }\vector{u}\in \kernel(\tensTwo{M})$ . ### 2.5 Implementation

The course of a quadratic modal analysis is, by construction, very close to that of a generalised problem. One will therefore attach oneself to the functional solution diagram of the GEPs described in [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem](doc:r5.01.01). Its stages break down as follows:

#### 2.5.1 Pre-processing (linearisation, computation of the shift)

Determination of the linearised form and of the associated spectral transformation according to the method retained (power, Lanczos, IRAM and QZ) and the search option `AJUSTE```PROCHE`` or `PLUS_PETITE`/`CENTRE`.

Note that, the eigenmodes being in the complex plane, the Sturm test currently implemented is inoperative. Therefore the heuristic associated with the option `PROCHE` and `AJUSTE` is not based on the modal positions, and the option `BANDE` is prohibited. Likewise for the post-verification based on the Sturm test [Boi12].

For the subspace methods (`OPTION` among `CENTRE` and `PLUS_PETITE` and `METHODE='TRI_DIAG'` or `METHODE='SORENSEN'`), once the linearised problem ${L}_{2}^{\prime \prime}$ has been updated, one applies a spectral transformation of “shift-and-invert” type (cf. [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem](doc:r5.01.01)). This will make it possible to complete the transformation into an SEP 14 ${S}_{1}$ , to direct the search of the spectrum into a zone of interest for the user and to separate the eigenvalues better.

The inverse-power method, for its part, applies a particular spectral transformation to the linearised problem ${L}_{1}^{\prime}$ .

As for the QZ method (`OPTION` among `CENTRE`, `PLUS_PETITE` and `TOUT` and `METHODE='QZ'`), this spectral transformation is superfluous. The algorithm treats the linearised problem ${L}_{2}^{\star}$ directly and computes all its modes. #### 2.5.2 Factorisation of dynamic matrices

As has already been emphasised, these factorisations of dynamic matrices of the type $\tensTwo{Q}(\lambda)$ do not serve to implement a Sturm test. The latter being illicit for QEPs. However they remain a very present and very costly ingredient 15 for:

- When one must handle a working matrix comprising inverses. ## 3 Inverse power method (OPTION among ['PROCHE','AJUSTE'])

### 3.1 Introduction

As for the treatment of the GEPs, in a QEP this operator works in two parts:

- The **localisation of the eigenvalues** . One determines an approximation of each eigenvalue contained in a given interval by **the Müller-Traub algorithm** [Mul56][DB08] or one takes an estimate supplied by the user.
- **The improvement of these estimates** and the computation of their associated eigenvectors by a method of **inverse iterations** (variant of Jennings[Jen77]).

The search for an approximate value for each eigenvalue considered is selected with the keyword OPTION:

- If OPTION='AJUSTE', in each frequency interval defined by the keyword CALC_FREQ=_F(FREQ), an approximate value of each eigenvalue contained in this interval is computed using the Müller-Traub method (cf. §3.2).
- Either one retains only the NMAX_FREQ (under the factor keyword CALC_FREQ) lowest frequencies contained in the maximum interval specified by the user, or one computes all the values of this interval (if NMAX_FREQ=0).
- If OPTION='PROCHE', the frequencies given by the keyword CALC_FREQ=_F(FREQ) are considered as the approximate values of the eigenvalues sought.

**Notes:**

- *Of course, as has already been specified, these options are to be used rather to determine or refine a few eigenvalues. For a more extended search one must use* the options ['CENTRE','PLUS_PETITE','TOUT'] *.*
- *With the option '* PROCHE *' one cannot compute multiple modes.*
- *It is a costly algorithm because it makes much use of the factorisations of a working dynamic matrix* $Q(\lambda ).$

### 3.2 Müller-Traub method

As in a QEP the clever Sturm test can no longer be applied, the heuristic for localising the eigenvalues must be based on another strategy. One then “falls back” on the **classical search for the zeros of the associated characteristic polynomial** of the QEP $p(\lambda )=\text{dét}\mathrm{Q}(\lambda )$ in the complex plane.

As one then no longer has an order relation, it is not possible to apply the dichotomy implemented for the GEPs. A three-point generalisation of the secant method nevertheless makes it possible to manage. This is the method proposed by D.E.Muller[Mul56][DB08] in 1956. It is an iterative method using as interpolation curve a parabola with horizontal axis. Whereas the secant method relies on a linear interpolation between the last two iterates $\left\lbrace ({\lambda}_{k-1},p({\lambda}_{k-1})),({\lambda}_{k},p({\lambda}_{k}))\right\rbrace ,$ the so-called “Müller-Traub” method seeks to construct the quadratic interpolant passing through the last three

$\left\lbrace ({\lambda}_{k-2},p({\lambda}_{k-2})),({\lambda}_{k-1},p({\lambda}_{k-1})),({\lambda}_{k},p({\lambda}_{k}))\right\rbrace .$

By writing that the Lagrange quadratic interpolant of the characteristic polynomial

$p(\lambda )\approx {a}_{0}{\lambda}^{2}+{a}_{1}\lambda +{a}_{2}$                             (3.2-1)

must pass through the last three points cited, one constructs a system of three equations in three unknowns. From its analytical solution, one deduces the root closest to the last iterate

$\begin{array}{ccc}{\rho}_{k+1}& =& \frac{-\mathrm{2p}({\lambda}_{k}){\delta}_{k}}{{g}_{k}\pm \sqrt{{g}_{k}^{2}-\mathrm{4p}({\lambda}_{k}){\delta}_{k}{\rho}_{k}\left[p({\lambda}_{k-2}){\rho}_{k}-p({\lambda}_{k-1}){\delta}_{k}+p({\lambda}_{k})\right]}}\\ \text{avec}& & {g}_{k}:=p({\lambda}_{k-2}){\rho}_{k}^{2}-p({\lambda}_{k-1}){\delta}_{i}^{2}+p({\lambda}_{k})({\rho}_{k}+{\delta}_{k})\\ & & {\rho}_{k}:=\frac{{\lambda}_{k}-{\lambda}_{k-1}}{{\lambda}_{k-1}-{\lambda}_{k-2}}\end{array}$         (3.2-2)

From ${\rho}_{k}$ , one then easily deduces the new iterate ${\lambda}_{k+1}$ . The sign of the denominator is chosen so as to minimise $\mid {\rho}_{k+1}\mid$ ; thus one favours the “conservative” choice of the zero closest to the previous iterate ${\lambda}_{k}$ . One considers that the method has converged when the following relative criterion has been reached (before $k+1<$ NMAX_ITER_AJUSTE iterations)

$\frac{∣{\lambda}_{k+1}-{\lambda}_{k}∣}{∣{\lambda}_{k}∣}<{\varepsilon}_{\text{PREC\_AJUSTE}}$                         (3.2-3)

This method is relatively easy to implement but it lends itself poorly to searches for zeros of real functions with real roots, because it plunges the interpolation into the complex plane and this even when starting from real values. **Its interest is linked to the class of this method known as “by interpolation curves”,** namely:

- The reliability of the bisection method, since the search is carried out in a ball of approximates ${({\lambda}_{k})}_{k}$ reducing progressively,
- Only the computation of the function is required (no computation of a derivative as, for example, in the Newton-Raphson method).
- The convergence is akin to a quadratic convergence ( *q* ~1.89).

In practice, the evaluation is done not directly on the determinant of the characteristic polynomial $p(\lambda ):=\text{dét}\mathrm{Q}(\lambda )$  but on **its “deflated” form in order to take into account the spectral information already extracted** (otherwise one always reconverges towards the same mode). On the other hand, after having computed $k$ eigenvalues ${({\lambda}_{i})}_{i=1}^{k}$ , one **also deflates taking their conjugate into account**  [18]_
so as not to recompute them

$p(\lambda ):=\frac{\text{dét}\mathrm{Q}(\lambda )}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda -{\lambda}_{i})(\lambda -\stackrel{ˉ}{{\lambda}_{i}})}$                             (3.2-4)

The evaluation of the denominator is trivial. That of the numerator, $\text{dét}\mathrm{Q}(\lambda )$ , is carried out by factorising [19]_
in ${\mathit{LDL}}^{T}$ form the dynamic matrix $\mathrm{Q}(\lambda )$ and by performing the product of the diagonal terms of the diagonal matrix $\mathrm{D}$ .

This factorisation of the dynamic matrix is very costly, with a computational complexity in $O(\mathrm{bn²})$ where $b$  is the bandwidth and $n$ the size of the problem. It is the part most greedy in processing capacity and in memory space of the algorithm. It is to be reproduced $p(i+2)$ times with $p$ the number of modes desired and $i$ the mean number of iterations to convergence. **The algorithm is therefore rather to be reserved for QEPs of small size (<104 degrees of freedom) or for method benchmarks.**

**Notes:**

- Müller[Mul56] proposes a particular and efficient procedure for initialising the process in the case where the function to be interpolated $p(\lambda )$ *is a polynomial of order* $n$ *whose coefficients are known. The case which interests us unfortunately does not fall into this class of problem. The initialisation of the algorithm thus requires 2 preliminary evaluations of* $\mathrm{p.}$
- *This method does not compute a modal position; it therefore handles multiplicities poorly. Unlike the dichotomy implemented for the GEPs. The notion of threshold below which two numerical values are assumed multiple is linked to the parameter* ${\varepsilon}_{\text{PREC\_AJUSTE}}$
- *One retains in formula (3.2-2) only the complex number with positive imaginary part.*

### 3.3 Inverse power method (variant of Jennings)

The second part consists in refining the estimates of the eigenvalues computed by the Müller-Traub method and in computing their associated eigenvectors. To do this, **one linearises the QEP according to the strategy**

**(L1)’(cf. §2.4) and one applies a particular spectral transformation due to Jennings** [Jen77](1977) **.** Its objective is to bring out, as matrix inverse, only that of the dynamic matrix $\mathrm{Q}{(\lambda )}^{-1}$ . The algorithm implemented in the code breaks down as follows:

- Initialisation of the eigenvalue from the estimate of the first phase: ${\lambda}_{0}$ .
- Construction of the dynamic matrix $\mathrm{Q}({\lambda}_{0})$ and of its factorisation.
- Initialisation of the iterative process by the vectors: ${\mathrm{x}}_{0}\text{aléatoire,}{\mathrm{y}}_{0}={\lambda}_{0}{\mathrm{x}}_{0}.$
- For $k=0,\text{NMAX\_ITER-1}$ do
- Normalise    ${\mathrm{x}}_{k}\leftarrow \frac{{\mathrm{x}}_{k}}{{({\stackrel{ˉ}{\mathrm{x}}}_{k})}^{t}{\mathrm{Mx}}_{k}},{\mathrm{y}}_{k}\leftarrow \frac{{\mathrm{y}}_{k}}{{({\stackrel{ˉ}{\mathrm{y}}}_{k})}^{t}{\mathrm{My}}_{k}}$
- Solve    $\mathrm{Q}({\lambda}_{0}){\mathrm{x}}_{k+1}={\mathrm{Cx}}_{k}+{\lambda}_{0}{\mathrm{Mx}}_{k-1}+{\mathrm{My}}_{k-1}$
- Compute    ${\mathrm{y}}_{k+1}=-{\lambda}_{0}{\mathrm{x}}_{k+1}+{\mathrm{x}}_{k}$
- Evaluate ${\lambda}_{k}=\frac{{(\stackrel{ˉ}{{\mathrm{x}}_{k}})}^{T}{\mathrm{Cx}}_{k}+\sqrt{{({(\stackrel{ˉ}{{\mathrm{x}}_{k}})}^{T}{\mathrm{Cx}}_{k})}^{2}-4({(\stackrel{ˉ}{{\mathrm{x}}_{k}})}^{T}{\mathrm{Mx}}_{k})({(\stackrel{ˉ}{{\mathrm{x}}_{k}})}^{T}{\mathrm{Kx}}_{k})}}{2({(\stackrel{ˉ}{{\mathrm{x}}_{k}})}^{T}{\mathrm{Mx}}_{k})}$ (3.3-1)
- If $\frac{∣{\lambda}_{k+1}-{\lambda}_{k}∣}{∣{\lambda}_{k}∣}<\text{PREC}$ and $\max(\mid \text{Re}({\lambda}_{k}-{\lambda}_{0})\mid ,\mid \text{Im}({\lambda}_{k}-{\lambda}_{0})\mid )<\text{PREC}$ then (3.3-2)
- The eigenmode solution is $({\lambda}_{k},{\mathrm{x}}_{k})$ , exit.

*Algorithm 2. Inverse power method (variant of Jennings).*

The stopping criterion PREC and the maximum number of iterations allowed NMAX_ITER are arguments of the factor keyword CALC_MODE.

**Notes:**

- The stopping criterion is based on the extension of the Rayleigh quotient (cf. [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem](doc:r5.01.01) § *4.3) to the QEP. Knowing an eigenvector* ${\mathrm{x}}_{k}$ *a good estimate of its eigenvalue* ${\lambda}_{k}$ *is supplied by formula (3.3-1). One stops the algorithm as soon as the relative variations (in complex) and absolute variations (on the real and imaginary parts separately), with respect to the initial estimate, are lower than a certain value. Thus jointly, one refines the initial estimate of the eigenvalue and one determines its associated eigenvector.*
- *Despite the linearisation of the initial problem, the “clever” formulation of Jennings handles only entities of size* $n$ *. In reality, algorithm 2 can be schematised in vector form*
- $\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{Q}({\lambda}_{0})& {0}_{n}\\ -{\lambda}_{0}{\mathrm{I}}_{n}& {\mathrm{I}}_{n}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{x}}_{k+1}\\ {\mathrm{y}}_{k+1}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left[\begin{array}{cc}-C-{\lambda}_{0}\mathrm{M}& -\mathrm{M}\\ -{\mathrm{I}}_{n}& {0}_{n}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{x}}_{k}\\ {\mathrm{y}}_{k}\end{array}\right\rbrace$ *(3.3-3)*

## 4 Subspace method (METHODE=’TRI_DIAG’/’SORENSEN’)

### 4.1 From the QEP to the SEP

#### 4.1.1 QEP/GEP passage: linearisation

As explained in §2.4, the passage from the QEP to the GEP is carried out *via*  the following linearisation

(L2)’’ $(\underset{\mathrm{A}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\begin{array}{cc}-\mathrm{K}& {0}_{n}\\ {0}_{n}& {\mathrm{M}}_{R}\end{array}\right]}}-\lambda \underset{\mathrm{B}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\begin{array}{cc}-\mathrm{C}& \mathrm{M}\\ \mathrm{M}& {0}_{n}\end{array}\right]}})\left[\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{u}\\ \lambda \mathrm{u}\end{array}\right]={0}_{\mathrm{2n}}$                     (4.1-1)

The GEP obtained is structurally real symmetric and inherits the properties of the matrices $\mathrm{K}$ , $\mathrm{M}$ and $\mathrm{C}$ . As soon as one of them is non-symmetric or complex (possible only with $\mathrm{K}$ ), the GEP becomes non-symmetric or complex. The symmetric or non-symmetric aspect of the GEP is in fact not blocking because **each method will set about finding the “working operator/scalar product” pair which allows it to function** . Moreover, only Lanczos needs a symmetric pair. The IRAM method can very well function in non-symmetric form and often with more robustness [21]_
.

#### 4.1.2 GEP/SEP passage: spectral transformation

Then a spectral transformation (cf. [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem](doc:r5.01.01) §3.7/§5) makes it possible to complete the pre-processing phase of the QEP by transforming the previous GEP $({L}_{2})’’$ into an SEP. When one handles complex modes, two cases are conceivable:

- Work completely in **complex arithmetic** ,
- Remain most often **in real arithmetic** by isolating the real and imaginary contributions of the classical spectral transformation.

**The first strategy** classically uses the spectral transformation known as “shift-and-invert” and transforms $({L}_{2})’’$ into

$({S}_{1})$ $\begin{array}{c}\underset{{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}\mathrm{B}}}\mathrm{w}=\mu \mathrm{w}\\ \text{avec}\mu :=\frac{1}{\lambda -\sigma },\mathrm{w}:=\left[\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{u}\\ \lambda \mathrm{u}\end{array}\right]\end{array}$                             (4.1-2)

It is implemented by activating the complex approach of IRAM (‘SORENSEN’+APPROCHE=’COMPLEXE’). It is the only alternative when one handles a complex matrix $\mathrm{K}$ [22]_
. It is usable with $\mathrm{K},\mathrm{M}$ and $\mathrm{C}$ symmetric or not.

**The second strategy** relies on the “double shift sum” spectral transformation introduced in 1987 by B.N.Parlett & Y.Saad[PS87], which leads to handling two types of SEP

$({S}_{2})$ $\begin{array}{c}\text{Re}\underset{{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{+}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{({(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}\mathrm{B})}}\mathrm{w}={\mu}^{+}\mathrm{w}\\ \text{avec}{\mu}^{+}:=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{\lambda -\sigma }+\frac{1}{\lambda -\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}),\mathrm{w}:=\left[\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{u}\\ \lambda \mathrm{u}\end{array}\right]\end{array}$                         (4.1-3)

$({S}_{3})$ $\begin{array}{c}\text{Im}\underset{{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{-}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{({(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}\mathrm{B})}}\mathrm{w}={\mu}^{-}\mathrm{w}\\ \text{avec}{\mu}^{-}:=\frac{1}{\mathrm{2i}}(\frac{1}{\lambda -\sigma }+\frac{1}{\lambda -\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}),\mathrm{w}:=\left[\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{u}\\ \lambda \mathrm{u}\end{array}\right]\end{array}$                     (4.1-4)

These two variants are implemented with Lanczos and IRAM *via* the values, respectively, ‘REEL’ and ‘IMAG’ of the keyword APPROCHE. With Lanczos, the field of use is limited to the classical QEPs (real symmetric), whereas IRAM is less restricted and also allows the non-symmetric case.

**Notes:**

- *The origin of the denomination “double shift sum” of the spectral transformation above appears more clearly if one reformulates the operators as follows*

> $\begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{+}:=\frac{1}{2}\left[{(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}+{(\mathrm{A}-\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}\mathrm{B})}^{-1}\right]\mathrm{B}\\ {\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{-}:=\frac{1}{\mathrm{2i}}\left[{(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}+{(\mathrm{A}-\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}\mathrm{B})}^{-1}\right]\mathrm{B}\end{array}$                 (4.1-5)

- The terminology “sum” is by opposition to the “double shift product” proposed initially by J.C.Francis and used, moreover, behind the scenes, by the QZ method and the “implicit” restarts of IRAM (cf. [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem](doc:r5.01.01) Appendix 1):

${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}:={\left[(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})+(\mathrm{A}-\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}\mathrm{B})\right]}^{-1}\mathrm{B}$                     (4.1-6)

*The latter has the major disadvantage of producing very filled working matrices (hence more costly to handle and to store) compared with the strategy of Parlett & Saad.*

**Once the working operator has been determined, it remains to choose its associated scalar product** . For IRAM, one can be content with a non-symmetric “operator/scalar product” pair (cf. [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem](doc:r5.01.01) §6), but for Lanczos, the latter must obligatorily be symmetric (cf. [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem](doc:r5.01.01) §7). Several choices of matrix (pseudo)scalar products allow this symmetry:

$\begin{array}{c}{(\mathrm{w},\mathrm{z})}_{\mathrm{B}}:={\mathrm{z}}^{T}\mathrm{Bw}\\ {(\mathrm{w},\mathrm{z})}_{+}:={\mathrm{z}}^{T}\text{Re}{\left[{(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}\right]}^{-1}\mathrm{w}\\ {(\mathrm{w},\mathrm{z})}_{-}:={\mathrm{z}}^{T}\text{Im}{\left[{(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}\right]}^{-1}\mathrm{w}\end{array}$                         (4.1-7)

The first works with both formulations (S2) and (S3), whereas the second (resp. the third) makes symmetric only ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{+}$ (resp. ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{-}$ ). In the effective implementation in *Code_Aster*, it is these last two which are retained for the real and imaginary approaches of Lanczos.

**Note:**

- *These scalar products are the “natural” extensions of the matrix (pseudo) scalar product used by the Newmann-Pipano variant in a GEP:*

$(\mathrm{w},\mathrm{z}):={\mathrm{z}}^{T}(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})\mathrm{w}$                             (4.1-8)

### 4.2 Implementation in Code_Aster

#### 4.2.1 Choice of the spectral shift

In a GEP there are four ways of choosing this shift (cf. [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem](doc:r5.01.01)§5.4). In a QEP, the option ‘BANDE’ being prohibited because of the absence of an adapted Sturm test, this figure is reduced to three:

- $\sigma =0,$ one looks for the smallest eigenvalues of the initial problem. This corresponds to OPTION='PLUS_PETITE' under the factor keyword CALC_FREQ.
- $\sigma ={\sigma}_{0}\text{avec}{\sigma}_{0}={(2\pi {f}_{0})}^{2},$ one looks for the frequencies close to the frequency FREQ= ${f}_{0}$ (OPTION='CENTRE').
- $\sigma ={\sigma}_{0}\text{avec}{\sigma}_{0}={(2\pi {f}_{0})}^{2}\text{et}{\sigma}_{1}=\eta {(2\pi {f}_{0})}^{2}/2$ one looks for the frequencies close to the frequency FREQ= ${f}_{0}$ (OPTION='CENTRE') and to the reduced damping AMOR_REDUIT= $\eta$ (OPTION='CENTRE').

The number of frequencies to be computed is given in general by the user with the aid of NMAX_FREQ under the factor keyword CALC_FREQ.

**The computation of the working operators** ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{\pm}$ **can be reduced to simple matrix-vector products and to an inversion of the dynamic matrix** $\mathrm{Q}(\lambda )$ (previously factorised) *via* the following (clever) formulation

${(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}\mathrm{B}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{0}_{n}& {0}_{n}\\ {\mathrm{M}}_{R}^{-1}\mathrm{M}& {0}_{n}\end{array}\right]-\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{Q}{(\sigma )}^{-1}& {0}_{n}\\ {0}_{n}& \sigma {\mathrm{M}}_{R}^{-1}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{Q}{(\sigma )}^{-1}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{C}+\sigma \mathrm{M}& \mathrm{M}\\ \mathrm{C}+\sigma \mathrm{M}& \mathrm{M}\end{array}\right]$         (4.2-1)

On the other hand, this formulation has the “good taste” of allowing treatments in pure real arithmetic and of preserving the sparse structure of the matrices. One can thus construct the following operating procedure:

- Preparation in $C$  [23]_n(only once, in the initialisation phase of the algorithm, cf. §2.5):

Form $\mathrm{Q}(\sigma ):=({\sigma}^{2}\mathrm{M}+\sigma \mathrm{C}+\mathrm{K})$

Factorise $\mathrm{Q}(\sigma )$ in LDLT form.

- Computation of $\begin{array}{c}\\ {\mathrm{A}}_{{\sigma}^{\pm}}\underset{\mathrm{w}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{u}\\ \mathrm{v}\end{array}\right]}}\end{array}$  (several times per Lanczos or IRAM iteration):

Form in $R{\mathrm{u}}_{1}:=\mathrm{Cu},{\mathrm{u}}_{2}:=\mathrm{Mu},{\mathrm{u}}_{3}:=\mathrm{Mv},$

Compute in $C{\mathrm{u}}_{4}:=\mathrm{Q}{(\sigma )}^{-1}+\sigma {\mathrm{u}}_{2}+{\mathrm{u}}_{3}$

- According to the choice of approach    $\begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{+}\mathrm{w}=\left[\begin{array}{c}-\text{Re}({\mathrm{u}}_{4})\\ {\mathrm{M}}_{R}^{-1}\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{u}-\text{Re}(\sigma {\mathrm{u}}_{4}))\end{array}\right]\\ {\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{-}\mathrm{w}=\left[\begin{array}{c}-\text{Im}({\mathrm{u}}_{4})\\ -{\mathrm{M}}_{R}^{-1}\mathrm{M}(\mathrm{u}-\text{Im}(\sigma {\mathrm{u}}_{4}))\end{array}\right]\end{array}$

*Algorithm 3. Operating procedure for computing the working operators* ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{\pm}$ *.*

In the complex approach, the computation is done directly *via* formula (4.1-2) in complex arithmetic.

Concerning the matrix scalar products, their manipulations in *Code_Aster* rely on the following formulations:

$\begin{array}{c}{(\mathrm{w},\mathrm{z})}_{+}:={\mathrm{z}}^{T}\left[(\mathrm{A}-\text{Re}(\sigma )\mathrm{B})+{\text{Im}}^{2}(\sigma )\mathrm{B}{(\mathrm{A}-\text{Re}(\sigma )\mathrm{B})}^{-1}\mathrm{B}\right]\mathrm{w}\\ {(\mathrm{w},\mathrm{z})}_{-}:={\mathrm{z}}^{T}\left[\frac{1}{\text{Im}(\sigma )}(\mathrm{A}-\text{Re}(\sigma )\mathrm{B}){\mathrm{B}}^{-1}(\mathrm{A}-\text{Re}(\sigma )\mathrm{B})+{\text{Im}}^{2}(\sigma )\mathrm{B}\right]\mathrm{w}\end{array}$         (4.2-2)

**Note:**

- *The computation of the matrix scalar products can be carried out only in real arithmetic. For the first scalar product one needs an additional dynamic matrix* $\mathrm{Q}(\text{Re}(\sigma ))$ *and its factorised form (hence an extra computational and memory cost).*

## 5 Global QZ method (METHODE=’QZ’)

### 5.1 From the QEP to the GEP

Unlike the methods seen previously, the QZ method is capable of handling a GEP directly. One therefore has **only a single transformation to apply to the initial QEP, which is the linearisation** . The one retained empirically and detailed in §2.4 is

(L2)* $(\underset{\mathrm{A}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\begin{array}{cc}-\mathrm{K}& {0}_{n}\\ {0}_{n}& \alpha {\mathrm{I}}_{n}\end{array}\right]}}-\lambda \underset{\mathrm{B}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{C}& \mathrm{M}\\ \alpha {\mathrm{I}}_{n}& {0}_{n}\end{array}\right]}})\left[\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{u}\\ \lambda \mathrm{u}\end{array}\right]={0}_{\mathrm{2n}}$                     (5.1-1)

The GEP obtained is not structurally symmetric (or Hermitian) but this is not prejudicial to the computation because the adapted LAPACK drivers work only in non-symmetric form. **For more robustness and in order to simplify the structuring of the code** , one formulates and solves the GEP (L2)* in the **complex plane** . As for the GEPs with complex modes one uses the standard LAPACK routines (ZGGEV) or expert ones (ZGGEVX) according to the parameter TYPE_QZ=’QZ_SIMPLE’/’QZ_EQUI’.

At the end of the modal computation, QZ returns to *Code_Aster*  the modes computed and the latter will be verified, filtered and ordered according to the same recommendations as for the complex modes of the GEPs (cf. [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem](doc:r5.01.01) §9.4).

## 7 Appendix 1. Interpretation of the complex eigenvalues

In the case of a **symmetric damping and in the absence of internal damping** , from orthogonality relations and from the fact that the eigen elements appear in conjugate pairs, one has the following relations:

$\begin{array}{c}\frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{{\text{*}}^{T}}\mathrm{C}{\Phi}_{i}}{{\Phi}_{i}^{{\text{*}}^{T}}\mathrm{M}{\Phi}_{i}}=\frac{{c}_{i}}{{m}_{i}}=2\text{Re}({\lambda}_{i})\\ \frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{{\text{*}}^{T}}\mathrm{K}{\Phi}_{i}}{{\Phi}_{i}^{{\text{*}}^{T}}\mathrm{M}{\Phi}_{i}}=\frac{{k}_{i}}{{m}_{i}}={∣{\lambda}_{i}∣}^{2}\end{array}$

If one denotes ${\lambda}_{i}={\alpha}_{i}\pm i{\beta}_{i}$ , one can then define

$\begin{array}{}{\omega}_{i}=\mid {\lambda}_{i}\mid =\sqrt{{\alpha}_{i}^{2}+{\beta}_{i}^{2}}=\frac{{\beta}_{i}}{{\xi}_{i}}\\ {\xi}_{i}=\text{Re}\frac{({\lambda}_{i})}{{\omega}_{i}}=\frac{{\alpha}_{i}}{\sqrt{{\alpha}_{i}^{2}+{\beta}_{i}^{2}}}{\omega}_{i}\end{array}$

One can write the complex eigenvalue in the following form

${\lambda}_{i}=-{\xi}_{i}{\omega}_{i}\pm i{\omega}_{i}\sqrt{1-{\xi}_{i}^{2}}$

This formulation induces the following **physical interpretation** :

- The real term represents the dissipative character of the system.
- The imaginary part represents the oscillatory part of the solution.
- ${\omega}_{i}$ is the circular frequency of the $i$ th mode.
- ${\xi}_{i}$ is the damping of the $i$ th mode,
- ${\omega}_{{d}_{i}}={\omega}_{i}\sqrt{1-{\xi}_{i}^{2}}$ is the reduced damping of the $i$ th mode.

As for **the physical interpretation of the eigenvectors** :

- The physical meaning of the existence of a complex eigenvector lies in the fact that if the structure vibrates on an eigenmode, its various degrees of freedom do not vibrate with the same phase with respect to one another.
- The modal antinodes and nodes do not correspond to stationary points, but move in the course of the motion.

**Notes:**

- *One recovers the classical formulation of damped systems with 1 degree of freedom .*
- ${k}_{i},{m}_{i}$ *and* ${c}_{i}$ *are real. They are indeed quantities intrinsic to a mode (modal quantities) and therefore dependent on its normalisation.*
- *We recall that the modes of the GEP (just like the complex modes of the GEP) do not diagonalise the matrices* $\mathrm{M},\mathrm{K}\text{et}\mathrm{C}.$
- *If the real part of the eigenvalue is negative, then the eigenmode is a damped periodic motion of circular frequency* $\omega$ *. If on the contrary it is positive, then the eigenmode is a periodic motion of increasing amplitude and therefore unstable.*
