# 1 **u2.04.07** Use of transient solution methods for strongly non-linear quasi-static problems

> **Summary.**

This document presents the use of transient solution methods (implicit or explicit) for the numerical simulation of quasi-static problems presenting strong material non-linearities. The only aim is to obtain solutions in which the dynamic effects are negligible.

These solution methods (based on the use of DYNA_NON_LINE) are designed as an alternative to the usual quasi-static approaches of *Code_Aster* (based on STAT_NON_LINE) when the latter prove unable to converge in an acceptable time. This may be the case for applications involving damaging materials.

The methods that are the subject of this document are of two types:

1. implicit transient solution,
2. explicit pseudo-dynamic solution.

The choice will depend on the nature of the problem to be treated, in particular in connection with the time scale considered. The use of these strategies being more delicate than the quasi-static approach, they are to be reserved for the most problematic cases.

Reading documentation U2.06.13 constitutes a strongly recommended prerequisite: the general advice on the use of DYNA_NON_LINE remains relevant.

## 1 Introduction

Many problems in mechanics require taking into account, at the material level, damaging constitutive behaviours: reinforced-concrete structures, soil models…

The constitutive relation may then present softening, and the known implicit quasi-static solution methods have difficulty in converging (the tangent stiffness operator becomes singular). In certain cases, even resorting to very high-performance strategies such as mixed line search or piloting is insufficient.

In order to be able to overcome these limitations, there exist alternative strategies which are based on methods inspired by the tools of direct transient analysis [bib1].

It is stated clearly that there is no question here of wanting to simulate a dynamic response of vibratory type or with wave propagation: the aim is to obtain slowly evolving solutions, and therefore solutions consistent with the quasi-staticity hypothesis. The usual dynamic methods must therefore be adapted to this framework, and the solution thus obtained will have to satisfy the hypotheses of a sufficiently slow evolution.

Finally, it should be kept well in mind that these transient approaches, because of their specific features, must be used as a last resort, when all the remedies available in STAT_NON_LINE have failed.

The prerequisite for the use of the methods presented in this documentation is therefore to have already developed and exhaustively tested the options available in STAT_NON_LINE for the application considered. It goes without saying that a good knowledge of STAT_NON_LINE and of DYNA_NON_LINE is also strongly recommended, as is reading the corresponding documentations: R5.03.01, R5.05.05 and above all U2.06.13.

In particular, the general advice on the use of the operator DYNA_NON_LINE given in documentation U2.06.13 remains valid, and it therefore constitutes an indispensable prerequisite for the proper implementation of the methods that are the subject of this documentation.

## 2 Features available in quasi-statics and absent in transient analysis

The problem of damaging laws, in the local version, will mainly be addressed here. Indeed, non-local approaches, such as GRAD_VARI, are not available in dynamics. Particular care must therefore be taken to define for oneself a suitable minimum mesh-cell size so as not to observe excessive localisation phenomena.

Next, methods of line-search type (mixed or otherwise) are not authorised in dynamics. This lack must be put into perspective, knowing that attempts to apply these methods to studies of reinforced-concrete structures in dynamics have not brought out any significant contribution to convergence, unlike what is observed in quasi-statics. Let us note, nevertheless, that no theoretical argument would forbid the use of these methods in dynamics.

Finally, the piloting techniques available in STAT_NON_LINE (arc length, for example) are forbidden in dynamics because they then have no meaning.

## 3 Passing from a quasi-static analysis to a transient analysis

Far from being limited to replacing the term STAT_NON_LINE by DYNA_NON_LINE in a command file (and to defining the densities, *at a minimum*…), the passage from the quasi-static to the transient must be accompanied by a certain number of indispensable precautions, on pain of strongly degrading the quality of the numerical solution obtained.

These adaptations, described in detail in documentation U2.06.13, concern:

- the regularisation in time of the boundary conditions,
- the definition of initial conditions which do not introduce numerical oscillations.

In addition to these general aspects, the user will have to pay attention to other, more specific adaptations.

- Definition of the densities:

from the physical point of view, the density must of course be defined at every point of the model. If the model comprises discrete elements, a discrete mass will have to be attached to them. The assembled mass operator must be invertible. The devices sometimes employed in quasi-statics, such as stiffenings on zones of the model (anchorages…) with materials having very large Young's moduli (or very large point stiffnesses), are to be handled with caution. Indeed, these very stiff zones will generate high-frequency disturbances in dynamics. Moreover, with an explicit time scheme, these very stiff zones risk causing the value of the critical time step to drop (*cf.* R5.05.05).

- Definition of the mesh-cell sizes and of the time steps:

as a preliminary to the transient analysis, it is strongly recommended to carry out a modal analysis (with CALC_MODES) in order to obtain modal information which will make it possible to qualify the quality of the model in dynamics and to adjust certain parameters. The objective not being to go into the details of modal analysis, a few rules can nevertheless be recalled.

Low-frequency solutions are sought, so only the first modes are relevant. Their proper representation can give indications of the mesh-cell sizes to be employed, in addition to the considerations already taken into account for the previous quasi-static analyses. Roughly, about ten mesh cells per smallest wavelength is sufficient.

The modal analysis will also make it possible to check that the model is free of problems such as contributions to the inertia or to the stiffness that are not defined.

Finally, the modal analysis is indispensable for the use of modal damping in DYNA_NON_LINE or for recalibrating the Rayleigh damping, as will be seen in what follows.

- Definition of the damping:

the user will also have to consider the question of the damping intrinsic to the model that he wants to use.

In DYNA_NON_LINE, apart from the discrete elements, a global damping of Rayleigh or modal type can be introduced. Since one is seeking to simulate slow evolutions, one may be tempted to use damping values higher than for the classical dynamic analyses. A compromise nevertheless remains to be found, case by case, between a problem that is insufficiently damped (which will present oscillations) and a problem that is too heavily damped (critical or even super-critical damping).

It is therefore advised to begin by implementing a "realistic" damping (therefore of a value identical to what is encountered in transient dynamics). Then, if this damping is judged insufficient, to increase it progressively.

On various applications [bib1], it has been possible to observe that a damping of Rayleigh type, recalibrated on an equivalent modal damping of the order of 20%, or even 30%, was suitable.

## 4 Numerical implementation

This chapter will address the choices to be favoured at the level of the numerical methods of DYNA_NON_LINE [bib2].

In a general manner, it is advised to follow the following logic:

1. once the possibilities of quasi-static solution through STAT_NON_LINE have been exhausted (including line search and piloting),
2. begin the dynamic approaches with an implicit solution,
3. in the event of failure of the transient implicit strategies (including by combining different dampings such as Rayleigh and a scheme of HHT type [bib2] and [bib3]), one switches to explicit (having first checked that the global damping does not depend on the stiffness matrix).

For each transient approach, one should begin with as little damping as possible and therefore, in particular, with non-dissipative time schemes (such as Newmark or the central differences [bib4]).

### 4.1 Discretisation in time

Unlike the quasi-static case, time has a physical meaning. Its discretisation is all the more sensitive for that.

A few rules can be stated:

- the evolution of the imposed loadings must be sampled sufficiently finely (between 5 and 10 time steps per shortest period of the signals considered),
- the modal behaviour of the structure must be well represented (as above, one must have between 5 and 10 time steps per lowest period of the modes considered).

Given the low-frequency character of the problems that one wants to address here, these two rules are in general not very penalising. Nevertheless, compared with the time steps of the previous quasi-static analyses, the dynamic time steps can be quite markedly smaller.

In the explicit case, the Courant condition must in addition be respected (CFL *cf.* [bib5], [bib2] and R05.05.05), on pain of numerical divergence. For an integration scheme of central-difference type, the critical time step is equal to $2/\omega$, where $\omega$ is the highest natural angular frequency of the system.

This angular frequency can be computed with CALC_MODES by choosing the option 'PLUS_GRANDE'.

For further details, the reader may refer to documentation U2.06.13.

For most structures, the Courant condition is very penalising: the wave speed often being of the order of a few thousand m / s, one arrives at time steps of less than 10-5 s.

### 4.2 Choice of the time-integration schemes

The implicit schemes can be classified in two categories (the schemes of order 1 and/or in velocity, which are more specifically suited to very irregular problems, are deliberately set aside):

- average acceleration (NEWMARK [bib4]), of order 2, which brings no numerical dissipation: to be used first,
- complete HHT (MODI_EQUI = 'OUI' [bib3]), which remains of order 2, unlike the case of the modified average acceleration (MODI_EQUI = 'NON', the default option). This scheme is specifically developed in order to introduce a high-frequency numerical damping and therefore not to disturb the low-frequency physical response. The damping is controlled directly by the parameter ALPHA of the scheme.

If high-frequency oscillations are observed in the numerical solution (roughly, oscillations whose period is of the order of a few time steps), the complete HHT scheme can be chosen, to begin with a value of the order of –0.1 for the parameter ALPHA. A value of –0.3 constitutes an upper limit that is still usable.

If more damping is desired in the medium-frequency range, then the modified average-acceleration scheme can be employed.

In the explicit case, two schemes are available:

- central differences (DIFF_CENT [bib4]), which is non-dissipative,
- Tchamwa-Wielgosz (TCHAMWA [bib6]), which is dissipative, in a manner comparable to HHT.

Here again, it is recommended to begin by using a non-dissipative scheme.

### 4.3 Damping models

The order of introduction and of use of the dissipation in the discretised model is the following:

1. intrinsic dissipation linked to the non-linear constitutive relations and to the links (friction),
2. global dissipation of structural-damping type (Rayleigh or modal),
3. numerical dissipation of the time scheme.

Ideally, the first category ought to be sufficient, but in practice, for reasons of simplification of the model, it is often indispensable to add structural damping, the damping of the scheme being the last resort.

Only the use of structural damping, in the Rayleigh sense, and that linked to the scheme will be addressed here.

Let us simply recall that the more the sources of dissipation are multiplied, the more arduous their control and their physical interpretation will be.

This model makes it possible to define the global damping matrix C as being a linear combination of the stiffness and mass matrices (in order to have a damping matrix that is diagonal on the basis of the usual dynamic modes):

#### 4.3.2 Damping due to the time scheme [bib2]

Documentations R05.05.05 and U2.06.13 present this aspect. Here one will confine oneself to recalling the broad trends.

To summarise, it can be recalled that, among the implicit schemes:

- the average-acceleration scheme does not dissipate,
- only the complete HHT scheme does not disturb the low-frequency domain,
- for one and the same value of the parameter ALPHA, the modified average acceleration introduces much more dissipation than the HHT scheme.

In order to highlight the influence of the high-frequency damping of the implicit schemes, documentation U2.06.13 presents examples of applications.

Finally, as regards the explicit schemes, the shape of the damping of the Tchamwa scheme is qualitatively close to that of the modified average acceleration.

### 4.4 Adaptation of the explicit methods

The conditional stability of the explicit schemes makes them very poorly suited to the simulation of slow phenomena. The explicit solution methods are not used here to capture rapid phenomena such as wave propagation; their use must rather be perceived as a particular solver that one adapts for slow problems.

In order to be able to increase the critical time step [bib2], the density of the structure can be increased (which lowers the wave speed in proportion to its square root):

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.07/Object_3.svg)

This must however be done progressively.

Indeed, two risks exist:

- if the time step becomes too large, the computed solution may miss certain phenomena, such as the appearance of shear bands, and go so far as to bifurcate towards a branch very different from the expected response,
- the increase in the density may be limited by the poor conditioning of the mass matrix.

As an indication, the maximum time step in the explicit case (and therefore the maximum density) may be of the same order of magnitude as the time step necessary for the implicit transient analysis; in any case, it must remain smaller than the quasi-static time step. These are rough trends, which do not dispense with a parametric study on the explicit time step.

If the model presents strong heterogeneities of stiffness (definition of several materials), it may be relevant to modify the densities separately, so as to have a Courant condition that is relatively homogeneous between the zones having different materials.

**Important note**

*If displacement boundary conditions which evolve in the course of time are imposed, account must be taken of the fact that these conditions are in fact imposed in acceleration in the explicit case (because that is the primal unknown). This means that one must enter into* *DYNA_NON_LINE the second derivative of the displacement signal that one wants to impose. This evolution of the imposed displacement must therefore be differentiable at least twice with respect to time…*

Finally, it is recommended to use a diagonal (lumped) mass matrix, which is obtained by the keyword MASS_DIAG = 'OUI' of DYNA_NON_LINE. This computation option not being available for all the finite elements, the user may be obliged to use the consistent mass, should the occasion arise, as in the implicit case.

## 5 Quality control of the computed solutions

### 5.1 Quantities of interest coming from the quasi-static case

As for the quasi-static analyses, the quantities relevant to post-processing can be classified in two categories [bib1]:

1. evolutions of force / displacement type: quantities making it possible to interpret the global response of the structure,
2. isovalues of fields such as the damage or the cumulative plastic strain.

### 5.2 Complementary dynamic quantities to be analysed

In addition to these analyses, for the dynamic analyses it is indispensable to check that the slow-evolution hypothesis is respected. To this end, one must make sure that the inertia forces remain small compared with the other forces in the system (external and internal forces). A simple means of having an evaluation of the evolution of the inertia forces in the course of the analysis consists in observing the acceleration field in the course of time.

A first simple criterion can be based on an (infinite) norm of the acceleration at each time instant. In the event of a notable and lasting increase in the acceleration (for example beyond 1 m/s2 over several successive time steps), this means that dynamic phenomena (therefore not taken into account by a quasi-static solution) are occurring:

- if one is within a physical framework, and therefore with a realistic density, the computed solution is then subjected to non-negligible dynamic phenomena;
- if one is within an explicit framework in which the density has been multiplied in order to increase the critical time step, then the inertia effects observed are the sign that the solution method is not suited. It is then imperative to modify the parameters, such as lowering the density, or modifying the damping…

In the first case, one can attempt to relaunch an analysis with a slightly finer time step and possibly a slightly higher Rayleigh damping, or even with a scheme of HHT type. If, even with all these modifications, the solution still presents inertia effects, then any quasi-static approach is unsuitable.

## 6 Optimisation of performance

In most cases it is recommended to carry out the analysis in quasi-statics when the non-linearities are moderate and then, as soon as the number of iterations to convergence for equilibrium increases significantly, to switch to dynamics (implicit, then explicit if need be). More precisely, it is advised to switch before the appearance of strong non-linearities, so that the dynamic analysis is initiated on an evolution that is still fairly "regular".

In quasi-statics, it is not rare to have to carry out more than 10 iterations in order to obtain convergence in the sense of the equilibrium residual. In implicit dynamics this value of 10 iterations constitutes, in general, a good starting value for the parameter ITER_GLOB_MAXI of CONVERGENCE. If one cannot converge in fewer than 10 to 20 iterations, it is then preferable to decrease the time step rather than to increase the maximum number of iterations allowed.

In the explicit case, there are no iterations for equilibrium, so the computation cost of each time step will therefore be constant, whatever the level of non-linearity (apart, possibly, from the local check of the constitutive behaviour). In the case of a quasi-static analysis in which the non-linearity increases with time, a crossover point can therefore be found, at the level of the CPU time, beyond which the efficiency of the explicit methods (or even of the implicit methods, depending on the cases) becomes greater than that of continuing in quasi-statics. The gain brought by a larger time step in quasi-statics is cancelled out by the necessity of carrying out more and more iterations at each step. Likewise, the computation cost in the explicit case remaining constant, one can also evaluate fairly precisely the total computation time necessary for the solution of the complete case, whereas in quasi-statics, the number of iterations at each step being highly variable and also being liable to entail an unknown number of subdivisions of the step, the total computation time is sometimes very difficult to predict.

The use, even as a matter of routine, of the explicit methods therefore seems very attractive in view of the CPU time, which remains under control. This optimism must however be tempered by keeping well in mind that one is depriving oneself of the safeguard constituted by the precise check of equilibrium and that, consequently, the quality of the explicit solution obtained must be analysed with greater care. The explicit algorithm will not diverge (if the Courant condition is respected), but the solution obtained is not guaranteed by an equilibrium-verification criterion. In particular, a parametric study on the time step is indispensable, because the appearance of the solution can vary strongly when this step becomes too large.

This obligation to check the explicit solution is all the greater in that one is furthermore seeking to verify the fundamental hypothesis of slow evolution.

## 7 Conclusion

This document presents the use of transient dynamic methods for the simulation of slow and strongly non-linear evolutions [bib1]. The dynamic operators thus employed for the solution can be seen as particular solvers making it possible, in certain cases, to obtain solutions for which the quasi-static algorithmics available in *Code_Aster* (around the operator STAT_NON_LINE) shows great difficulties of convergence (in the sense of the verification of equilibrium).

These dynamic methods are to be used as a last resort, because mastering them is more delicate than in the quasi-static framework. This is even more marked with the explicit methods, since the quality of the solution is not guaranteed by a precise verification of equilibrium at each time instant.

The first stage is the adaptation of the model to the dynamic methods. It is mainly a matter of making sure of the proper regularity of the imposed conditions and of the correct definition of the density and of the global damping (Rayleigh). The piloting methods, the non-local approaches and the line search cannot be used in dynamics. Moreover, it is necessary to introduce a global damping model (Rayleigh or modal) whose level is higher than in classical dynamics (commonly 20% equivalent modal damping).

Next, it is recommended to begin by using an implicit transient method (DYNA_NON_LINE with a time scheme of NEWMARK type, or HHT). From the point of view of the optimisation of the computation time, it is recommended to carry out all the weakly non-linear phases in quasi-statics and to switch to transient analysis (implicit to begin with) only upon the appearance of notable non-linearities (this may take the form of a clear increase in the number of iterations to convergence, or even of a subdivision of the time step).

In the event of failure, including with a complete HHT scheme and a relatively strong structural damping (up to about 30% equivalent modal damping), the user can switch to explicit. Further adaptations must then be made, such as increasing the density in order to obtain a sufficiently large critical time step, and modifying the damping.

In all the dynamic cases, it is indispensable to analyse the evolution of the acceleration in the course of time, in order to make sure of the validity of the slow-evolution hypothesis. The inertia effects must remain small.
