# r4.02.01 Finite elements in acoustics

**Summary:**

This document describes, for stationary linear acoustics at low frequency in a bounded medium, the equations used, the variational formulations which follow from them, and the corresponding translation into isoparametric finite elements by means of a usual formulation with one unknown $p$ (acoustic pressure). This document corresponds to PHENOMENE='ACOUSTIQUE'.

## 1 Introduction

One places oneself in the framework of linear acoustics, in a bounded medium, which considers a compressible fluid, with small amplitudes of perturbations or fluctuations of pressure and of density known.

The equations to be solved are those describing the local equilibrium of the fluid and the conservation of mass, together with the linear viscoacoustic constitutive relation (of hysteretic type).

A modelling option has been developed in *Code_Aster* , making it possible to study stationary linear acoustic propagation at low frequency, in a bounded medium, for propagation domains of complex topology, that is to solve, under the conditions stated, the Helmholtz equation.

In order to know the paths of propagation of energy in the fluid, the acoustician has available the active acoustic intensity $I$ :

$$
I=\frac{1}{2}\text{Re}\left[p{v}^{\text{*}}\right]
$$

And the reactive acoustic intensity $J$ :

$$
J=\frac{1}{2}\text{Im}\left[p{v}^{\text{*}}\right]
$$

Where ${v}^{\text{*}}$ denotes the complex conjugate of the vibratory velocity. Knowledge of these quantities provides a very important complement of information in the solution of problems of every kind, for example the measurement of the powers radiated by machines, and the recognition and location of sources.

## 2 Equations and boundary conditions of the problem

The equation to be solved in the frequency domain is the Helmholtz equation [bib2]:

$$
(\Delta +{k}^{2})p=s
$$

$p$ is a complex quantity denoting the acoustic pressure and $s$ , also complex, represents the source terms of the problem. The parameter $k$ denotes the wave number of the problem treated; it may be complex or real, according to whether or not the propagation takes place in a domain comprising a certain attenuation modelled by means of a complex wave speed, for example if the material is “porous” [bib6]:

$$
k=\frac{\omega}{c}
$$

with $c$ denoting the speed of sound, which may be complex in the case of propagation in a “porous” or viscoacoustic medium, and $\omega$ is a real number in every case, which denotes the angular frequency such that:

$$
\omega =2\pi f
$$

$f$ is the working frequency of the harmonic problem. The figure shows the arbitrary confined domain in which the Helmholtz equation () applies, and the boundary conditions. $\Omega$ is a bounded open set of ${ℝ}^{3}$ with regular boundary $\partial \Omega$ , partitioned into $\partial {\Omega}_{v}$ and $\partial {\Omega}_{z}$ :

$$
\partial \Omega =\partial {\Omega}_{v}\cup \partial {\Omega}_{z}
$$

Figure 2.1-1: Configuration of the acoustic problem in a bounded domain

The equation () is to be solved in a bounded domain $\Omega$ . The boundary conditions to be taken into account on the boundary $\partial \Omega$ of the domain $\Omega$ are expressed in their most general form as:

$$
\alpha p+\beta \frac{\partial p}{\partial n}=\gamma
$$

Classically, $\partial /\partial n$ denotes the normal-derivative operator. $\alpha$ , $\beta$  and $\gamma$ are complex operators, which may be scalars, or integral operators, according to whether the boundary on which the boundary condition is applied is of local reaction or of non-local reaction (the case of fluid–structure interaction).

The developments currently carried out in *Code_Aster*  concern only boundary conditions of local reaction, for which $\alpha$ , $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are scalars; the cases which may be specified are the following:

- The case $\alpha =0$ , $\beta \ne 0$ and $\gamma \ne 0$ denotes a boundary of the domain with imposed vibratory velocity. Indeed, there exists a relation linking the gradient of the complex acoustic pressure to the complex particle normal vibratory velocity. That is:

$$
\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}=-j\omega {\rho}_{0}{V}_{n}
$$

where ${\rho}_{0}$ denotes the density of the fluid considered, and one imposes ${V}_{n}$ , the vibratory velocity normal to the wall (${V}_{n}=v\cdot n$  where $n$ denotes the unit vector of the outward normal to the boundary $\partial \Omega$ ). A sealed wall thus satisfies the natural condition: $\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}=0$ .

- The case $\alpha \ne 0$ , $\beta \ne 0$ and $\gamma =0$ concerns a boundary with imposed acoustic impedance $Z$ . The acoustic impedance $Z$ is defined as the ratio of the pressure to the particle vibratory velocity normal to the neighbourhood of the wall with imposed impedance:
- The case $\alpha \ne 0$ , $\beta =0$ and $\gamma \ne 0$ represents the case in which one imposes the acoustic pressure $p$ on a boundary (most often $\gamma =0$ , corresponding to $p=0$ ).

$$
Z=\frac{p}{{V}_{n}}
$$

- The case $\alpha \ne 0$ , $\beta =0$ and $\gamma \ne 0$ represents the case in which one imposes the acoustic pressure $p$ on a boundary (most often $\gamma =0$ , corresponding to $p=0$ ).

## 3 Formulation in pressure

### 3.1 Mathematical expression of the problem

One uses the standard procedure aimed at posing the problem with classical isoparametric finite elements. One assumes the solution of the problem to be sufficiently regular, $p\in {H}^{2}\left(\Omega \right)$ . One multiplies the Helmholtz equation () without source term $s$ by a test function $\varphi$. One integrates over $\Omega$ and uses Green's formula. According to (), the boundary $\partial \Omega$ of the domain $\Omega$ is subdivided into two zones, a zone with imposed vibratory velocity, $\partial {\Omega}_{v}$ , and a zone with imposed acoustic impedance, $\partial {\Omega}_{Z}$ . The equation obtained may be rewritten in the form:

$$
{\int}_{\Omega}\mathrm{grad}(p)\cdot \mathrm{grad}(\varphi )\mathit{dV}-{\int}_{\Omega}\frac{{\omega}^{2}}{{c}^{2}}p\varphi \mathit{dV}+j{\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{Z}}\frac{{\rho}_{0}\omega }{Z}p\varphi \mathit{dS}+j{\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{v}}{\rho}_{0}\omega {V}_{n}\varphi \mathit{dS}=0
$$

where $\mathit{dV}$ represents a differential volume element in $\Omega$ and $\mathit{dS}$ represents a surface element on $\partial \Omega$ . The particle vibratory velocity is then determined by:

$$
\mathrm{v}=\frac{j}{{\rho}_{0}\omega }\mathrm{grad}(p)
$$

### 3.2 Discretisation by finite elements

In the case of the classical isoparametric finite elements, the elementary integrals are ${\mathrm{K}}^{e}$ , ${\mathrm{M}}^{e}$ , ${\mathrm{C}}^{e}$ and ${\mathrm{S}}^{e}$ according to the decomposition indicated by () (${\mathrm{K}}^{e}$  is the “stiffness” matrix, ${\mathrm{M}}^{e}$ the “mass” matrix, ${C}^{e}$ the damping matrix and ${\mathrm{S}}^{e}$ the source vector). Two of them come from volume integrals, the other two are the result of integrals respectively over a vibrating surface and over a surface with imposed impedance. One will assume that the global coordinates of an element may be written by means of the data of $m$ elementary shape functions ${H}_{i}$ :

$$
{\mathit{OM}}^{e}={\sum}_{i=1}^{m}{N}_{i}{\mathit{OM}}_{i}^{e}
$$

One is given, in addition, basis functions ${N}_{i}$ , in order to describe the elementary acoustic pressure. The pressure inside an element may be written:

$$
{p}^{e}(x,y,z)={\sum}_{i=1}^{m}{N}_{i}{p}_{i}^{e}
$$

Where ${p}_{i}^{e}$ is the acoustic pressure at the node $i$ of the element $e$ . In the case of isoparametric finite elements, the basis functions ${N}_{i}$ are equal to the shape functions ${H}_{i}$ . On each element of the domain, the finite-element problem in acoustic pressure is written:

$$
\left({\mathrm{K}}^{e}-{\omega}^{2}{\mathrm{M}}^{e}+j\omega {\mathrm{C}}^{e}\right){p}^{e}=-j\omega {\mathrm{S}}^{e}
$$

Where ${p}^{e}$ is the column vector of the nodal values of the acoustic pressure on the element.

#### 3.2.1 The “stiffness” matrix

The “stiffness” matrix ${\mathrm{K}}^{e}$ corresponds to the computation of the first term of (), that is:

$$
{\int}_{{\Omega}^{e}}\left(\mathrm{grad}(p)\cdot \mathrm{grad}(\varphi )\right)\mathit{dV}
$$

It corresponds to the kinetic energy of the acoustic fluid. Its general term is:

$$
{K}_{ij}^{e}={\int}_{{\Omega}^{e}}\left(\nabla {N}_{i}\nabla {N}_{j}\right).\mathit{dV}
$$

**Note:**

*because of the choice of pressure as the principal variable and of the second derivative with respect to time,* *the* *first term of () corresponds to*  the kinetic energy *.* *For a practical reason one nevertheless calls it the “stiffness” matrix.*

#### 3.2.2 The “mass” matrix

The “mass” matrix ${\mathrm{M}}^{e}$ corresponds to the computation of the second term of (), that is:

$$
{M}_{ij}^{e}={\int}_{{\Omega}^{e}}\frac{1}{{c}^{2}}p\varphi \mathit{dV}
$$

It corresponds to the compressibility energy of the acoustic fluid. Its general term is:

$$
{M}_{ij}^{e}={\int}_{{\Omega}^{e}}\frac{1}{{c}^{2}}{N}_{i}{N}_{j}.\mathit{dV}
$$

**Note 1:**

*Because of the choice of pressure as the principal variable and of the second derivative with respect to time,* *the second term of () corresponds to*  the *elastic* energy *.* *For a practical reason one nevertheless calls it the “mass” matrix.*

**Note 2:**

*In the case of propagation* *in a hysteretic viscoacoustic medium, the speed of sound* $c$ *which appears in () is a complex number.*

#### 3.2.3 The damping matrix

The damping matrix ${\mathrm{C}}^{e}$ corresponds to the computation of the third term of (), that is:

$$
{C}_{ij}^{e}={\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{Z}^{e}}\frac{{\rho}_{0}}{Z}p\varphi \mathit{dS}
$$

Its general term, on the boundaries concerned, is:

$$
{C}_{ij}^{e}={\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{Z}^{e}}\frac{{\rho}_{0}}{Z}{N}_{i}{N}_{j}\mathit{dS}
$$

#### 3.2.4 The source vector

The source vector ${\mathrm{S}}^{e}$ corresponds to the computation of the last term of (), that is:

$$
{S}_{i}^{e}={\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{v}}{\rho}_{0}{V}_{n}\varphi \mathit{dS}=0
$$

Its general term is:

$$
{S}_{i}^{e}={\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{v}^{e}}{\rho}_{0}{V}_{n}{N}_{i}\mathit{dS}

{S}_{i}^{e}={\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{v}^{e}}{\rho}_{0}{V}_{n}{N}_{i}\mathit{dS}
$$

## 4 Commands specific to acoustic modelling

In a study by modelling with acoustic finite elements with *Code_Aster*  one uses general commands and commands which are specific to acoustics, or whose keywords and options are particular to this discipline; the list of them is presented below.

The nodal values of the acoustic pressure on the finite element (degrees of freedom) are denoted: PRES.

### 4.1 Definition of the characteristics of the propagation media

It is necessary to give the density (real value) and the propagation speed (complex value); one uses for this the command DEFI_MATERIAU
[[U4.43.01]](https://www.code-aster.org/V2/doc/default/fr/man_u/u4/u4.43.01.pdf) with the following keywords:

| factor keyword: | FLUIDE |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| keywords: | RHO | (density ${\rho}_{0}$ ) |
|  | CELE_C | (complex speed ${c}_{0}$ ) |

Example:

> air = DEFI_MATERIAU(FLUIDE=_F(RHO= 1.3, CELE_C = ('RI',343.0,0.,))
> In this case: ${c}_{0}=343.0$  is real (the imaginary part – which would represent a dissipation of material energy – is zero).

### 4.2 Boundary conditions

One must assign values of normal vibratory velocity per face (or per edge in two dimensions) to the mesh cells defining the source boundaries, and also values of acoustic impedance per face (edge in two dimensions) to the mesh cells defining the boundaries with imposed impedance. One uses the command specific to acoustics AFFE_CHAR_ACOU
[[U4.44.04]](https://www.code-aster.org/V2/doc/default/fr/man_u/u4/u4.44.04.pdf)

### 4.3 Computation of the elementary matrices

The various elementary matrices (stiffness, mass and damping) are computed by specific options. One uses the command CALC_MATR_ELEM
[[U4.61.01]](https://www.code-aster.org/V2/doc/default/fr/man_u/u4/u4.61.01.pdf) with the keyword OPTION, for which one specifies the possible assignment values:

These matrices are of complex type.

**Note:**

*The assembled matrices may be obtained directly with the macro-command* *ASSEMBLAGE and the same options.*

### 4.4 Computation of the elementary source vector

The elementary vector is computed by a specific option; the loading must be indicated. One uses the command CALC_VECT_ELEM
[[U4.61.02]](https://www.code-aster.org/V2/doc/default/fr/man_u/u4/u4.61.02.pdf) with the keyword OPTION

### 4.5 Computation of the solution

After assembly of the elementary matrices and vector, the harmonic solution may be computed directly with the command DYNA_VIBRA
[[U4.53.03]](https://www.code-aster.org/V2/doc/default/fr/man_u/u4/u4.53.03.pdf).

One may also compute, with the assembled matrices, the perfect acoustic modes (without viscosity, hence with a real wave speed) on the bounded domain modelled. One must therefore use the real parts of the assembled matrices as input to the modal analysis command
[[U4.52.02]](https://www.code-aster.org/V2/doc/default/fr/man_u/u4/u4.52.02.pdf). The result concept is then of type or 'MODE_ACOU’.

### 4.6 Post-processing

From the result of the solution, post-processing commands make it possible to obtain the nodal fields of the following acoustic quantities:

- reactive acoustic intensity $J=\frac{1}{2}\text{Im}\left[p{v}^{\text{*}}\right]$

## 5 Conclusion

A modelling has therefore been added, making it possible to carry out interior-acoustics analyses at low frequencies for complex geometries by means of classical acoustic finite elements. The formulation has been validated by comparison with an analytical solution; test cases are presented in the Validation manual V7 under the code AHLV100.
