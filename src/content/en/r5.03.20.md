# r5.03.20 Non-linear elastic constitutive relation in large displacements

**Summary:**

The aim here is to describe a non-linear elastic constitutive relation which coincides with the elastoplastic Hencky-VonMises law (isotropic hardening) in the case of a loading which induces a radial and monotonic evolution of the stresses at every point of the structure. This model is chosen in the command STAT_NON_LINE by means of the keyword RELATION='ELAS_VMIS_LINE' or 'ELAS_VMIS_TRAC' under the factor keyword COMPORTEMENT.

This constitutive relation is then extended to large displacements and large rotations, inasmuch as it derives from a potential (hyperelastic law); this feature is chosen by means of the keyword DEFORMATION='GROT_GDEP'. It is available for all 2D and 3D isoparametric elements.

## 1 Non-linear elastic constitutive relation: ELAS_VMIS_LINE and ELAS_VMIS_TRAC

### 1.1 Objective

Within the framework of the global approach in fracture mechanics, the energy release rate can be given a meaning only for hyperelastic constitutive relations, that is, relations which derive from a potential, the free energy. In order nevertheless to be able to treat elastoplastic problems, a non-linear elastic constitutive relation is proposed which leads to results identical to those obtained by the plastic constitutive relation of Hencky-VonMises (isotropic hardening) in the case of a radial and monotonic evolution of the loading at every point. The definition of the material characteristics (keyword DEFI_MATERIAU) is identical to that of isotropic plastic behaviour. For further information on the model, one may refer to [bib1]. In order to illustrate the points in common and the differences between the plastic and elastic models, a tension then compression curve obtained for a one-dimensional bar is presented below.

### 1.2 Constitutive relation

After integration in time of the Hencky-VonMises constitutive relation, formulated in strain rates and stress rates in [R5.03.02](doc:r5.03.02), whose notation is adopted, the expression of the stresses as a function of the strains is:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{ccccc}\sigma =& K(\text{tr}\varepsilon )\text{Id}+& G({\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}})\tilde{\varepsilon}& & \text{éq 1}.2-1\\ -\text{si}& {\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}\le \frac{{\sigma}^{y}}{2\mu }& & & \\ & G=2\mu & \text{et}p=0& & \\ -\text{si}& {\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}>\frac{{\sigma}^{y}}{2\mu }& & & \\ & G=\frac{R(p)}{{\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}}& \text{et}p\text{tel que :}& p+\frac{R(p)}{3\mu }=\frac{2{\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}}{3}& \text{éq 1}.2-2\end{array}$

In a manner similar to plasticity, the hardening function $R(p)$ is deduced from the data supplied by a simple tension test (linear hardening with the keyword ELAS_VMIS_LINE, or else defined by points with the keyword ELAS_VMIS_TRAC, cf. [R5.03.02](doc:r5.03.02)).

As for the variable $p$, it deserves a few moments of attention. In the plastic model, its meaning is clear. It is the cumulative plastic strain, always increasing; it is an internal variable of the model. By contrast, in the elastic case, it no longer has the status of an internal variable, since there is no dissipation. Furthermore, it decreases during unloadings. In fact, its value coincides with that obtained in plasticity so long as the evolution of the loading is radial and monotonic.

Besides the constitutive relation proper, it is necessary to know the value of the free energy for a given state for the computations of the energy release rate. Without proof, this potential, from which the constitutive relation derives, is worth:

| If ${\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}\le \frac{{\sigma}^{y}}{2\mu }$ | $\psi (\varepsilon )=\frac{1}{2}K{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}^{2}+\frac{2\mu }{3}{\varepsilon}_{{\text{eq}}^{2}}$ |
| --- | --- |
| If ${\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}>\frac{{\sigma}^{y}}{2\mu }$ | $\psi (\varepsilon )=\frac{1}{2}K{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}^{2}+\frac{R{(p({\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}))}^{2}}{6\mu }+{\int}_{0}^{p({e}_{\text{eq}})}R(s)\text{ds}$ |

> eq 1.2-3

### 1.3 Solution of the equation in $p$

It could be observed in the previous paragraph that the expression of the stresses requires the solution of an equation in the variable $p$. Inasmuch as the hardening function $R$ is increasing, this equation may be written by gathering the terms in which $p$ appears into the left-hand side (which is then increasing with $p$):

More precisely, the left-hand side is piecewise linear in $p$. To solve the equation, it is then sufficient to run sequentially through each of the intervals until the one in which the solution lies is found. An affine equation then supplies the value of $p$.

### 1.4 Computation of the constitutive relation and tangent stiffness

The computation of the stresses and of the tangent stiffness, that is, the variation of the stresses with respect to the strains, is performed according to the algorithm presented below. Adopting the convention of *Code_Aster*, the stresses and the strains are stored in a vector with six components, while the tangent stiffness is a $6\times 6$ matrix.

$\left\lbrace \varepsilon \right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\varepsilon}_{xx}\\ {\varepsilon}_{yy}\\ {\varepsilon}_{zz}\\ \sqrt{2}{\varepsilon}_{xy}\\ \sqrt{2}{\varepsilon}_{xz}\\ \sqrt{2}{\varepsilon}_{yz}\end{array}\right\rbrace \left\lbrace \sigma \right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{xx}\\ {\sigma}_{yy}\\ {\sigma}_{zz}\\ \sqrt{2}{\sigma}_{xy}\\ \sqrt{2}{\sigma}_{xz}\\ \sqrt{2}{\sigma}_{yz}\end{array}\right\rbrace \left\lbrace 1\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}1\\ 1\\ 1\\ 0\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace$

**Constitutive relation:**

$\left\lbrace \sigma \right\rbrace =K(\text{tr}\varepsilon )\left\lbrace 1\right\rbrace +G\left\lbrace \tilde{\varepsilon}\right\rbrace$

**Tangent stiffness:**

$\frac{d\left\lbrace \sigma \right\rbrace }{d\left\lbrace \varepsilon \right\rbrace }=K=\left[{K}_{1}\right]+\left[{K}_{2}\right]$

- $\left[{K}_{1}\right]=\frac{\mathrm{3K}-G}{3}\left\lbrace 1\right\rbrace \otimes \left\lbrace 1\right\rbrace +G\left[\text{Id}\right]$
- $\left[{K}_{2}\right]=\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\left[0\right]& \text{si}{\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}\le \frac{{\sigma}^{y}}{2\mu }\\ \frac{3}{2{\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}^{2}}\left[\frac{2\mu {R}^{'}(p)}{{R}^{'}(p)+3\mu }-G\right]\left\lbrace \tilde{\varepsilon}\right\rbrace \otimes \left\lbrace \tilde{\varepsilon}\right\rbrace & \text{si}{\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}>\frac{{\sigma}^{y}}{2\mu }\end{array}$

### 1.5 Taking thermal strains into account

In a manner identical to plasticity, the total strain is split into a mechanical part which satisfies the previous constitutive relation [eq 1.2-1], [eq 1.2-2] and a thermal part, a function of the temperature. Note furthermore that the various characteristics of the material may also depend on the temperature.

$\varepsilon ={\varepsilon}^{m}+{\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}$

$\text{avec}\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\sigma =K(\text{tr}{\varepsilon}^{m})\mathrm{Id}+G({\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}})\tilde{\varepsilon}& \text{éq 1.5.1}\\ {\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}=\alpha (T-{T}_{\text{réf}})\text{Id}& \end{array}$

$\alpha$: thermal expansion coefficient

${T}_{\text{ref}}$: reference temperature

It remains to complete the free-energy potential [eq 1.2-3] in order to include the temperature in it. Several choices are possible, depending on the way in which one wishes to define the entropy (derivative of the free energy with respect to the temperature). In our case, the potential adopted is:

| If ${\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}\le \frac{{\sigma}^{y}}{2\mu }$ | $\psi (\varepsilon ,T)=\frac{1}{2}K{(\text{Tr}(\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}))}^{2}+\frac{2\mu }{3}{\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}^{2}$ |
| --- | --- |
| If ${\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}>\frac{{\sigma}^{y}}{2\mu }$ | $\psi (\varepsilon ,T)=\frac{1}{2}K{(\text{Tr}(\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}))}^{2}+\frac{R{(p({\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}))}^{2}}{6\mu }+{\int}_{0}^{p({\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}})}R(s)\text{ds}$ |

### 1.6 Particular treatment of plane stresses

Usually, one seeks to determine the stresses knowing the strains and the temperature. However, this is no longer quite the case under the plane-stress assumption, inasmuch as three of the components of the strain tensor are henceforth unknown, the dual quantities being fixed:

| ${\varepsilon}_{xz}$, ${\varepsilon}_{yz}$ and ${\varepsilon}_{zz}$ | unknown |
| --- | --- |
| ${\sigma}_{xz}={\sigma}_{yz}={\sigma}_{zz}$ | $=0$ |

These unknown components must therefore first be determined. The method adopted is set out in [bib1] and [R5.03.02](doc:r5.03.02). It may however be recalled here that the components $xz$ and $yz$ pose no problem, given the form of the constitutive relation [eq 1.2-1]:

${\varepsilon}_{xz}={\varepsilon}_{yz}=0$

On the other hand, the determination of the component $zz$ requires the (numerical) solution of a non-linear scalar equation.

Finally, one last warning is called for. Unlike plane strains, the solutions obtained under the plane-stress assumption are generally not exact, inasmuch as they do not satisfy the conditions of geometric compatibility (integrability of the strain field). They are only approximate solutions.

## 2 Elasticity in large transformations

### 2.1 Objective

Henceforth, the aim is to take into account large displacements and large rotations, a feature accessible through the keyword DEFORMATION='GROT_GDEP' in the command STAT_NON_LINE. Let it be stated at once that one restricts oneself to isoparametric finite elements (D_PLAN, C_PLAN, AXIS and 3D) for which the discretisation of the continuous problem poses no particular difficulty, cf. [R3.01.00].

To this end, it is accepted that the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor, $S$, derives from the Hencky-VonMises potential expressed by means of the Green-Lagrange strain $E$:

$\mathrm{S}=\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial E}\left(\mathrm{E}\right)$

Let us also recall the definitions of $E$ and $S$. Further information may also be found in [bib1].

$\begin{array}{}F=\text{Id}+\text{Grad}(u)\text{}E=\frac{1}{2}({}^{T}\text{}\text{FF}-\text{Id})\\ S=\text{Det}(F){F}^{-1}\sigma {}^{T}\text{}{F}^{-1}\end{array}$

Such a constitutive relation, said to be hyperelastic, makes it possible in all rigour to take large strains and large rotations into account. However, we limit ourselves to small strains, and this for two reasons. First, the constitutive relation adopted does not possess the right properties (polyconvexity) to ensure the existence of solutions, and nor does it control large compressions. Second, plastic behaviour differs markedly from a hyperelastic behaviour as soon as the strains become appreciable. It is for these reasons that we have chosen to retain the small-strain assumption, thus avoiding the controversy over large strains.

### 2.2 Virtual work of the external forces: dead-load assumption

In order to treat the problem of the analysis of hyperelastic structures, one seeks to write equilibrium in variational form on the initial configuration. In particular, the virtual work of the external forces must be expressed on this same initial configuration, which requires the additional assumption of dead loads: it is assumed that the loading does not depend on the geometric transformation. Typically, an imposed force is a dead load, whereas pressure is a follower loading, since it depends on the orientation of the face of application, hence on the transformation. Under this assumption, the virtual work of the external forces is written as a linear form:

${\mathit{dW}}_{\text{ext}}.\delta \mathrm{v}=\underset{{\Omega}_{o}}{\int}{\rho}_{o}{F}_{i}\delta {v}_{i}d{\Omega}_{o}+\underset{{\partial}_{F}{\Omega}_{o}}{\int}{T}_{i}^{d}\delta {v}_{i}{\text{dS}}_{o}$

$F$: volumetric loading

${T}^{d}$: surface loading acting on the boundary ${\partial}_{F}{\Omega}_{o}$

### 2.3 Virtual work of the internal forces
