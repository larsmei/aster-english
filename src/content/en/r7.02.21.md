# 1 **r7.02.21** Cohesive constitutive law

# 2 **r7.02.21** dependent on the loading mixity: CZM_TURON

**Summary:**

The cohesive constitutive law CZM_TURON makes it possible to model the damage of a cracking interface such that the opening of the crack generates cohesive forces between the faces of the latter.

It relates the cohesive forces, homogeneous to a stress, to the displacement discontinuity across the interface according to a bilinear law, which depends on the mixity ratio of the loading: an interface subjected to a mixed loading will damage earlier than in pure mode.

This model was developed by the team of Turon of the University of Girona (Spain) [ 1 ]. It is for example used in the framework of the modelling of bonded joints in composite material in the blades of wind turbines.

The law is supported by joint finite elements whose degrees of freedom are the displacement jumps across the interface (see R3.06.13).

We present here the formulation of the cohesive law as well as the internal variables of the model. The existence of instabilities during crack propagation requires a control by the elastic prediction, whose elements specific to these laws are presented.

## 1 Generalities on the cohesive laws

In brittle fracture, in order to remove the problem of infinite stresses at the crack tip, one can introduce cohesive forces that impose an initiation criterion in stress [ 2 ]. The forces are exerted between the particles on either side of the separation plane of the crack (see Figure ). Historically, one of the first to propose this type of approach is Barenblatt [ 3 ] at the beginning of the 1960s. Based on this idea, numerous models have appeared in the literature under the name: "Cohesive zone models" or CZM in English. For a more complete bibliography one may refer to [ 4 ].

$\underset{u}{\min}(\Phi +\Psi +{W}^{\mathrm{ext}})$

## 2 Definition of the law CZM_TURON

### 2.1 Definitions

Let us begin by introducing the parameters of the model. One has:

- the critical surface-energy densities in pure normal mode and in pure tangential mode, denoted ${G}_{c,N}$ (keyword GC_N) and ${G}_{c,T}$ (keyword GC_T) which express the energy cost of cracking in each of the two modes;
- the critical stresses at rupture denoted ${\sigma}_{N}^{c}$ (keyword SIGM_C_N) and ${\sigma}_{T}^{c}$ (keyword SIGM_C_T);
- the initial stiffness of the interface $K$ (keyword K);
- the parameter

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.21/Shape2.gif)

of the Benzeggagh-Kenane criterion (keyword ETA_BK);

- the dimensionless parameter C_RUPT which makes it possible to define a residual "post-rupture" stiffness (when the element is completely damaged and the cohesive stress is zero), as a fraction of the initial stiffness. It is equal to 0.001 by default and cannot exceed 0.1;
- the parameter CRIT_INIT which makes it possible to choose the type of damage-initiation criterion, among 'YE' (elliptic criterion) or 'TURON' (Benzeggagh-Kenane type criterion).

The material parameters proper, namely GC_N, GC_T, SIGM_C_N, SIGM_C_T, K and ETA_BK, are to be identified from standard quasi-static tests. For example, in the framework of the simulation of a bonded joint, the adhesive being modelled with the cohesive-zone model, the parameters will be identified by carrying out the following tests on "sandwich" specimens:

- the DCB test ("Double Cantilever Beam"), which loads the joint in pure normal mode, makes it possible to identify the parameters GC_N and SIGM_C_N;
- the ENF test ("End-notched flexure"), corresponding to 3-point bending, loads the joint in pure tangent mode and makes it possible to identify the parameters GC_T and SIGM_C_T;
- the MMB test ("Mixed-mode bending") is a combination of the two previous tests and makes it possible to subject the joint to a mixed loading, the mixity ratio being in the hands of the experimenter, who can adjust the device. In addition to the previous tests, it is used to calibrate the parameter ETA_BK.

As regards the parameter $K$ , it is generally observed that it influences little the simulated global response and that it is therefore difficult to identify it by adjustment of the numerical and experimental curves. In practice, it is often assumed *ad hoc*.

**Note:**

> *The following notations are adopted: for every vector* $x$ *one denotes:*
> $x={x}_{N}N+{x}_{{T}_{1}}{T}_{1}+{x}_{{T}_{2}}{T}_{2}$
> ${x}_{T}={x}_{{T}_{1}}{T}_{1}+{x}_{{T}_{2}}{T}_{2}$
> $⟨x⟩=⟨{x}_{N}⟩N+{x}_{{T}_{1}}{T}_{1}+{x}_{{T}_{2}}{T}_{2}$ *with*     $⟨{x}_{i}⟩=max({x}_{i},0)$
> $\Vert x\Vert =\sqrt{x.x}$ *and*     ${\Vert x\Vert }_{+}=\sqrt{⟨x⟩.⟨x⟩}$

In particular, one will denote $\lambda ={\Vert \Delta \Vert }_{+}$ the equivalent displacement of the cohesive element and ${\lambda}_{T}=\Vert {\Delta}_{T}\Vert$ the norm of the tangential jump.

Finally one calls:

- ${\Delta}^{0}$ the value that the equivalent jump must reach for the damage to initiate. It is therefore called the damage-initiation jump;
- ${\Delta}^{f}$ the value that the equivalent jump must reach for the element to break. It is therefore called the crack-propagation jump (but this name must not mislead the reader: modellings with a CZM law do not present a crack proper, for example directly meshed or else defined by level sets).

The initial stiffness $K$ makes it possible to regularise the free energy at 0. The present law is therefore a law of regularised type. Note that in the case where the interface is supposed to be infinitely stiff, for example when the interface models a cracking path in a brittle material, then the initial stiffness must be small enough for the energy to be well regularised, but this can in return generate a global softening of the structure that is not physical.

In the framework of the modelling of an interface possessing a certain "compliance" that is physical (for example a layer of adhesive between two adherends that will deform elastically before starting to damage), this problem does not arise.

It should be recalled that there must not be too great a difference in stiffness between the cohesive elements of type JOINT and the directly neighbouring elements (generally solid), at the risk of encountering conditioning problems of the stiffness matrix of the system, specific to this type of elements [ 5 ]. In practice, one can check that the parameter $K$ and the quantity $\frac{E}{{h}_{\mathit{elem}}}$ are of the same order of magnitude, $E$ being the Young modulus of the adherend and ${h}_{\mathit{elem}}$ being the size of the mesh cells adjacent to the joint elements. One can, to a certain extent, adapt the size of the adjacent mesh cells. But if the conditioning problems cannot be removed given too great a difference between the material properties of the cohesive interface and of the adherend, one will rather turn towards elements of type INTERFACE [ 5 ,  6 ], on which certain CZM laws are available [ 7 ].

### 2.2 Constitutive equations

In this part one presents the surface energy $\Psi$ as well as the stress vector which

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.21/Shape3.gif)

is derived from it. The formulation proposed ensures the irreversibility of the damage.

#### 2.2.1 Free surface energy

The free energy per unit area $\Psi$ , defined on a discontinuity $\Gamma$ , depends on the displacement-jump vector between the faces of the crack $\Delta$ (denoted $\Delta$ in order to lighten the writing) and on an internal variable that manages the irreversibility of the cracking.

One first defines the variable $v$ which stores the largest norm of the jump (equivalent displacement) reached during the opening. Its evolution law between two successive loading increments - and + is written:

${v}^{+}=max({\lambda}_{+},{v}^{-})$

One also defines the threshold variable denoted $r$ which gives the minimum jump to be reached in order to (re)start damaging:

${r}^{+}=max({\Delta}^{0,}{v}^{-})$

Let us finally define the damage variable $d$, whose evolution law will be given in what follows. The element is sound when $d=0$ and completely damaged, broken, when $d=1$ .

One notes that from the thermodynamic point of view, the variable $d$ used here is the internal variable of the constitutive behaviour. On the other hand, from the point of view of the numerical implementation, the variable $v$ will be used as internal variable, the other variables $r$ and $d$ being computed from $v$ .

The following free energy per unit area is given:

$$
\Psi (\Delta ,d)=(1-d){\Psi}^{0}(\Delta )
$$

with ${\Psi}^{0}(\Delta )=\frac{1}{2}{\Delta}_{i}{D}_{ij}^{0}{\Delta}_{j}$         for    $i,j=1,3$

${\Psi}^{0}$ being a convex function in the space of the displacement jumps.

The negative values of ${\Delta}_{N}$ have no meaning because the interpenetration of the faces is made impossible by the contact. In order to prevent interpenetration after complete decohesion, the modification of the equation according to the following form is proposed:

$\Psi (\Delta ,d)=(1-d){\Psi}^{0}(\Delta )-d{\Psi}^{0}({\delta}_{Ni}⟨-{\Delta}_{N}⟩)$

where ${\delta}_{ij}$ is the Kronecker symbol.

#### 2.2.2 Expression of the stress

The constitutive equations are obtained by differentiating the free energy with respect to the displacement jumps:

$$
{\tau}_{i}=\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial {\Delta}_{i}}=(1-d){D}_{ij}^{0}{\Delta}_{j}-d{D}_{ij}^{0}{\delta}_{\mathit{Nj}}⟨-{\Delta}_{N}⟩
$$

where ${D}_{ij}^{0}={\delta}_{ij}K$ is the initial stiffness tensor, with $K$ the penalisation stiffness.

Assuming that $N\equiv 1$ and that $({T}_{1,}{T}_{2})\equiv (2,3)$ , the equation can be written in vector form:

$\tau =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\tau}_{1}\\ {\tau}_{2}\\ {\tau}_{3}\end{array}\right\rbrace =(1-d)K\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\Delta}_{1}\\ {\Delta}_{2}\\ {\Delta}_{3}\end{array}\right\rbrace -dK\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}⟨-{\Delta}_{1}⟩\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace$

The energy dissipated during the evolution of the damage is denoted $\Xi$ and is obtained by differentiating the free energy with respect to the internal damage variable:

$\Xi =-\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial d}\dot{d}$

The constitutive model given in the equation is well evaluated if the damage variable $d$ can be computed at each time instant of the strain process, which is possible by giving oneself an initiation criterion and an evolution law for the damage.

#### 2.2.3 Evolution of the damage

The evolution of the damage is formulated in the form of a threshold function in the space of the displacement jumps. It is written, at each time instant $t$ :

$F({\lambda}^{t},{r}^{t}):={\lambda}^{t}-{r}^{t}\le 0$     $\forall t\ge 0$

where ${r}^{t}$ is the damage threshold at the current time instant.

If one denotes ${r}^{0}$ the initial damage threshold then one must have ${r}^{t}\ge {r}^{0}$ at each time instant. The damage threshold can only increase. The damage initiates when the norm of the displacement jump $\lambda$ exceeds the initial threshold :math:`{r}^{0}$ , which is computed in particular from the material characteristics.

In the present formulation, an equivalent expression easier to treat from the algorithmic point of view will be used:

$\overline{F}({\lambda}^{t},{r}^{t}):=G({\lambda}^{t})-G({r}^{t})\le 0$     $\forall t\ge 0$

where $G$ is a monotonic scalar function going from 0 to 1. The evolution of the threshold and of the damage are given by their increments:

$\dot{r}=\dot{\mu}$

$\dot{d}=\dot{\mu}\frac{\partial \overline{F}(\lambda ,r)}{\partial \lambda }=\dot{\mu}\frac{\partial G(\lambda )}{\partial \lambda }$

the variable $\mu$ being the Lagrange multiplier that makes it possible to define the loading–unloading conditions according to the Kuhn and Tucker relations:

$\dot{\mu}\ge 0$ (a); $\overline{F}({\lambda}^{t},{r}^{t})\le 0$ (b); $\dot{\mu}\overline{F}({\lambda}^{t},{r}^{t})=0$ (c)

For pedagogical reasons, the integration — very classical — of the Kuhn and Tucker relations will be carried out explicitly. For this purpose, the two "phases" are separated:

*Phase A* *(non-damaging)* *:*

As long as ${\lambda}^{t}<{r}^{t}$ , one has $G({\lambda}^{t})<G({r}^{t})$ by increase of the function $G$ , and therefore $\overline{F}({\lambda}^{t},{r}^{t})<0$ (consistent with (b)).

By (c), one necessarily has $\dot{\mu}=\dot{r}=0$ . Therefore $r$ remains constant, equal to its value at the beginning of the phase, in particular to ${r}^{0}$ if it is the initial time instant.

*Phase B* (damaging) *:*

If one looks at the other expression of (b), where $\overline{F}({\lambda}^{t},{r}^{t})=0$ , then ${\lambda}^{t}={r}^{t}$ (one cannot have ${\lambda}^{t}>{r}^{t}$ otherwise (b) is no longer satisfied). Let us set aside the case where $\dot{\mu}=0$ corresponding to the case where $\lambda$ does not evolve. Then $\dot{\mu}>0$ according to (a) and therefore $r$ increases, at the same time as $\lambda$ . Moreover, one can write that $\dot{d}=\dot{r}\frac{\partial G(r)}{\partial r}$ whence the direct integration of $d$ .

The two phases can alternate according to the loading path, and one can therefore integrate the threshold variable $r$ and the damage variable $d$ in the following manner:

${r}^{t}=max({r}^{0},{max}_{s}\lambda )$ $0\le s\le t$

${d}^{t}=G({r}^{t})$

In what follows one will denote ${r}^{0}={\Delta}^{0}$ the damage threshold.

The scalar function $G$ defines the evolution of the damage variable. For a given mixity ratio, a function of the following form is proposed:

$G(\lambda )=\frac{{\Delta}^{f}(\lambda -{\Delta}^{0})}{\lambda ({\Delta}^{f}-{\Delta}^{0})}$

which corresponds to a constitutive equation between the equivalent stress and jump of bilinear form, with ${\Delta}^{0}$ the displacement jump at the initiation of the damage, hence ${\Delta}^{0}={r}^{0}$ , and ${\Delta}^{f}$ the final displacement jump when the damage reaches its maximum value of 1.

One can easily write the equation of the straight lines in the two regimes starting from the equation written with the equivalent stress and jump $\tau =K(1-d)\lambda =K(1-G(r))\lambda$ :

- in the elastic regime (non-damaging phase), with $\lambda <r$ , one has $\tau =K\frac{{\Delta}^{0}({\Delta}^{f}-r)}{r({\Delta}^{f}-{\Delta}^{0})}\cdot \lambda ` , which does indeed correspond to the equation of a straight line since :math:`r$ does not evolve. In particular, $\tau =K\cdot \lambda ` when :math:`r={\Delta}^{0}$ ;
- in the dissipative regime (damaging phase), with $r=\lambda ` , one has $\tau =Kfrac{{\Delta}^{0}{\Delta}^{f}}{{\Delta}^{f}-{\Delta}^{0}}-Kfrac{{\Delta}^{0}}{{\Delta}^{f}-{\Delta}^{0}}\cdot \lambda$ which is indeed the equation of a decreasing affine straight line.

The bilinear law of this type can be visualised in the Figure.

The displacement jumps at initiation and at propagation are obtained from the corresponding threshold surfaces, which therefore remain to be defined in order to complete the formulation of the constitutive equations.

In the present model, these criteria take into account the interaction between the normal and tangential loading modes. They therefore depend on the mixity ratio of the loading defined in the following manner:

$\beta =\frac{{\Delta}_{T}}{⟨{\Delta}_{N}⟩+{\Delta}_{T}}$

#### 2.2.4 Crack-propagation criterion (threshold surface for $d=1$)

The proposed mixed-mode "crack" propagation criterion is established from the critical energy-release rate. For systems of composite-joint type, a common criterion is that of Benzeggagh-Kenane, coming from the field of fracture mechanics:

${G}_{c}={G}_{\mathit{Nc}}+({G}_{T,c}-{G}_{N,c}){\left(\frac{{G}_{T}}{{G}_{\mathit{tot}}}\right)}^{\eta}$

where ${G}_{\mathit{tot}}={G}_{N}+{G}_{T}$ .

It is specified that the criterion, written in the framework of fracture mechanics, involves the quantities ${G}_{I,c}$ , ${G}_{\mathit{II},c}$ and ${G}_{\mathit{III},c}$ which are the critical energies according to the three classical loading modes of a crack. But in the framework of an interface modelled by a CZM interface law, one can only describe

${G}_{\mathit{II},c}={G}_{\mathit{III},c}={G}_{T,c}$

since the definition of what are commonly called modes II and III is made from the crack front. Now here, we do not model a crack but a damaging interface, for which one can at best define a normal direction and a tangential direction.

This criterion can be rewritten from the displacement jumps by noting that the critical energy, corresponding to the area under the bilinear curve of the Figure, is written:

${G}_{c}=\frac{1}{2}K{\Delta}^{0}{\Delta}^{f}$

which gives

${\Delta}^{f}=\frac{{\Delta}_{N}^{0}{\Delta}_{N}^{f}+({\Delta}_{T}^{0}{\Delta}_{T}^{f}-{\Delta}_{N}^{0}{\Delta}_{N}^{f}){\left(\frac{{G}_{T}}{{G}_{\mathit{tot}}}\right)}^{\eta}}{{\Delta}^{0}}$

with ${\Delta}_{N}^{0}$ and ${\Delta}_{T}^{0}$ the damage-initiation criteria, and ${\Delta}_{N}^{f}$ and ${\Delta}_{T}^{f}$ the propagation criteria in pure modes, computed from the material data for each mode given the bilinear form of the law (see figure):

${\Delta}_{N/T}^{0}=\frac{{\sigma}_{c,N/T}}{K}$ and ${\Delta}_{N/T}^{f}=\frac{2{G}_{c,N/T}}{{\sigma}_{c,N/T}}$

It will be noted that the mixity ratio can be measured by another quantity $B$ from the energy-release rates:

$B=\frac{{G}_{T}}{{G}_{\mathit{tot}}}$

The two definitions are related by the following formula, as is demonstrated in [ 1 ]:

$B=\frac{{\beta}^{2}}{1+2{\beta}^{2}-2\beta }$

#### 2.2.5 Damage-initiation criterion (threshold surface for $d=0$)

When the loading is in pure mode N or T, the damage is initiated when the cohesive forces reach their respective critical value ${\sigma}_{c,N/T}$ (critical stresses). Under a mixed loading, an interaction between the modes must be taken into account. There exist different models defining the initiation criterion as a function of the mixity ratio. The present constitutive law proposes the Benzeggagh–Kenane model (which is therefore used both for the initiation criterion and for the propagation criterion — and this with the same power $\eta$). This criterion is designated by "Turon criterion", the fact of adopting the same mode-coupling equation for initiation and propagation being a choice made by the authors of [1]. Another purely elliptic criterion, designated by "Ye criterion", is also proposed.

- The elliptic criterion of Ye

It is a simple and rather widespread criterion. It is based on a quadratic interaction of the critical stresses:

${\left(\frac{⟨{\sigma}_{N}⟩}{{\sigma}_{c,N}}\right)}^{2}+{\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{{T}_{1}}}{{\sigma}_{c,T}}\right)}^{2}+{\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{{T}_{2}}}{{\sigma}_{c,T}}\right)}^{2}=1$

which is written equivalently in terms of displacement jump:

${\left(\frac{⟨{\Delta}_{N}⟩}{{\Delta}_{N}^{0}}\right)}^{2}+{\left(\frac{{\Delta}_{{T}_{1}}}{{\Delta}_{T}^{0}}\right)}^{2}+{\left(\frac{{\Delta}_{{T}_{2}}}{{\Delta}_{T}^{0}}\right)}^{2}=1$

One therefore indeed has an elliptic criterion in the general anisotropic case where the critical stresses and the associated displacement jumps are different for the normal and tangent modes.

This criterion is accessible via the value of the keyword CRIT_INIT = ‘YE’ in DEFI_MATERIAU / RUPT_TURON.

The criterion proposed in the law CZM_LIN_REG is a particular case of this criterion, and corresponds to the case where the joint is isotropic, that is to say when the normal and tangent properties are equal.

- The Turon criterion

It is a Benzeggagh–Kenane criterion for initiation. Although it is difficult to have experimental data validating the choice of one or the other of the two criteria mentioned, the idea here is to formulate the initiation and propagation threshold surfaces in a dependent manner. It is written:

${\sigma}_{c}^{2}={\sigma}_{c,N}^{2}+\left({\sigma}_{c,T}^{2}-{\sigma}_{c,N}^{2}\right){B}^{\eta}$

and, equivalently:

${({\Delta}^{0})}^{2}={({\Delta}_{N}^{0})}^{2}+\left({({\Delta}_{T}^{0})}^{2}-{({\Delta}_{N}^{0})}^{2}\right){B}^{\eta}$

This criterion is accessible via the value of the keyword CRIT_INIT = ‘TURON’ in DEFI_MATERIAU.

## 3 Control of the loading by elastic prediction

The control of the loading is available for the cohesive constitutive laws. Of elastic-prediction type: PRED_ELAS (see [R5.03.80](doc:r5.03.80)), it makes it possible to follow dissipative solutions presenting instabilities. This technique is described precisely in the documentation [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11) concerning other types of cohesive laws available in code_aster. One may also refer to [ 4 ] for further details.

## 4 Keywords

The constitutive law CZM_TURON can be used in STAT_NON_LINE and DYNA_NON_LINE. It is usable on 2D or 3D joint elements with the modellings: PLAN_JOINT, AXIS_JOINT or 3D_JOINT.

Four parameters are to be entered in DEFI_MATERIAU under the keywords RUPT_TURON:

GC_N: critical surface-energy density in pure mode N (Pressure x Length)

GC_T: critical surface-energy density in pure mode T (Pressure x Length)

SIGM_C_N: critical stress in pure mode N (Pressure)

SIGM_C_T: critical stress in pure mode T (Pressure)

K: initial stiffness of the interface (Pressure x Length -1)

ETA_BK: power parameter in the Benzeggagh-Kenane law

C_RUPT: residual post-rupture stiffness parameter (dimensionless, equal to 0.001 by default and 0.1 at most)

CRIT_INIT: parameter for selection of the damage-initiation criterion (equal to ‘YE’ or ‘TURON’)

**Commands**

**STAT_NON_LINE**         COMPORTEMENT/RELATION    CZM_TURON

**DYNA_NON_LINE**         COMPORTEMENT/RELATION    CZM_TURON

**AFFE_MODELE**         MODELISATION                PLAN_JOINT
