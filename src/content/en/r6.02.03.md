# r6.02.03 General information on direct linear solvers and use of MUMPS

**Summary**

In the context of thermo-mechanical simulations with *Code_Aster*, **most of the calculation costs often come from the construction and solution of linear systems**. To perform these solutions more efficiently, **Code_Aster has chosen to integrate the direct method deployed in the MUMPS** package (‘Multifrontal Massively Parallel sparse direct Solver; P.R.Amestoy, J.Y.L’Excellent et al; CERFACS/CNRS/ENS Lyon/IRIT/INRIA/Université de Bordeaux). This is in addition to its “in-house” multifrontal (C.Rose) and its other solvers: ‘LDLT’, ‘GCPC’ and ‘PETSC’.

In distributed parallel mode and in Out-Of-Core, the *Aster* +MUMPS coupling provides **CPU gains of the order of a dozen** on 32 processors. And this, for **RAM consumption**, **behaviour/functional scope** and **precision of results** at least as good as **those of the native multifrontal** of the code.

On large problems, by activating the BLR compressions, one can even gain a further factor of two or three. This product, *via* the parallelism it exhibits and its advanced features (pivoting, pre/post-processing, solution quality, …) greatly eases the running of standard studies. Moreover, it often remains the **only viable alternative** for exploiting certain modellings/analyses (quasi-incompressible, X-FEM, …) or for running very large studies (as an accurate direct solver or as a preconditioner, cf. option 'LDLT_SP' of 'GCPC'/'PETSC').

**In the first part of the document** we summarize the general problem of solving linear systems, then we approach the large families of sparse direct solvers and their variations in public domain libraries.

All things to keep in mind before tackling, **in the second part**, the MUMPS package through its main characteristics and advanced functionalities. Then we detail the numerical, IT and functional aspects of its integration into *Code_Aster*. Finally, we conclude with some numerical results.

**For more details and advice** on the use of linear solvers, you can consult the specific user instructions [U4.50.01](doc:u4.50.01)/[U2.08.03]. The related issues of improving performance (RAM/CPU) of a calculation and the use of parallelism are also the subject of detailed notices: [U1.03.03](doc:u1.03.03) and [U2.08.06](doc:u2.08.06).

## 1 General information about direct solvers

### 1.1 Linear system and associated solution methods

In numerical simulation of physical phenomena, **a significant computational cost often comes from the construction and solution of linear systems**. Structural mechanics is no exception to the rule! The cost of building the system depends on the number of integration points and the complexity of the constitutive laws, while that of the resolution is linked to the number of unknowns, the models retained and the topology (bandwidth, conditioning). When the number of unknowns explodes, the second step becomes preponderant 1
and it is therefore the latter which will mainly interest us here. Moreover, when it is possible to be more efficient in this resolution phase, thanks to access to a parallel machine, we will see that this advantage can be propagated to the phase of constitution of the system itself (elementary calculations and assemblies).

These **inversions of linear systems are in fact omnipresent in codes and often buried deep within other numerical algorithms**: non-linear diagram, time integration, modal analysis, etc. We seek, for example, the vector of nodal displacements (or displacement increments) $u$ verifying a linear system of the type

$\mathrm{Ku}=\mathrm{f}$                                         (1.1-1)

with $K$ a rigidity matrix and $\mathrm{f}$ a vector reflecting the application of generalized forces to the mechanical system.

Generally speaking, **resolving this type of problem requires (more) broad questioning** than it seems:

- Do we have access to the matrix or do we simply know its action on a vector?
- Is this matrix hollow or dense?
- What are its numerical properties (symmetry, positive definite, etc.) and structural properties (real/complex, in bands, in blocks, etc.)?
- Do we want to solve a single system (1.1-1), several simultaneously 2 or consecutively 3? Or even several different and successive systems whose matrices are very close 4?
- In the case of successive resolutions, can we reuse previous results in order to facilitate future resolutions (see restart technique, partial factorisation)?
- What is the order of magnitude of the size of the problem, of the matrix and its factorisation in relation to the processing capacities of the CPUs and associated memories (RAM, disk)?
- Do we want a very precise solution or simply an estimate (see nested solvers)?
- Do we have access to linear algebra libraries (and their MPI, BLAS, LAPACK, etc. prerequisites) or should we use “in-house” products?

**In** **Code_Aster**, we explicitly construct the matrix and store it in MORSE 5 format
. With most modelling, the matrix is sparse (due to finite element discretisation), potentially poorly conditioned 6

and often real, symmetrical and indefinite 7
. In nonlinear, modal or thermo-mechanical chaining, we often deal with “multiple second members” type problems. Discrete contact-friction methods take advantage of partial factorisation capabilities and the domain decomposition method. On the other hand, *Code_Aster* also uses simultaneous resolution scenarios (Schur complements of contact and substructuration, etc.).

As for the sizes of the problems, even if they increase from year to year, they remain modest compared to CFD: of the order of a million unknowns but for hundreds of time steps or iterations of Newton.

Furthermore, from a **middleware and hardware” point of view**, the code now relies on numerous optimised and long-lasting libraries (MPI, BLAS, LAPACK, (PAR)METIS, (PT)SCOTCH, PETSc, MUMPS, etc.) and is mainly used on SMP clusters (fast networks, large RAM and disk storage capacity). We therefore mainly seek to optimise the use of linear solvers with this in mind.

**For 60 years, two types of techniques have been competing for supremacy in the field, direct solvers and iterative solvers** (cf. [Che05][Dav03][Duf06][Gol96][Las98][Liu89][Meu99][Saa03]).

**The first** are robust and result in a finite number of operations (theoretically) known in advance. Their theory is relatively well completed and their variation according to many types of matrices and software architectures is very complete. In particular, their multi-level algorithm is well suited to the memory hierarchies of current machines. However, they require storage capacities which increase rapidly with the size of the problem, which limits the scalability of their parallelism 8
. Even if this parallelism can be broken down into several independent layers, thus increasing performance.

On the other hand, **iterative methods** are more “scalable” when increasing the number of processors. Their theory is full of many "open problems", especially in finite arithmetic. In practice, their convergence in a “reasonable” number of iterations is not always acquired, it depends on the structure of the matrix, the starting point, the stopping criterion… This type of solver has more difficulty breaking through into the mechanics of industrial structures where we often accumulate heterogeneities, non-linearities and junctions of models which corrupt the conditioning of the working operator. On the other hand, they are not suitable for efficiently solving “multiple second members” type problems. Except these are very common in the algorithms of mechanical simulations.

Unlike their direct counterparts, it is not possible to propose THE iterative solver that will solve any linear system. The suitability of the type of algorithm to a class of problems is done on a case-by-case basis. They nevertheless present other advantages which historically have given them the right to use for certain applications. With equivalent memory management, they require less than direct solvers, because we just need to know the action of the matrix on any vector, without having to actually store it. On the other hand, we are not subject to the “diktat” of the filling phenomenon which deteriorates the profile of the matrices, we can effectively exploit the sparse nature of the operators and control the precision of the results 9
. **In short, the use of direct solvers is more of a technical matter while choosing the right iterative method/preconditioner pair is more of an art!** Despite its biblical simplicity on paper, the solution of a linear system, even a positive definite symmetric one, is not “a long, quiet river”. Between two evils, filling/pivoting and preconditioning, you have to choose!

**Notes:**

- A third class of methods tries to take advantage of the respective advantages of direct and iterative methods: Domain Decomposition methods.
- The two main families of methods should be seen more as complementary than as competitors. We often try to mix them: DD methods, preconditioner by incomplete factorisation (see [R6.01.02](doc:r6.01.02) *§4.2) or multigrid type, iterative refinement procedure at the end of the direct solver…*

### 1.2 Linear algebra libraries

To effectively solve a linear system, **the question of using a library or an external product is now unavoidable**. Why? Because this strategy allows:

- Less technical, less invasive and much faster developments in the host code.
- To acquire, at a lower cost, a wide scope of use while outsourcing many of the associated contingencies (typology of the problem cf. §1.1, data representation, architecture of the target machine, etc.).
- To benefit from feedback from a varied community of users and the (very) advanced skills of international teams.

**These libraries often combine efficiency, reliability, performance and portability**:

- Efficiency because they exploit the spatial and temporal locality of the data and play on the memory hierarchy (example of the different BLAS categories).
- Reliability because they offer tools to estimate the error made on the solution (estimation of conditioning and ‘backward/forward errors’) or even to improve it (for direct, balancing of the matrix and iterative refinement).

Let us cite non-exhaustively: ScaLAPACK (Dongarra & Demmel 1997), SparseKIT (Saad 1988), PETSc (Argonne 1991), HyPre (LL 2000), TRILINOS (Sandia 2000)…

### 1.3 Direct methods: the principle

The basic idea of direct methods is to **decompose the problem matrix** $K$ **into a product of particular matrices** (lower and upper triangular, diagonal) that are easier to “invert”. This is called **factoring** [12]_
of the working matrix:

- *If* $K$ *is SPD*, it admits the unique “Cholesky factorisation”: $K=L{L}^{T}$ with lower triangular $L$;
- *If* $K$ *is any symmetric and regular*, it admits at least one “factorisation $LD{L}^{T}$ *”*: $PK=LD{L}^{T}$ with $L$ lower triangular with diagonal coefficients equal to unity, $D$ a diagonal matrix [13]_ and $P$ a permutation matrix;
- *If* $K$ *is arbitrary and regular*, it admits at least one “$LU$ factorisation”: $PK=LU$ with $L$ lower triangular than unit diagonal, $U$ upper triangular and $P$ a permutation matrix;

**Note:**

- *For example, the symmetrical and regular matrix* $K$ *below is decomposed into the following form* $LD{L}^{T}$ *(without needing permutation here* $P=\mathrm{Id}$ *)*

> $K:=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}10& & \text{sym}\\ 20& 45& \\ 30& 80& 171\end{array}\right]=\underset{L}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\begin{array}{ccc}1& 0& 0\\ 2& 1& 0\\ 3& 4& 1\end{array}\right]}}\underset{D}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\begin{array}{ccc}10& 0& 0\\ 0& 5& 0\\ 0& 0& 1\end{array}\right]}}\underset{{L}^{T}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\begin{array}{ccc}1& 2& 3\\ 0& 1& 4\\ 0& 0& 1\end{array}\right]}}$         (1.3-1)

Once this decomposition is carried out, solving the problem is greatly facilitated. It is only expressed in the form of the simplest linear resolutions possible: based on triangular or diagonal matrices. These are the famous “**forward/backward algorithms**”. For example in the case of a factorisation $LU$, the system (1.1-1) will be solved by

$\begin{array}{c}\text{Ku}=f\\ \text{PK}=\text{LU}\end{array}\rangle \Rightarrow \begin{array}{c}\text{Lv}=\text{Pf}(\text{descente})\\ \text{Uu}=v(\text{remontée})\end{array}$                         (1.3-2)

In the first lower diagonal system (descent), we determine the intermediate solution vector $v$. The latter then serves as a second member of the upper diagonal system (ascent) of which the vector $u$ which interests us is the solution.

This phase is inexpensive (in dense, of the order of ${N}^{2}$ against ${N}^{3}$ for factorisation [14]_
with $N$ the size of the problem) and can therefore be repeated many times while maintaining the same factorisation. Which is very useful when solving a **multiple second members** type problem or when you want to carry out **simultaneous resolutions**.

In the **first scenario**, the matrix $K$ is fixed and we successively change the second member ${f}_{i}$ to calculate as many solutions ${u}_{i}$ (the resolutions are interdependent). This makes it possible to pool and therefore amortize this initial cost of factorisation. This strategy is widely used, notably in *Code_Aster*: nonlinear loop with periodic updating (or no updating) of the tangent matrix (e.g. operator *Aste* r STAT_NON_LINE), subspace or inverse power methods (without Rayleigh acceleration) in modal calculation (CALC_MODES), thermo-mechanical chaining with material characteristics independent of temperature (MECA_STATIQUE), etc.

In the **second scenario**, we are aware of all the ${f}_{i}$ at the same time and we organize, in blocks, the descent-ascent phases, to simultaneously calculate the independent ${u}_{i}$ solutions. We can thus use more efficient high-level linear algebra routines, or even play on memory consumption by storing the vector ${f}_{i}$ hollowly. This strategy is (partially) used in *Code_Aster*, for example, in the construction of Schur complements of contact-friction algorithms or for substructuring.

**Note:**

- *The MUMPS product provides for these two types of strategy and even offers features to facilitate the construction and resolution of Schur's complement.*

Now let's look at the **factoring process itself**. It is already clearly explained in the other theoretical documentation of *Code_Aster* on the subject[R6.02.01][R6.02.02], as well as in the bibliographic references already cited[Duf06][Gol96][Las98]. So we will not detail it. Let us just point out that it is an iterative process organized schematically around three loops: one “called in $i$” (on the lines of the work matrix), the second “in $j$” (resp. columns) and the third “in $k$” (resp. stages of the factorisation). They iteratively construct a new matrix ${\tilde{A}}_{k+1}$ from certain data from the previous one, ${\tilde{A}}_{k}$, *via* the classic factorisation formula which is formally written:

$\begin{array}{c}\text{Boucles en}i,j,k\\ {\tilde{A}}_{k+1}(i,j):={\tilde{A}}_{k}(i,j)-\frac{{\tilde{A}}_{k}(i,k){\tilde{A}}_{k}(k,j)}{{\tilde{A}}_{k}(k,k)}\end{array}$                     (1.3-3)

Initially the process is activated with ${\tilde{A}}_{0}=K$ and at the last step, we recover in the square matrix ${\tilde{A}}_{N}$ the triangular parts ($L$ and/or $U$) or even diagonal ($D$) which interest us. For example, in the case $LD{L}^{T}$:

$\begin{array}{c}\text{Boucles en}i,j\\ \text{si}i<j:L(i,j)={\tilde{A}}_{N}(i,j)\\ \text{si}i=j:D(i,j)={\tilde{A}}_{N}(i,j)\end{array}$                         (1.3-4)

**Note:**

- *The formula* (1.4-3) *contains in seed the problems inherent to direct methods: in sparse storage, the fact that the term* ${\tilde{A}}_{k+1}(i,j)$ *can become non-zero while* ${\tilde{A}}_{k}(i,j)$ *is (notion of filling of the factorised, 'fill-in', therefore implying a renumbering or 'ordering'); the propagation of rounding error or division by zero via the term* ${\tilde{A}}_{k}(k,k)$ *(notion of pivoting and balancing of the terms of the matrix or ‘scaling’).*

- Whether we exploit certain properties of the matrix (symmetry, definite-positivity, band, etc.) or whether we seek the widest scope of use;
- Whether we carry out scalar or block processing;

- That we reintroduce zero terms into the blocks to facilitate access to data [15]_ and generate very efficient algebraic operations, often *via* BLAS3 [16]_ (cf. multifrontal by C.Rose and MUMPS);
- Whether contributions affecting a block of rows/columns are grouped ('fan-in' approach, cf. PaStiX) or applied as soon as possible ('fan-out');
- That in parallelism, we seek to provide different levels of independent task sequences, that we order them statically or dynamically, that we cover calculation with communication…
- That we apply pre- and post-processing to reduce filling and improve the quality of the results: renumbering of unknowns, scaling of matrix terms, partial (row) or total (row and column) pivoting, scalar or by diagonal blocks, iterative refinement, etc.

To **group them together, we often distinguish four categories**:

- The **classic algorithms**: Gauss, Crout, Cholesky, Markowitz (*Matlab, Mathematica, Y12M* …);
- **frontal** methods (*MA62* …);
- **multifrontal** methods (*MULT_FRONT Aster, MUMPS, SPOOLES, TAUCS, UFMPACK, WSMP* …)
- The **supernodals** (*SuperLU, PaStiX, CHOLMOD, PARDISO*…).

### 1.5 Direct methods: the main steps

When dealing with sparse systems, the numerical factorisation phase (1.3-3) does not apply directly to the initial matrix $K$, but to **a working matrix** ${K}_{\mathrm{travail}}$ resulting from a **preprocessing phase**. And this **in order to reduce filling, improve the precision of calculations** and therefore optimise subsequent costs in CPU and memory. Roughly this work matrix can be written in the form of the following matrix product

${\mathrm{K}}_{\text{travail}}:={\mathrm{P}}_{o}{\mathrm{D}}_{r}\mathrm{K}{\mathrm{Q}}_{c}{\mathrm{D}}_{c}{\mathrm{P}}_{o}^{T}$                             (1.5-1)

the different elements of which we will describe below. We can thus break down the operation of a direct solver into four steps:

1. *Preprocessing and symbolic factorisation*: it inverts the order of the columns of the working matrix (*via* a permutation matrix ${Q}_{c}$) in order to avoid divisions by zero of the term ${\tilde{A}}_{k}(k,k)$ and to reduce filling. In addition, it rebalances the terms in order to limit rounding errors (*via* the scaling matrices ${D}_{r}/{D}_{c}$). This phase can also be **crucial for algorithmic efficiency** (gain of a factor of 10 sometimes observed) and the quality of the results (gain of 4 or 5 decimal places).

> In this phase, we also create the storage structures for the sparse factorised matrix and the auxiliaries (dynamic pivoting, communication, etc.) required by the following phases. In addition, we estimate the dependency tree of the tasks, their initial distribution according to the processors and the total expected memory consumption.

1. *The renumbering step*: it inverts the order of the unknowns in the matrix (*via* the permutation matrix ${P}_{o}$) in order to reduce the filling involved in factorisation. Indeed, in formula (1.3-3) we see that the factorised (${\tilde{A}}_{k+1}(i,j)\ne 0$) can contain a new non-zero term in its profile while the initial matrix did not include one (${\tilde{A}}_{k}(i,j)=0$). Due to the term $\frac{{\tilde{A}}_{k}(i,k){\tilde{A}}_{k}(k,j)}{{\tilde{A}}_{k}(k,k)}$ not necessarily zero. In particular, it is non-zero when we can find non-zero terms of the initial matrix of the type ${\tilde{A}}_{k}(i,l)\text{ou}{\tilde{A}}_{k}(l,j)(l<i\text{et}l<j)$. This phenomenon can lead to very significant memory and calculation overheads (the factored matrix can be 100 times larger than the initial sparse matrix!).

Hence the idea of renumbering the unknowns (and therefore of permuting the lines and columns of $K$) in order to slow down this phenomenon which is the **real “Achilles heel” of live feeds**. To do this, we often use external products ((PAR)METIS, (PT)SCOTCH, CHACO, JOSTLE, PARTY, etc.) or heuristics embedded with solvers (AMD, RCMK, etc.). Of course, these products display different performances depending on the matrices processed, the number of processors, etc. Among them, **METIS and SCOTCH are very widespread and often “stand out from the crowd”** (gain of up to 50%).

1. *The numerical factorisation phase*: it implements formula (1.3-3) *via* the methods discussed in paragraph §1.4 above. This is the phase, by far, **the most expensive** which will explicitly construct the sparse factorisations $L{L}^{T},LD{L}^{T}$ or $LU$.

1. *The resolution phase*: it carries out the descents-ascents (1.3-2) from which the solution $u$ “jumps out” (finally!). It is **inexpensive** and **possibly pools a subsequent numerical factorisation** (multiple second members, simultaneous resolutions, restart of calculation, etc.).

**Notes:**

- *Steps 1 and 2 only require knowledge of the connectivity and the graph of the initial matrix. So ultimately only data that can be stored and manipulated in the form of integers. Only the last two steps use real numbers on the effective terms of the matrix. They only need the terms of the matrix if the scaling steps are engaged (calculation of* ${D}_{r}/{D}_{c}$ *).*
- *Steps 1 and 4 are independent while 1, 2 and 3, conversely, are linked. Depending on the algorithmic products/approaches, they are grouped differently: 1 and 2 are linked in MUMPS, 1 and 3 in SuperLu and 1, 2 and 3 in UFMPACK… MUMPS allows steps 1+2, 3 and 4 to be carried out separately but successively, or even to pool their results to carry out various sequences. For the moment, in Code_Aster, we alternate the sequences 1+2+3 and 4, 4, 4… and again 1+2+… (see next chapter).*
- *Some products offer to test several strategies in one or more stages and choose the most suitable: SPOOLES and WSMP for stage 1, TAUCS for stage 3 etc.*
- The renumbering tools of the first phase are based on very varied concepts: engineering methods, geometric or optimisation techniques, graph theory, spectral theory, taboo methods, evolutionary algorithms, memetic ones, those based on so-called “ant colonies”, neural networks, etc. Anything goes to improve the local optimum in the form of which the renumbering problem is expressed. These tools are also often used to partition/distribute mesh cells. For the moment, Code_Aster uses the renumberers METIS/AM/AMD (for* *METHODE=‘MULT_FRONT’ and ‘MUMPS’), AMF/QAMD/PORD (for ‘MUMPS’) and RCMK* [17]_ *(for* *’GCPC’, ‘LDLT’ and* *‘PESTC’).*
- *Whatever the linear solver* [18]_ *used, Code_Aster performs a preliminary factorisation phase (step 0) to describe the unknowns of the problem (physical or late degree of freedom link/line number of the matrix via the data structure* NUME_DDL *) and plan the ad hoc storage of the MORSE profile of the matrix.*

### 1.6 Direct methods: the difficulties

Among the difficulties that “direct sparse methods” must overcome are:

- The **manipulation of complex data structures** which optimise storage (see matrix profile) but which complicates the algorithm (see pivoting, OOC, etc.). This helps to lower the “computation/data access” ratio.
- **Efficient data management with respect to the memory hierarchy** and the IC/OOC switch [19]_. This is a recurring question with many problems, but which is significant here due to the high calculation consumption.
- Management of the **sparse/dense compromise** (for front-based methods) with regard to memory consumption, ease of access to data and the efficiency of elementary linear algebra bricks.
- The choice of the **correct renumbering**: it is an NP-complete problem! For large problems, we cannot find the optimal renumbering in a “reasonable” time. We must settle for a “local” solution.
- Effective management of the **propagation of rounding errors** via scaling, pivoting and error calculations on the solution (forward/inverse error [20]_ and conditioning).
- The **size of the factorised which is often the “bottleneck” n°1**. Its distribution between processors (*via* distributed parallelism) and/or OOC do not always make it possible to overcome this pitfall (see figure 1.6-1).

### 2.3 Focus on some technical points

#### 2.3.1 Pivoting

The pivoting technique consists of choosing a suitable ${\tilde{A}}_{k}(k,k)$ term (in formula 1.4-3) to avoid dividing by a term that is too small (which would amplify the propagation of rounding errors when calculating the following ${\tilde{A}}_{k+1}(i,j)$ terms). To do this, we permute rows (partial pivoting) and/or columns (resp. total) to find the appropriate denominator of (1.4-3). For example, in the case of partial pivoting, we choose as “pivot” the term ${\tilde{A}}_{k}(r,k)$ such that

${\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{k}(r,k)\ge u\underset{i}{\max}\mid {\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}_{k}(i,k)\mid \text{avec}u\in ]0,1]$                     (2.3-1)

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.03/Shape8.gif)

*Figure 2.3-1._ Choice of partial pivot at step* *k.*

Hence an amplification of rounding errors to a maximum of ($1+$) at this stage. **What is important here is not so much to choose the largest possible term in absolute value** ( *u* =1) **as to avoid choosing the smallest!** The opposite of these pivots also occurs during the descent-ascent phase, so we must spare these two sources of error amplification by choosing a median *u*. MUMPS, like many packages, offers by default *u* =0.01 (MUMPSCNTL(1) parameter).

To pivot, we generally use scalar diagonal terms but also blocks of terms (2x2 diagonal blocks).

In MUMPS, two types of pivoting are implemented, one called 'static' (during the analysis phase), the other called 'numerical' (resp. numerical factorisation). They can be configured and activated separately (see MUMPS parameters CNTL(1), CNTL(4) and ICNTL(6)). For SPD or dominant diagonal matrices, these pivoting capabilities can be deactivated without risk (the calculation will gain speed), on the other hand, in other cases, they must be initialised to manage possible very small or zero pivots. This generally involves additional filling of the factorisation but increases numerical stability.

**Notes:**

- *This pivoting functionality makes MUMPS essential for processing certain modellings of Code_Aster (quasi-incompressible elements, mixed formulations, X-FEM, etc.). At least until other direct solvers including pivoting are available in the code.*
- *The Aster user does not have direct access to the fine configuration of these pivoting capabilities. They are enabled with default values. He can just partially disconnect them by setting* *SOLVEUR/PRETRAITEMENTS=’SANS’(default=’AUTO’).*
- *The additional filling due to numerical pivoting must be scheduled as early as possible in MUMPS (from the analysis phase). And this, by arbitrarily predicting a percentage of excess memory consumption compared to the planned profile. This figure must be entered in percent in the MUMPS* parameter *ICNTL(14)(accessible to the Aster user via the keyword* *SOLVEUR/PCENT_PIVOT initialised by default at 20%).* Subsequently, if this evaluation proves insufficient, depending on the type of memory management chosen (keyword SOLVEUR/GESTION_MEMOIRE), either the calculation stops in ERREUR_FATALE, or the analysis retries a numerical factorisation several times, each time doubling the size of this space reserved for pivoting.
- *Certain products restrict their scope/robustness by not offering a pivoting strategy (SPRSBLKKT, MULT_FRONT_Aster…), others are limited to scalar pivots (CHOLMOD, PaStiX, TAUCS, WSMP…) or offer specific strategies (perturbation method + Bunch-Kaufmann correction for PARDISO, Bunch-Parlett for SPOOLES…).*

#### 2.3.2 Iterative refinement

At the end of the resolution, having obtained the solution $u$ of the problem, we can easily evaluate its residue $r:=\text{Ku}-f$. Already knowing the factorisation of the matrix, this residue can then inexpensively power **the following iterative improvement process** (in the general non-symmetric case):

$\begin{array}{c}\text{Boucle en}i\\ (1){\mathrm{r}}^{i}=\mathrm{f}-{\text{Ku}}^{i}\\ (2)\text{LU}\delta {\text{u}}^{i}={\mathrm{r}}^{i}\\ (3){\mathrm{u}}^{i+1}\mathrm{\Leftarrow }{\mathrm{u}}^{i}+\delta {\text{u}}^{i}\end{array}$                                 (2.3-2)

This process is “painless” [21]_
since it mainly only costs the price of the descent-ascent of stage (2). It can thus iterate up to a certain threshold or up to a maximum number of iterations. If the residual calculation does not include too many rounding errors, that is to say if the resolution algorithm is rather reliable (see following paragraph) and the conditioning of the matrix system is good, this iterative refinement process [22]_
is very beneficial on the quality of the solution.

In MUMPS this process can be activated or not (parameter ICNTL(10)<0) and limited by a maximum number of iterations ${N}_{\mathit{err}}$ (ICNTL(10)). The process (2.3-2) continues as long as the “balanced residue” ${B}_{\mathrm{err}}$ is greater than a configurable threshold *threshold* (CNTL(2), set by default to $\sqrt{\varepsilon}$ with $\varepsilon$ machine precision)

${\mathrm{B}}_{\text{err}}:=\underset{j}{\max}\frac{\mid {\mathrm{r}}_{j}^{i}\mid }{{(\mid \mathrm{K}\mid \mid {\mathrm{u}}^{i}\mid +\mid \mathrm{f}\mid )}_{j}}<\text{seuil}$                     (2.3-3)

or that it does not decrease by a factor of at least 5 (non-configurable). Usually one or two iterations are enough. If this is not the case, it is often indicative of other problems: poor packaging or the opposite error (see next paragraph).

**Notes:**

- *For the Code_Aster user these MUMPS parameters are not directly accessible. The functionality can be activated or not via the keyword* *POSTTRAITEMENTS.*
- *This functionality is present in many packages: Oblio, PARDISO, UFMPACK, WSMP…*

#### 2.3.3 Reliability of calculations

To estimate the quality of the solution of a linear system[ADD89][Hig02], MUMPS offers numerical tools deduced from the theory of **inverse analysis of rounding errors initiated by Wilkinson** (1960). In this theory, rounding errors due to several factors (truncation, finite arithmetic operation, etc.) are assimilated to disturbances on the initial data. This makes it possible to compare them to other sources of errors (measurement, discretisation, etc.) and to manipulate them more easily *via* three indicators obtained in post-processing:

- *Conditioning* $\mathrm{cond}(K,f)$: it measures the **sensitivity of the problem to data** (unstable, poorly formulated/discretised problem, etc.). That is to say, the multiplicative factor that the manipulation of data will operate on the result. To improve it, we can try to change the formulation of the problem or to balance the terms of the matrix, outside of MUMPS or *via* MUMPS (SOLVEUR/PRETRAITEMENTS='OUI' in *Code_Aster*).
- *The reverse error* $\mathrm{be}(K,f)$ (‘backward error’): it measures the **propensity of the resolution algorithm to transmit/amplify rounding errors**. A tool is said to be “reliable” when this figure is close to machine precision. To improve it, we can try to change the resolution algorithm or modify one or more of its steps (in *Code_Aster* we can play on the parameters SOLVEUR/TYPE_RESOL, PRETRAITEMENTS and RENUM).
- *The direct error* $\mathrm{fe}(K,f)$ (‘forward error’): it is the product of the two previous figures and provides an **upper limit of the relative error on the solution**.

$\frac{\parallel \delta \text{u}\parallel }{\parallel u\parallel }<\underset{\text{fe}(K,f)}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\text{cond}(K,f)\times \text{be}(K,f)}}$                             (2.3-4)

We can give a graphic representation (see figure 2.3-2) of these notions by expressing **the inverse error** as the difference between “the initial data and the disturbed data”, while **the direct error** measures the difference between “the exact solution and the solution actually obtained” (that of the problem disturbed by rounding errors).

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.03/1000000000000175000000C4C7481FFC220ABD39.png)

*Figure 2.3-2._ Graphical representation of the concept of direct and inverse errors.*

In the context of linear systems, the inverse error is measured *via* the **balanced residual**

$\text{be}(K,f):=\underset{j\in J}{\max}\frac{{\mid f-\text{Ku}\mid }_{j}}{{(\mid K\mid \mid u\mid +\mid f\mid )}_{j}}$                             (2.3-5)

It cannot always be evaluated on all the indices ($J\ne {\left[1,N\right]}_{{\rm N}}$). In particular when the denominator is very small (and the numerator is not zero), we prefer the formulation (with ${J}^{\text{*}}$ such as $J\cup {J}^{\text{*}}={\left[1,N\right]}_{{\rm N}}$)

${\text{be}}^{\text{*}}(\mathrm{K},\mathrm{f}):=\underset{j\in {J}^{\text{*}}}{\max}\frac{{\mid \mathrm{f}-\text{Ku}\mid }_{j}}{{(\mid \mathrm{K}\mid \mid \mathrm{u}\mid )}_{j}+{\mathrm{\parallel }{\mathrm{K}}_{j.}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{\infty}{\mathrm{\parallel }\mathrm{u}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{\infty}}$                         (2.3-6)

where ${K}_{j}$ *.* represents the ${j}^{\mathrm{ième}}$ row of the matrix $K$. To these two indicators, we associate two estimates of matrix conditioning (one linked to the lines retained in the set $J$ and the other to its complement ${J}^{\text{*}}$): $\mathrm{cond}(K,f)$ and ${\mathrm{cond}}^{\text{*}}(K,f)$. The theory then provides us with the following results:

- **The approximate solution** **is the exact solution of the disturbed problem**

$\begin{array}{c}(\mathrm{K}+\delta \mathrm{K})\mathrm{u}=(\mathrm{f}+\delta \mathrm{f})\\ \text{avec}\delta {\mathrm{K}}_{ij}\le \max(\text{be},{\text{be}}^{\text{*}})\mid {\mathrm{K}}_{ij}\mid \\ \text{et}\delta {\mathrm{f}}_{i}\le \max(\text{be}.\mid {\mathrm{f}}_{i}\mid ,{\text{be}}^{\text{*}}.{\mathrm{\parallel }{\mathrm{K}}_{i.}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{\infty}{\mathrm{\parallel }\mathrm{u}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{\infty})\end{array}$                         (2.3-7)

- We have the following increase (*via* the direct error $\mathrm{fe}(K,f)$) on **the relative error in solution**

$\frac{\parallel \delta \text{u}\parallel }{\parallel u\parallel }<\underset{\text{fe}(K,f)}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\text{cond}\times \text{be}+{\text{cond}}^{\text{*}}\times {\text{be}}^{\text{*}}}}$                             (2.3-8)

In practice, we mainly scrutinize this last estimate $\mathrm{fe}(K,f)$ and its components. Its order of magnitude indicates *roughly* the number of “true” decimal places of the solution calculated. For poorly conditioned problems, a tolerance of ${10}^{\text{-3}}$ is not uncommon, but it must be taken seriously because this type of pathology can seriously disrupt a calculation.

Even in the very precise framework of solving linear systems, there are many ways to define the sensitivity to rounding errors of the problem considered (i.e. its conditioning). That retained by MUMPS and, which is a reference in the field (cf. Arioli, Demmel and Duff 1989), is inseparable from the 'backward error' of the problem. The definition of one has no meaning without that of the other. This type of conditioning should therefore not be confused with the notion of classical matrix conditioning.

On the other hand, **the conditioning provided by MUMPS takes into account the SECOND MEMBER of the system as well as the SPARSE CHARACTER of the matrix**. Indeed, there is no need to take into account possible rounding errors on matrix terms that are zero and therefore not provided to the solver! The corresponding degrees of freedom do not “talk to each other” (seen through the finite element lens). Thus, this MUMPS conditioning respects the physics of the discretised problem. It does not plunge the problem back into the too rich space of full matrices.

**Thus, the packaging figure displayed by MUMPS is much less pessimistic than the standard calculation that another product can provide** (Matlab, Python...). But let us emphasize that it is only its product with the 'backward error', called 'forward error', which is of interest. And only, within the framework of a linear system resolution *via* MUMPS.

#### 2.3.4 Memory management (In-Core versus Out-Of-Core)

We have seen that the major disadvantage (see §1.7) of direct methods lies in the size of the factorisation. **To allow larger systems to move to RAM, MUMPS suggests offloading this object to disk**: this is the **Out-Of-Core** mode (keyword GESTION_MEMOIRE='OUT_OF_CORE') as opposed to the **In-Core** mode (keyword GESTION_MEMOIRE='IN_CORE') where all the data structures reside in RAM (see figures 2.2-1 and 2.3-3). This method of saving RAM is complementary to the data distribution that parallelism naturally induces. The added value of OOC is therefore especially significant for moderate numbers of processors (<32 processors).

On the other hand, the MUMPS team was very attentive to the additional CPU cost generated by this practice. By reworking the manipulations of the unloaded entities in the algorithmic code, they were able to limit these additional costs to the strict minimum (a few percent and especially in the resolution phase).

#### 2.3.5 Management of singular matrices

One of the product's strong points is its **management of singularities**. It is not only capable of **detecting numerical singularities** [23]_
of a matrix and to synthesize the information for external use (rank calculation, warning to the user, display of expertise, etc.), but in addition, despite this difficulty, it calculates a **“regular**” solution [24]_
**»** or even all or part of the **associated core**.

These new developments were one of the deliverables of the ANR SOLSTICE[SOL]. We requested them from the MUMPS team (in partnership with the CERFACS Algo team) to make this product iso-functional compared to other direct *Code_Aster* solvers.

And in practice, how does MUMPS work?

**In broad strokes**, during the construction of the factorised matrix, it detects the lines containing pivots [25]_
very small (compared to a CNTL(3) criterion [26]_
). It lists them in the vector PIVNUL_LIST(1:INFOG(28)) and, depending on the case, it either replaces them with a pre-fixed value ( *via* CNTL(5) [27]_
), or it stores them separately. The block thus formed (smaller in size) will subsequently undergo an *ad hoc* QR algorithm.

**And finally**, the iterations of iterative refinement complete this skein. As they only use this “retouched” factorisation as a preconditioner, and as they benefit, on the other hand, from the exact information of the matrix-vector product, they bring back [28]_
the “biased” solution in the right direction!

## 3 Implementation in Code_Aster

### 3.1 Context/summary

To improve the performance of the calculations carried out, the **strategy adopted by** **Code_Aster** [Dur08], as by most of the major general codes in structural mechanics, consists in particular of diversifying its panel of linear solvers [32]_
in order to better target the needs and constraints of users: local machine, cluster or computing center; memory and disk footprint; CPU time; industrial or more exploratory study…

On the parallelism and linear solver side, one path is particularly explored [33]_
:

- the “**numerical parallelism**” of external solver libraries such as MUMPS and PETSc, possibly supplemented by “computer parallelism” (internal to the code) for elementary calculations and matrix/vector assemblies;

We are interested here in the first scenario through MUMPS. **This external solver is “plugged” into** **Code_Aster and accessible to users since v8.0.14**. It thus allows us to benefit, “at a lower cost”, from the Rex of a large community of users and the very specialized skills of international teams. All while combining efficiency, performance, reliability and a wide scope of use.

This work was first carried out by exploiting the product's In-Core sequential mode. In particular, thanks to its pivoting capabilities, it provides valuable services by processing new modelling (quasi-incompressible elements, X-FEM, etc.) which can prove problematic for other linear solvers.

Since then, **MUMPS has been used daily in studies** [GM08][Tar07][GS11]. Our feedback has of course expanded and we maintain an **active partnership relationship with the MUMPS development team** (in particular *via* the ANR SOLSTICE[SOL] and a thesis in progress). On the other hand, its integration into *Code_Aster* benefits from continuous enrichment: centralised parallelism IC[Des07] (since v9.1.13), distributed parallelism IC[Boi07] (since v9.1.16) then IC and OOC[BD08] mode (since v9.3.14).

In distributed parallel mode, the use of MUMPS provides **CPU gains** (compared to the default method of the code) **of the order of a dozen on 32 processors** of the *Aster* machine. In very favorable cases this result can be much better and, for “frontier studies”, MUMPS sometimes remains the only viable alternative (see tank internals [Boi07]).

As for RAM consumption, we saw in previous chapters that this is the main weakness of direct solvers. Even in parallel mode, where there is naturally a distribution of data between the processors, this factor can prove to be a handicap. To overcome this problem it is possible to activate in *Code_Aster*, a recent functionality of MUMPS (developed within the framework of the aforementioned ANR): the “Out-Of-Core” (OOC), equivalent to the “In-Core” (IC) by default. It reduces this bottleneck by offloading a lot of data to disk. Thanks to OOC, we can thus **approach the RAM consumption of the native multifrontal** of *Code_Aster* (even sequentially), or even go below by combining the efforts of parallelism and this offloading to disk. The first tests show a gain in RAM between the OOC and the IC of at least 50% (or even more in favorable cases) for a limited additional CPU cost (<10%).

The MUMPS solver therefore not only makes it possible to solve numerically difficult problems, but, inserted into an Aster calculation process that is already partially parallel, it increases its performance. It provides the code with a powerful, generic, robust and general public parallel framework. It thus facilitates the passage of standard studies (< million degrees of freedom) and makes the treatment of large cases (~ several million degrees of freedom) accessible to as many people as possible.

### 3.2 Two types of parallelism: centralised and distributed

#### 3.2.1 Principle

MUMPS is a parallelised linear solver. This parallelism can be activated in several ways, notably depending on the sequential or parallel aspects of the code that uses it. Thus, this **parallelism can be limited to data flows/processing internal to MUMPS**, or, *a contrario* **integrate into a flow of** **parallel data/processing already organized upstream of the solver**, from the elementary calculation phases of *Code_Aster*. The first mode (‘CENTRALISE’) has robustness and a wider scope of use, the second (‘GROUP_ELEM’/’MAIL_***’ and ‘SOUS_DOMAINE’) is less generic but more effective.

Because the phases that are often the most costly in CPU time of a simulation are: the construction of the linear system (purely *Code_Aster*, divided into three positions: symbolic factorisation, elementary calculations and matrix/vector assemblies) and its resolution (in MUMPS, cf. §1.6: renumbering + analysis, numerical factorisation and descent-ascent). The first mode of parallelisation only benefits from the parallelism of steps 2 and 3 of MUMPS, while the three others also parallelise the elementary calculations and the assemblies of *Code_Aster (* cf. figure 2.2-1 and 3.2-1 *)*.

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.03/Shape10.gif)

Figure 3.2-1._ Data flows/parallel processing of centralised/distributed MUMPS.

#### 3.2.2 Different distribution methods

In this paragraph we describe the different choices for distributing elementary calculations to the processors participating in the calculation. This distribution is parameterized during the construction of the finite element model.

A first choice consists of not distributing elementary calculations between processors. If we wish to distribute the elementary calculations, we can rely on a distribution of the finite elements or a distribution of the mesh cells (independently of the finite elements carried by these mesh cells). We can finally combine distribution by finite element and by mesh. Let's detail each mode:

- *CENTRALISE:* **The mesh cells are not distributed** (as in sequential). Elementary calculations are not parallelised. Parallelism only begins at the MUMPS level. Each processor constructs and provides the linear solver with the entire system to solve. This mode of use is useful for non-regression tests. In all cases where elementary calculations represent a small part of the total time (e.g. in linear elasticity), this option may be sufficient.
- '*GROUP_ELEM':* A second type of distribution consists of forming homogeneous groups of finite elements (by type of finite element) then distributing the elementary calculations between the processors to balance the load as best as possible (in terms of number of elementary calculations of each type). Each processor allocates the entire matrix but only performs and assembles the elementary calculations assigned to it.
- *'MAIL_DISPERSE/MAIL_CONTIGU’* **:**

We carry out a distribution of the mesh cells of the model either by **packets of contiguous mesh cells** (‘MAIL_CONTIGU’), or by **cyclic distribution** (‘MAIL_DISPERSE’). This distribution is independent of the type of finite element carried by the mesh cells. For example, with a model comprising 8 mesh cells and for a calculation on 4 processors, we have the following load distributions:

| *Method of distribution* | *Mesh 1* | *Mesh 2* | *Mesh 3* | *Mesh 4* | *Mesh 5* | *Mesh 6* | *Mesh 7* | *Mesh 8* |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| MAIL_CONTIGU | Proc. 0 | Proc. 0 | Proc. 1 | Proc. 1 | Proc. 2 | Proc. 2 | Proc. 3 | Proc. 3 |
| MAIL_DISPERSE | Proc. 0 | Proc. 1 | Proc. 2 | Proc. 3 | Proc. 0 | Proc. 1 | Proc. 2 | Proc. 3 |

Each processor allocates the entire matrix but only performs and assembles the elementary calculations assigned to it.

- *'SOUS_DOMAINE'(default)*: This type of distribution is a hybrid distribution of elementary calculations which is based on a distribution of mesh cells (from a partitioning of the global mesh into subdomains) then on a distribution of finite elements by type within each subdomain. Each processor allocates the entire matrix but only performs and assembles the elementary calculations assigned to it.

#### 3.2.3 Load balancing

The distribution by mesh is very simple but can lead to load imbalances because it does not explicitly take into account spectator mesh cells, skin mesh cells (see figure 3.2-2 on an example comprising 8 volume mesh cells and 4 skin mesh cells), particular zones (non-linearities, etc.). Distribution by subdomains is more flexible and can prove to be more efficient by allowing the data flow to be adapted to its simulation.

Another cause of unbalance can come from Dirichlet conditions by dualization (DDL_IMPO, LIAISON_*** …). For reasons of robustness, their processing is assigned only to the master processor. This work overload, often negligible, nevertheless introduces in certain cases a more marked imbalance. The user can compensate for this by entering one of the CHARGE_PROC0_MA/SD keywords. This differentiated treatment in fact concerns all cases involving so-called “late” mesh cells (Dirichlet *via* Lagrange multipliers but also nodal force, contact continuous method, etc.).

For more details on the computer specifications and the functional implications of this mode of parallelism, you can consult the [U2.08.03](doc:u2.08.03) and [U4.50.01](doc:u4.50.01) documentations.

**Notes:**

- *Without the option* *MATR_DISTRIBUEE (see following paragraph), the different strategies are equivalent in terms of memory occupation. We dry up the flow of data and instructions as soon as possible. This involves selectively processing matrix/vector blocks of the overall problem, which MUMPS will bring together.*
- *In distributed mode, each processor only handles partially filled matrices. On the other hand, in order to avoid introducing too many MPI communications into the code (stopping criteria, residual, etc.), this scenario was not retained for the second members. Their construction is well parallelised, but at the end of assembly, the contributions of all the processors are summed and sent to everyone. Thus all processors fully know the vectors involved in the* calculation.
- *Similarly, the matrix is currently duplicated: in the JEVEUX space (RAM or disk) and in the F90 space of MUMPS (RAM). Ultimately, due to the unloading of the factorisation onto disk, it will become a RAM dimensioning object. It will therefore be necessary to build it directly via MUMPS.*

#### 3.2.4 Resizing Code_Aster objects

In parallel mode, when we distribute the JEVEUX data upstream of MUMPS, we do not necessarily redefine the data structures concerned. With the option MATR_DISTRIBUEE='NON', all distributed objects are allocated and initialised to the same size (the same value as sequentially). On the other hand, each processor will only modify the parts of JEVEUX objects for which it is responsible. This scenario is particularly suited to the distributed parallel mode of MUMPS (default mode) because this product internally groups these incomplete data streams. Parallelism then makes it possible, in addition to savings in calculation time, to reduce the memory space required by the MUMPS resolution but not that necessary for the construction of the problem in JEVEUX.

This is not a problem as long as the RAM space for JEVEUX remains much lower than that required by MUMPS. As JEVEUX mainly stores the matrix and MUMPS, its factored (generally tens of times larger), the RAM bottleneck of the calculation is theoretically on MUMPS. But as soon as we use a few dozen processors in MPI and/or we activate OOC, as MUMPS distributes this factorisation by processor and unloads these pieces on disk, the “ball comes back into JEVEUX's court”.

Hence the option MATR_DISTRIBUEE which resizes the matrix, as closely as possible to the non-zero terms for which the processor is responsible. The JEVEUX space required then decreases with the number of processors and drops below the RAM necessary for MUMPS. ### 3.4 Special management of double Lagrange multipliers

Historically, the direct linear solvers of *Code_Aster* (‘MULT_FRONT’ and ‘LDLT’) did not have a pivoting algorithm (which seeks to avoid accumulations of rounding errors by division by very small terms). To get around this problem, the taking into account of the boundary conditions by Lagrange multipliers (AFFE_CHAR_MECA/THER…) was modified by introducing double Lagrange multipliers. Formally, we do not work with the initial matrix **K** 0

> ${\mathrm{K}}_{0}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{K}& \mathrm{blocage}\\ \mathrm{blocage}& 0\end{array}\right]\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{u}\\ \mathrm{lagr}\end{array}$

but with its doubly dualized form **K** 2

> ${\mathrm{K}}_{2}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\mathrm{K}& \mathrm{blocage}& \mathrm{blocage}\\ \mathrm{blocage}& -1& 1\\ \mathrm{blocage}& 1& -1\end{array}\right]\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{u}\\ {\mathrm{lagr}}_{1}\\ {\mathrm{lagr}}_{2}\end{array}$

Hence an additional memory and calculation cost.

As MUMPS has pivoting capabilities, this choice of dualization of boundary conditions can be called into question. By initialising the keyword ELIM_LAGR to ‘LAGR2’, **we only take into account one Lagrange, the other being a spectator** [34]_
. Hence a simply dualized work matrix ${K}_{1}$

> ${\mathrm{K}}_{1}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\mathrm{K}& \mathrm{blocage}& 0\\ \mathrm{blocage}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& -1\end{array}\right]\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{u}\\ {\mathrm{lagr}}_{1}\\ {\mathrm{lagr}}_{2}\end{array}$

smaller because the extra-diagonal terms of the rows and columns associated with these spectator Lagrange multipliers are then initialised to zero. *A contrario*, with the value ‘NON’, MUMPS receives the usual dualized matrices.

For **problems with many Lagrange multipliers (up to 20% of the number of total unknowns)**, activating this parameter often pays off (smaller matrix). But when **this number explodes (>20%)**, this process can be counterproductive. The gains made on the matrix are canceled by the size of the factor and especially the number of late pivots that MUMPS must perform. Imposing ELIM_LAGR='NON' can then be very interesting (40% gain in CPU on the mac3c01 benchmark).

## 4 Conclusion

In the context of thermo-mechanical simulations with *Code_Aster*, **most of the calculation costs often come from the construction and solution of linear systems**. For 60 years, two types of techniques have competed for supremacy in the field, direct solvers and iterative ones. *Code_Aster*, like many general codes, has chosen a diversified offer in the field. With, however, **a rather** orientation **direct sparse solvers**. These are more suited to its needs which can be summarized under the triptych “robustness/multiple second member type problems/moderate parallelism”. The code now relies on numerous optimised and long-lasting “middlewares” (MPI, BLAS, LAPACK, METIS, etc.) and is mainly used on SMP clusters (fast networks, large RAM and disk storage capacity), we seek to optimise the processing of linear solvers with this in mind.

**Taking into account the technical expertise required** [36]_
**and a rich international offering** [37]_
, **to** **effectively carry out these solutions, the question of using an external product is now unavoidable**. This makes it possible to acquire, at a lower cost, a functionality that is often effective, reliable, efficient and benefits from a wide scope of use. We can thus benefit from the feedback of a large community of users and the (very) specialized skills of international teams.

Thus **Code_Aster has chosen to integrate the parallel multifrontal solver of the MUMPS package**. This is in addition, in particular, to its in-house multifrontal. Although the latter benefits from a long adaptation to the *Aster* modellings, it remains poorer in features (pivoting, pre/post-processing, solution quality, …) and less efficient in parallel (for RAM consumptions of the same order). In order to exploit certain modellings (quasi-incompressible elements, X-FEM, …) or to run “frontier studies” (cf. reactor-vessel internals), this “*Code_Aster* + MUMPS” coupling sometimes becomes the only viable alternative.

Since then, its integration into *Code_Aster* has benefited from continuous enrichment and **MUMPS** (SOLVEUR/METHODE=’MUMPS’) **is used daily in studies**. Our feedback has of course expanded and we maintain an **active partnership relationship with the MUMPS “core-team”** (in particular *via* ANR SOLSTICE and a thesis).

In **parallel** mode, the use of MUMPS provides **CPU gains** (compared to the default method of the code, the “in-house” multifrontal) **of the order of a dozen on 32 processors** of the *Aster* machine. In more favorable cases or by exploiting a second level of parallelism or BLR compressions, this CPU gain can be much better.

The MUMPS solver therefore not only makes it possible to solve numerically difficult problems, but, inserted into an already partially parallel *Aster* calculation process, it increases its performance. **It provides the code with a powerful, generic, robust and general public parallel framework**. It thus facilitates the passage of standard studies (<million degrees of freedom) and makes the treatment of large cases (~several million degrees of freedom) accessible to as many people as possible.

To give an order of magnitude, the MUMPS package has more than 105 lines (F90/C).

In the public domain alone, there are dozens of packages, libraries, “macro-libraries”…

## 7 Appendix n°1: Principle of BLR compressions in MUMPS

This paragraph summarizes the different technical aspects of Clément Weisbecker's **thesis work** on low-rank compressions[CW15][Boi13]. This work was continued and improved by the MUMPS team and is partly available in the consortium[MUC] version of the product.

For more details, you can consult the thesis document itself [CW13]. The figures reused here come from elsewhere, either from this document or from the set of slides from his defense.

> Principle of the multifrontal method

The **multifrontal method is a class of direct method** used to **solve linear systems**. It is this method which is implemented in the MUMPS product and it is this which is generally used in the major commercial codes in structural mechanics (ANSYS, NASTRAN, etc.). Even if there are other strategies (supernodal, etc.) and they are starting to be implemented in public packages (SuperLU, PastiX, etc.), this thesis work focused exclusively on this standard strategy that is multifrontal. However, many of its results can undoubtedly be extended or adapted to these other solution methods.

The **multifrontal method**, developed by I.S.Duff and J.K.Reid (1983), consists in particular of using graph theory [38]_
to construct, from a sparse matrix, an **elimination tree efficiently organizing the calculations** (see figures 7.1.1). The black squares represent the non-zero matrix terms. To manipulate, “by interposed graphs”, this type of data, the following rule is usually imposed: a point on the graph represents an unknown and, an edge between two points, a non-zero matrix term.

So in the example below, variables 1 and 2 must be connected by an edge (in the initial numbering). Generally, the **initial numbering of the matrix is not optimal**. In order to reduce the memory footprint of the factorised [39]_
, the number of flops of subsequent manipulations and in order to try to guarantee a good level of precision of the result, **we renumber this initial matrix**. We thus see that the number of additional terms (‘fill-in’) created by the factorisation goes from 16 to 10.

It is from this renumbered matrix that we construct the elimination tree specific to the multifrontal. **This tree-based vision has the great merit of concretely organizing the tasks:** we precisely determine their **dependency** or not (to provide parallelism), their **precedence** (to make groupings in order to optimise BLAS performance); we can **plan certain consumption** (in time and memory) or even try to limit numerical problems.

So in the example below, the treatment of variable 1 has no impact on variables 2, 4 and 5. On the other hand, it will impact variables 3 and 7 which occupy the upper levels of the tree (they are its ascendants).

The **second strong idea of the multifrontal method** is to bring together as many variables as possible (we speak of **amalgamation**) in order to **constitute (dense) “fronts”** whose numerical processing will be much more efficient (*via* BLAS type routines).

Even if it means filling the corresponding matrix sub-block with real zeros and handling them as they are [40]_
. This is for example what is done in the tree in Figure 7.1.2 between the amalgamated variables 7, 8 and 9. There are many amalgamation techniques based on graph criteria, numerical aspects or computational considerations (e.g. distributed parallelism).

- *For the sake of simplification, we will not address in this summary the other numerical processing which often intervenes in the process: scaling of terms (‘scaling’), permutation of columns and static/dynamic pivoting. As the “devil is often in the details”, it is these related treatments which have complicated Clément’s numerical work and IT developments. They are essential to the smooth running of many of our industrial simulations with Code_Aster.*

### 7.1 Elimination tree

Algorithmic processing of the tree starts from the bottom (at the “leaves”) to the top (towards the “root”). **Within each leaf or each branch “node” we associate a dense front**. In a front we distinguish two types of variables:

- the **‘Fully Summed’ (FS)** which, as their name indicates, are completely processed and will no longer be updated;
- the *‘Non Fully Summed’ (NFS)* who are still waiting for contributions from other branches of the tree.

The first type of variables can be completely “**eliminated**”: the terms of the factorised matrix concerning them (rows of **U** and columns of **L**) are calculated and stored [41]_
once and for all. The **second type produces a block of contributions** [42]_
(denoted **CB** for ‘Contribution Blocks’) which will be added at the upper level of the tree with other CBs associated with the same variables (see figure 7.2.1).

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.03/10000201000000FF000000D8FE8B85CBBE2AC5BF.png)

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.03/10000201000000E9000000D298B8E9D63CA5E4D4.png)

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.03/100000000000015B000000AC1703DA2C62F6F108.png)

*Figure 7.2.1_ General structure of a “front” before and after its treatment.*

In turn, certain variables associated with these blocks will be eliminated and others, on the contrary, will continue to actively participate in the process by providing new CBs. And so on… down to the root of the tree. At this last stage, there are only variables eliminated and no more CB!

Thus in the fronts associated with the left leaves of Figure 7.2.1, variables 1 and 2 are FS, while variables 3, 7 and 9 are NFS. The latter provide CBs which will be accumulated in the front of the upper level bringing together variables 3, 7, 8 and 9. The latter will “relieve” the algorithmic process of variable FS 3 (it will be eliminated), while NFS 7, 8 and 9 will continue their progression in the tree.

**In the elimination trees** presented previously, it is **this type of operation which is carried out within each front**, then between each front and its “father”. Low-rank compression will aim to reduce their algorithmic complexity as well as their memory footprint (peak RAM and disk consumption).

### 7.3 Memory management

On this last point, often the most crucial for our studies, the entire problem lies in the “delayed management of CBs”**. During step *k* of algorithm 1.3, we must keep in RAM not only the “active” edge, but also the CBs awaiting processing and the (possible) factors not unloaded to disk (if OOC is activated). They constitute the active memory of the process (blue and green zone in Figure 7.4.1).

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.03/10000201000001FF000000C200E20BF9D3919E67.png)

*Figure 7.4.1_ Memory management of the different elements manipulated by the multifrontal.*

This **active memory** is managed like a stack. It **constantly fluctuates**. It increases when a front is loaded. Then it decreases as CBs associated with this front are consumed. Finally it grows again with the new factors and the new CB resulting from this front.

The new factors are then possibly copied onto disk. **The memory footprint of these factors** (red area on the left of the graph) is easier to analyze: **it only grows as the tree goes up!**

In any case, **these two memory areas are not easy to predict** **a priori**. They depend in particular on renumbering, the construction of the elimination tree but also numerical preprocessing. This is one of the tasks performed by the analysis step of MUMPS. It is useful both for multifrontal to efficiently allocate its memory areas, but also for the calling code in order to best manage its own internal objects [43]_
. The optimised management of these elements is further complicated by the numerical processing which is carried out dynamically during factorisation: for example, the organisation of pivoting and distribution of data in parallel.

**It is the peak of this active memory which constitutes the essential issue of this thesis**. It is often much greater than the size of the factorised. However, this is already often 500N in our current studies (with N the size of the problem). Thus the processing of a matrix comprising 1 million unknowns requires at least 500*1*12 [44]_
=6GB of RAM (real double precision). And this figure tends to increase with the size of the problem (we go from 500N to 1000N or even more).

We can therefore reach RAM peaks that even parallel data distribution combined with OOC will not be able to manage effectively [45]_
. **So any significant reduction in this peak would be a very significant gain for our large current and future studies**.

### 7.5 Multifrontal 'Block Low-Rank' (BLR)

**The objective of this thesis is to exploit this type of compression by decomposing in low-rank form the largest fronts of the elimination tree of a multifrontal**. Because it is these dense matrix sub-blocks which generate the most flop and which burden the RAM peak (due to all the CBs that they will agglomerate). Generally these big fronts are located in the last levels of the elimination tree.

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.03/10000000000003DB0000017F4C956465DEF88C9D.png)

*Figure 7.6.1_ Structure of a compressed front after its factorisation.*

As illustrated below (see figure 7.6.1) we will therefore, firstly, **decompose into columns and rows of sub-blocks** (decomposition by ‘panels’), the matrix blocks corresponding to the **four types of front terms** (following the terminology of figure 7.2.1):

- FSxFS (‘block (1,1)’),
- FSxNFS (‘block (1,2)’),
- NFSxFS (‘block (2,1)’),
- NFSxNFS (‘block (2,2)’).

Then each of these sub-blocks will be compressed into low-rank following the formula (7.5-1). At least when this compression is legal [48]_
. Except the diagonal sub-blocks of the FSxFS sub-part which remain in full-rank (to optimise their subsequent manipulations).

We **adapt the size of the panels** so that it is:

- *Large enough* to take advantage of the performance of BLAS kernels (loop invariant, minimization of cache misses and optimised management of the memory hierarchy);
- *Not too large* to provide flexibility in subsequent numerical (pivoting, scaling) and computer (distributed parallelism) manipulations;
- *Medium size* in order to optimise the costs of MPI communications (latency *versus* bandwidth).
- *Medium size* in order to provide flexibility in the grouping of variables that will follow (‘clustering’). This reorganisation should generate maximum compression.
- *Not too big* so as not to cost too much in SVD or RRQR.

**But the whole problem lies in the fact that these matrix sub-blocks have no reason to be low-rank!** Even if they are large, the 'demoted' front parts can turn out to be full rank. The costs of SVDs or RRQRs will then not be compensated and the compression objective will prove to be compromised!

Inspired by work already carried out by other compression techniques (hierarchical matrices of type H, H2, HSS/SSS, etc.), criteria have been developed to decompose the variables composing a front and renumber them so that they generate low-rank matrix sub-blocks. This criterion is based on the following empirical observation:

**The further apart two blocks of variables are, the lower the numerical rank of the matrix block it implies becomes.**

### 7.6 Some additional elements

**It is therefore on an admissibility criterion of this type that MUMPSt will base its clustering of the variables composing a front**. He worked a lot to make this “key operation” of the method compatible with the numerical-computing constraints of multifrontal and the MUMPS tool; while ensuring maximum compression and limiting its additional cost (in time).

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.03/100002010000012E000000CCE3C9AA7B978DEFD5.png)

*Figure 7.7.1_ “Inherited” type clustering between the NFS variables of a front*

*and those FS of an anterior front.*

Example of sophistication: **this clustering is operated separately on the two types of variables, FS and NFS**. As by traversing the elimination tree, the NFS variables of a front become the FS of the fronts of higher levels, we decompose this task. On the other hand, we do not completely start these divisions again on each front. We pool certain information in order to limit additional costs (in terms of time). This **particular clustering algorithm is called “inherit”** as opposed to the **standard so-called “explicit” algorithm** for which we start the two clusterings again each time a new front is manipulated.

The additional cost of the “explicit” variant of clustering can be prohibitive on very large problems (several times the cost of the entire analysis phase!) while the cost of optimised clustering remains reasonable: only a few percent of this analysis phase. The low-rank gains of the two variants are very close, however the implementation of the optimised version is more complex.

Finally, these clusterings are carried out by **standard tools, METIS or SCOTCH**. But we do not apply them directly to the variables of the fronts but to halos encompassing them. This tip makes it possible to properly orient these divisions so that they produce groups of contiguous homogeneous nodes.

On the other hand, to be more precise, the processing of a front has five steps (already mentioned in the algorithms in fig. 7.3.1). We start by **dividing the variables into two nested levels of panels:**

- the first level, the largest, the one which is outside (called ‘**outer panel**’ or ‘**BLR panel**’), results from low-rank clustering;
- while the second, the one inside the previous one (' **inner panel** ' or ' **BLAS panel** ') groups the variables by sub-packets of 32, 64 or 96 contiguous variables in order to feed the calculation cores more efficiently (and reduce the cost of MPI communications and I/Os).

Then, we have two nested loops: the first on the BLR panels and the second on the BLAS panels. For a given BLAS panel, we perform:

- the Factorisation step (**F**),
- the Solve step (**S**),
- the Internal Upgrade (**UI**) between all the following sub-panels of the BLR panel,
- the External Upgrade (**EU**) between all the following BLR panels.

The standard order of operations can therefore be roughly denoted **FSUU**. Depending on the level at which we use low-rank Compression (**C**), we then distinguish 4 variants:

1. FSUUC,
2. FSUCU,
3. **FSCUU,**
4. FCSUU.
