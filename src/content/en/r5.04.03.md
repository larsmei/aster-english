# r5.04.03 Second-gradient modellings

**Summary**

The second-gradient modellings arising from the work of Chambon et al. ([1], [2], [3]) and the second gradient of dilatation arising from the work of Fernandes et al. ([4], [5]), and of Gantier ([14]), are presented here.

These modellings fall within the framework of media with microstructure and have as their numerical objective to give results that converge with respect to the fineness of the spatial discretisation, in order to avoid obtaining localised solutions. They must be used as soon as the mechanical rheological laws considered present a softening of the behaviour reflecting the damage or the degradation of a material before cracking.

The second-gradient-of-dilatation modelling is, in fact, a simplified approach to the second gradient restricted to materials subject to the phenomenon of dilatancy. The numerical objective is to reduce the computation times significantly. It lends itself particularly well to geomaterials.

Note: The second-gradient modelling has been withdrawn from the code. Only the dilatation-gradient modelling is available.

## 1 The second-gradient model

### 1.1 Brief presentation of media with microstructure

At the origin of this theory, one finds the work of Mindlin ([6], [7]) within the framework of linear elasticity. This work was then taken up by Germain ([8], [9]), who gave an expression of it by application of the principle of virtual work, the basis of numerical methods with a view to an application by finite elements.

This theory implies the definition of an enriched kinematics. In addition to the classical displacement field ${u}_{i}$ , one considers the second-order tensor, denoted ${f}_{ij}$ and called the microscopic kinematic gradient, which models both the strains and the rotations at the scale of the grains of the structure. One draws attention here to the fact that, within the framework of media with microstructure, the microscopic strain gradient has no reason to be linked to the gradient of any field depending on the macroscopic displacement. It is not necessarily symmetric. The microscopic strain gradient ${f}_{ij}$ is a variable in the same way as the macroscopic displacement ${u}_{i}$ , unlike the classical (macroscopic) strain field, which is obtained by derivation

$$
{\epsilon}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial {u}_{i}}{\partial {x}_{j}}+\frac{\partial {u}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{i}})
$$

An essential fact at the basis of the writing of this theory concerns the statement of the principle of objectivity or, equally, of material indifference:

*The virtual power of the internal forces of a system is zero in any virtual motion that rigidifies the system at the instant considered.*

Neglecting the expression of the external body forces for reasons of simplification of the writing, the consequence of the axiom of the virtual powers of the internal forces leads to the expression of the variational formulation, for any kinematically admissible field $({u}_{i}^{\text{*}},{f}_{ij}^{\text{*}})$

$$
\underset{\Omega}{\int}({\sigma}_{ij}\frac{\partial {u}_{i}^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{j}}+{\tau}_{ij}({f}_{ij}^{\text{*}}-\text{}\frac{\partial {u}_{i}^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{j}})+{\Sigma}_{\text{ijk}}\frac{\partial {f}_{ij}^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{k}})\text{dv}=\underset{\partial \Omega }{\int}({t}_{i}{u}_{i}^{\text{*}}+{T}_{ij}{f}_{ij}^{\text{*}})\text{ds}
$$

where ${t}_{i}$ and ${T}_{ij}$ are respectively the traction forces and the double forces corresponding to the boundary conditions, on the boundary $\partial \Omega$ , conjugates of the kinematic variables.

The variational formulation () is another means of expressing the equilibrium relations, which are expressed

$$
\frac{\partial ({\sigma}_{ij}-{\tau}_{ij})}{\partial {x}_{j}}=0
$$

$$
\frac{\partial {\Sigma}_{\text{ijk}}}{\partial {x}_{k}}-{\tau}_{ij}=0
$$

and one finds for the expression of the boundary conditions

$$
{t}_{i}=({\sigma}_{ij}-{\tau}_{ij}){n}_{j}
$$

$$
{T}_{ij}={\Sigma}_{\text{ijk}}{n}_{k}
$$

where ${n}_{j}$ denotes the outward normal to the boundary $\partial \Omega$ .

To complete the problem, one must define the constitutive laws which will link the static variables ${\sigma}_{ij}$ , ${\tau}_{ij}$ , ${\Sigma}_{\text{ijk}}$ respectively to the history of the kinematic variables of $\frac{\partial {u}_{i}}{\partial {x}_{j}}$ , $\left({f}_{ij}-\frac{\partial {u}_{i}}{\partial {x}_{j}}\right)$ and $\frac{\partial {f}_{ij}}{\partial {x}_{k}}$ .

These models have already proved that they were effective from the point of view of regularisation. However, they are complex in their use because of the different constitutive laws to be specified. Moreover, the discretisation by the finite-element method in 3D induces the addition of 9 supplementary degrees of freedom per node corresponding to the components ${f}_{ij}$ . The computation times are then relatively large and consequently incompatible with the type of studies that we wish to carry out.

### 1.2 The second-gradient model

Starting from the preceding model, expressed by the relation (), one can restrict the kinematics by forcing the microscopic gradient to be equal to the macroscopic gradient (see Chambon et al. [2] for a detailed analysis)

$$
{f}_{ij}=\frac{\partial {u}_{i}}{\partial {x}_{j}}
$$

The advantage of this assumption is to reduce the number of independent variables and to introduce simpler constitutive laws. The expression of the virtual powers, rearranged after a few algebraic manipulations, is then written, for any kinematically admissible field ${u}_{i}^{\text{*}}$

$$
\underset{\Omega}{\int}({\sigma}_{ij}\frac{\partial {u}_{i}^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{j}}+{\Sigma}_{\text{ijk}}\frac{{\partial}^{2}{u}_{i}^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{j}\partial {x}_{k}})\text{dv}=\underset{\partial \Omega }{\int}({p}_{i}{u}_{i}^{\text{*}}+{P}_{i}{\text{Du}}_{i}^{\text{*}})\text{ds}
$$

where ${p}_{i}$ and ${P}_{i}$ are the boundary conditions defined by

$$
{p}_{i}={\sigma}_{ij}{n}_{j}-{n}_{k}{n}_{j}{\mathit{D\Sigma }}_{\text{ijk}}-\frac{{\mathit{D\Sigma }}_{\text{ijk}}}{{\text{Dx}}_{k}}{n}_{j}-\frac{{\mathit{D\Sigma }}_{\text{ijk}}}{{\text{Dx}}_{j}}{n}_{k}+\frac{{\text{Dn}}_{l}}{{\text{Dx}}_{l}}{\Sigma}_{\text{ijk}}{n}_{j}{n}_{k}-\frac{{\text{Dn}}_{j}}{{\text{Dx}}_{k}}{\Sigma}_{\text{ijk}}
$$

$$
{P}_{i}={\Sigma}_{\text{ijk}}{n}_{j}{n}_{k}
$$

with

$\mathit{Dq}$ which denotes the normal derivative of the variable $q$ : $\mathit{Dq}=\frac{\partial q}{\partial {x}_{j}}{n}_{j}$

$\frac{Dq}{D{x}_{j}}$ which denotes the tangential derivative of the variable $q$ : $\frac{Dq}{D{x}_{j}}=\frac{\partial q}{\partial {x}_{j}}-{n}_{j}\mathit{Dq}$

The assumption of equality between the microscopic and macroscopic strain fields (6) has a direct impact on the expression of the boundary conditions because the variables ${u}_{i}^{\text{*}}$ and ${f}_{ij}^{\text{*}}$ are no longer independent.

It has already been shown that this model corrects the dependence of the thickness of the localisation bands with respect to the discretisation of the mesh (see Chambon et al. [1] or Matsushima et al. [3]). For this, the model can be used by taking into account two different constitutive laws, one to describe the classical first-gradient part and the other for the second gradient. As regards the latter, any relation could be considered, but until today, it is in general linear elasticity which has been chosen.

## 2 The second-gradient-of-dilatation model

The principle of media with microstructure is based on taking the microscopic strains into account in order to introduce an internal length into the expression of the model. If the effectiveness of the regularisation brought by the second-gradient model is beyond doubt, the computation times of the simulations can become prohibitive. In order to reduce them within the particular framework of dilatant materials (for which the volumetric strain evolves as a function of the loading) one restricts the generality of the regularising effect to dilatant media with microstructure.

### 2.1 Dilatant media with microstructure

The kinematics of these media is defined by the usual displacement field ${u}_{i}$ , the microscopic volumetric variation denoted $\chi$ and its gradients. By duality with this enriched kinematics, the static variables of the classical macroscopic stresses ${\sigma}_{ij}$ , the microscopic dilatation stress $\kappa$ and the vectorial double dilatation stresses ${S}_{j}$ are introduced. The variable $\kappa$ is the component conjugate to the relative volumetric strain (of the macroscopic field with respect to the microscopic one) ${\epsilon}_{V}-\chi$ , while the components ${S}_{j}$ define a vector which is the conjugate of the gradient of the microscopic dilatation $\frac{\partial \chi }{\partial {x}_{j}}$ .

In a manner analogous to the expression of the virtual power of the media with microstructure of chapter 1.1, and again neglecting the expression of the external body forces for analytical simplification, one finds for the dilatant media with microstructure, for any kinematically admissible field $({u}_{i}^{\text{*}},{\chi }^{\text{*}})$

$$
\underset{\Omega}{\int}({\sigma}_{ij}\frac{\partial {u}_{i}^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{j}}+\kappa ({\epsilon}_{V}^{\text{*}}-{\chi }^{\text{*}})+{S}_{j}\frac{\partial {\chi }^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{j}})\text{dv}=\underset{\partial \Omega }{\int}({t}_{i}{u}_{i}^{\text{*}}+{\mathrm{m\chi }}^{\text{*}})\text{ds}
$$

for which

$$
{t}_{i}=\left({\sigma}_{ij}+{\text{κδ}}_{ij}\right){n}_{j}
$$

$$
m={S}_{j}{n}_{j}
$$

are the boundary conditions, expressed on the boundary $\partial \Omega ` , conjugated by duality to the kinematic variables :math:`{u}_{i}$ and $\chi$ respectively.

The equilibrium relations of this problem are written

$$
\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial {x}_{j}}+\frac{\partial \kappa }{\partial {x}_{i}}=0
$$

$$
\kappa +\frac{\partial {S}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{j}}=0
$$

The system of equations composed of [(), (), () and ()] is obtained classically by application of the divergence theorem and by an integration by parts of ().

### 2.2 The second-gradient-of-dilatation model

Following a principle analogous to that implemented for the second-gradient model of chapter 1.2, one introduces a mathematical constraint in order to force the equality between the macroscopic volumetric strain ${\epsilon}_{V}$ and the microscopic one $\chi$

$$
\chi ={\epsilon}_{V}
$$

The expression of the virtual powers is then the following, for any kinematically admissible field ${u}_{i}^{}$

$$
\underset{\Omega}{\int}({\sigma}_{ij}\frac{\partial {u}_{i}^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{j}}+{S}_{j}\frac{{\partial}^{2}{u}_{i}^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{i}\partial {x}_{j}})\text{dv}=\underset{\partial \Omega }{\int}({p}_{i}{u}_{i}^{\text{*}}+{\text{Pn}}_{i}{\text{Du}}_{i}^{\text{*}})\text{ds}
$$

where ${p}_{i}$ and $P$ are the boundary conditions defined by

$$
{p}_{i}={\sigma}_{ij}{n}_{j}-{n}_{i}{n}_{j}{\text{DS}}_{j}-\frac{{\text{DS}}_{j}}{{\text{Dx}}_{j}}{n}_{i}-\frac{{\text{DS}}_{j}{n}_{j}}{{\text{Dx}}_{i}}+\frac{{\text{Dn}}_{p}}{{\text{Dx}}_{p}}{S}_{j}{n}_{j}{n}_{i}
$$

$$
P={S}_{j}{n}_{j}
$$

As for the second-gradient model, the assumption of equality between the microscopic and macroscopic volumetric strain fields () has a direct impact on the expression of the boundary conditions because the variables ${u}_{i}^{\text{*}}$ and ${\chi }^{\text{*}}$ are no longer independent.

For reasons of simplicity, one assumes that $P=0$ . The consequence of this assumption is that ${S}_{j}{n}_{j}=0$ on the boundary, which reduces the expression () to

$$
{p}_{i}={\sigma}_{ij}{n}_{j}-\frac{\partial {S}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{j}}{n}_{i}
$$

A remarkable property of this simplification comes from the fact that the expression of the boundary conditions then decomposes into the classical part ${\sigma}_{ij}{n}_{j}$ and a second term which does not induce shear components.

The equilibrium equation is written:

$$
\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial {x}_{j}}-\frac{{\partial}^{2}{S}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{i}\partial {x}_{j}}=0
$$

### 2.3 Spatial discretisation by finite elements

#### 2.3.1 Historical discretisation of the second-gradient-of-dilatation model

The aim of the second-gradient-of-dilatation approach is to define a regularising model, making it possible to ensure the independence of the results with respect to the spatial discretisations, by introducing a minimum of nodal unknowns in its finite-element approach. For this one restricts oneself to applications considering dilatant materials. Now, discretising by the finite-element method the expression () has as a consequence the imposition that the field of the displacement unknowns as well as its divergence be continuous and differentiable. This amounts to taking into account C1-continuous finite elements.

One then proposes to introduce the mathematical constraint () into the expression of the dilatant media with microstructure by means of a coupling of Lagrange multipliers and of penalisation. One then obtains, for any kinematically admissible field $\left({u}_{i}^{\text{*}},{\chi }^{\text{*}},{\lambda}^{\text{*}}\right)$

$$
\begin{array}{c}\underset{\Omega}{\int}\left({\sigma}_{ij}\frac{\partial {u}_{i}^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{j}}+{S}_{j}\frac{\partial {\chi }^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{j}}-\lambda \left({\epsilon}_{V}^{\text{*}}-{\chi }^{\text{*}}\right)+{\lambda}^{\text{*}}\left({\epsilon}_{V}-\chi \right)+r\left({\epsilon}_{V}-\chi \right)\left({\epsilon}_{V}^{\text{*}}-{\chi }^{\text{*}}\right)\right)\text{dv}\\ \phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}=\underset{\partial \Omega }{\int}\left({p}_{i}{u}_{i}^{\text{*}}+{\text{Pn}}_{i}{\text{Du}}_{i}^{\text{*}}\right)\text{ds}\end{array}
$$

this historical formulation is available under the name DIL. The numerical implementation proposed is detailed in chapter 3. One specifies here only the approximation spaces of the variables defining the kinematic field. The polynomial interpolations are P2-P1-P1 (in plane strain and in 3D):

1. second-order shape functions for the displacement variables $u$
2. first-order shape functions for the microscopic volumetric strain tensor $\chi$ and the Lagrange multipliers $\lambda$

#### 2.3.2 Incompressible modelling of the second-gradient-of-dilatation model

This modelling proposed by Gantier [14] is a variation of the modelling presented in the paragraph, adapted to behaviours in which the dilatancy tends towards zero with hardening. This modelling is inspired by the three-field incompressible elements (see R3.06.08). It is not available for the THM modellings with second gradient.

$$
\begin{array}{c}\underset{\Omega}{\int}\left({\sigma}_{ij}\left({{\varepsilon}}_{ij}^{D}(u\text{*})+\frac{1}{3}\chi \text{*}{\delta}_{ij}\right)+{S}_{j}\frac{\partial {\chi }^{\text{*}}}{\partial {x}_{j}}+\lambda \left({\epsilon}_{V}^{\text{*}}-{\chi }^{\text{*}}\right)+{\lambda}^{\text{*}}\left({\epsilon}_{V}-\chi \right)+r\left({\epsilon}_{V}-\chi \right)\left({\epsilon}_{V}^{\text{*}}-{\chi }^{\text{*}}\right)\right)\text{dv}\\ \phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}=\underset{\partial \Omega }{\int}\left({p}_{i}{u}_{i}^{\text{*}}+{\text{Pn}}_{i}{\text{Du}}_{i}^{\text{*}}\right)\text{ds}\end{array}
$$

In this expression, ${{\varepsilon}}^{D}$ represents the deviatoric strain and ${\delta}_{ij}$ the Kronecker symbol.

This formulation is therefore inspired by the three-field incompressible elements: it is the variable $\chi$ which intervenes in the first-gradient constitutive law.

Moreover, it has been demonstrated (see Gantier [14]) that the use of the penalisation term is optional: the use of a Lagrange multiplier is sufficient to impose the equality between the microscopic and macroscopic volumetric strains.

The shape functions used are identical to those of the historical formulation of the second gradient. They are of type P2-P1-P1:

1. second-order shape functions for the displacement variables $u$
2. first-order shape functions for the microscopic volumetric strain tensor $\chi$ and the Lagrange multipliers $\lambda$

## 3 Numerical integration of the second-gradient models

The strain fields, the associated stresses and the tangent matrix in mechanics with the historical formulation of the second-gradient-of-dilatation model are detailed below. There is no internal variable specific to the second gradient. As a reminder, in mechanics, the nodal variables defining the degrees of freedom are the following: $\left({u}_{i},\chi ,\lambda \right)$ .

For the THM modellings with second gradient, there exist no couplings between the generalised second-gradient strains and stresses and those of the thermo-hydraulic part. Thus, the generalised strain and stress fields, as well as the elementary stiffness matrices associated with the second gradient, are identical to those of the mechanical modellings.

### 3.1 The generalised strain field

$E=\left(\begin{array}{c}\epsilon \\ {\epsilon}_{V}\\ \chi \\ \frac{\partial \chi }{\partial {x}_{j}}\\ \lambda \end{array}\right)$

### 3.2 The generalised stress field

$\Sigma =\left(\begin{array}{c}\sigma \\ r\left({\epsilon}_{V}-\chi \right)+\lambda \\ -r\left({\epsilon}_{V}-\chi \right)-\lambda \\ {S}_{j}\\ \left({\epsilon}_{V}-\chi \right)\end{array}\right)$

### 3.3 The tangent matrix

The elementary tangent matrix of the second-gradient modelling is composed, among other things:

1. of the elementary stiffness tangent matrix ${D}^{1d}$ associated with the first-gradient constitutive law
2. of the elementary stiffness tangent matrix ${D}^{2d}$ associated with the second-gradient-of-dilatation constitutive law which links the double stresses ${S}_{j}$ to the gradients of the microscopic volumetric strains $\frac{\partial \chi }{\partial {x}_{j}}$ .

${K}^{\text{el}}=\left(\begin{array}{ccccc}{\left({D}^{1d}\right)}_{\text{dim}\left(j\right)}& 0& 0& 0& 0\\ 0& r& -r& 0& 1\\ 0& -r& r& 0& -1\\ 0& 0& 0& {\left({D}^{2d}\right)}_{\text{dim}\left(j\right)}& 0\\ 0& 1& -1& 0& 0\end{array}\right)$

### 4.1 Second-gradient linear elasticity

To use the second-gradient modellings one must define two constitutive laws in order to describe respectively the macroscopic relations between stresses ${\sigma}_{ij}$ and strains ${\epsilon}_{ij}$ and the microscopic relations between double stresses (${\Sigma}_{\text{ijk}}$ for the second gradient or ${S}_{j}$ for the second gradient of dilatation) and the strain gradient $\frac{\partial {f}_{ij}}{\partial {x}_{k}}$ for the second gradient or the volumetric-strain gradient $\frac{\partial \chi }{\partial {x}_{j}}$ for the second gradient of dilatation.

For the choice of the first-gradient type of behaviour — the one which intervenes in the local modelling described in section 3 — there is no restriction: all the constitutive laws are theoretically possible. ### 4.3 Estimation of the regularisation parameter A1 (2nd gradient of dilatation)

With the aim of characterising as well as possible the value to be assigned to the regularisation parameter, A1, of the 2nd gradient of dilatation, it is possible to determine an upper bound on this parameter as a function of the rate tangent matrix of any softening constitutive model.

In this way, the user will be able to determine, as a function of the size of the mesh cells of the problem that he treats, the value of the parameter A1 best suited to his problem.

This computation is based on an analytical $\mathrm{2D}$ problem of a shear band [bib2 and bib11].

Failure in geomaterials is often characterised by the formation of zones with localised strains, reflecting a passage from zones of homogeneous strains towards non-homogeneous strain modes. The appearance of this phenomenon can be seen on the theoretical level as the spontaneous change of the strain mode, assimilated to a bifurcation of a branch of the equilibrium, that is to say the intersection of two branches of solutions that are functions of the control parameters. Within the framework of continuous media, it is possible to extract, under restricted conditions, bifurcation criteria making it possible to identify the control parameter causing the appearance of the shear band, as well as the potential orientations of the latter. On the other hand, for classical continuous media, the “post-localisation” regime, notably the width of the shear band, cannot be characterised. The experimental results nevertheless show that this width is an element intrinsic to the properties of the material, linked to its microstructure (shape and size of the grains, Desrues and Viggiani [bib12]) and to its initial state (void ratio and stress state). The use of the local second-gradient-of-dilatation theory makes it possible to enrich the kinematics of the medium, thus reflecting the effects of the microstructure at the global scale. In this section, we shall extract from a two-dimensional analytical problem the elements making it possible to characterise the width of the shear band during the “post-localisation” regime.

The formulation of the problem in rate form is then written in the following form, considering the body forces constant:

$$
\underset{\Omega}{\int}{\dot{\sigma}}_{ij}{\dot{\epsilon}}_{ij}({u}^{\text{*}})+{\dot{S}}_{j}{\eta}_{j}({u}^{\text{*}})\mathrm{dv}=\underset{\partial \Omega }{\int}{\dot{F}}_{i}{u}_{i}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{ds}\forall {u}^{\text{*}}\in {\text{V}}_{0}
$$

${\dot{\sigma}}_{ij}$ , ${\dot{S}}_{j}$ and ${\dot{F}}_{i}$ are the time derivatives of the terms introduced in the equation.

#### 4.3.1 Analytical solution

The two-dimensional analytical problem addressed below was solved by [bib2] for a complete second-gradient model with the local elastoplastic Mohr–Coulomb model described by [bib13]. The example consists in applying the second-gradient-of-dilatation model in a sheared layer and in determining the solutions of the rate problem.

![Figure](/figures/r5.04.03/10000000000001DA00000183F5E847212F64364E.jpg)

Figure 4.3-a: Domain studied
One considers a layer defined in the plane $(x,y)$ with $z$ the axis perpendicular to the plane. Chambon et al. [bib2] consider an infinite layer lying between two planes defined by $x=0$ and $x=l$ (Figure ). The displacement fields are denoted $u$ in the direction $x$ and $v$ in the direction $y$ . $\dot{u}$ and $\dot{v}$ are assumed to depend only on $x$ and their derivatives with respect to $x$ will be denoted $'$ .

The initial state of the material is defined as homogeneous. The evolutions of the boundary conditions applied to the domain are the following:

- for $x=0$ , $\dot{u}=0$ , $\dot{v}=0$
- for $x=l$ , ${\dot{F}}_{i}$ are known.

The body forces are assumed constant.

The symmetry conditions applied to the boundary $x=0$ indicate that the domain studied has a total width of $\mathrm{2l}$ .

This problem can be seen as the analysis of the behaviour of a localisation band, where the orientation of the band is assumed and the stress state is left free of any restriction. The boundary of the domain at $x=l$ defines the transition zone between the localisation zone (domain studied) and the domain of an arbitrary solid where the solutions are assumed regular and stable.

The gradient of the displacement field in the domain takes the following form:

$$
{\dot{u}}_{i,j}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\dot{u}'& 0\\ \dot{v}'& 0\end{array}\right]
$$

The macroscopic strain field is written explicitly:

$$
{\dot{\epsilon}}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}({\dot{u}}_{i,j}+{\dot{u}}_{j,i})=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\dot{u}'& \frac{1}{2}\cdot \dot{v}'\\ \frac{1}{2}\cdot \dot{v}'& 0\end{array}\right]
$$

The only non-zero term of the gradient of the volumetric strains is:

$$
\frac{\partial {\dot{\epsilon}}_{v}}{\partial {x}_{1}}=\dot{u}\text{''}
$$

The application of the principle of virtual work to the imposed displacement field after 2 integrations by parts gives the following equilibrium equations for the rate problem:

$$
\frac{\partial {\dot{\sigma}}_{ij}}{\partial {x}_{j}}-\frac{{\partial}^{2}{\dot{S}}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{i}\partial {x}_{j}}=0\phantom{a}\Rightarrow \phantom{a}\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{\dot{\sigma}}_{11}^{'}-{\dot{S}}_{1}^{\text{''}}& =& 0\\ {\dot{\sigma}}_{12}^{'}& =& 0\end{array}
$$

The boundary conditions supply at $x=l$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{\dot{\sigma}}_{11}-{\dot{S}}_{1}^{'}& =& {\dot{F}}_{1}\\ {\dot{\sigma}}_{12}& =& {\dot{F}}_{2}\\ 3{a}^{1}{\dot{u}}^{'''}& =& 0\end{array}
$$

After spatial integration of the equilibrium equations taking into account the boundary conditions for a homogeneous problem, the solutions of the problem satisfy the following equations:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{\dot{\sigma}}_{11}-{\dot{S}}_{1}^{'}& =& {\dot{F}}_{1}\\ {\dot{\sigma}}_{12}& =& {\dot{F}}_{2}\end{array}
$$

By using the constitutive laws of the first and second gradients, the equations become:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{H}_{1111}{\dot{u}}^{'}+{H}_{1112}{\dot{v}}^{'}-{\mathrm{3a}}^{1}{\dot{u}}^{\text{'''}}& =& {\dot{F}}_{1}\\ {H}_{1211}{\dot{u}}^{'}+{H}_{1212}{\dot{v}}^{'}& =& {\dot{F}}_{2}\end{array}
$$

The system of coupled differential equations can be reduced to one equation, the second equation expressing a linear relation between the first gradients of the vertical and horizontal displacements in the band.

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{H}_{1111}{\dot{u}}^{'}+{H}_{1112}\frac{{\dot{F}}_{2}-{H}_{1211}{\dot{u}}^{'}}{{H}_{1212}}-{\mathrm{3a}}^{1}{\dot{u}}^{\text{'''}}& =& {\dot{F}}_{1}\\ \frac{{\dot{F}}_{2}-{H}_{1211}{\dot{u}}^{'}}{{H}_{1212}}& =& {\dot{v}}^{'}\end{array}
$$

The solutions of this system of linear differential equations of order 2 are expressed as the sum of a particular solution satisfying the imposed boundary conditions and of a partial solution established from the roots of the characteristic polynomial of this system: ${\dot{u}}^{'}={\dot{u}}_{0}^{'}+{\dot{u}}_{p}^{'}$

One assumes for ${\dot{u}}_{p}^{'}$ a solution of the type ${\dot{u}}_{p}^{'}={e}^{\eta x}$ which makes it possible to establish the following characteristic equation:

$$
{H}_{1111}-{H}_{1112}\frac{{H}_{1211}}{{H}_{1212}}-{\mathrm{3a}}^{1}{\eta}^{2}=0
$$

One can then give the expression of the partial solution as a function of the material parameters of the first and second gradient:

$$
\textrm{soit}
{\eta}^{2}=\frac{{H}_{1111}{H}_{1212}-{H}_{1112}{H}_{1211}}{{\mathrm{3a}}^{1}{H}_{1212}}
$$

The first-gradient constitutive law being elasto-plastic, the solutions will be different according to the state of the region considered. In a region with elastic behaviour, the constitutive law is written $\dot{\sigma}=A\dot{\epsilon}$ and in a region with plastic behaviour $\dot{\sigma}={H}^{\mathrm{ep}}\dot{\epsilon}$ .

Finally the solutions of the problem take the following forms as a function of the sign of

$$
{\eta}^{2}=\Delta =\frac{{H}_{1111}{H}_{1212}-{H}_{1112}{H}_{1211}}{{\mathrm{3a}}^{1}{H}_{1212}}
$$

- If $\Delta >0$ , then

$$
{\dot{u}}^{'}={\dot{u}}_{0}^{'}+({C}_{11}\exp({\eta}_{1}x)+{C}_{12}\exp({\eta}_{2}x))
$$

with ${\eta}_{i}=\pm \sqrt{\Delta}$ and ${\dot{u}}_{0}^{'}$ obtained from the equation of the particular solution:

$$
{\dot{u}}_{0}^{'}=\frac{{H}_{1212}{\dot{F}}_{1}-{H}_{1112}{\dot{F}}_{2}}{{H}_{1111}{H}_{1212}-{H}_{1112}{H}_{1211}}
\textrm{et}
{\dot{v}}_{0}^{'}=\frac{{H}_{1111}{\dot{F}}_{2}-{H}_{1211}{\dot{F}}_{1}}{{H}_{1111}{H}_{1212}-{H}_{1112}{H}_{1211}}
$$

Moreover, the partial solutions ${\dot{u}}_{p}^{'}$ satisfy the following equations:

$$
\frac{{H}_{1111}{H}_{1212}-{H}_{1112}{H}_{1211}}{{\mathrm{3a}}^{1}{H}_{1212}}{\dot{u}}_{p}^{'}-{\dot{u}}_{p}^{\text{'''}}=0
$$

From the symmetry conditions of the domain studied and ${\dot{u}}_{p}^{'}(l)=0$ , one deduces that the coefficients ${C}_{11}$ and ${C}_{12}$ are zero.

- If $\Delta <0$ , then

$$
{\dot{u}}^{'}={\dot{u}}_{0}^{'}+({C}_{21}\cos(\omega x)+{C}_{22}\sin(\omega x))
$$

with $\omega =\sqrt{\frac{{H}_{1211}{H}_{1112}-{H}_{1212}{H}_{1111}}{{\mathrm{3a}}^{1}{H}_{1212}}}$ . Just as for the solutions previously established for the zones where $\Delta >0$ , the partial solution ${\dot{u}}_{p}^{'}$ satisfies the equation.

The expression of the solution in the zone where $\Delta <0$ brings out trigonometric functions. It is therefore clear that a localisation zone can appear in the structure. One can then admit that the structure favours an internal length of ${l}_{c}=2\pi /\omega$ , expressed according to the first mode of lowest energy.

One also observes that the dependence of the internal length ${l}_{c}$ is in $\sqrt{{a}^{1}}$ . It is interesting to note likewise that the condition for the appearance of the bifurcation solutions is controlled solely by the terms of the first-gradient model. In other words, taking into account an enriched kinematics restricted to second-gradient media does not modify the value of the control parameter causing the appearance of a bifurcated solution in shear bands.

#### 4.3.2 Numerical solution

The elementary computation option PDIL_ELGA determines, for a given initial state of the stresses and internal variables, the value of A1_LC2 established according to the following formula:

$$
\text{A1\_LC2}=\frac{{a}^{1}}{{{l}_{c}}^{2}}=\frac{\left[{H}_{1211}{H}_{1112}-{H}_{1111}{H}_{1212}\right]}{3{H}_{1212}{(2\pi )}^{2}}
$$

for different orientations of the shear band. The explicit writing of the components of the stiffness tensor of the classical local model is obtained via the routines for the computation of the rate tangent matrices.

A rotation of the shear band by an angle $\theta$ implies a local rotation applied to the components of the stress tensor. It makes it possible to estimate the new components of the local constitutive tensor of the classical constitutive model, relating to the first gradient of the displacements.

The angular discretisation used is first of $5°$ . Around the first maximum recorded, one carries out a discretisation at $1°$ then $0.2°$ . In this way, one ensures that one obtains a precise value of the parameter A1_LC2.

The user can then define the value of A1 suited to the spatial discretisation of the structure studied, knowing that a minimum of 6 elements over LC appears necessary in order to guarantee the mesh-independence of the results.

The numerical validation of the computation option PDIL_ELGA is carried in the test cases SSNV208A, SSNP125A and WTNV132C.
