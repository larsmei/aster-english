# u2.05.08 Carrying out an analysis for the prediction of cleavage fracture

**Summary:**

This document is intended to supply the information necessary to a user wishing to simulate a fracture by cleavage.

The definition of cleavage is first recalled. One then presents in turn the models available for predicting this type of fracture, and methodological and implementation advice. The models described here are, in turn: Beremin, Bordet, Gp and Le Corre.

## 1 Introduction

Elastic fracture mechanics, based on the classical criteria of energy release rate, of contour integral $J$ and of stress intensity factor $K$, does not, in the general case, make it possible to treat problems in which plasticity plays an important role. In this case, other approaches must be put in place. In the case of proportional monotonic loading, the two-parameter approaches, such as the approaches $(J,Q)$ or $(K,T)$ ([1]), are generally satisfactory. Unfortunately, the domain of validity of these approaches is limited to proportional loadings.

This is why elastoplastic fracture mechanics, which must make it possible to extend the validity of fracture mechanics, is being developed. The cleavage mechanism (with confined plasticity) falls particularly within its scope.

The objective of this document is to supply methodological assistance for the use of the models of elastoplastic fracture mechanics within the framework of the prediction of cleavage. It in no way dispenses one from reading the Reference and User documents relating to the models and commands in question here.

The phenomenon of cleavage is first explained briefly. The four available models (Beremin, Bordet, ${G}_{P}$ and Le Corre) are described in turn, with assistance for their respective use. These models being of the post-processing type of a thermo-mechanical analysis, it is appropriate that this analysis be as reliable as possible, and therefore that the precautions recalled here be taken.

## 2 Brief general points on cleavage

This paragraph is in particular inspired by [2], to which one may refer for more details, microstructural ones in particular.

Cleavage is a fracture mode whose principal mechanism is the separation of the atomic planes, practically without strain. It is the principal mechanism of brittle fracture in metals, in particular in the case of the low crystal symmetries such as the body-centred cubic or the hexagonal ones. This fracture mode being in competition with plastic strain, it is facilitated by a low temperature (the deformation mechanisms are then less activated). This leads to the existence of a brittle (at low temperature)–ductile (at higher temperature) transition. Among the models which are defined here, the two deterministic methods are at present tested in the case of the transition zone in order to predict the risk of cleavage. This use nevertheless requires precautions; to our knowledge, no method can to this day be considered reliable for predicting the risk of cleavage in the transition zone.

The morphology of the cleavage fracture surfaces corresponds to a transgranular propagation. It can easily be observed by microscopy, as in the Figure. It is frequently characterised by the presence of lines parallel to the direction of propagation, which one calls rivers.

![Figure](/figures/u2.05.08/10000200000000D5000000E9F8FBE83B76C37FC6.png)

Figure 2-1: Microscopic fracture appearance of cleavage

Fracture by cleavage, like the other modes of fracture by cracking, distinguishes in theory two mechanisms: initiation and propagation. Initiation corresponds to the development of a microcrack inside the sound metal; it is generally accepted that this stage requires a fairly small prior plastic strain, which gives rise to a pile-up of dislocations and a singularity of the stresses, and the reaching of a limit fracture stress, henceforth denoted ${\sigma}_{c}$. The propagation of the cleavage cracks is unstable, of high speed (of the order of 40% of the speed of sound in the metal), and must satisfy an energy criterion of Griffith type [3].

Cleavage not being accompanied by large strains, it does not require a large energy, unlike ductile fracture. This is why the Charpy test, measuring the resilience (energy necessary for fracture by impact of a standardised specimen), makes it possible to distinguish these two types of fracture for one and the same material (see the Figure). At low temperature, the energy necessary for fracture is low: the fracture mode is cleavage. At high temperature, the energy necessary for fracture is high: fracture takes place by tearing. The limit temperature between these two phenomena is called the transition temperature; there exist several definitions of it, including those based on a given energy level (one speaks for example of ${T}_{\mathrm{k65}}$, which would be equal here to about $-20°C$).

The irradiation of metals induces an increase of the yield stress and makes the pile-up of dislocations more difficult, and *in fine* favours cleavage: the transition temperature of an irradiated material is higher than that of the same non-irradiated material. In the transition zone, that is to say the temperature range close to this transition temperature, fracture may occur by cleavage, by tearing, or by a tearing over a finite length followed by cleavage. The prediction of cleavage in the transition zone remains in the domain of research: even if methodologies are proposed here, they still require validation and must not be seen as “turnkey” methods.

## 3 Methodology of an analysis with the Gp energy model

The energy model ${G}_{P}$ is developed at EDF R&D. It is deterministic and therefore makes it possible to write a criterion that is simple to interpret. It makes it possible to treat all types of loading and takes account of numerous effects (small defect, triaxiality, warm preloading), and studies in order to validate it in the ductile–brittle transition are in progress.

### 3.1 Theoretical framework

The energy model ${G}_{P}$ is initially based on the variational approach to fracture proposed by Francfort and Marigo [4]. It makes it possible to define an initiation criterion valid in the cleavage domain whatever the type of loading. It is characterised by the representation of the crack by a notch, the principle of minimisation of the energy, and it postulates that initiation can be described by an energy criterion. It makes it possible in particular to take account of the small-defect effects, of triaxiality, and of the effects of warm preloading (which are however underestimated). For more details, one may refer to [5] or [R7.02.16](doc:r7.02.16).

One specifies here the elements essential to the use of this approach in code_aster, within the framework of cleavage and of the transition zone.

### 3.2 Necessary mesh

In every case, the mesh must comprise a defect (crack) represented by a notch (crack whose front is not a tip but a semicircle) of initial radius ${R}_{0}$. This radius is a parameter to be identified. Its identification is described further on. In the case of proportional loading, one may use any radius that is sufficiently small.

#### 3.2.1 In 2D

In 2D, the user has the choice between two types of mesh.

In the first case, the mesh must comprise specific analysis zones called chips, each chip being defined by a mesh-cell group. An example of a mesh with chips is presented in the Figure. The size of the chips must be supplied by the user. One advises using a chip thickness (mesh-cell size ${t}_{e}$) at most equal to one fifth of the initial notch radius ${R}_{0}$.

The user may however use in 2D an arbitrary mesh (with representation of the defect by a notch) sufficiently refined in the zone close to the notch. The chips are then defined automatically with user parameters. An example of a refined mesh usable for the method is presented in the Figure.

![Figure](/figures/u2.05.08/1000000000000534000003600620F6357A2570DF.png)

Finally, a user who does not have a specific dedicated 2D mesh may also use the command RAFF_GP in order to obtain a mesh that is sufficiently fine and regular in the zone of interest of the notch (the chips). The notch must nevertheless be represented geometrically, and the mesh sufficiently regular in the chip zone, with “clean” mesh cells (weakly distorted).

#### 3.2.2 In 3D

One of the principal difficulties of the use of this approach in 3D is due to the fact that it requires one to have produced a mesh defining the analysis zones known as chips and slice (see the Figure). This zone must be meshed with hexahedra. One advises using a chip thickness (mesh-cell size ${t}_{e}$) at most equal to one fifth of the initial notch radius ${R}_{0}$:

$$
{t}_{e}\le \frac{{R}_{0}}{5}
$$

The notion of slice corresponds to an angular sector in the direction of propagation of the notch (see the Figure).

In the case of a semi-elliptical front, it is advised to produce a mesh containing at least 16 slices along the notch front, or even 32 or 64 if it is elongated.

The meshes are preferably quadratic, which makes it possible, for a given number of nodes, to have a more accurate analysis and to capture the stress gradients better.

The chip mesh-cell groups must furthermore be arranged in a list of mesh-cell groups arranged slice by slice in the following manner (for p slices of n chips): tranche_1_copeau_1, tranche_1_copeau_2, ..., tranche_1_copeau_n, tranche_2_copeau_1, ..., tranche_p_copeau_n.

### 3.3 Identification of the parameters

The energy model is based on two parameters: the initial notch radius ${R}_{0}$ and the critical parameter ${G}_{\mathrm{pc}}$. These two parameters are to be identified from a test on a CT specimen.

One assumes known, for the material considered:

- Young's modulus $E$,
- the critical stress ${\sigma}_{c}$,
- the surface energy ${G}_{C}$.

The parameter ${G}_{\mathit{PC}}$ is determined by simulation of a test on a $\mathit{CT}$ specimen in which the crack is represented by a notch of given radius $R$. This parameter corresponds to the maximum spatial value (over all the chips of all the slices) of the parameter ${G}_{P}$ when the loading is such that $G={G}_{C}$.

The identification procedure is as follows:

- mesh with definition of the chips in 3D, with or without definition of the chips in 2D
- carrying out of the elastoplastic mechanical analysis; it is advised to use under-integrated elements (D_PLAN_SI, 3D_SI, etc.) in order to reduce the effects of incompressibility
- first post-processing: computation of the energy release rate by the operator CALC_G
- post-processing of ${G}_{P}$ *via* the command CALC_GP

The identification procedure presented here is valid for a given temperature. Strictly speaking, one would have to identify the pair of parameters $({G}_{\mathrm{PC}},{R}_{0})$ for several temperatures in the range of interest of the analysis. However, even if the temperature of the component evolves in the course of time, one readily understands that it is not possible to make the notch radius evolve in the course of the analysis. This is why the global identification must be carried out at a temperature close to the temperature of future interest (that at which one wishes to carry out the prediction of cleavage); if other temperatures are necessary, one will keep the radius ${R}_{0}$ initially identified and one will identify afresh only the critical value ${G}_{\mathrm{PC}}$.

### 3.4 Prediction of the cleavage of a structure

The energy model has been validated for numerous situations in the cleavage zone, but still requires validation tests in the transition zone (see the Figure for the definition of the cleavage or transition zones).

The first question to be asked is the following: shall I carry out the mechanical analysis (the one to which I am going to apply my cleavage criterion) in large or small strains?

This choice must be carried over to the identification procedure defined above: **the mechanical identification analysis on the CT specimen must be conducted with the same type of strains and the same radius as the study on the structure.**

The methodology to be followed is as follows:

- identify the parameters of the approach ${G}_{\mathrm{PC}}$ and ${R}_{0}$ on a CT specimen (with a notch, of course).
- produce a mesh of the structure of notch type with chips, as defined in paragraph 3.2, with the notch radius ${R}_{0}$.
- conduct the mechanical simulation of the structure with the same type of strain as that of the identification, preferably with an under-integrated formulation (D_PLAN_SI, 3D_SI, etc.)
- post-process with the operator CALC_GP.

Initiation will take place when ${G}_{P}$ reaches, inside the chip zone (wherever that may be), the value ${G}_{\mathrm{PC}}$; the critical value is generally reached at a non-zero distance from the crack front.

## 4 Methodology for the prediction of cleavage with the Beremin model or its industrial version

The Beremin model has a high international recognition. It makes it possible to take account of numerous effects (small defect, triaxiality, warm preloading) in situations of complex loading. It is a probabilistic model.

The industrial version of the Beremin model is a deterministic approach which relies on the surface density of Weibull stress [12] [14].

### 4.1 Theoretical framework

The Beremin model is based on the existence of a mechanical cleavage stress. Starting from this point, an estimate of the probability that a damage site (micro-defect) reaches the cleavage stress is established. By using a weakest-link assumption (if one site breaks, the entire structure breaks), it is then possible to determine the probability of complete fracture of the structure from the stress fields (and of plasticity within it).

The industrial version of the Beremin model considers that, in the most penalising case, the instability of brittle fracture comes from the interaction between the maximum principal stress $\sigma_I$ and a distribution of initiation sites liable to initiate cleavage in the plane perpendicular to the most highly loaded defect. The combination of these two factors leads one to quantify the risk of sudden fracture by means of a surface density of Weibull stress, denoted $\sigma_W^{2D}$. It is a deterministic approach.

For more details, one may refer to [R7.02.04](doc:r7.02.04), [6] or [14].

One specifies here the elements essential to the use of these two approaches (Beremin model and its industrial version), within the framework of cleavage and of the transition zone.

### 4.2 Mesh and modelling

This model being based on the distribution of the stresses in a neighbourhood of the defect, it is necessary to take precautions in the choice of the mesh and of the finite elements at the crack tip.

The use of elements with a gradient of internal variables (GRAD_INCO or GRAD_VARI) is the available approach giving the most accurate stress field at the crack tip. In practice, within the framework of an industrial approach, it is rather recommended to use the same modelling assumptions for the reference specimen and the target structure [13].

The mesh-cell size perpendicularly to the crack front must be sufficiently fine to capture the stress field close to the defect, of the order of $50 \mu m$ or less. Indeed, the distance from the crack tip at which the maximum of the stresses $\sigma_I$ is reached is approximately $\frac{2K_I^2}{E \sigma_Y}$, which corresponds to a few tens of micrometres for a low-alloy steel. The mesh-cell size must be identical between the mesh of the geometry of interest and the mesh of the reference geometry. In order to be able to mesh components of large size, one advises choosing mesh cells of the order of $50 \mu m$, as was recommended in the historical studies on the Beremin model [6].

The analysis formalism may be that of the small perturbations or of the large transformations (large displacements accompanied by large strains). Once again, it is appropriate to adopt a formalism that is consistent between the simulation on the reference geometry and the simulation on the geometry of interest.

### 4.3 Identification of the parameters

Several material parameters enter into the definition of the Beremin model: ${V}_{0}$, $m$ and potentially ${\sigma}_{u}$ and ${\sigma}_{th}$.

In practice, the exponent $m$ expresses the scatter of the defects liable to initiate brittle fracture and ${\sigma}_{u}$ may be likened to the critical fracture stress of an elementary volume ${V}_{0}$. The threshold stress ${\sigma}_{th}$ corresponds to the stress below which fracture by cleavage may be excluded. The three parameters ${\sigma}_{u}$, ${\sigma}_{th}$ and $m$ are characteristic of the material considered. The elementary volume ${V}_{0}$ must be large enough to encompass about ten grains and small enough for the stress field to be quasi-homogeneous. One uses the same value of ${V}_{0}$ in the analyses D_PLAN_SI, AXIS, 3D_SI, etc. because the type of modelling (that is to say the manner in which the plastified volume is computed) is already defined by the operand COEF_MULT in POST_BEREMIN.

The identification of these parameters is not unique, but depends on the choice made for the modelling. In the same way, the parameters must be identified on an analysis presenting the same characteristics as the prediction analysis (same definition of the Weibull stress).

An example of such an identification in an industrial framework is for example presented in [13]:

- Identification of the parameters entering into the computation of the Weibull stress with the *Beremin model*
- Application of the *industrial version of the Beremin model* with the same set of material parameters

### 4.4 Prediction of the cleavage of a structure

Once the parameters have been identified, the analysis is conducted with the same recommendations as for the identification and with the same type of modelling (mesh-cell size at the crack tip, type of finite elements, options for the computation of the Weibull stress).

With the probabilistic Beremin model, it is possible, for a given loading level, to know the probability of fracture by cleavage of the structure. One will note that, in the procedures for determining the risks of cleavage, one generally seeks the loadings leading to fracture probabilities of 5%, 50% and 95%.

The industrial version of the Beremin model is deterministic [12]. If at every point of the crack front the loading of the structure is lower than the maximum loading seen by a hypothetical CT specimen presenting an envelope toughness of the material considered, then the structure will not be able to fracture suddenly.

In practice, one determines a curve $K_J \ - \sigma_W^{2D}$ on a reference CT specimen. The local loading of an arbitrary structure, denoted $K_J ^{CT}$, is obtained for the same surface density of Weibull stress $\sigma_W^{2D}$ as the reference CT specimen. Fracture of the structure is excluded if $K_J ^{CT} < K_J^C$

## 5 Methodology for the prediction of cleavage with the probabilistic Bordet model

The Bordet model is relatively recent (2005). It was introduced in 2010 into *Code_Aster*, and thus this model does not for the moment have a very substantial feedback of experience at EDF R&D. It has benefited from the reputation of the Beremin model in order to penetrate the scientific world fairly well, but remains confined to the industrial world. It is presented as better in the cases of loading with large plastic strains.

### 5.1 Theoretical framework

The Bordet model is a modification of the Beremin model, based on the same microstructural foundations and the same weakest-link assumption. The difference comes from the account taken of plasticity. In the Beremin model, one assumes the creation of microcracks at the moment when the plasticity threshold is reached, and these microcracks remain potentially active throughout the loading which follows. However, in steels, the global fracture is principally linked to newly created microcracks. It is therefore appropriate to take account of the level of plastic strain reached at each time instant. In the Bordet model, this is taken into account by considering that the probability of fracture by cleavage is the product of the probability of nucleation and of propagation at the same time instant. For more details, one may refer to [R7.02.06](doc:r7.02.06) and [10].

### 5.2 Necessary mesh and modelling

This model being a modification of the Beremin model, the same recommendations may be made. One will therefore refer to Paragraph 4.2.

### 5.3 Identification of the parameters

The principal defect of the Bordet model is its large number of material parameters to be identified: 7 in its complete version, and 6 in its simplified version (accessible by PROBA_NUCL = 'NON').

The first three parameters, ${V}_{0},{\sigma}_{u}$ and $m$, have the same meaning as in the Beremin model; however, the author states that their respective value is not necessarily that of the classical Beremin model...

To these is added a cleavage threshold stress ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{th}}$, which may be taken equal to the critical fracture stress ${\sigma}_{c}$ useful for the identification of the energy model, and the yield stress ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{ys}}$ of the material at a reference temperature and as a function of the temperature and of the plastic strain rate.

The last parameter, useful only in the case of the complete model, is the reference plastic strain of cleavage, ${\varepsilon}_{p,0}$, for which a cleavage microcrack, nucleated by plastic strain, is deactivated if it has not immediately caused fracture. In practice, the authors of the model themselves dispense with this parameter... One therefore advises, unless one obtains a value for it, using the version of the model which does not require this parameter.

In the end, one possibility is to use the version without nucleation probability (PROBA_NUCL = 'NON'), to use the Beremin parameters ${V}_{0},{\sigma}_{u}$ and $m$ (procedure described in Paragraph 4.3), the fracture stress and the yield stresses identified on a simple tensile test at different loading rates.

The other possibility, more delicate, is to simulate both tests on CT specimens and on Charpy specimens and to identify the whole set of parameters from the experimental data.

In both cases, one may use a pragmatic or an advanced modelling (see Paragraph 4.2), which lead to different values.

## 6 Methodology for the prediction of cleavage with the Le Corre model

The Le Corre model is a model developed by the Commissariat à l'Energie Atomique (CEA) with the principal aim of characterising qualitatively the initiation (or the propagation) of a defect: cleavage or ductile. It is at present in the course of validation by the CEA, is still subject to modifications/enrichments, and is used principally in the ductile–brittle transition zone. For the moment it is not the subject of a dedicated command in *Code_Aster*, but may nevertheless be used by “indirect” means made explicit here. These indirect means will very shortly be made simpler by programmed evolutions of the code.

### 6.1 Theoretical framework

The Le Corre model relies on arguments close to those of Beremin and falls within the framework of the approaches known as “local”. It is based on the distribution of the stresses around the defect in order to determine whether the initiation will be stable or unstable (cleavage). It does not, on the other hand, set out to determine the instant of initiation, but indeed its type. In order to define the moment of initiation, it is generally coupled to a classical method based on the integral $J$ in particular.

For more details, one may refer to [11].

### 6.2 Necessary mesh and modelling

This model being based on the distribution of the stresses in a zone close to the defect front (notch or crack), particular care must be taken with the mechanical analysis. Furthermore, the criterion determining the moment of initiation being based on the energy release rate, it is appropriate to follow the advice for the proper conduct of an analysis of this type, given in [U2.05.01](doc:u2.05.01).

The CEA uses free crack-type quadratic meshes, with mesh-cell sizes around the defect of $50$ to $100\mu m$. In order to increase the quality of the result, in particular for the computation of the fracture-mechanics parameters by the $\theta$ method, one nevertheless advises using either a mesh such as defined in Paragraph 4.2.2, or a crack-type mesh refined at the defect front, and preferably radiating for a better computation of the energy release rate $G$.

### 6.3 Identification of the parameters

The Le Corre model is based on two material parameters: a limit stress denoted here ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{LC}}$ and a limit volume denoted here ${V}_{\mathit{th}}$. The first parameter is identified from the experimental fracture data of an axisymmetric notched specimen, whereas the second is identified on the simulation of a CT specimen.

By assumption, the limit stress ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{LC}}$ is supposed independent of the temperature. In order to determine it, one needs to have tensile tests on an AE specimen at a temperature low enough for it to fracture in cleavage (in practice, the temperature is generally of the order of $-150°C$).

One defines the mean stress at fracture as follows:

${\sigma}_{\mathrm{moy}}=\frac{\mathrm{4F}}{\pi {D}_{0}^{2}(1-\frac{\Delta D}{{D}_{0}})}$

With the notations of the Figure.

One then determines the smallest mean stress at the moment of fracture of the various tests, and one defines:

${\sigma}_{\mathrm{LC}}=1.2\mathrm{Min}({\sigma}_{\mathrm{moy}})$

The identification of the second parameter, ${V}_{\mathrm{th}}$, requires the knowledge of the results of tensile tests on a CT specimen at low temperature (fracture must take place for a very small propagation of the crack), that is to say *a minima* the value of the energy release rate at fracture.

Two 2D numerical simulations of the test are carried out. A first one in small strains (for the computation of the energy release rate), and a second one in large strains (for the computation of ${V}_{\mathrm{th}}$). One then plots the curve $J$ / defect opening for the simulation in small strains. One places oneself at the value of $J$ at fracture and one determines the defect opening ${\mathrm{ouv}}_{\mathrm{rup}}$ at this loading level.

On the simulation in large strains, one seeks the loading level such that the opening is equal to ${\mathrm{ouv}}_{\mathrm{rup}}$. For this loading level, one determines the volume of material over which the stress exceeds the value ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{LC}}$. This step may be carried out by means of the command POST_ELEM in the following manner:

VOLUM=POST_ELEM(

One multiplies this figure by the total volume of the component, and one obtains the parameter ${V}_{\mathrm{th}}$.

The identification of the parameters is summarised as follows:

- on tensile tests on AE specimens at low temperature, determine the mean stress at fracture ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{moy}}$;
- deduce from it the parameter ${\sigma}_{\mathit{LC}}=1.2\mathit{Min}({\sigma}_{\mathit{moy}})$;
- carry out a simulation, in 2D small strains, of tension on a CT specimen whose $J$ at fracture is known;
- determine the defect opening when the $J$ at fracture is reached ($J$ computed by CALC_G);
- carry out a simulation, in 2D large strains, of the same test on the CT specimen;
- determine the loading level for which the opening of the defect equals the opening at fracture obtained in small strains;
- at this loading level, use the command POST_ELEM in order to know the volume of material over which the maximum principal stress exceeds the stress ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{LC}}$: this volume is equal to ${V}_{\mathrm{th}}$ (caution with the symmetries: a multiplicative factor must be added in order to account for the symmetries).

### 6.4 Prediction of the cleavage of a structure

As for the other models, one must use the same type of simulation as that carried out for the identification of the parameters (crack or fine notch).

One then carries out two thermo-mechanical analyses of the structure: one in small strains, the other in large strains.

The analysis in small strains makes it possible to know the initiation loading, which is such that the integral $J$ reaches the $J$ at fracture (in general, the CEA uses the parameter ${J}_{0.2}$, corresponding to the value of $J$ for an initiation of $\mathrm{0.2mm}$ on a CT specimen). At this loading level, one determines the opening of the defect.

The analysis in large strains makes it possible to know the nature of the initiation; one places oneself at a loading level such that the opening in large strains is equal to the opening in small strains when the $J$ at fracture is reached. At this loading level, one determines the volume of material ${V}_{\mathrm{LC}}$ over which the maximum principal stress exceeds ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{LC}}$: ${V}_{\mathrm{LC}}=\frac{\sum_{i}({V}_{i}\vee {\sigma}_{i}\ge {\sigma}_{\mathrm{LC}})}{{V}_{\mathrm{total}}}$

However, the parameter ${V}_{\mathrm{th}}$ has been identified on 2D tests. Furthermore, one suspects that if the crack front has an infinite length, the exceedance volume ${V}_{\mathrm{LC}}$ will likewise tend towards infinity. This is why the volume ${V}_{\mathrm{LC}}$ must be divided by the length of the crack front ${L}_{\mathrm{front}}$. If $\frac{{V}_{\mathrm{LC}}}{{L}_{\mathrm{front}}}<{V}_{\mathrm{th}}$, then cleavage cannot take place, and the initiation will be ductile; if on the contrary $\frac{{V}_{\mathrm{LC}}}{{L}_{\mathrm{front}}}>{V}_{\mathrm{th}}$, then the risk of cleavage is non-zero and increases with ${V}_{\mathrm{LC}}$. There exists an expression of the probability of cleavage as a function of ${V}_{\mathrm{LC}}$, but it has not been sufficiently validated up to the present for us to mention it here. It is however described in [11].

### 6.5 Precautions and limits of this approach

From our point of view, this approach presents a few limits and precautions which are mentioned briefly here.

First of all, the limit initiation loading is defined by the integral $J$; the latter being rigorously computable only in elasticity (linear or not), the initiation loading cannot theoretically be determined when one leaves elasticity. However, in the case of monotonic proportional loading, the elastoplastic analysis may be considered as non-linear elastic, and the computation of $J$ is valid. Be that as it may, the method does not make it possible to predict the moment of initiation for an arbitrary loading.

Furthermore, in order to use the model completely, it is appropriate to carry out an analysis in small strains and an analysis in large strains, and to establish a link from the defect openings in the two cases. This type of analogy, although common, is not entirely satisfactory.

These first two remarks could be got round by using another criterion for determining the initiation loading, computable in large strains and valid for an arbitrary loading. Unfortunately, this is not possible for the moment.

At the level of the type of initiation itself, the criterion is based on a volume of exceedance of a critical value of stress in the zone close to the defect. Now, in the zones close to the defect, and even more so when the latter is modelled by a crack, the computation of the stresses is difficult because of the singularities generated and of the problems of incompressibility. It is therefore appropriate to visualise the stresses (in particular the hydrostatic ones, not the equivalent ones) at the defect front and to make sure that their distribution is physical. A common practice is to take the average of the stresses per element and to use it for the computation of the volume; one will recall that the average of two wrong results is not necessarily right.

## 7 Conclusion

In code_aster, the prediction of cleavage may be carried out with four models; three of them (Beremin, Bordet, Le Corre) are based on a stress criterion (approach known as local), the last one (${G}_{P}$) being based on the elastic energy (approach known as energy). Their respective domains of validity make it possible to cover the situations of complex loading, in particular the cases of non-proportional loading. The analyses in the ductile/brittle transition zone pose more difficulty, and still deserve fairly extensive validation.

The principal piece of advice is to ensure that the thermo-mechanical analysis proceeds as well as possible. If the mechanical fields are computed in too approximate a manner, the quality of the predictions will be affected.

The second piece of advice is to compare the predictions of several of the approaches and, as for all numerical simulations, to take a critical look at the results obtained.
