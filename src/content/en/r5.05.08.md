# r5.05.08 Modelling of cracked rotors by an equivalent stiffness as a function of the rotation angle

**Summary:**

This document describes a method for taking cracks into account in rotors modelled in 1D.

It is based on theoretical developments described in notes [1] and [2] and published in [3], establishing, under certain hypotheses, the equivalence between a non-linear 3D model with a contact computation and a beam model in which the non-linearity due to the crack is simulated by an equivalent stiffness concentrated between two beam elements and a tabulated non-linear function of the angle of the bending moment with respect to the crack. This method, used in the shaft-line computation code CADYRO, has been extended and validated against experiment on the EUROPE test rig [4]. It is taken up in the operator DYNA_VIBRA of *Code_Aster*.

## 1 Introduction and objective

For reasons of performance and of practical implementation, shaft lines in dynamics are not modelled by 3D finite elements but by beam elements, which naturally represent the slenderness of the rotors and which, in linear elasticity, approximate the real behaviour of the system at a low cost.

However, one wishes to take into account, in the analysis of the dynamics of shaft lines, the impact of a crack on the rotor. If the 3D analysis, with a fine modelling of the behaviour of the crack by a contact algorithm, is conceptually conceivable, it is extremely demanding in computing resources. This is why one models the behaviour of the crack on the usual beam model of the shaft line by creating two nodes on the axis of the cracked rotor at the location of the crack and by introducing a non-linear “crack constitutive law” linking the mean rotations of the two nodes representing the lips of the crack to the bending moments applied to the beam.

The crack constitutive law is tabulated from 3D analyses. One shows that it is not necessary to sweep the whole field of the pairs $(\mathit{My},\mathit{Mz})$ but that one analysis for each angle of the bending moment with respect to the crack is sufficient.

## 2 Hypotheses

The hypotheses used to build the 1D crack model are:

- the rotor has an isotropic elastic behaviour;
- the crack may be of arbitrary shape but it is contained in a beam cross-section;
- the lips of the crack are linked by a contact condition *without* friction;
- the normal and shear forces are neglected (under certain hypotheses one can take the torsional forces into account, but within the framework of this element they are omitted);
- the initial gap of the crack is zero;
- the behaviour of the crack is quasi-static and the inertia effects are negligible.

## 3 Frames and notations

By default the local frame of the beams in Code_Aster is the axis $X$ . This convention is retained.

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.08/Shape1.gif)

The cracked-rotor element is characterised by:

- its length $\mathrm{2L}$ ;
- its radius $R$ ;
- the position of the cracked section (at the middle of the rotor)

One considers the following mechanical characteristics:

- Young's modulus: $E$
- Inertia of the uncracked beam: $I$

One follows the classical description of beam kinematics. The cross-sections of the beam perpendicular to it remain straight and their rotation is described by:

$\theta =\left(\begin{array}{c}{\theta}_{y}(x)\\ {\theta}_{z}(x)\end{array}\right)$

The beam is subjected to a bending moment:

$M=(\begin{array}{c}{M}_{y}\\ {M}_{z}\end{array})$ , which may open or close the crack.

The strain energy of the cracked part of the rotor under the imposed bending loading is denoted ${W}^{f}(M)$ .

The strain energy contributed by the discrete element modelling the crack for a rotation discontinuity $[\theta ]$ is denoted ${W}^{d}([\theta ])$ .

The description of the cracked section, although averaged in the beam modelling, nevertheless requires a certain amount of attention, because the orientation of the response at the crack is not systematically the same as that of the force. It depends on the shape of the crack.

Two angles are therefore defined in the frame of the cracked section:

- $\Phi$ , the orientation of the imposed force in the rotating frame;
- $\varphi$ , the orientation of the response in the rotating frame.

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.08/10000000000002450000022884C9E79C1803F4BF.png)

Finally, one denotes by $s(M)$ the local flexibility of the equivalent 1D element representing the crack and by $k([\theta ])$ its local stiffness.

## 4 Determination of the equivalent stiffness of the crack

### 4.1 Equivalent crack model

One seeks in this chapter to determine the constitutive law $M=f([\theta ])$ relating, for the 1D crack element, the bending forces $M$ to the strains expressed by the rotation jump $[\theta ]=\left(\begin{array}{c}[{\theta}_{y}(x)]\\ [{\theta}_{z}(x)]\end{array}\right)$ of the lips of the crack represented by the two boundary nodes of the crack.

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.08/10000000000004F20000013684BB7AAC93364A46.png)

### 4.2 Strain energy

#### 4.2.1 Formula for the strain energy at imposed force

In [1] one establishes that the energy of the 3D model in quasi-statics is given by a “Clapeyron” formula:

${W}^{f}({M}_{y},{M}_{z})=\frac{1}{2}\left({M}_{y}{\theta}_{y}(\mathrm{2L})+{M}_{z}{\theta}_{z}(\mathrm{2L})\right)$

On the 3D finite-element model, one can, thanks to this formula, compute the energy of the system for all the possible pairs $({M}_{y},{M}_{z})$ and determine the equivalent stiffness $k({M}_{y},{M}_{z})$ for the cracked 1D beam element.

However, exploring a space equivalent to ${ℝ}^{2}$ would be too costly for a practical application. The properties of the energy functional of the elastic problem with contact make it possible to reduce the problem to a single variable.

One shows that ${W}^{f}$ is convex and positively homogeneous of degree 2 with respect to the applied moments:

$\forall \lambda \ge 0,{W}^{f}(\lambda {M}_{y},\lambda {M}_{z})={\lambda}^{2}{W}^{f}({M}_{y},{M}_{z})$

This property is interesting because it means, in practice, that the contact zone on the lips of the crack is independent of the amplitude $M$ of the bending. The energy is quadratic in $M$ , as in the linear case. Its form depends only on the direction of $({M}_{y},{M}_{z})$ . One can therefore rewrite the energy function with, as variables, the amplitude of the bending force $\mid \mid M\mid \mid$ and its angle $\phi$ .

In the cylindrical frame the moment is written:

$\begin{array}{c}{M}_{y}=\mid \mid M\mid \mid \cos\varphi \\ {M}_{z}=\mid \mid M\mid \mid \sin\varphi \end{array}$ and the strain energy: ${W}_{f}(M)=\frac{1}{2}{\mid \mid M\mid \mid }^{2}S(\varphi )$

The problem reduces to the identification of $S(\phi )$ on the interval $[0,2\pi ]$ .

The energy is rewritten in a form which distinguishes the flexibility $S(\varphi )$ , contributed by the beams (sound and cracked parts), and the flexibility $s(\phi )$ , contributed by the crack:

${W}^{f}(M)=\frac{L}{\mathit{EI}}{\mid \mid M\mid \mid }^{2}(1+s(\varphi ))$

By computing the strain energy on a 3D finite-element model of the cracked bar (figure ), one can determine $s(\varphi )$ .

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.08/100000000000027D000000CE071208D9BF190E32.jpg)

Illustration 1: 3D mesh of the cracked bar

#### 4.2.2 Energy at imposed displacement

It remains now to establish the relation between the energy and $[\theta ]$ . One uses for this the energy at imposed displacement ${w}^{d}$ . It possesses the same properties of convexity and of homogeneity of degree 2 as the energy at imposed force.

Setting:

$\begin{array}{c}[{\theta}_{x}]=\mid \mid [\theta ]\mid \mid \cos(\phi )\\ [{\theta}_{y}]=\mid \mid [\theta ]\mid \mid \sin(\phi )\end{array}$

one obtains the following expression for the strain energy:

${w}^{d}([\theta ])=\frac{\mathit{EI}}{\mathrm{4L}}{\mid \mid [\theta ]\mid \mid }^{2}k(\phi )$

#### 4.2.3 Constitutive law of the cracked element

One obtains $M$ as a function of $[\theta ]$ by differentiation of the energy:

$\begin{array}{c}{M}_{y}=\frac{\mathit{EI}}{\mathrm{4L}}\frac{\partial (k(\varphi )({[{\theta}_{y}]}^{2}+{[{\theta}_{z}]}^{2}))}{\partial {[\theta ]}_{y}}\\ {M}_{z}=\frac{\mathit{EI}}{\mathrm{4L}}\frac{\partial (k(\varphi )({[{\theta}_{y}]}^{2}+{[{\theta}_{z}]}^{2}))}{\partial {[\theta ]}_{z}}\end{array}$ with $[\theta ]=\sqrt{{[{\theta}_{y}]}^{2}+{[{\theta}_{z}]}^{2}}(\begin{array}{c}\cos(\varphi )\\ \sin(\varphi )\end{array})$

A few intermediate computations lead to the following relations:

$\left(\begin{array}{c}{M}_{y}\\ {M}_{z}\end{array}\right)=\frac{\mathit{EI}}{\mathrm{2L}}\left(\begin{array}{c}k(\phi )-\frac{1}{2}k'(\phi )\\ \frac{1}{2}k'(\phi )k(\phi )\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}{c}[{\theta}_{y}(x)]\\ [{\theta}_{z}(x)]\end{array}\right)$ where $\varphi =\arctan(\frac{[{\theta}_{z}(x)]}{[{\theta}_{y}(x)]})$

#### 4.2.4 Relation between the stiffness of the crack and its opening angle

The direction $\phi$ of the mean opening angle of the crack $[\theta ]$ is not *a priori* the same as $\phi$ , that of the forces of the moments $M$ .

It is therefore now a matter of establishing a direct relation between the stiffness (or its inverse, the flexibility) and the opening of the crack. It is established in [1] and [2].

By exploiting the principle of complementary energy and thanks to the convexity of the strain energy, one shows that:

${w}^{d}([\theta ])=\underset{M}{\mathit{Sup}}(\mathit{M.}[\theta ]-\frac{L}{\mathit{EI}}{\mid \mid M\mid \mid }^{2}s(\phi ))$

The function $s(\phi )$ was established in the previous paragraphs. A complementary mathematical treatment consists in applying to $s(\phi )$ a quadratic interpolation outside the nullity interval of $s(\phi )$ and a power interpolation at the bounds of the nullity of $s(\phi )$ .

Furthermore, in the same way as the energy at imposed moment, the energy at imposed strain is convex and homogeneous of order 2. This implies that it does not depend on the norm of $[\theta ]$ , but only on its direction $\varphi$ . One can therefore limit oneself to identifying ${w}^{d}([\theta ])$ for $\mid \mid [\theta ]\mid \mid =1$ .

The energy is then written:

${w}^{d}([\theta ])=\frac{\mathit{EI}}{\mathrm{4L}}k(\varphi )$ .

If $\lambda$ is the amplitude of the applied moment and $\phi$ its direction, by identification one finds the formula giving the equivalent crack stiffness:

$k(\varphi )=\frac{\mathrm{4L}}{\mathit{EI}}\underset{M=1}{\mathit{Sup}}\underset{\lambda \ge 0}{\mathit{Sup}}(\lambda \mathit{M.}[\theta ]-\frac{L}{\mathit{EI}}{\lambda}^{2}s(\phi ))$

In practice this amounts to finding the domain $[{\varphi}_{1,}{\varphi}_{2}]$ which the direction of the crack can have, then to maximising, for all the possible crack angles $\varphi$ , the following expression:

$k(\varphi )=\underset{\phi \in [\varphi -\frac{\pi}{2,}\varphi +\frac{\pi}{2}]}{\mathit{Sup}}\frac{{\cos}^{2}(\phi -\varphi )}{s(\phi )}$

The derivative $k'(\varphi )=\frac{dk(\varphi )}{d\varphi }$ is then obtained by means of a differentiation by Finite Differences of $k(\varphi )$ .
