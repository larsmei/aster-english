# r7.01.26 Constitutive law BETON_RAG

**Summary:**

This document presents the constitutive model BETON_RAG, used to estimate the long-term behaviour of structures affected by the alkali-aggregate reaction.

It also details the formulation and the numerical treatment of the model.

## 1 Introduction

A certain number of civil-engineering structures of the EDF generating fleet, mainly dams, present pathologies of swelling of the concretes due to the alkali-aggregate reaction (AAR). In order to assess the safety and operating margins of these installations, and to control the maintenance costs, EDF and the Laboratoire Matériaux et Durabilité des Constructions (LMDC) of the Université Paul Sabatier (Toulouse III) have developed a numerical model, Grimal , making it possible to simulate the mechanical behaviour of the structures affected by the AAR. The aim of this model is to evaluate the strains and the anisotropic damage (cracking) of the structures affected. Indeed, recent experimental research confirms that the swelling due to the AAR becomes strongly anisotropic when the stress state becomes deviatoric. Moreover, the kinetics and the amplitude of the reaction depending strongly on the water content and on the temperature of the concrete, the model takes these environmental phenomena into account. Furthermore, compressive and tensile creeps play a significant role in the behaviour of the structures; they have therefore been treated in a specific manner.

In *code_aster* , the model is used under the name BETON_RAG.

## 2 Description of the model

### 2.1 General principle

A large part of the mechanical model presented here and of the couplings which enter into it is inspired by the model developed in the thesis of E. Grimal .

The alkali-aggregate reaction and its effects are modelled using a phenomenological approach. This approach takes into account the various important phenomena evolving within the concrete and influencing the chemical reaction. The main developments proposed in this model concern the interactions between the pressure of chemical origin and, the delayed strains on the one hand and the anisotropic strains induced by the presence of oriented cracks on the other hand.

The effects of humidity on the development of the alkali-reaction as well as on the capillary pressure inducing the shrinkage of the matrix are also considered. The dependence between the evolution of the swellings and the stress state is then a consequence of all these elementary phenomena; the mechanical effects of the alkali-reaction are therefore the consequences of an internal “long-term” loading due to the evolution of the chemical pressure (${P}_{g}$  in figure r7.01.26-fig-2-1), combined with the external loading ${\sigma}_{i}$ .

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.26/Object_1.svg)

*Principle of the constitutive model of concrete subjected to a swelling pressure*

${P}_{g}$ , in addition to the external stress ${\sigma}_{i}$ , loads the cement matrix. The latter is considered as a damageable visco-elasto-plastic medium (VEPD module in figure r7.01.26-fig-2-1). The next paragraph presents how the pressure due to the formation of gel is evaluated, in accordance with the environmental conditions and the strain state. Then, the mechanical model is set out within the framework of the thermodynamics of irreversible processes.

### 2.2 Evolution law of the internal pressure

The pressure ${P}_{g}$ due to the formation of gel in the porosity is evaluated by making the hypothesis that the stress state does not modify the chemical progress of the alkali-aggregate reaction.

In accordance with the various available modellings, studied in the bibliographic part Ulm , Lemarchand , Coussy , the concrete is considered as a porous medium made up of a solid matrix and of gel occupying a part of the connected porosity. This porosity is formed of two types of pores, those initially connected to the reaction sites and those generated by the volumetric strain modifying the porosity (${b}^{g}\mathit{tr}(\varepsilon )$ in the equation ).

The porosity connected to the reaction sites is written in the form ${\varphi}_{0}={A}_{0}{V}^{g}$ , in which ${A}_{0}$ is the progress from which the initial connected porosity is filled. Thus, as long as the volume of gel created (${\mathit{AV}}^{g}$ ) is less than ${A}_{0}{V}^{g}$ , the gel lodges in the connected porosity and ${P}_{g}$ remains zero. ${P}_{g}$ will increase only from the moment when all the porosity is filled:

$$
{\text{AV}}^{g}\ge \langle {A}_{0}{V}^{g}+{b}^{g}\text{tr}(\varepsilon )\rangle
$$

Taking these remarks into account, an expression relating the gel pressure and the volume of gel created (${\mathit{AV}}^{g}$ ) is proposed:

$$
\mathit{Pg}={M}_{g}{⟨A.{V}_{g}-{⟨{A}_{0}.{V}_{g}+{b}_{g}\mathit{tr}(\varepsilon )⟩}_{+}⟩}_{+}
$$

In this expression:

- ${V}_{g}$  is the maximum volume of gel which can be created by the chemical reaction.
- $A$  is the progress of the chemical reaction, increasing from $0$ for the sound concrete to $1$ when the reaction is completed.
- ${M}_{g}$  is comparable to an elasticity modulus of the gel and ${b}_{g}$ may be comparable to a Biot coefficient for the gel.

The various positive parts allow:

- a taking into account of the influence of the volumetric strain induced by an external loading on a variation of the connected porosity,
- an increase in the pressure of chemical origin if and only if the gel manages to fill the connected porosity.

**Note:**

The pressure output by the model in the internal variables corresponds to the effective pressure, that is to say the product ${b}_{g}{P}_{g}$.

### 2.3 Progress of the reaction

The progress of the reaction $A$ mentioned previously is a function of the temperature and of the water content of the concrete.

The evolution law used to evaluate the chemical progress is inspired by the work of S. Poyet . He shows that the gel created during the time ($t$ ) and its creation kinetics are proportional to the degree of saturation ($\mathit{Sr}$ ) of the concrete, the degree of saturation being defined by:

$$
\text{Sr}=\frac{C}{{C}_{\text{sat}}}
$$

In this expression, $C$ is the concentration of free water contained per unit volume of concrete and ${C}_{\mathit{sat}}$ is the value of $C$ when the concrete is totally saturated.

In order to take the effect of temperature into account, the Arrhenius law is used to model the thermal activation (Capra ). Finally, the following law is proposed:

$$
\frac{\partial A}{\partial t}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}=\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{\alpha}_{0}.\exp\left[\frac{{E}_{a}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{{T}_{\mathit{ref}}}-\frac{1}{T}\right)\right]\frac{{⟨{S}_{r}-{S}_{r}^{0}⟩}_{+}}{\left(1-{S}_{r}^{0}\right)}{⟨{S}_{r}-A⟩}_{+}
$$

In this law, ${\alpha}_{0}$  is a kinetic parameter, ${E}_{a}$ is the activation energy of the alkali-aggregate reaction (usually of a value close to $47000J/\mathit{mol}/°K$ Lombardi ), $R$ is the perfect-gas constant ($8.31J/\mathit{mol}$ ), ${T}_{\mathit{ref}}$ (in kelvin) is the absolute temperature of the test allowing the identification of ${\alpha}_{0}$ , $T$ is the temperature of the material point. The term $\langle \text{Sr}-A\rangle$ means that the chemical affinity of the reaction is conditioned by the degree of saturation of the concrete, Poyet  having shown in his thesis that the progress $A$ (which is a normalised variable) cannot exceed a limit value very close to the degree of saturation; he explains this by the fact that only the fraction of aggregate in contact with water can lead to the reaction. Consequently the amplitude of the reaction is proportional to the degree of saturation ${S}_{r}$ .

In the same way, the path travelled by the ions in order to reach the reactive silica is all the greater as the degree of saturation is low, which leads to a decrease in the kinetics. This kinetic effect of the degree of saturation is taken into account by means of the term $\frac{⟨\mathit{Sr}-{S}_{r}^{0}⟩}{\left(1-{S}_{r}^{0}\right)}$ , in which ${S}_{r}^{0}$ represents the saturation threshold from which the evolution of the chemical reaction becomes possible.

Figure r7.01.26-fig-2-2  presents a few examples of variations of the progress $A$ for various humidity states. The greater ${S}_{r}$ is, the greater $A$ is, $A$ being maximal ($A=1$ ) when the concrete is saturated (${S}_{r}=1$ ). If the concrete remains in an unsaturated state (${S}_{r}<1$ ), the reaction is never complete ($A<1$ ). On the other hand, if the saturation state changes and passes for example from $0.6$ to $1.0$ , the progress curve is modified in order to rejoin the state of maximal progress.

### 2.4 Dependence between damage and swelling

The alkali-reaction produces important swellings which ought to be accompanied by important damage of the structure. But various experiments (Larive , Multon , Gravel 2001) have shown that the decrease in the mechanical properties remained small compared with the strains reached.

A decrease of $20\text{\%}$ in the mechanical characteristics is observed for a volumetric swelling of $0.1\text{\%}$ (Figure r7.01.26-fig-2-4). A direct tensile test leading to a comparable strain state would produce a complete damage of the cement matrix. The particular behaviour of concrete affected by the alkali-aggregate reaction can be explained by two complementary phenomena: first a phenomenon of localised cracking around the reactive aggregate and then a viscoplastic adaptation for the long term of the cement paste. The cracking leads to large strains because of the cumulative effect of the opening of the microcracks. The visco-plastic behaviour of the cement paste (in particular thanks to the C-S-H, see ) can limit the stress concentration and thus the propagation of microcracks, and the associated damage is also limited.

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.26/1000B93A0000307100001E9592814F4F406C25B1.svg)

*Viscoelastoplastic model*

The compatibility between the important swelling due to the AAR and the moderate associated damage is modelled by using a plastic strain linked to the tensile damage (Grimal , ). This plastic strain limits the damage by allowing a relaxation of the stresses caused by the gel pressure on the cement paste. Although necessary in order to model the swelling of the alkali-reaction, the latter is naturally insufficient to explain other long-term strains such as the multiaxial creep of the undamaged concrete. Thus, in order to obtain reliable predictions of the long-term strains induced by the pressure due to the presence of gel and to the loading effects, a creep strain has been integrated into the model.

Consequently, the damageable visco-elasto-plastic module (VEPD in figure r7.01.26-fig-2-1) has been divided into two complementary levels (Figure r7.01.26-fig-2-3):

- a module (${\mathrm{VD}}^{t}$ ), dedicated to the modelling of the strain ${\varepsilon}^{\text{vdt}}$ (Figure r7.01.26-fig-2-3), respecting the empirical relation existing between swelling due to the AAR and damage (Figure r7.01.26-fig-2-4).
- a visco-elasto-plastic module (VEP), corresponding to the strain ${\varepsilon}^{\text{vep}}$ in figure r7.01.26-fig-2-3, making it possible to model other aspects of the behaviour of the concrete such as elasticity and creep.

### 2.5 Modelling of the anelastic behaviour of the concrete

We shall now describe successively the VEP and VDt modules.

#### 2.5.1 Rheological module simulating the creep and the shrinkage of the concrete (VEP)

Acker  proposes to explain the origins of creep by the particular behaviour of the CSH, the only constituent to have a viscous behaviour. According to him, given the particular structure of the CSH, two strain mechanisms are possible:

- Slips between the sheets of the CSH
- Collapses in the stacking of the sheets and a consolidation of the CSH.

The first mechanism takes place at constant volume and suggests a non-asymptotic long-term behaviour. The second mechanism implies a departure of water from the CSH towards the capillary porosity, with an asymptotic long-term behaviour.

In order to model the difference in behaviour, the VEP module is split into two parts, a “spherical” part and a “deviatoric” part. The spherical part (denoted VEPs in figure r7.01.26-fig-2-5 ) expresses the evolution of the structure of the CSH subjected to a hydrostatic stress linked to the departure of water as well as to the viscoplastic settlement by “random” slips that are globally isotropic. The deviatoric part expresses the slip of the sheets subjected to a shear stress. The pressure ${P}^{w}$ present in figure r7.01.26-fig-2-5 corresponds to the water pressure. It generates a shrinkage if it is negative and a swelling if it is positive.

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.26/Object_18.svg)

*Decomposition into spherical and deviatoric parts of the VEP module*

With:

${P}_{w}$ : pressure of the intra-pore water

${\mathrm{VEP}}^{s}$ : spherical part of the VEP module

${\mathrm{VEP}}^{d}$ : deviatoric part of the VEP module

Given the heterogeneous character of the pore distribution, we propose to model the creep phenomenon as a hydromechanical consolidation problem in the following way: when a loading is applied on the representative elementary volume (REV), interstitial overpressures appear in the water network; the first overpressures to disappear are those present in the water of the connected macroporosity; the loading initially taken up by these overpressures is transferred towards the solid skeleton (figure r7.01.26-fig-2-6). This part therefore comes under classical hydromechanics. The solid skeleton is itself made up, if one observes it at a finer scale, of a connected microporosity and of a solid skeleton which will in turn become overloaded when the overpressures present in its microporosity are evacuated towards the macroporosity. Creep then appears as a succession of consolidations at finer and finer scales, thus calling on transfers of water that is less and less free. This interpretation of the creep phenomenon reveals a “fractal” character of the consolidation mechanism, since the transfer of the stresses towards the solid skeleton takes place in an analogous way at finer and finer scales (figure r7.01.26-fig-2-7); this hypothesis is also put forward by Acker . For the finest scale, an irreversible character of the creep may be present; the interfoliar water molecules are driven out irreversibly by the settlement of the hydrates.

##### 2.5.1.1 Spherical creep

These spherical creep mechanisms are modelled by a Burger rheological model with non-linear irreversible viscosity, in a manner similar to what is described in [r7.01.35](doc:r7.01.35). A schematic representation of this model is given in r7.01.26-fig-2-8.

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.26/modele_rheologique_BETON_BURGER.svg)

*Spherical visco-elasto-plastic decomposition modelling the spherical creep VEP*

The creep model considered here breaks down into three levels:

- Level 0: This level is associated with the macroporosity subjected to the capillary pressure (${P}_{w}={P}_{c}<0$ ) if the medium is unsaturated, or to the interstitial pressure (${P}_{w}>0$ ) if the model is used in a classical hydromechanical approach. ${\sigma}_{0}^{s}$ is the effective spherical stress on the solid skeleton. This level involves a purely elastic component of the concrete and can be schematised and written in equation form in the following way:

$$
{\sigma}_{0}^{s}={k}_{e}{\varepsilon}_{e}^{s}
$$

- Level 1: This level corresponds to a part of the microporosity weakly connected to the open macroporosity and subject to reversible water movements. The associated viscoelasticity is modelled by a Kelvin-Voigt solid (see r7.01.26-fig-2-8):

$$
{\sigma}^{s}={k}_{r}{\varepsilon}_{r}^{s}+{\eta}_{r}{\dot{\varepsilon}}_{r}^{s}
$$

- Level 2: This level corresponds here to the interfoliar nanoporosity of the CSH. This level ensures the irreversible character of the viscous strains of the CSH. In this last expression, ${\varepsilon}_{i}^{s}$ is an irreversible strain.

$$
{\sigma}_{i}^{s}={\eta}_{i}{\dot{\varepsilon}}_{i}^{s}
$$

The rheological diagram of r7.01.26-fig-2-8 makes it possible to establish the equation:

$$
{\dot{\varepsilon}_{i}^{s}}=\frac{{k}_{r}^{s}{\varepsilon}_{r}^{s}+{\eta}_{r}^{s}{\dot{\varepsilon}_{r}^{s}}}{{\eta}_{i}^{s}}
$$

Since the damper linked to $\eta_{i}$ is not placed in parallel with a spring, under constant stress it can lead to creep strains proportional to the duration of the loading. In order to avoid the divergence of these strains, a viscous non-linearity is considered. Physically, this corresponds to a consolidation of the specimen and to entanglements of the CSH sheets.

This requires the introduction of an internal variable corresponding to the maximum of the norm of the irreversible creep strain tensor reached before the time step $i$ (see equation ).

$$
\Vert \varepsilon_{éq,\, irr,\, i} \Vert = \underset{t \leq t_i}{\max}\left(\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{irr}^f:\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{irr}^f}\right)
$$

In this equation, ${\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}_{irr}^f$ denotes the complete irreversible creep strain tensor, comprising the spherical and deviatoric parts. The symbol $:$ corresponds to the doubly contracted product.

The initial irreversible viscosity $\eta_{irr}^0$ is multiplied by an exponential increase factor depending on the norm of the irreversible creep strain tensor $\Vert \varepsilon_{éq,\, irr,\, i} \Vert$ according to the equation , where $\kappa_{irr}$ is a parameter controlling the equivalent irreversible strain for which the viscosity increases so much that there is no longer any significant evolution of the irreversible creep strains.

$$
\eta_{irr} = \eta_{irr}^0 exp\left(\frac{\Vert \varepsilon_{éq,\, irr,\, i} \Vert}{\kappa_{irr}}\right)
$$

##### 2.5.1.2 Deviatoric creep

In accordance with the observations of Acker , , Bernard et al. , the viscoelastic aspect of the deviatoric behaviour is attributed to the interfoliar shear of the CSH sheets. This shear occurs at two scales:

- A nanoscopic viscoelastic scale corresponding to the water weakly bound to the sheets,
- A microscopic scale corresponding to free water weakly adsorbed between the sheets and subject to desiccation.

Finally, the part of the cement paste made up of better crystallised hydrates (portlandite and hydrated calcium aluminate) has an almost elastic behaviour.

The deviatoric viscosity therefore keeps a multi-scale character; one then proposes to represent it in a manner similar to the spherical branch, which moreover simplifies the implementation of the model (rheological equations similar to those of the deviatoric branch).

The system governing the strains of the deviatoric rheological model is written, for each component $i,\,j$ of the deviatoric strain tensor:

$$
{\dot{\varepsilon}_{irr,\,i,j}^{d}}=\frac{{k}_{r}{\varepsilon}_{r,\,i,j}^{d}+{\eta}_{r}{d}{\dot{\varepsilon}_{r,\,i,j}^{d}}}{{\eta}_{irr}^{d}}
$$

In this equation, the variables linked to the irreversible creep have been renamed ${toto}_{irr}$ in order to bring more clarity with respect to the introduction of the indices $i,\,j$.

One can specify at this stage of the modelling that the systems of rheological equations admit an analytical solution for a loading in total strain linear as a function of time. We shall take advantage of this solution in the implementation of the numerical verification test cases.

#### 2.5.2 Module dedicated to the modelling of the anisotropic AAR swelling (VDt)

The VDt module (figure r7.01.26-fig-2-3) models an increase in the anelastic strain when the concrete is subjected to a tensile stress induced by the AAR leading to damage.

We know that in the presence of the AAR, the following strain–damage relation must be respected (figure r7.01.26-fig-2-3):

$$
{\varepsilon}_{i}^{\text{vdt}}={\varepsilon}_{0}\frac{{d}_{i}^{\text{RO}}}{1-{d}_{i}^{R0}}
$$

In this equation, ${\varepsilon}_{0}$ is a parameter identified on a large number of tests and on concretes of various natures (Sellier ). Usually, its value is close to 0.35%.

${d}_{i}^{\mathit{R0}}$ is a principal value of the tensor of the damages due to the tensile stresses induced by the AAR. Here, it will be evaluated in the following way:

$$
{d}_{i}^{R0}=min\left\lbrace {d}_{i}^{t};1-\exp\left(-\frac{1}{{m}^{t}}{\left(\frac{{b}_{g}.{P}_{g}}{{\sigma}^{\text{u t}}}\right)}^{{m}^{t}}\right)\right\rbrace
$$

In this expression, ${d}_{i}^{t}$ is a principal value of the tensile damage tensor. ${m}^{t}$ and ${\sigma}^{\text{u t}}$ are parameters of the damage evolution law. We shall return to the choice of the damage variables and of their evolution law in the following chapter.

### 2.6 Damage model

After having given the broad principles of the model, we shall first recall the thermodynamic concepts on which we must base our formulation. Secondly, we propose the free-energy potentials and the complementary laws defining the model. This presentation makes it possible to verify the dissipation conditions imposed by the thermodynamics of irreversible processes necessary for the convergence of the analyses.

#### 2.6.1 Principle of the modelling

The alkali-aggregate reaction causes swellings of the structure. These swellings can be very different according to the loadings of the damaged structures. The expansion considered isotropic in unrestrained swelling becomes a strongly anisotropic expansion as soon as the concrete is subjected to a deviatoric stress state (Multon , ). The microcracking generated by the intra-pore pressure is then prevented in the loading direction and the swelling appears systematically associated with the directions where the compressive stresses are the weakest. This phenomenon is particularly visible in beams where the cracks orient themselves parallel to the reinforcement. A macrocracking mechanism, due to the opening of the cracks in the direction where the material is the most damaged, amplifies this anisotropy. The micro- and macrocrackings generated lead to a drop in the elastic modulus of the concrete; this drop is usually modelled by damage theory. We present below the principles of the damage model which we shall apply to the stresses coming from the rheological scheme presented in the previous chapter.

In order to simulate the behaviour of concrete degraded by the alkali-aggregate reaction, an anisotropic damage model, based on a realistic cracking criterion, must be used. This damage model must be formulated within the thermodynamic framework (Lemaitre ). The thermodynamic framework makes it possible to avoid possible inconsistencies of formulation which would lead to problems of physical representation and of numerical convergence. To this end, a thermodynamic free-energy potential from which the constitutive laws of the material derive must be proposed. In the case where the mechanical dissipation can be decoupled from the other dissipations (thermal, hydric or chemical), the dissipation condition is given by the Clausius-Duhem equation (equation ). This relation expresses the fact that the power of the “external forces” is at each instant greater than the recoverable power stored by the material; a part of the power of the “external forces” being dissipated mechanically by decohesion, friction, etc. The Clausius-Duhem equation for an isothermal mechanical transformation is classically written:

$$
\sigma :\dot{\varepsilon}-\rho \dot{\psi}\ge 0
$$

In this expression, $\sigma$ is the stress tensor, $\varepsilon$ that of the total strains and $\rho \psi$ the volumetric free-energy potential. The latter is chosen so that the dissipation is zero in the case of perfectly elastic loading.

This restriction implies that the stresses $\sigma$ derive from the free-energy potential with respect to the elastic strains ${\varepsilon}^{e}$ , that is:

$$
\sigma =\rho \frac{\partial \phi }{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}
$$

With the usual hypothesis of partition of the total strain:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{e}=\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{\text{an}}
$$

Where ${\varepsilon}^{\text{an}}$ is the anelastic strain. Two internal variables, linked by this partition equation, are thus defined: the anelastic strain ${\varepsilon}^{\text{an}}$ and the thermoelastic strain ${\varepsilon}^{\text{e}}$ . In our model, ${\varepsilon}^{\text{e}}$ is the strain at level 0 of the rheological scheme; ${\varepsilon}^{\text{an}}$ contains the strains ${\varepsilon}_{\mathit{vdt}}$ and ${\varepsilon}_{\mathit{vep}}$ presented in figure r7.01.26-fig-2-3 as well as the thermal strain if applicable.

The micro- and macrocrackings generated by the stresses lead to a drop in the elastic modulus of the concrete; this drop is usually modelled by damage theory. We present below the principles of a damage model

The model proposed is based on a tensorial representation of the damage. This representation is formulated so that the unilateral aspect of the behaviour can be treated simply and independently in each of the three principal directions of the effective stresses.

The effective stress tensor is estimated from the principle of equivalence in elastic strain, at level 0 of the rheological scheme (figure r7.01.26-fig-2-3):

$$
\tilde{\sigma}={R}^{0}:{\varepsilon}^{e}
$$

With ${R}^{0}$ the fourth-order elasticity tensor of the sound concrete, computed from the Young modulus and the Poisson ratio of the uncracked concrete.

The apparent stresses are linked to the effective stresses by the damage tensor $D$ :

$$
\sigma =(I-D):\tilde{\sigma}
$$

In this expression, ($I-D$ ) is an integrity tensor, also of fourth order, made up on the one hand of the identity matrix ($I$ ) and on the other hand of the damage tensor.

By treating the cracking phenomenon from the fourth-order damage tensor $D$ , the free-energy potential takes the following general form:

$$
\rho\psi=\frac{1}{2}(I-D):{R}^{0}:{\varepsilon}^{e}:{\varepsilon}^{e}
$$

In indicial notation this last relation is written:

$$
\rho\psi=\frac{1}{2}{(I-D)}_{ijkl}{R}_{\text{klmn}}^{0}{\varepsilon}_{\text{mn}}{\varepsilon}_{ij}
$$

Thus, the previous equations lead to writing the Clausius-Duhem relation in the following form:

$$
\sigma :{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\text{an}}-\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial D}:\dot{D}-\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial {V}^{\text{an}}}:{\dot{V}}^{\text{an}}\ge 0
$$

Where the ${V}^{\mathrm{an}}$ are the internal variables associated with the anelastic strains. Or again with the indicial notation:

$$
\underset{{\phi}^{\text{an}}}{\underbrace{\sigma_{ij} \dot{\varepsilon}_{ij}^{\text{an}} - \rho \frac{\partial\psi}{\partial V^{\text{an}}_{ij}} \dot{V}^{\text{an}}_{ij}}}\quad\underset{\phi^D}{\underbrace{\underset{Y_{ijkl}}{\underbrace{- \rho \left(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial D}\right)_{ijkl}}} \dot{D}_{ijkl}}}\ge 0
$$

This last relation makes it possible to define the dissipation by anelastic strain ${\phi }^{\text{an}}$ as well as the dissipation by mechanical damage ${\phi }^{D}$ . It also makes it possible to define the thermodynamic forces ${Y}_{ijkl}$ called energy-release rates.

A simple way of ensuring the positive character of the dissipation whatever the state of the material and of the loading is to postulate the decoupling of the dissipations by anelastic strains and by damage; one must then verify separately ${\phi }^{\text{an}}\ge 0$ and ${\phi }^{D}\ge 0$ , or again:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{ij}{\dot{\varepsilon}}_{ij}^{\text{an}}-\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial {V}_{ij}^{\text{an}}}{\dot{V}}_{ij}^{\text{an}}\ge 0\\ -{Y}_{ijkl}{\dot{D}}_{ijkl}\ge 0\end{array}\right.
$$

In viscoelasticity, the condition of dissipation by anelastic strain leads to choosing, for the viscosity laws, positive coefficients.

In plasticity, one postulates the existence of a dissipation pseudo-potential (${\phi }^{\text{*}\text{an}}$ ). The latter being a positive convex function in stress space and zero at the origin, so that the positivity of the dissipation is automatically verified. The flow law of the anelastic strains can be written in the form:

$$
{\dot{\varepsilon}}_{ij}^{\text{an}}=\dot{\lambda}\frac{\partial {\phi }^{\text{*}\text{an}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}
$$

Where $\dot{\lambda}$ is a positive scalar called the plastic multiplier. The latter is computed so that the increase in the anelastic strain leads to a relaxation of the stresses, this making it possible to verify the plasticity criterion, itself also expressed in stress space.

It remains to verify the positivity condition of the dissipation by damage. One can for this draw inspiration from the formalism briefly described previously for plasticity. That is to say, give oneself a dissipation pseudo-potential that is convex and zero at the origin in the space of the energy-release rates, from which the increments of the components of the damage tensor derive. One then has the following damage evolution law:

$$
{\dot{D}}_{ijkl}=-\dot{\lambda}\frac{\partial {\phi }^{\text{*}D}}{\partial {Y}_{ijkl}}
$$

The “damage multiplier” $\dot{\lambda}$ having to be computed so as to verify the damage criterion. The construction of the pseudo-potential being arduous, the use of a more rational means, making it possible to ensure a form of the damage tensor consistent with the physical phenomena at the origin of the damage, is regularly used. For this, one calls on homogenisation theory. The damage tensor $D$ is estimated there from the constitutive law of the sound material (${R}^{0}$ ) and from that of a representative elementary volume (REV) of cracked material (${R}^{D}$ ); one then has:

$$
{D}_{ijkl}={\delta}_{\text{ik}}{\delta}_{\text{jl}}-{R}_{\text{ijmn}}^{0}{R}_{\text{mnkl}}^{{D}^{-1}}
$$

The representativeness of the constitutive law of the equivalent damaged material will depend on the quality of the homogenisation of the REV. The unilateral character of the behaviour of the concrete is induced by the restoration of stiffness linked to the re-closures of the cracks. In the case of a cyclic uniaxial loading with change of the sign of the stress, one can observe experimentally the creation of tensile cracks perpendicular to the loading axis and of compressive cracks parallel to the loading axis. The losses of stiffness in tension and in compression are therefore attributed to at least two orthogonal networks of cracks. When the stress passes from tension to compression, the tensile cracks are closed and the compressive cracks are activated. When the stress passes from compression to tension, the tensile cracks open and are activated in their turn.

In order to model “physically” the unilateral behaviour of the concrete, it is convenient to reason from a tensorial description of the crack network. The losses of stiffness in tension or in compression can thus be treated in each direction of space as a function of the sign of the stresses and of the orientation of the cracks: if a principal stress is positive, the most active cracks will be those whose orientation is globally perpendicular to the stress, whereas if the principal stress is negative, the most active cracks will be parallel to the stress.

##### 2.6.1.1 Anisotropic damage by tension

The various reasons briefly stated previously lead us to propose a damage model based on a representation of tensile cracking by a second-order tensor estimated from the effective tensile stress tensor ${\tilde{\sigma}}^{t}$ and not from the extensions. These are here the effective stresses in the sense of damage; they must not be confused with the effective stresses in the sense of the mechanics of porous media, which affect the whole solid skeleton, whereas the effective stresses in the sense of damage affect only the undamaged part of the solid skeleton.

The effective tensile stress tensor is obtained from the principal effective stresses, themselves coming from the application of the principle of equivalence in strain to the rheological model used for the solid skeleton:

$$
{\tilde{\sigma}}^{t}=\langle {\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}\rangle .({\overrightarrow{e}}_{i}\otimes {\overrightarrow{e}}_{i})
$$

Where ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{i}$ is the eigenvector associated with $\langle {\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}\rangle$ which is the positive part of the effective principal stress ${\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}$ .

Using the principle of equivalence in strain stated by Lemaitre , the effective tensile stresses make it possible to define the associated elastic strain tensor ${\varepsilon}^{\text{et}}$ :

$$
{\varepsilon}^{\text{et}}=\frac{1+{\nu}^{0}}{{E}^{0}}.{\tilde{\sigma}}^{t}-\frac{{\nu}^{0}}{{E}^{0}}\text{tr}({\tilde{\sigma}}^{t}).\mathit{Id}
$$

The cracking criterion retained for the tensile damage is that of Rankine; the threshold stress tensor is denoted ${\sigma}^{R}$ ; the criterion expresses in each principal direction ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{i}$ of the effective stress tensor the non-exceeding of the normal component of the threshold stress:

$$
{f}_{i}=\langle {\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}\rangle -{\sigma}^{R}:({\overrightarrow{e}}_{i}\otimes {\overrightarrow{e}}_{i})
$$

The updating of the threshold stress tensor is in accordance with the consistency condition which is written simply:

$$
{\sigma}_{ii}^{R}=\sup\left({⟨\tilde{{\sigma}_{i}}⟩}_{+},{\sigma}_{ii}^{R}\right)
$$

where the ${\sigma}_{ii}^{R}$ are the terms of the diagonal of the tensor ${\sigma}^{R}$ expressed in the principal basis of the effective stresses.

Admitting that the “equivalent” cracks, and therefore the damages, have the same principal directions as the threshold stresses, one can define the damage tensor in the basis of the principal stresses of ${\sigma}^{R}$ :

$$
{d}^{t}={d}_{i}^{t}.({\overrightarrow{v}}_{i}\otimes {\overrightarrow{v}}_{i})
$$

The damage ${d}^{t}$ represents a set of three orthogonal networks of plane cracks coexisting within one and the same representative elementary volume. The terms ${\overrightarrow{v}}_{i}$ are the eigenvectors of ${\sigma}^{R}$ and ${d}_{i}^{t}$ the eigenvalues of the damage tensor estimated from the eigenvalues ${\sigma}_{i}^{R}$ of ${\sigma}^{R}$ , and from the following evolution law inspired by the Weibull law (Sellier ).

Damage evolution laws:

$$
{d}_{i}^{t}=1-\exp\left(\underset{{\beta}_{i}}{\underbrace{{-\frac{1}{{m}^{t}}{\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{i}^{R}}{{\sigma}^{\text{ut}}}\right) }^{{m}^{t}}}}}\right)
$$

In this expression ${m}^{t}$ is a parameter all the greater as the material is brittle, ${\sigma}^{\mathit{ut}}$ is also a “material” parameter; it is comparable to a cohesion; in practice ${m}^{t}$ can be identified from the experimental damage measured from the peak of the constitutive law, ${\sigma}^{\mathit{ut}}$ is directly linked to the strength. We shall give the relations between the parameters of the model and the measurable quantities (grandeurs) in the following paragraphs. Introducing the cracking index defined by:

$$
{\beta}_{i}=\frac{1}{{m}^{t}}{\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{i}^{R}}{{\sigma}^{\text{ut}}}\right) }^{{m}^{t}}
$$

There comes the relation between the damage and the cracking index:

$$
\frac{1}{1-{d}_{i}^{t}}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}=\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{e}^{{\beta}_{i}}
$$

This last relation will be practical for the construction of the free-energy potential. The internal variables introduced at this level will also make it possible to memorise the cracking state. It is also necessary, from this point of view, to note that the damage is an increasing and continuous function of the cracking index, which is itself an increasing function of the threshold stress. As the latter can only increase, the principal values of the damage tensor can only grow.

As regards the normal strain-stress relations, the passage from the damage tensor to the constitutive law of the damaged material must express on the one hand a decrease in the Young modulus in the direction normal to the crack and on the other hand an attenuation of the Poisson effect between the direction orthogonal to the plane of the crack and the directions contained in its plane. In order to respect these conditions, we propose to use the following constitutive law:

$$
{\varepsilon}_{ii}^{e}=\frac{{\sigma}_{ii}^{t}}{{E}^{0}(1-{d}_{i}^{t})}-\frac{{\nu}^{0}}{{E}^{0}}({\sigma}_{jj}^{t}+{\sigma}_{kk}^{t})
$$

Or again as a function of the cracking indices:

$$
{\varepsilon}_{ii}^{e}=\frac{{\sigma}_{ii}^{t}}{{E}^{0}}{e}^{{\beta}_{i}}-\frac{{\nu}^{0}}{{E}^{0}}({\sigma}_{jj}^{t}+{\sigma}_{kk}^{t})
$$

In this expression ${E}^{0}$ is the Young modulus of the sound material and ${\nu}^{0}$ its Poisson ratio. The relation is written in the principal basis of the damages. It must be noted that this law is expressed here as a function of the apparent stresses and not of the effective stresses; it is this particular writing which makes it possible to benefit simply from an attenuation of the Poisson effect as a function of the cracking in the directions $j$ and $k$ as well as from the symmetry of the compliance tensor of the constitutive law. The shear slip-stress relations are also written in the principal damage basis; the relation proposed is the following:

$$
{\varepsilon}_{ij}^{e}=\frac{{\sigma}_{ij}^{t}}{2{\mu}^{0}(1-{d}_{i}^{t})(1-{d}_{j}^{t})}
$$

Which one can also express as a function of the cracking indices:

$$
{\varepsilon}_{ij}^{e}=\frac{{\sigma}_{ij}^{t}}{2{\mu}^{0}}{e}^{{\beta}_{i}+{\beta}_{j}}
$$

The shear slip-stress relation involves the damages on the two principal directions of cracking loaded in shear; the combination of the damages is of the weakest-link type. Besides the interaction between damages which this writing provides, one can attribute to it a statistical meaning by considering that the damages are comparable to probabilities of encountering surface discontinuities on the facets (Sellier ). Under this hypothesis, the weakest-link theory indicates that the probability that a shear stress passes over two orthogonal facets is equal to the probability of having simultaneously two orthogonal unfailed surfaces. Neglecting the statistical dependences between the two cracking planes, this probability is equal to the product of the probabilities of having on each of these two facets a continuity of the material; one deduces from it the denominator of the equation . Finally, the last reason for which we have adopted this form for the damage of the shear coefficient is that the resulting constitutive law presents the response expected from the test of rotation of the principal directions (known as the Willam test).

In order to express the free-energy potential as a function of the elastic strains, it is necessary to ensure the invertibility of the stress-strain relations proposed previously. For this, let us express the apparent normal stresses as a function of the elastic strains:

$$
{\sigma}_{ii}^{t}=\frac{{E}^{0}}{{D}^{n}}\left[\left({e}^{{\beta}_{j}+{\beta}_{k}}-{\nu}^{{0}^{2}}\right){\varepsilon}_{ii}^{\mathit{et}}+{\nu}^{0}{\varepsilon}_{jj}^{\mathit{et}}\left(\nu +{e}^{{\beta}_{k}}\right)+{\nu}^{0}{\varepsilon}_{kk}^{\mathit{et}}\left(\nu +{e}^{{\beta}_{j}}\right)\right]`
$$

with

${D}^{n}={e}^{{\beta}_{i}+{\beta}_{j}+{\beta}_{k}}-{\nu}^{{0}^{2}}\left({e}^{{\beta}_{i}}+{e}^{{\beta}_{j}}+{e}^{{\beta}_{k}}\right)-2{\nu}^{{0}^{3}}$

One shows that ${D}^{n}$ is always positive if the following two conditions are verified:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{c}{\beta}_{i}\ge 0\forall i\in \left\lbrace 1,2,3\right\rbrace \\ \nu \le 0.5\end{array}\right.
$$

The condition on the Poisson ratio is verified; it is the same for the condition on the ${\beta}_{i}$ , given the evolution law of the damages proposed previously. The invertibility of the normal strain – normal stress relation is therefore assured whatever the level of damage.

As regards the relations between the shear rates and the shear stresses, the inversion is immediate and one has:

$$
{\sigma}_{ij}^{t}=\frac{{E}^{0}{\varepsilon}_{ij}^{\mathit{et}}}{(1+{\nu}^{0}){e}^{{\beta}_{i}+{\beta}_{j}}}
$$

As the denominator of this expression lies in the interval $[1,+\infty [$ the invertibility is there too assured.

By integrating the stress-strain relations with respect to the elastic strains associated with the effective tensile stresses, one obtains the elastic free-energy potential associated with the effective tensile stresses. This potential, denoted $\rho {\Psi}^{t}$ in what follows, is here expressed in the principal basis of the damages:

$$
\rho {\Psi}^{t}=\rho {\Psi}^{t(n)}+\rho {\Psi}^{t(s)}
$$

With ${\rho\psi}^{t(n)}$ the potential of the free energies associated with the extensions:

$$
{\rho\psi}^{t(n)}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{{E}^{0}}{{D}^{n}}\left( \begin{array}{c}({e}^{{\beta}_{j}+{\beta}_{k}}-{\upsilon }^{{0}^{2}}){\varepsilon}_{ii}^{{\text{et}}^{2}}+({e}^{{\beta}_{i}+{\beta}_{k}}-{\upsilon }^{{0}^{2}}){\varepsilon}_{jj}^{{\text{et}}^{2}}+({e}^{{\beta}_{j}+{\beta}_{i}}-{\upsilon }^{{0}^{2}}){\varepsilon}_{kk}^{{\text{et}}^{2}}\\ +{\mathrm{2\upsilon }}^{0}({\varepsilon}_{jj}^{\text{et}}{\varepsilon}_{ii}^{\text{et}}({\upsilon }^{0}+{e}^{{\beta}_{k}})+{\varepsilon}_{kk}^{\text{et}}{\varepsilon}_{ii}^{\text{et}}({\upsilon }^{0}+{e}^{{\beta}_{j}})+{\varepsilon}_{jj}^{\text{et}}{\varepsilon}_{kk}^{\text{et}}({\upsilon }^{0}+{e}^{{\beta}_{i}}))\end{array}\right)
$$

And ${\rho\psi}^{t(s)}$ that associated with the elastic shear strains:

$$
{\rho\psi}^{t(s)}=\frac{1}{2}{G}^{0}({e}^{-({\beta}_{i}+{\beta}_{j})}{\varepsilon}_{ij}^{{\text{et}}^{2}}+{e}^{-({\beta}_{i}+{\beta}_{k})}{\varepsilon}_{\text{ik}}^{{\text{et}}^{2}}{e}^{-({\beta}_{j}+{\beta}_{k})}{\varepsilon}_{\text{jk}}^{{\text{et}}^{2}})
$$

The potential can also be expressed simply as a function of the apparent stresses ${\sigma}^{t}$ associated with the effective tensile stresses. That is, as regards the potential associated with the extensions:

$$
{\rho\psi}^{t(n)}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{ii}^{{t}^{2}}}{{E}_{i}}+\frac{{\sigma}_{jj}^{{t}^{2}}}{{E}_{j}}+\frac{{\sigma}_{ii}^{{t}^{2}}}{{E}_{i}}+2\frac{{\upsilon }^{0}}{{E}^{0}}({\sigma}_{ii}^{t}{\sigma}_{jj}^{t}+{\sigma}_{ii}^{t}{\sigma}_{kk}^{t}+{\sigma}_{kk}^{t}{\sigma}_{jj}^{t})\right)
$$

The ${E}_{i}={E}^{0}(1-{d}_{i}^{t})$ being the Young moduli of the damaged material. And for the potential associated with the shears:

$$
{\rho\psi}^{t(s)}=(1+{\upsilon }^{0}){E}^{0}\left( \frac{{\sigma}_{ij}^{{t}^{2}}}{{E}_{i}{E}_{j}}+\frac{{\sigma}_{\text{ik}}^{{t}^{2}}}{{E}_{i}{E}_{k}}+\frac{{\sigma}_{\text{kj}}^{{t}^{2}}}{{E}_{k}{E}_{j}}\right)
$$

The cracking indices ${\beta}_{i}$ being able to be linked bijectively to the principal tensile damages ${d}_{i}^{t}$ , the expression of Clausius-Duhem can be expressed as a function of the cracking indices. For more clarity we here momentarily disregard the role of the anelastic strains, by assuming a damageable elastic transformation involving only the effective tensile stresses:

$$
\sigma :{\dot{\varepsilon}}_{ij}^{\text{et}}-\left( \underset{-{\varphi}^{\text{Dt}(n)}}{\underbrace{\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{t(n)}}{\partial {\beta}_{i}}{\dot{\beta}}_{i}}}+\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{t(n)}}{\partial R}:\dot{R}+\underset{-{\varphi}^{\text{Ft}(s)}}{\underbrace{\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{t(s)}}{\partial {\beta}_{i}}\dot{{\beta}_{i}}}}+\frac{\partial {\psi}^{t(s)}}{\partial R}:\dot{R}+\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{t}}{\partial {\varepsilon}_{I}^{\text{et}}}{\varepsilon}_{I}^{\text{et}}\right) \ge 0
$$

In this expression, ${\varepsilon}_{I}^{\text{et}}$ is a principal value of the tensor of the strains associated with the effective tensile stresses, ${\varphi}^{\text{Dt}(n)}$ is the dissipation associated with the normal strains and ${\varphi}^{\text{Dt}(s)}$ that associated with the shears. $R$ is the passage matrix from the principal basis of the elastic strains to the principal basis of the damages; $\dot{R}$ can be interpreted as a rotation rate of the damage tensor if the principal directions of strain do not rotate. Taking into account the form of the free-energy potential associated with the shears then makes it possible to compute the dissipation due to the damage associated with the shears. One has:

$$
{\phi }^{\text{Dt}(s)\dot{\beta}}={\dot{\beta}}_{i}\underset{\ge 0}{\underbrace{\left( \frac{1}{2}{G}^{0}({e}^{-({\beta}_{i}+{\beta}_{j})}{\varepsilon}_{ij}^{{\text{et}}^{2}}+{e}^{-({\beta}_{i}+{\beta}_{k})}{\varepsilon}_{\text{ik}}^{{\text{et}}^{2}})\right) }}
$$

This expression is always positive since ${\dot{\beta}}_{i}$ is positive; the positivity of the dissipation associated with the shears is therefore verified.

As regards the dissipation associated with the normal strains, one can remark that by using the stress expressions of ${\psi}^{t(n)}$ and of ${\varepsilon}^{\text{et}}$ in the Clausius-Duhem equation, one has:

$$
\sigma :{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\text{et}}-\rho {\dot{\psi}}^{t(n)}=\left( \underset{-\frac{{{\sigma}_{i}^{t}}^{2}}{{E}_{i}^{2}}}{\underbrace{\sigma :\frac{\partial {\varepsilon}^{\text{et}}}{\partial {E}_{i}}}}-\underset{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{{{\sigma}_{i}^{t}}^{2}}{{E}_{i}^{2}}}{\underbrace{\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{t(n)}}{\partial {E}_{i}}}}\right) \dot{{E}_{i}}=\underset{\le 0}{\underbrace{-\frac{1}{2}\frac{{{\sigma}_{i}^{t}}^{2}}{{E}_{i}^{2}}}}\dot{E}_{i}\ge 0
$$

Which implies that ${\dot{E}}_{i}\le 0$ . Now ${\dot{E}}_{i}=-{E}^{0}{\dot{d}}_{i}^{t}$ and ${\dot{d}}_{i}^{t}$ being positive, the second principle is also verified for the damage associated with the normal strains.

In the case of non-radial loading, the Clausius-Duhem relation contains the terms due to the evolutions of the principal damages, which are always positive as we have just seen, as well as terms due to the rotations of the damage directions. The latter express a drawing together of the orthotropy directions of the material and those of the loading. From an analytical point of view, this second transformation is analogous to a rotation of the principal directions of strain without damage. It therefore takes place without dissipation; the following relation must therefore be respected at each instant:

$$
\left( \sigma :\frac{\partial {\varepsilon}^{\text{et}}}{\partial R}-\rho \frac{\partial {\Psi}^{t}}{\partial R}\right) \dot{R}=0
$$

Which implies:

$$
\sigma :\frac{\partial {\varepsilon}^{\text{et}}}{\partial R}=\rho \frac{\partial {\Psi}^{t}}{\partial {\varepsilon}^{\text{et}}}:\frac{\partial {\varepsilon}^{\text{et}}}{\partial R}
$$

Now, by construction, $\sigma =\rho \frac{\partial {\Psi}^{t}}{\partial {\varepsilon}^{\text{et}}}$ , so this condition is identically respected and the rotation of the damage directions does not entail any complementary dissipation but induces a variation of the free-energy potential compatible with the evolution of the loading.

We have just presented the principles of an orthotropic damage model in effective stress; the latter falls within the thermodynamic framework and moreover benefits from a direct numerical solution method in accordance (no sub-iterations at the Gauss points). Indeed, the computation of the damage as a function of the effective stress is direct, as is that of the apparent stress as a function of the effective stress and of the damage.

##### 2.6.1.2 Isotropic damage by compression

Under tensile loading, the criterion usually used is that of Rankine (principal tensile stress); this criterion accounts for a propagation of cracks in mode $I$ . In compression, the criteria of Mazars  or of Drücker-Prager are commonly employed; the Mazars criterion involves the notion of extension of the REV, the Drücker-Prager criterion accounts for the unfavourable effect of the stress deviator. In practice, failure in compression is induced by the elastic heterogeneities leading to the appearance of local fields of self-equilibrated stresses whose positive part causes the initiation and the propagation of cracks (figure r7.01.26-fig-2-9). By their origins, these induced positive stresses can exist only in the directions orthogonal to the compressive stresses which generate them. When these self-stresses reach the tensile strength a crack initiates and the compressive damage appears. In ordinary concretes, this damage appears progressively and it is possible to observe multiple vertical cracks distributed over a wide localised and inclined shear band (figure r7.01.26-fig-2-10).

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.26/100F16F4000069D50000328A08612449DBF23BAF.svg)

*Cracking pattern as a function of the loading path (Torrenti )*

The cracks parallel to the free edges are initiated in the neighbourhood of the aggregates by the induced self-stresses; they crack the concrete forming small “columns” which perish by lateral instability as figure r7.01.26-fig-2-10 shows.

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.26/10062BB6000069F00000297248F701419BA32F69.svg)

*Appearance of “columns” in the neighbourhood of the aggregates (Torrenti )*

Thus, the propagation of the compressive cracks can be likened to a phenomenon of multiple instabilities in compression along a shear band. Now the phenomena of mechanical instability are very closely linked to the conditions of lateral restraint of the compressed element. The instability always occurring in the direction of weakest restraint, this explains the cracks parallel to the unloaded edges in figure r7.01.26-fig-2-9.

Besides the failure mode described above, it is important to recall how sensitive the compressive strength is to confinement, as the various biaxial and triaxial tests of the literature show. Given on the one hand the complexity of the failure mode in compression and on the other hand the sensitivity to confinement, it is usual, in order to model the damage of concrete in compression, to adopt a macroscopic isotropic Drücker-Prager criterion if one reasons in terms of stresses, or a Mazars criterion if one reasons in terms of strains. We have chosen here to adopt the Drücker-Prager criterion in order to be consistent with the formalism in effective stresses introduced during the presentation of the tensile damage model. The stresses at the origin of the compressive damage are therefore assumed to be the negative effective stresses ${\tilde{\sigma}}^{c}$ ; the latter are defined in the principal basis of the effective stresses by:

$$
{\tilde{\sigma}}^{c}=\tilde{\sigma}-{\tilde{\sigma}}^{t}
$$
Where $\tilde{\sigma}$ is the effective stress coming from the rheological model and ${\tilde{\sigma}}^{t}$ the effective tensile stress defined by the equation . The equivalent Drücker-Prager stress is then written:

$$
{\sigma}^{\text{ceq}}=\sqrt{{J}_{2}}+{\delta}_{\text{hom}}\frac{{I}_{1}}{3}
$$

Where ${I}_{1}$ and ${J}_{2}$ are the first two invariants of the effective compressive stress tensor:

$$
\left\{   \begin{array}{c}{J}_{2}=\frac{1}{6}({({\tilde{\sigma}}_{1}^{c}-{\tilde{\sigma}}_{2}^{c})}^{2}+{({\tilde{\sigma}}_{1}^{c}-{\tilde{\sigma}}_{3}^{c})}^{2}+{({\tilde{\sigma}}_{2}^{c}-{\tilde{\sigma}}_{3}^{c})}^{2})\\ {I}_{1}={\tilde{\sigma}}_{1}^{c}+{\tilde{\sigma}}_{2}^{c}+{\tilde{\sigma}}_{3}^{c}\end{array}\right.
$$

The flow is carried out by considering that the damage threshold stress memorises the maximum value of the Drücker-Prager stress:

$$
{\sigma}^{\text{DP}}=\sup({\sigma}^{\text{DP}},{\sigma}^{\text{ceq}})
$$

The damage then evolves non-linearly as a function of the threshold stress:

$$
{d}^{c}=1-\exp\left( -\frac{1}{{m}^{c}}{\left( \frac{{\sigma}^{\text{DP}}}{{\sigma}^{\text{uc}}}\right)}^{{m}^{c}}\right)
$$

The free-energy potential associated with the effective compressive stresses is written very simply because of the isotropy hypothesis of the compressive damage:

$$
\rho {\psi}^{c}=(1-{d}^{c})\frac{1}{2}\left[{\lambda}^{0}{(\text{tr}({\varepsilon}^{\text{ec}}))}^{2}+2{\mu}^{0}\text{tr}({({\varepsilon}^{\text{ec}})}^{2})\right]
$$

The dissipation associated with the compressive damage is written:

$$
{\phi }^{\text{dc}}=-\underset{{Y}^{c}}{\underbrace{\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{c}}{\partial {d}^{c}}}}{\dot{d}}^{c}
$$

The energy-release rate ${Y}^{c}$ being negative and the damage strictly increasing, the positivity of the dissipation by compressive damage is assured.

##### 2.6.1.3 Coupling of the tensile and compressive damages

The predominant isotropic character of the compressive damage is linked to the “quasi-random and therefore statistically isotropic” complexity of the cracking paths generated during the degradation process. It is obvious that the cracks created will affect the tensile behaviour. A simple way of accounting for the decrease in the elastic moduli in tension as a function of the compressive damage is to consider that the isotropic compressive damage also reduces the tensile free-energy potential, which becomes:

$$
\rho {\psi}^{t\text{*}}=((1-{d}^{c})\underset{\rho {\psi}^{t}}{\underbrace{(\rho {\psi}^{t(n)}+\rho {\psi}^{t(n)})}}
$$

The term $\rho {\psi}^{t}$ being positive, the positivity of the dissipation by compressive damage remains assured:

$$
-\rho \left(\frac{\partial {\psi}^{c}}{\partial {d}^{c}}+\frac{\partial {\psi}^{t\text{*}}}{\partial {d}^{c}}\right)\dot{{d}^{c}}⩾0
$$

And the term $(1-{d}^{c})$ also being positive, the dissipation by tensile damage remains positive.

**Free-energy potential associated with the macroscopic elastic strains**

The partition of the strains as a function of the sign of the effective stresses having been done in equations  and , the free-energy potential is the sum of the free energies associated with the effective tensile stresses on the one hand and with the effective compressive stresses on the other hand:

$$
\rho \psi =(1-{d}^{c})\underset{\rho {\psi}^{t}}{\underbrace{(\rho {\psi}^{t(n)}+\rho {\psi}^{t(s)})}}+\rho {\psi}^{c}
$$

**Free-energy potential associated with an expansive internal chemical reaction**

If the model integrates a pressure (${P}_{g}$ ) created by the expansive chemical reaction of progress $A$ , the free-energy potential, at imposed temperature and at a given instant, becomes a function of $A$ :

$$
\rho \psi ({\varepsilon}^{\text{ec}},{\varepsilon}^{\text{et}},A,{d}^{t},{d}^{c},{V}^{\text{an}})
$$

In this expression ${V}^{\mathit{an}}$ represents the internal variables associated with the anelastic phenomena. The Clausius-Duhem inequality therefore becomes:

$$
\sigma :({\dot{\varepsilon}}^{e}+{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\text{an}})+{P}_{g}(\dot{A}{V}^{g})-\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{e}-\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial {d}^{t}}:{\dot{d}}^{t}-\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial {d}^{c}}{\dot{d}}^{c}-\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial A}\dot{A}-\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial {V}^{\text{an}}}{\dot{V}}^{\text{an}}\ge 0
$$

In this expression, the term ${P}_{g}(\dot{A}{V}^{g})$ is the mechanical power supplied to the solid skeleton by the chemical reaction. If one envisages a physical transformation of the REV without damage or anelastic strain, hence without mechanical dissipation, this energy must be found entirely in the free-energy potential, which is written:

$$
{P}_{g}(\dot{A}{V}^{g})=\rho \frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial A}\dot{A}
$$

On the other hand the physical considerations lead us to express the gel pressure as a function of the volume fraction of matter in excess with respect to the volume of the voids connected to the reactive site on the one hand and of the total strains on the other hand, equation .

Respect of the two previous relations leads to proposing for the part of the potential due to the gel the following form:

$$
{\Psi}^{g}=\frac{1}{2}{M}^{g}{b}^{{g}^{2}}{\left(\frac{A-{A}^{0}}{{b}^{g}}{V}^{g}-\text{tr}({\varepsilon}^{e}+{\varepsilon}^{\text{an}})\right)}^{2}
$$

This form of potential is close to that proposed by Ulm et al . The derivative of this potential with respect to the elastic strains gives the part of the macroscopic stress induced by the internal gel pressure in the solid skeleton:

$$
{\pi}^{g}=-{b}^{g}{P}_{g}=\rho \frac{\partial {\Psi}^{g}}{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}
$$

##### 2.6.1.4 Free-energy potentials associated with the water pressures

In a saturated medium, the intra-pore pressures act on the solid skeleton by deforming the walls of the pore network; the Clausius-Duhem equation is written, in the absence of damage and of anelastic phenomena:

$$
-({P}_{w}-{P}_{\mathit{w0}}){\dot{\varphi}}^{w}-\rho \frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial {\varphi}^{w}}{\dot{\varphi}}^{w}=0
$$

In this expression ${\varphi}^{w}=\frac{{m}^{w}}{{\rho}^{\mathit{w0}}}$ is the normalised water mass supply, ${m}^{w}$ being the mass supply and ${\rho}^{\mathit{w0}}$ the density of the water defined at the reference pressure ${P}_{\mathit{w0}}$ . Moreover the physical considerations lead to expressing the water pressure in the form (Coussy ):

$$
{P}_{w}-{P}_{\mathit{w0}}={M}^{w}({\varphi}^{w}-{b}^{w}\text{tr}(\varepsilon ))
$$

An admissible form for the free-energy potential associated with these equations is then:

$$
\rho {\psi}^{w}=\frac{1}{2}{M}^{w}{b}^{{w}^{2}}{\left( \frac{{\varphi}^{w}}{{b}^{w}}-\text{tr}(\varepsilon )\right)}^{2}
$$

The derivative of this potential with respect to the elastic strains gives the part of the macroscopic stress induced by the water pressure:

$$
{\pi}^{w}=\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{w}}{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}=-{b}^{w}({P}_{w}-{P}_{\mathit{w0}})
$$

If the reference state is defined for the material saturated at atmospheric pressure $({P}_{\mathit{w0}}=0)$ , then the previous expression is valid only if the fluid mass supply is positive. Otherwise, the concrete desaturates and the water pressure is no longer governed by the relative compressibilities of the various phases but by the capillary phenomena. The mean water pressure ${P}_{w}$ is therefore a function of the capillary pressure and of the degree of saturation ${S}^{w}$ . Besides the variation of pressure in the water induced by the capillary phenomena, it is also necessary to consider the action of the interfaces of the fluid with the gaseous medium and the solid. Indeed, these interfaces are subject to capillary tension phenomena which themselves also act directly on the solid skeleton. In the case where the intra-pore gas pressure is in equilibrium with the atmospheric pressure (Coussy ), the part of the macroscopic stress induced by these two phenomena can be put in the following general form:

$$
{\pi}^{w}={b}^{w}{f}^{{w}_{({S}^{w})}}{P}^{c}
$$

Where ${f}^{w}$ is a function taking into account on the one hand the decrease in the volume of water and on the other hand the increase in the number of solid sites subjected to the interfacial surface tension. In the present modelling, we are interested in structures situated in a partially saturated zone; in this case we propose to use a function ${f}^{w}$ linear of the form ${k}^{w}\cdot {S}^{w}$ ; the contribution of the hydric effects in the macroscopic stress then has the following form:

$$
{\pi}^{w}=-{b}^{w}{S}^{w}{k}^{w}{P}^{c}=\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{w}}{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}
$$

Considering that the variation of the porosity $\varphi$ due to the strain remains small compared with ${\varphi}^{w}$ , one can admit that for the unsaturated medium $\varphi \approx {\varphi}^{0}$ . If on the other hand one admits that the capillary pressure curve ${P}^{c}({S}^{w})$ is almost insensitive to the strain state of the solid skeleton, then:

$$
{\pi}^{w}\approx {b}^{w}({\varphi}^{0}+{\varphi}^{w}){k}^{w}{P}^{{c}_{({\varphi}^{0}+{\varphi}^{w})}}=\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{w}}{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}
$$

If the capillary pressure curve admits an equation of “Van Genuchten” type then ${\pi}^{w}$ takes the following form:

$$
{\pi}^{w}={\text{-b}}^{w}({\varphi}^{0}+{\varphi}^{w})(a{({({\varphi}^{0}+{\varphi}^{w})}^{-b}-1)}^{(1-\frac{1}{b})})
$$

Where $a$ and $b$ are two calibration constants.

The free-energy potential can then be put in the form:

$$
\rho {\psi}^{w}=-{b}^{w}({\varphi}^{0}+{\varphi}^{w})(a{({({\varphi}^{0}+{\varphi}^{w})}^{-b}-1)}^{(1-\frac{1}{b})})\text{tr}(\varepsilon )
$$

The thermodynamic force associated with the variation of the water mass is obtained by derivation of this potential with respect to ${\varphi}^{w}$ .

##### 2.6.1.5 Dissipation due to the anelastic strains

The Clausius-Duhem equation brings out the anelastic strains ${\varepsilon}^{\text{an}}$ in the term $\sigma :{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\text{an}}$ . This is the power of the external forces of the anelastic strain field; the evolution of these strains induces an evolution of the associated internal variables ${V}^{\mathit{an}}$  possibly causing a variation of the free-energy potential:

$$
\rho \frac{\partial \phi }{\partial {V}^{\text{an}}}{\dot{V}}^{\text{an}}
$$

This last term represents the elastic energy blocked in the solid skeleton by the anelastic strain. In our model the anelastic strains have a viscoelastic or viscoplastic origin and can directly be used as internal variables, that is ${V}^{\text{an}}={\varepsilon}^{\text{an}}$ .

Thus, in the particular case of a purely anelastic transformation, the dissipation reduces to:

$$
{\phi }^{\text{an}}=\sigma :{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\text{an}}-\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial {\varepsilon}^{\text{an}}}{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\text{an}}\ge 0
$$

In the model proposed here we have adopted the principle of partition of the total strain into an elastic part and an anelastic part, which makes it possible to express the elastic strains as a function of the anelastic strains and to conclude that the thermodynamic force associated with the anelastic strain is the stress:

$$
\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}=-\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial {\varepsilon}^{\text{an}}}=\sigma
$$

The second principle is therefore verified if $\sigma :{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\text{an}}\ge 0$ . In the model studied here, the anelastic strain associated with long-term creep or with the expansive chemical reaction is proportional to the stress, which one can write in abbreviated form ${\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\text{an}}=k\sigma$ , where $k$ is a positive coefficient depending on the internal variables for each component of the strain tensor. Thus the anelastic dissipation due to the long-term strains is a viscous dissipation of the form $k{\sigma}^{2}\ge 0$ . Concerning the AAR strain, we authorise its evolution only if the dissipation computed numerically over the time step is indeed positive.

##### 2.6.1.6 Total free-energy potential and state laws

The free-energy potential of the solid skeleton is obtained by summing the energy contributions of the various phenomena studied previously:

$$
\psi ={\psi}^{t\text{*}}+{\psi}^{c}+{\psi}^{g}+{\psi}^{w}
$$

The state laws are obtained by derivation of the total potential:

$$
\sigma =\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}
$$

If one expresses the stresses in the principal basis of the tensile damages, one has for the diagonal terms of the stress tensor:

$$
{\sigma}_{ii}\phantom{\rule{4em}{0ex}}=\phantom{\rule{4em}{0ex}}\begin{array}{c}\left(1-{d}^{c}\right)\frac{{E}^{0}}{{D}^{n}}\left[\left({e}^{{\beta}_{j}+{\beta}_{k}}-{\nu}^{{0}^{2}}\right){\varepsilon}_{ii}^{\mathit{et}}+{\nu}^{0}{\varepsilon}_{jj}^{\mathit{et}}\left(\nu +{e}^{{\beta}_{k}}\right)+{\nu}^{0}{\varepsilon}_{kk}^{\mathit{et}}\left(\nu +{e}^{{\beta}_{j}}\right)\right]\\ +\left(1-{d}^{c}\right)\left[{\lambda}^{0}\text{tr}\left({\varepsilon}^{\mathit{ec}}\right)+2{\mu}^{0}{\varepsilon}_{ii}^{\mathit{ec}}\right]-\left(1-{d}^{c}\right)\left({b}^{g}{P}_{g}+{b}^{w}{P}_{w}\right)\end{array}
$$

and for the off-diagonal terms:

$$
{\sigma}_{ij}=\left(1-{d}^{c}\right)2{\mu}^{0}\left(\frac{{\varepsilon}_{ij}^{\mathit{et}}}{{e}^{{\beta}_{i}+{\beta}_{j}}}+{\varepsilon}_{ij}^{\mathit{ec}}\right)
$$

With ${D}^{n}={e}^{{\beta}_{i}+{\beta}_{j}+{\beta}_{k}}-{\nu}^{{0}^{2}}({e}^{{\beta}_{i}}+{e}^{{\beta}_{j}}+{e}^{{\beta}_{k}}+2{\nu}^{0})$  and ${e}^{{\beta}_{i}}=\frac{1}{1-{d}_{i}^{t}}$

Let us recall that the elastic tensile strains ${\varepsilon}^{\text{et}}$ and compressive strains ${\varepsilon}^{\text{ec}}$ result from two successive decompositions, the partition of the total strain on the one hand:

$$
\varepsilon ={\varepsilon}^{e}+{\varepsilon}^{\text{an}}+{\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}+{\varepsilon}^{0}
$$

and on the other hand the partition as a function of the sign of the effective stress in the sense of damage:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{e}={\varepsilon}^{\text{et}}+{\varepsilon}^{\text{ec}}
$$

And the equation of the thermal strain:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}={\alpha}^{\theta}(T-{T}_{0})I
$$

In the equation , ${\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}$ is the strain due to thermal expansion defined classically by the equation  where ${\alpha}^{\theta}$ is the coefficient of thermal expansion, $T$ the temperature and ${T}_{0}$ the reference temperature. Let us note that in the equation , a reference strain ${\varepsilon}^{0}$ is also present; it is a non-zero reference strain corresponding to a stress-free state. It can be associated only with a volumetric variation of the solid skeleton of chemical origin (chemical shrinkage linked to hydration for example). Indeed, the hydric shrinkage strains or the swelling strains by internal expansive products are manifestations of the intra-pore pressures appearing via the elastic and anelastic strains; they must therefore not be confused with this “imposed reference chemical strain” which is not accompanied by internal pressures and therefore cannot create damage in free strain.

The elastic and anelastic strains are obtained by solution of the system of rheological equations presented in the previous chapter. If the solution of the system of equations of the rheological model is direct (without non-linearity requiring numerical sub-iterations at the Gauss point in the finite-element implementation), then the solution of the whole model is direct for the given total strain field. Let us recall that this aspect of the model meets the objective of ease of implementation and of reduction of the analysis time which we initially set ourselves. #### 2.6.2 Coupling of the damage model and of the rheological model

The rheological model and the damage model having been described individually in the previous paragraphs, they must now be related. As we mentioned in the previous paragraph, the effective stresses and the strains referred to in the chapter devoted to the damage model come from the rheological scheme. The coupling between the viscoelastic phenomena and the damage therefore consists in defining on the rheological scheme the effective stresses and the strains to be used to compute the damage. Currently, the effective stresses used in the damage model come directly from the rheological model for which the integration of equations  and  is carried out with the viscoelastic characteristics of the sound material. The coupling between the damage and the rheology is therefore immediate. Indeed, if the damage increases during a creep test, then the effective stresses will increase in the rheological model, leading to an increase in the creep rate.
