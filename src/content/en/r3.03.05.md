# r3.03.05 Elimination of the dualised boundary conditions

**Summary** :

The taking into account of the boundary conditions in *code_aster*  may be carried out either by a direct elimination technique in simple cases (AFFE_CHAR_CINE), or by means of a dualisation technique (AFFE_CHAR_MECA), and by the introduction of Lagrange multipliers, for the most general cases.

Nevertheless, this approach presents two drawbacks. On the one hand, the addition of Lagrange multipliers increases the number of degrees of freedom of the global problem, and therefore the size of the system to be solved. This point may become penalising as soon as the number of boundary conditions increases (numerous interfaces, kinematic links, impositions of rigid-body motions, etc.). On the other hand, the particular dualisation technique leads to the loss of the positive semi-definite character of the stiffness matrix. This change of nature may lead to shortcomings in robustness, or even to failure of the solution in the case of iterative solvers.

This document presents a method making it possible to eliminate a posteriori the Lagrange multipliers in order to take affine boundary conditions into account. This approach does not concern the Lagrange multipliers linked to the modelling of contact.

The elimination of the boundary conditions is carried out either by the use of the keyword ELIM_LAGR in SOLVEUR [U4.50.01](doc:u4.50.01), or directly with the operator ELIM_LAGR [U4.55.03](doc:u4.55.03), which builds a new concept matr_asse_elim.

## 2 Introduction

The taking into account of the boundary conditions in *Code_Aster*  may be carried out either by a direct elimination technique in simple cases (AFFE_CHAR_CINE), or by means of a dualisation technique (AFFE_CHAR_MECA), and by the introduction of Lagrange multipliers, for the most general cases. This latter approach is very general and makes it possible to treat, under one and the same formalism, all the types of boundary conditions.

Nevertheless, this approach presents two drawbacks. On the one hand, the addition of Lagrange multipliers increases the number of degrees of freedom of the global problem, and therefore the size of the system to be solved. This point may become penalising as soon as the number of boundary conditions increases (numerous interfaces, kinematic links, impositions of rigid-body motions, etc.). On the other hand, the particular dualisation technique leads to the loss of the positive semi-definite character of the stiffness matrix. The search for the solution is then no longer a search for a minimum, but a search for a saddle point []. This change of nature may lead to shortcomings in robustness, or even to failure of the solution in the case of iterative solvers.

This document presents an alternative method to the introduction of Lagrange multipliers in order to take affine boundary conditions into account. This approach does not replace the introduction of the Lagrange multipliers, in particular for studies presenting contact. On the other hand, in the case of studies in which the boundary conditions are fixed, this approach allows gains in robustness, and in performance in the case of a large number of boundary conditions. One first recalls the general technique which governs the elimination of the boundary conditions, then one presents the procedure retained in order to eliminate the boundary conditions in practice, as well as the various associated algorithms. Finally, one presents the elimination technique in the case of an eigenvalue problem.

## 3 Principle of the solution by elimination

### 3.1 Definition of the problem to be solved

The system to be solved may be put in the following general form:

$$
\left[K\right]\lbrace u\rbrace =\lbrace f\rbrace
$$

under the constraint

$$
\left[B\right]\lbrace u\rbrace =\lbrace {u}_{0}\rbrace
$$

With the introduction of Lagrange multipliers, the problem becomes

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\left[K\right]\lbrace u\rbrace +{\left[B\right]}^{T}\lbrace \lambda \rbrace =\lbrace f\rbrace \\ \left[B\right]\lbrace u\rbrace =\lbrace {u}_{0}\rbrace \end{array}
$$

and is put in the general form

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc}K& {B}^{T}\\ B& 0\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}u\\ \lambda \end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}f\\ {u}_{0}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

In the case of double dualisation (AFFE_CHAR_MECA), this matrix is augmented, and the system to be solved becomes

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}K& {B}^{T}& {B}^{T}\\ B& -\alpha {I}_{d}& \alpha {I}_{d}\\ B& \alpha {I}_{d}& -\alpha {I}_{d}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}u\\ {\lambda}_{1}\\ {\lambda}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}f\\ {u}_{0}\\ {u}_{0}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

after having naturally set ${\lambda}_{1}={\lambda}_{2}=\lambda /2$ .

In order to restore its positive semi-definite character to the generic problem $\mathit{Ku}=f$ , in the case where the matrix $K$ presents a topology resulting from being put in the form () or (), one will seek to decompose the solution $u$ of the problem in the form $u={u}_{p}+{u}_{g}$ , where

- ${u}_{p}$ is the particular solution of the problem,
- ${u}_{g}$ is the general solution of the problem, associated with the boundary conditions $[B]\lbrace {u}_{g}\rbrace =\lbrace 0\rbrace$ .

### 3.2 Search for the particular solution

The search for the particular solution consists simply in finding ${u}_{p}$ such that $[B]\lbrace {u}_{p}\rbrace =\lbrace {u}_{0}\rbrace$ . In order to compute ${u}_{p}$ , one considers the decomposition onto the range and the kernel of $B$ , namely ${u}_{p}={u}_{p-i}+{u}_{p-n}$ , with ${u}_{p-i}$ belonging to the range of $B$ and ${u}_{p-n}$ belonging to the kernel of $B$ .

This problem may be solved in the least-squares sense, and in this case one considers the solution of () of minimum ${L}_{2}$ norm, namely ${u}_{p}={u}_{p-i}$ . $\lbrace {u}_{p-i}\rbrace$ is the solution of the minimisation problem:

$$
\lbrace {u}_{p-i}\rbrace =\underset{\lbrace {u}_{p}\rbrace }{\text{ArgMin}}\left({\Vert [B]\lbrace {u}_{p}\rbrace -\lbrace {u}_{0}\rbrace \Vert }^{2}\right)
$$

In order to compute ${u}_{p-i}$ efficiently, provided that $B$ is of full rank (rank equal to $\mathit{Nb}$ , hence no redundant constraints), one exploits the following property:

$$
\left([B]{[B]}^{T}\right){\left([B]{[B]}^{T}\right)}^{-1}={\left[{I}_{d}\right]}_{\mathit{NbxNb}}
$$

and therefore

$$
\left([B]{[B]}^{T}\right){\left([B]{[B]}^{T}\right)}^{-1}\lbrace {u}_{0}\rbrace =\lbrace {u}_{0}\rbrace
$$

The sought solution is naturally given by:

$$
\lbrace {u}_{p}\rbrace =\lbrace {u}_{p-i}\rbrace ={[B]}^{T}{\left([B]{[B]}^{T}\right)}^{-1}\lbrace {u}_{0}\rbrace
$$

In practice, one carries out a Cholesky factorisation of the symmetric matrix ${\mathit{BB}}^{T}$ , with the aid of the MUMPS library, then one solves

$$
[B]{[B]}^{T}\lbrace y\rbrace =\lbrace {u}_{0}\rbrace
$$

Finally, one obtains $\lbrace {u}_{p}\rbrace$ :

| $\lbrace {u}_{p}\rbrace =\lbrace {u}_{p-i}\rbrace ={[B]}^{T}\lbrace y\rbrace$ |
| --- |

### 3.3 Search for the general solution

The general solution ${u}_{g}$ , satisfying $[B]\lbrace {u}_{g}\rbrace =\lbrace 0\rbrace$ , belongs to the kernel of $B$ . The search for the general solution therefore passes through a stage of construction of a basis $Z$ of the kernel of $B$ . One may then project the problem onto the kernel, and solve this problem, which is better posed and of reduced size.

#### 3.3.1 Obtaining a basis of the kernel

Formally, the construction of the kernel of $B$ may be carried out in several ways:

- Singular value decomposition of $B$ , namely $B={\mathit{USV}}^{T}$ . $\mathit{Ker}(B)$ is the space spanned by the columns of ${V}^{T}$ associated with a zero singular value.
- $\mathit{QR}$ decomposition of ${B}^{T}$ , namely $B={R}^{T}{Q}^{T}$ . $\mathit{Ker}(B)$ is the space spanned by the columns of $Q$ associated with a zero value on the diagonal of ${R}^{T}$ .
- $\mathit{LU}$ decomposition of ${B}^{T}$ . The computation of the kernel is not direct, and is obtained from sub-blocks of $L$ .

It is this third method which is implemented in code_aster. One uses the SuperLU library in order to construct the $\mathit{LU}$ factorisation of ${B}^{T}$ :

The blocks :math:`{L}_{1}` and :math:`{L}_{3}` are lower triangular and the block :math:`{U}_{1}` is upper triangular.

It is then easy to see that a basis of $\mathit{Ker}(B)$ is given by:

$$
Z={P}_{r}^{-1}\left[\begin{array}{c}-{[{L}_{1}]}^{-T}{[{L}_{2}]}^{T}\\ {[{I}_{d}]}_{(N-{N}_{b})\times (N-{N}_{b})}\end{array}\right]
$$

One makes here the assumption that $B$ is of full rank. If this is not the case, then it is sufficient to construct ${L}_{1}$ from the block of $L$ associated with the non-zero terms of the diagonal of $U$ .

#### 3.3.2 Projection and solution

Once the basis of the kernel has been computed, the solution is direct. One sets ${u}_{g}=Z\overline{u}$ , and the solution of the problem is therefore given directly by

$$
{[Z]}^{T}[K][Z]\lbrace \overline{u}\rbrace ={Z}^{T}\lbrace f\rbrace
$$

since, by construction,

$$
{[Z]}^{T}{[B]}^{T}\lbrace \lambda \rbrace =\lbrace 0\rbrace
$$

In certain cases, it is necessary to have access to the Lagrange multipliers. They must therefore be reconstructed, from the solutions ${u}_{p}$ and ${u}_{g}$ . To do this, one must solve the first row of the system (), namely

$$
{[B]}^{T}\lbrace \lambda \rbrace =\lbrace f\rbrace -[K]\left([Z]\lbrace \overline{u}\rbrace +\lbrace {u}_{p}\rbrace \right)
$$

If $B$ is of full rank, the solution is written formally:

$$
\lbrace \lambda \rbrace ={\left([B]{[B]}^{T}\right)}^{-1}[B]\lbrace f\rbrace -[K]\left([T]\lbrace \overline{u}\rbrace +\lbrace {u}_{p}\rbrace \right)
$$

and is computed in practice by re-using the factorisation of $B{B}^{T}$ computed for the search for the particular solution. In an analogous manner, if $B$ is not of full rank, it is possible to remove the redundant constraints.

## 4 Eigenmodes and elimination

This section echoes section §7 of the documentation R3.03.01, and justifies the elimination method for the computation of the eigenmodes. One is interested here in the following general problem:

$$
\textrm{Trouver les couples}
\left({\omega}_{0}^{2},\lbrace \varphi \rbrace \right)
$$

The matrices ${M}_{c}$ and ${K}_{c}$ must take the boundary conditions into account, and are assumed here to be obtained from shape functions ${u}_{N}$ satisfying ${\mathit{Bu}}_{N}=0$ . Now, in the present case, the assembled problem is not the problem (), but a problem of larger size, for which the shape functions do not satisfy the kinematic constraints. The matrices ${M}_{b}$ and ${K}_{b}$ are of dimension $(N-\mathit{Nb})\times (N-\mathit{Nb})$ .

Starting from the assembled matrices $M$ and $K$ , of size $N\times N$ , built on the basis of shape functions $u$ which do not satisfy $\mathit{Bu}=0$ , there exist two alternatives for solving the problem (). The first consists in augmenting the matrix $K$ in order to bring out the kinematic constraints, by adding unknowns in the form of Lagrange multipliers. This is the approach retained until now in the code. The problem to be solved is written, with the double-dualisation technique:

$$
\left(\left[\begin{array}{ccc}K& {B}^{T}& {B}^{T}\\ B& -\mathit{Id}& \mathit{Id}\\ B& \mathit{Id}& -\mathit{Id}\end{array}\right]-{\omega}_{0}^{2}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}M& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array}\right]\right)\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\varphi \\ {\lambda}_{1}\\ {\lambda}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

The approach by addition of Lagrange multipliers, in the case of the search for the eigenmodes, is not satisfactory, since, in addition to the loss of the positivity properties of the operator, one adds a significant number of degrees of freedom, and one therefore widens the spectrum of the problem. This widening of the spectrum poses numerical problems, which oblige one to a significant amount of work in this case.

The second approach, the one proposed here, is more natural, and comes closer to the initial problem. The mass and stiffness matrices are indeed assembled on the basis of shape functions which do not satisfy the boundary conditions, but the search for the eigenmodes and the eigenvalues may be carried out in a suitable subspace. It is sufficient to construct a basis of the subspace of the vectors $v$ satisfying $\mathit{Bv}=0$ . This subspace is naturally the kernel of $B$ . It is then sufficient to seek the modes of the unconstrained problem projected into the kernel of $B$ . Letting $Z$ be a basis of the kernel, one then seeks the pairs $\left({\omega}_{0}^{2},\lbrace \psi \rbrace \right)$ which satisfy

$$
\left[{Z}^{T}\left(K-{\omega}_{0}^{2}M\right)Z\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\psi \end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

One then identifies ${M}_{b}={Z}^{T}MZ$ and ${K}_{b}={Z}^{T}KZ$ .

After solution of the reduced modal problem, one may compute the value of the Lagrange multipliers $\lambda$  by the relation:

$$
\left\lbrace \lambda  \right\rbrace  = - \left(BB^T\right)^{-1} B\left(K-{\omega}_{0}^{2}M\right)Z \left\lbrace  \psi   \right\rbrace
$$
