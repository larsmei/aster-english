# u2.08.08 Use of the Method of Manufactured Solutions for software verification

**Summary:**

This document presents the use of the Method of Manufactured Solutions for the software validation of *Code_Aster*. The principle of this method is detailed on a simple example of thermal diffusion. This example allows us to specify the interest of this approach and, in particular, to present the validation procedure which can be implemented. Finally, the software implementation of the Method of Manufactured Solutions, based on the use of symbolic computation, is detailed.

## 1 Introduction

The verification of a scientific software consists in making sure of the absence of error in the programming of the modelling implemented. A classical and very reliable approach consists in comparing the result of the software with that of an analytical solution. Unfortunately, for complex and/or non-linear problems, analytical solutions become very difficult to obtain. It is in this context that the Method of Manufactured Solutions is particularly interesting.

The Method of Manufactured Solutions is a method for the verification of scientific software. It is a systematic method for obtaining analytical solutions for problems which may be very complex and non-linear. Its principle is simple: one gives oneself explicitly the solution sought in the form of an analytical expression and, from this solution, one constructs the data (constraints, loadings) necessary to obtain this solution.

It is interesting to note that this method is very commonly used in fluid mechanics. Thus the “American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics” (AIAA) included it in its software quality-assurance standards in 1998 [bib1]. This practice is much rarer in solid mechanics; thus the “American Society of Mechanical Engineers” (ASME) integrated it into its standard only in 2006 [bib2], that is to say much more recently. This is not linked to any particular difficulty, extensive studies in solid mechanics having been carried out thanks to this method [bib3].

In the remainder of this document, we shall set out the principle of this method on a simple example, then the applicable validation procedure. We shall then detail the software implementation in *Code_Aster*.

## 2 General principle and use for verification

### 2.1 The Method of Manufactured Solutions in thermal analysis

The principle of this method is very simple; we shall illustrate it on an example of linear 2D thermal analysis. First of all, we recall the equations of a linear thermal problem. In the domain $\Omega$ , one seeks the temperature field $T(x,y)$ such that:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}-\lambda \Delta T=s\text{dans}\Omega \\ T={T}_{d}\text{sur}\partial {\Omega}_{d}\\ \lambda \overrightarrow{\nabla}T\cdot \overrightarrow{n}={q}_{n}\text{sur}\partial {\Omega}_{n}\end{array}
$$

where ${T}_{d}$ and ${q}_{n}$ are respectively the temperature and the flux imposed on parts of the boundary.

The steps of the Method of Manufactured Solutions are the following:

- One chooses an arbitrary 2D study domain $\Omega$ comprising the boundaries $\partial {\Omega}_{d}$ with Dirichlet conditions and $\partial {\Omega}_{n}$ with Neumann conditions. By “choose”, one means “choose the shape of the domain”. Thus we choose the shape of the figure .

$$

$$

- of the Neumann term:

$$
{q}_{d}(x,y)=\lambda \overrightarrow{\nabla}T(x,y)\cdot \overrightarrow{n}
$$

If one wants to impose this condition on the edge $\partial {\Omega}_{n}$ , then one knows the expression of the normal $\overrightarrow{n}$ , namely $\overrightarrow{n}={[1,0]}^{T}$ . One therefore obtains:

$$
{q}_{d}(x,y)=\lambda \overrightarrow{\nabla}T(x,y)\cdot \overrightarrow{n}=3\lambda {x}^{2}
$$

At this stage, one has the information necessary for the modelling of the problem studied. It is nevertheless essential to make the following remark: in order to obtain the solution sought, one must indeed assign boundary conditions on the whole boundary $\partial \Omega$ of the study domain. At one's choice, following a partition of the boundary, this may be a mixture of Dirichlet and Neumann conditions. The obligation to impose conditions on the whole boundary is due in particular to the fact that an absence of boundary condition is implicitly a zero Neumann condition. Now the equation shows that one does not have the choice of the value of this condition. In the present example, we choose to impose a Neumann condition on $\partial {\Omega}_{n}$ and Dirichlet conditions on $\partial {\Omega}_{d}$ , $\partial {\Omega}_{d}^{1}$ and $\partial {\Omega}_{d}^{2}$ .

Once the previous equations have been obtained, one moves on to the modelling phase. One begins by choosing a finite-element discretisation ${V}_{h}$ (linear, quadratic). One models, in one's preferred finite-element software, the domain ${\Omega}_{h}$ to which one applies the loadings ${s}_{h}(x,y)$ , ${T}_{\mathrm{dh}}(x,y)$ and ${q}_{\mathrm{dh}}(x,y)$ . After solution, one obtains the approximate solution ${T}_{h}(x,y)$ .

### 2.2 Verification techniques

Thanks to the MMS, various verifications can be carried out.

#### 2.2.1 Choosing a reference solution in the approximation space $T\in {V}_{h}$

This is the simplest verification because one must then obtain $T(x,y)={T}_{h}(x,y)$ . Note that, because of the numerical solution, one will not have perfect equality but the difference must be of the order of machine precision $\text{~}1.E-15$ . In concrete terms, if one uses linear elements, one will then have to choose a linear solution.

#### 2.2.2 Choosing a reference solution outside the approximation space $T\notin {V}_{h}$

In the case where $T\notin {V}_{h}$ , one no longer has the equality of the previous paragraph. On the other hand, one can fairly easily measure the rate of convergence of ${T}_{h}$ towards $T$ when $h\to 0$ .

One specifies that the rate of convergence, with the fineness $h$ of the mesh, of the computed solution towards the analytical solution, in a given norm, is defined as the largest real number $\alpha >0$ such that $\parallel T(x,y)-{T}_{h}(x,y)\parallel <C\ast {h}^{\alpha}$ where $C$ is independent of $h$ .

To measure the rate of convergence, one proceeds as follows:

- One produces increasingly fine meshes of the domain $\Omega$
- For each of the meshes, one determines the solution ${T}_{h}$
- For each of the meshes, one measures, in a well-chosen norm, the error

### 2.3 Software aspects

#### 2.3.1 Symbolic computation

On reading the main principles of the method, it is obvious that the passage from the analytical expression of the solution sought to the input data of the finite-element problem to be solved requires numerous differentiation and algebra operations. To facilitate and automate this step, one relies on symbolic-computation functionalities. They are provided by the Sympy module [bib4] of the Python language. It is a module written entirely in Python and of great simplicity of use.

To facilitate the implementation of the MMS, a Python class TensorModule has been developed which defines the tensor object as well as its methods (singly and doubly contracted product, trace, determinant, …). We have also developed the main operators applying to these tensors (gradient, symmetric gradient, divergence, Laplacian). On the basis of this class, we have defined another module HookTensor which makes it possible simply to implement various Hooke tensors (isotropic, anisotropic, orthotropic with nautical angles).

These classes are placed in the directory bibpyt/Utilitai of the Code_Aster sources.

#### 2.3.2 Implementation in Code_Aster

In this part, we describe how the MMS is implemented in the command language of Code_Aster. To do this, we rely on the previous linear thermal example and detail the main steps required. The precise implementation is carried out in the test case tplp107a.

| import sympy |
| --- |

One imports the symbolic-computation module Sympy, in order to use its functionalities.

| X,Y=sympy.symbols('XY'); |
| --- |

One defines the space variables which will be used for the symbolic computation.

| T=sympy.Function('T'); S=sympy.Function('S'); |
| --- |

One defines the names of the functions; $T$ will of course be the temperature and $f$ the source term.

| T=100*(X**6+Y**6); |
| --- |

One gives the expression of the manufactured solution that one seeks to recover.

| S=-Lambda*(sympy.diff(sympy.diff(T,X),X)+sympy.diff(sympy.diff(T,Y),Y)); |
| --- |

One deduces the expression of the source term from the equation .

| SS=FORMULE(NOM_PARA=('X','Y'),VALE=str(S)); |
| --- |

This command makes it possible to transform the previous expression into a Code_Aster formula object which can be evaluated in the software in the following manner:
