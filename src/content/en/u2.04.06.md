# u2.04.06 How to excavate a tunnel: excavation methodology

> **Summary.**

This note proposes a methodology for simulating the excavation of an underground gallery with *code_aster*. The basic method is one commonly used in this kind of study: the "convergence–confinement" method.

After a reminder of the principle of the method, the main stages of the *code_aster* command file are described. Various numerical examples make it possible to validate the procedure.

## 1 How to simulate the excavation of a tunnel?

**Context**

Geomechanics studies are generally based on a simulation of the excavation of an underground gallery. Examples of application may be cited:

1. assessing the excavation damaged zone (EDZ) around a storage gallery;
2. studying the resaturation of a storage cell by the site water.

A certain number of studies have already been carried out by the AMA department on this subject, with *code_aster*. However, few practical elements are available in the documentation for reproducing this type of analysis. The MMC department has undertaken such a modelling with *code_aster*, in order to master the procedure for applying the method classically used for this kind of analysis: the "convergence–confinement" method. What emerges from this experience is that this application is not completely trivial and that it is necessary to ask oneself a few practical technical questions of implementation. Capitalising this experience for future users appeared fairly important, in the collective interest of the studies on storage in particular.

**Objective**

The main objective of this note is to provide a few preliminary technical pieces of advice to users of *code_aster* wishing to model an underground excavation.

**Methodology**

This note presents an application, to a *code_aster* command file, of the convergence–confinement method. After a brief reminder of the principle of the method, a practical and operational description of the commands to be used is given. The method is illustrated by *code_aster* validation analyses, whose command files are supplied in an annex.

**Result**

Thanks to the implementation of the protocol proposed, two *code_aster* validation analyses have been carried out. The relative difference between the numerical results and the analytical solution is less than 2%.

**Prospects**

The method can be extended to non-linear analyses (plasticity, damage) and to coupled THM analyses, in particular within the framework of studies intended for the storage of nuclear waste.

## 2 Introduction

For several years, studies have been carried out with *code_aster* in order to model the behaviour of geotechnical structures (earth dams, tunnels, engineered barriers for waste storage…).

*code_aster* has already been used in particular to simulate the excavation of galleries or shafts, within the framework of the project *Geological storage of high-level long-lived nuclear waste (HAVL)* (T4-01-10) or during earlier studies on deep storage. The reports written up to now (for example [bib6]_, [bib7]_, [bib4]_) naturally focus on the results, in order to answer the precise technical question that motivated the study. Now, simulating an excavation with the aid of a finite-element code is not necessarily an easy thing, and even if the general principles are recalled in the documents cited above, one ultimately finds few elements on the structure of the command files that supported the analyses.

In order to help the engineers in charge of future studies of underground excavation with *code_aster*, this note gives a few practical pieces of advice for getting started with this type of analysis. Indeed, within the framework of the Storage project, MMC decided to master completely the approach implemented by AMA in 2000 and in 2001. To achieve this, the whole approach was reproduced with version 6 of *code_aster*, on the basis of a new mesh and by exploring a few variants. MMC also benefited from the assistance of the AMA staff. Furthermore, this work led to a validation of *code_aster* against classical analytical formulae in linear elasticity (formulae of Kirsch and the convergence-confinement method, [bib5]_).

This report therefore presents:

1. the classical method for simulating an underground excavation in 2D by means of a finite-element code;
2. the different options available for applying this method with *code_aster*;
3. two test cases for the validation of *code_aster* for underground-excavation problems.

The prerequisite for a profitable reading of this note is the basic training in the use of *code_aster*, together with a minimum of familiarity with the software package. The detail of the various commands used is given by the *code_aster* User documentation ([https://www.code-aster.org](https://www.code-aster.org)).

## 3 A method for simulating the excavation of a gallery from a 2D model: the convergence-confinement method

### 3.1 General principle

This part draws largely on [bib5]_. It should be noted that the CIH and TEGG have also carried out a certain number of studies with this method (for example, [bib2]_). The reader is advised to refer to these documents for further information on the principle of the method. The paragraphs that follow summarise only the essentials of the approach.

The convergence-confinement method is commonly used in the engineering of underground structures. Its objective is to obtain an order of magnitude of the displacements of the tunnel walls and of the forces taken up by the rock and by the support. This method makes it possible to simplify the analysis of a three-dimensional structure by a two-dimensional analysis, through the introduction of a dimensionless parameter $lambda$ called the "deconfinement rate". It rests on the following hypotheses:

1. plane strain with the small-perturbation hypothesis;
2. the tunnel is assumed to be of circular cross-section and of horizontal axis;
3. homogeneous ground of infinite extent;
4. rock mass following a linear elastic or elastoplastic constitutive behaviour;
5. initial stress state assumed isotropic and homogeneous;
6. deep tunnel: no significant variation of the stresses over the height of the gallery. In practice, if $H$ is the mean depth of the structure and $R$ its radius, this hypothesis is assumed to be satisfied if $H/R>10$;
7. quasi-static equilibrium (no acceleration terms).

One is interested in a section situated in a plane perpendicular to the axis of the tunnel, and one wishes to carry out a two-dimensional analysis. The parameter $lambda$ is supposed to take into account the mechanical influence of the proximity of the tunnel face to this section, that is to say of a phenomenon whose origin lies outside the plane considered by the analysis. $lambda$ depends on several parameters (rock, support, length of unsupported tunnel behind the face…) and its determination is not necessarily immediate (numerous publications on the subject, for example [bib1]_). This problem of the analytical determination of the deconfinement rate lies outside the scope of this document.

In fact, $lambda$ is introduced in order to consider a fictitious stress tensor $underline{underline{sigma}}$ in the ground, which is a fraction of the initial stress ${sigma}_{0}$:

$underline{underline{sigma}}=(1-lambda ).{underline{underline{sigma}}}^{0}$ with $0le lambda le 1$

Figure u2.04.06-fig-3-1 below illustrates the evolution of $lambda$ and of the radial stress ${sigma}_{R}$ for an unsupported tunnel.

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.06/10002B48000069D5000060A3DE7678F233882525.svg)

*Evolution of the deconfinement rate $lambda$ and of the radial stress ${sigma}_{R}$ in the case of an unsupported tunnel*

Let us note that $lambda =1$ corresponds to the total deconfinement of the rock: the influence of the face on the behaviour of the slice of tunnel has disappeared and the tunnel can be likened to a very thick tube.

Since part, or even the whole, of the stresses initially present within the rock mass disappear (this is precisely the phenomenon of deconfinement), the walls of the excavation will tend to draw closer in order to reach a new mechanical equilibrium. This is the phenomenon of "convergence". This phenomenon can lead to the failure of the structure if the structure does not manage to recover a stable state of equilibrium following the excavation.

If, for reasons of safety or of stability, one decides to install a support or a lining at the wall of the tunnel, these will, through their mechanical stiffness, oppose the natural phenomenon of convergence. In this case, the final equilibrium therefore depends on the mechanical interaction between the rock and the lining. In general, this equilibrium does not allow the stresses in the rock mass to vanish as in the case of the unsupported tunnel. The ground is then said to be confined, whence the name of the "convergence-confinement" method.

Graphically, the application of this method amounts to seeking the point of intersection of the convergence curve, deduced from the behaviour of the rock mass, and of the confinement curve, deduced from the behaviour of the support (u2.04.06-fig-3-2).

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.06/10000000000001C7000001127E986BF212274A6E.png)

*Example of convergence and confinement curves*

The equations of the "convergence-confinement" method in the case of a linear elastic rock mass are supplied in u2.04.06-formules-analytiques-methode-convergence-confinement.

Whether for analytical or numerical analyses, this method makes it possible, with the aid of a simple 2D model, to treat the 3D problem constituted by the simulation of an excavation.

### 3.2 Application of the method for a numerical analysis by finite elements

A particularity of excavation analyses by finite elements is the necessity of implementing several models (in the broad sense).

Indeed, a classical course of the modelling can be summarised by the following stages:

1. stage 1: initialisation of the *in situ* stresses;
2. stage 2: computation of the nodal reactions at the walls of the excavation;
3. stage 3: deconfinement of the rock mass in order to simulate the progressive excavation and the moving away of the face;
4. stage 4: possible installation of a support / lining and end of the deconfinement.

If the study requires it, the sequence of stages 2, 3 and 4 can be repeated (the case of an excavation in divided sections, for example).

In most cases, the sequence of the analyses is therefore based on four configurations (u2.04.06-fig-3-3).

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.06/1000245C000069D500001A9096C63620667DC575.svg)

*Example of a typical sequence of an excavation analysis with an analysis code*

The first configuration serves to:

1. initialise the stresses of geostatic origin;
2. initialise the hydrostatic pressure due to the possible presence of water and the temperature (the present note does not treat this precise point in detail);

The second configuration makes it possible to compute the reactions at the nodes representing the edge of the excavation.

At these stages of the modelling, all the elements of the mesh therefore correspond to a material of soil or rock type. One therefore obtains a soil mass in which there reigns a stress state corresponding to the *in situ* stress state in the plane perpendicular to the axis of the gallery. The nodal reactions at the edge of the excavation are also known, which will make possible a partial or total deconfinement of the rock mass in the stages that follow.

The third configuration is devoted to the deconfinement: the nodal reactions at the edge of the excavation are decreased in order to simulate the excavation of the tunnel. When this stage is carried out, the finite elements in the region corresponding to the interior of the gallery must no longer contribute to the stiffness of the model. As will be seen further on, this can be taken into account in several ways in practice.

One possibly passes to a fourth stage if one wants to simulate the installation of a concrete support in the course of deconfinement, for example. In this case, elements with the characteristics of concrete are added and the decrease of the nodal reactions computed in stage no. 1 is continued in order to complete the analysis.

It is therefore observed that certain parts of the initial model will be assigned successively properties of soil, of "void" and then of concrete. The source of a few difficulties intrinsic to this kind of analysis lies in this sequence.

The application of this approach by means of *code_aster* is the subject of the following chapters. It is based on a simple case.

## 4 Before tackling the code_aster command file…

This chapter concerns a few particular points of modelling which it seems important to comment on before turning to the command files proper. It is composed of a series of paragraphs each treating a question that an engineer may ask when carrying out a classical excavation analysis with the aid of a finite-element code such as *code_aster*.

### 4.1 How to define the models from a simple mesh?

The mesh chosen in this study represents a quarter of a model representing a cylindrical gallery in an infinite medium. The radius of the gallery is $1,50m$, the thickness of the concrete is $0,30m$ and the mesh is a square of $20m$ on a side. According to the usual rules of modelling, the ratio between the excavated radius $R$ and the characteristic dimension of the mesh $L$ is sufficient to consider that the boundary conditions do not disturb the behaviour of the excavation ($Lge 10times R$).

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.06/maillage_utilise_materiaux.png)

*Mesh used and materials*

From the point of view of the models (in the *code_aster* sense), a few particular zones of the mesh must be distinguished (in addition to the other, more classical zones, such as the edges of the mesh) and the following objects created (the names refer to the command files presented in the Annex):

1. the excavated edge where the nodal reactions will be applied in order to simulate the deconfinement (called $text{BORD}$);
2. the two points which are situated at the ends of this curve, and which are concerned both by the deconfinement loading and by the boundary conditions at the edge of the rock mass.

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.06/10000686000069D500006299CC0B444332CFF180.svg)

*Points and set of particular points to be identified in the *code_aster* models*

One can therefore define (for example, because several configurations are possible):

1. a model $text{SOL}$, in which the whole mesh is assigned finite elements;
2. a model $text{SOL_REST}$ which does not include the mesh cells corresponding to the excavated part (they are not assigned finite elements);
3. a model $text{SOL_REST0}$ which comprises $text{SOL_REST}$ and the mesh cells corresponding to the concrete lining assigned finite elements.

**Note:**

The use of such a geometry for a real excavation analysis is partly open to criticism, because the symmetry proposed risks generating a non-physical loading. In the event of application of the self-weight, for example, the latter would be directed upwards in the lower part of the tunnel!

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.06/100025180000521000006D73C830635FBC80FB6F.svg)

*Example of an aberration that the use of a quarter of a model can generate in the simulation of a tunnel*

For a realistic study in which one would wish to initialise the stresses by means of a self-weight loading, it would therefore be necessary to "mesh the ground" down to the rigid substratum (rock considered non-deformable), or at least down to a depth sufficient to be free of the problem mentioned above. A half-model is therefore meshed in that case.

However, within the framework of our study, this meshing constraint did not hinder us, since we took into account neither the self-weight nor the THM couplings. The loading simulated is entirely compatible with the analytical solutions tested.

### 4.2 How to initialise the stresses?

The *in situ* stresses are generally represented by a tensor of order 2 whose principal directions correspond to the vertical and to the horizontal. The vertical stress is generally equal to the weight of the different formations situated above the point considered, and the horizontal stress is proportional to the vertical stress:

$begin{array}{c}{sigma}_{v}=gamma .z\ {sigma}_{h}={K}_{0}.{sigma}_{v}end{array}$

with $gamma$ the unit weight of the overlying ground (in $mathit{kN}/{m}^{3}$ for example) and ${K}_{0}$ a dimensionless coefficient. ${K}_{0}$ can be determined by *in situ* measurements or estimated by more or less empirical relations. In the case of a semi-infinite rock mass subjected to an external stress on its upper edge or to its self-weight, the theory of linear elasticity supplies a value of ${K}_{0}$ as a function of the Poisson's ratio $nu$:

${K}_{0}=frac{nu}{1-nu }$

Two methods have been tested with *code_aster* for initialising the stresses in the surrounding ground:

1. carrying out an analysis (command STAT_NON_LINE) with a fictitious material endowed with a Poisson's ratio making it possible to obtain the desired ratio ${K}_{0}$. This analysis is carried out on the model that takes up the whole mesh of the study (for example, the model named $text{SOL}$ in the previous chapter). In this case, ${K}_{0}le 1$ (the case of linear elasticity). There exist numerous cases where ${K}_{0}ge 1$ (if the soil is subjected to tectonic stresses, for example). In this case, the following method becomes obligatory;
2. assigning the stresses directly to all the elements of the mesh by the command CREA_CHAMP(OPERATION = ‘AFFE’);

The first solution requires a fictitious material to be defined and a further analysis to be carried out. However, if the loading is the self-weight (which is not the case of the test case that we propose), this method seemed to us both intuitive and simple. In the case of a uniform stress field, the use of CREA_CHAMP is undeniably the most attractive method: it saves computation time and its call is even simpler. For more complex stress distributions, CREA_CHAMP also works but we have not used it (refer to the documentation [U4.72.04](doc:u4.72.04), which gives an example that can be adapted to our problem).

### 4.3 How to compute the nodal reactions at the edge of the "future" gallery?

To compute the nodal reactions at the edge of the gallery, it is necessary to block this part of the mesh. This operation must not generate non-physical stresses through incompatibility with the loading applied during the stress-initialisation phase. One possibility offered consists in imposing the same loading as during the initialisation of the stresses while blocking the nodes of the edge of the gallery *only during this analysis stage*. This operation has no effect on the global result, which remains identical to that of the previous stage, but the "temporary" blocking of the nodes of the edge of the gallery makes it possible to evaluate the nodal reactions there.

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.06/100005A800004DD300003E03AE68D24ED22180B4.svg)

*Blocking of the nodes of the edge of the gallery in order to compute the nodal reactions there*

Concretely, this relative blocking of the edge of the gallery is possible thanks to the option DIDI (for differential Dirichlet) of the keyword EXCIT of the operator STAT_NON_LINE (Document [U4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03)). The blocking of these nodes applies only to the displacement increment considered and not to the total displacement (one imposes $Delta u=0$ and not $u=0$).

The initial state of this analysis (keyword ETAT_INIT of the operator STAT_NON_LINE) is defined by the stress field obtained at the end of the previous stage.

Once this intermediate analysis has been carried out, the computation of the nodal reactions is carried out simply with the command CALC_CHAMP (FORCE = ‘REAC_NODA’). One must then supply to the command CALC_CHAMP all the loadings that produced the result from which the nodal reactions are computed, without omitting the volumetric loadings if they exist (not taken into account in the examples treated here).

A loading vector is then built by recovering the nodal reactions (CREA_CHAMP with the keywords TYPE_CHAM = ’NOEU_DEPL_R’, NOM_CHAM = ’REAC_NODA’ and OPERATION = ’EXTR’). It should be noted that, according to paragraph §3.1.1 of the *code_aster* User documentation [u4.72.04](doc:u4.72.04), the option TYPE_CHAM = ‘NOEU_DEPL_R’ of the command CREA_CHAMP is in fact without effect here (but nevertheless obligatory from the syntactic point of view), since an extraction is being carried out. This vector is then defined by the command AFFE_CHAR_MECA with the keyword VECT_ASSE as a loading for the following call to the command STAT_NON_LINE (corresponding to the progressive excavation of the gallery). This loading is associated with a function (operator DEFI_FONCTION) describing the evolution of the deconfinement rate as the excavation progresses.

Let us also note that all the nodal reactions are extracted: those which act on the edge of the gallery as well as those which act on the other edges of the model. Since the latter act on points that are blocked *at all the stages of the excavation analysis*, their injection as a loading into the following STAT_NON_LINE is without effect on the stresses and the strains within the structure.

### 4.4 How to simulate the creation of a "void" in the rock mass and the placing of the concrete?

Once the computation of the nodal reactions has been carried out, the question arises of the "elimination" of the excavated part of the numerical model so that its stiffness does not hinder the convergence of the tunnel. To achieve this, two methods have been retained (u2.04.06-fig-4-5):

1. method $A$: quasi-cancellation of the mechanical properties of the elements situated in the excavated zone (example: $E=0,0001mathit{Pa}$), then introduction of more realistic properties at the installation of the support or of the lining. This method makes it possible to simplify the *code_aster* command file and gives correct results for the simple case that we have studied (small circular gallery, excavated in a single section in an elastic rock mass). For carrying out more elaborate studies in which the numerical treatment could be affected by the presence of a finite element of very low stiffness, it nevertheless seems to us preferable to rely on the following method;
2. method $B$: initialisation of the stresses directly by creation of fields at the Gauss points coming from an analysis concerning a previous stage.

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.06/100025B4000069D500003ACFDB795A15E5CFB870.svg)

*Different modelling principles for simulating the deconfinement of a rock mass*

Other methods that we have not tested can no doubt be applied to the problem studied (such as the creation of double nodes at the boundaries between materials, which make it possible to tie the two structures together or not).

#### 4.4.1 Method A

Method $A$ calls for no particular comment: it is sufficient to assign very weak mechanical characteristics to the mesh cells that become "void" during the deconfinement analysis.

One proceeds in two stages:

1. a first command STAT_NON_LINE, which makes it possible to reinject the loading composed of the vector of the nodal reactions and of the boundary conditions. The "void" mesh cells therefore correspond to a very soft material;
2. a second call to STAT_NON_LINE which introduces the concrete support or lining by assigning to the corresponding mesh cells characteristics that are realistic for such a material.

At each call, the initialisation of the analysis takes up the whole of the fields coming from the previous analyses (operand EVOL_NOLI for the keyword ETAT_INIT).

#### 4.4.2 Method B

This procedure is based on the chaining of several models (in the *code_aster* sense). The analysis is carried out by copying certain fields from one model to the other.

The fields to be assigned to the model corresponding to stage $B-3$ of figure u2.04.06-fig-4-5 are formally the linear combination of two fields:

1. the fields coming from the previous analysis stage ($B-2$) and which concern only the model corresponding to the soil mass deprived of the excavated zone;
2. the fields assigned to the elements of the mesh-cell group which represent the concrete segments, in the model which comprises the rock mass and the lining of the gallery. In our case, these fields must be initialised to 0 in $B-3$. For this, one can for example assign a zero weight to their contribution in the linear combination. Thus these fields can in fact be obtained by an intermediate analysis without any real physical meaning, for example the simple application of the boundary conditions.

The command CREA_CHAMP with the option ASSE is used to assign to the Gauss points of the third model the linear combination of fields coming from previous analyses.

### 4.5 Summary of the methods proposed

To initialise the stresses, two methods can be called upon:

1. Method $I$: carry out an analysis (call to STAT_NON_LINE) on a fictitious material;
2. Method $mathit{II}$: create the desired stress field by CREA_CHAMP.

To simulate the excavation and the placing of the segments, one has the choice between:

1. Method $A$: which consists in assigning mechanically very "soft" characteristics in the excavated zone;
2. Method $B$: which resorts to the use of several models that follow one another and which is closer to the physical reality of the structure modelled, the materials appearing and disappearing by activation from one model to the other. This method will be preferred.

A summary flow chart is proposed in u2.04.06-organigramme-synthese-methodes.

## 5 Examples of command files

This part presents examples of structures of *code_aster* command files concerning a circular excavation in an infinite linear elastic medium, within the framework of a purely mechanical study (no THM coupling). The cases presented here are the subject of the validation test cases SSLP105A, SSLP105B and SSLP105C.

Three analysis cases are presented in this part:

1. an excavation without support, with initialisation by an analysis on a fictitious material in order to obtain the desired stress field (method $I$);
2. an excavation with support, initialisation of the stresses by a call to CREA_CHAMP and following of method $A$ for the deconfinement and the placing of the segments (methods $mathit{II}+A$);
3. an excavation with support, initialisation of the stresses by a call to CREA_CHAMP and following of method $B$ for the deconfinement and the placing of the segments (methods $mathit{II}+B$).

For cases 2 and 3, the excavation scenario is the following: excavation, deconfinement to 50% ($lambda =0.5$), placing of the segments of thickness $30mathit{cm}$ and end of the deconfinement.

### 5.2 Case no. 1: excavation without support, with initialisation of the stresses by an analysis and "softening" of the "excavated" elements

This example is relatively simple: it is a matter of simulating an excavation without installation of the support, with total deconfinement at the edge of the gallery. Only one model is therefore used for the whole analysis.

The initial state is generated by an analysis (STAT_NON_LINE) which concerns the whole of the mesh. The properties of the elements are assigned as a function of the stress state that one wants to reach (here ${K}_{0}=1$, therefore $nu =0,4999$; the value $0,5$, which would mean the incompressibility of the rock, cannot be used).

The following analysis concerns the nodal reactions at the edge of the future gallery. It is initialised from the stresses coming from the first call to STAT_NON_LINE.

The last call to STAT_NON_LINE serves to reinject the nodal reactions into a model in which the mechanical properties of the excavated elements have been very strongly weakened ($E$ tends towards $0.$). The ground is then completely deconfined by making these reactions tend towards $0$.

The command file corresponds to the test case SSLP105C.

### 5.3 Case no. 2: excavation with support, with initialisation of the stresses by a call to CREA_CHAMP and deconfinement following method A

The excavation scenario described above is followed. Only one model is used for the whole analysis. An additional command STAT_NON_LINE makes it possible to introduce the segments with a realistic stiffness after a deconfinement of 50%.

The command file corresponds to the test case SSLP105B.

### 5.4 Case no. 3: excavation with support, with initialisation of the stresses by a call to CREA_CHAMP and deconfinement following method B

The excavation scenario described above is still followed. This time, three models are used and an intermediate analysis (without physical reality, called a "dummy") is necessary in order to transfer the fields of variables from one model to the other at the moment of the installation of the segments after a deconfinement of 50%.

The command file corresponds to the test case SSLP105A.

## 7 By way of conclusion: advice and prospects

This note proposes a methodology which makes it possible to carry out excavation analyses with the aid of *code_aster*. Several excavation scenarios are reviewed and several methods are proposed.

The method and the software package are validated in the case of a circular gallery, excavated in an infinite rock mass constituted by a linear elastic material. *code_aster* reproduces in an entirely satisfactory manner the behaviour of such an underground structure, with or without taking the support and/or the lining into account.

From the user's point of view, it seems more practical and quicker to initialise the stresses by a call to the command CREA_CHAMP rather than by an analysis on a fictitious material.

If one seeks to model a purely mechanical behaviour and if the phasing of the excavation is relatively simple, working with a single model appears to be the easiest method. It is sufficient to assign very weak material properties to the mesh cells that become "void". In the more complicated cases, the implementation of several models used successively may prove more reliable from the point of view of the practical implementation (modelling error) and from the numerical point of view (analysis error), despite the intermediate procedures for transferring the fields (stresses, displacements, pressures, temperatures, internal variables…) from one model to the other.

A later stage of validation of *code_aster* could be carried out on the coupled linear problems (THM in a saturated elastic medium) or on coupled and/or non-linear problems (CJS 1 model akin to the Mohr-Coulomb model, short-term excavation under undrained conditions to be compared with [bib3]_).
