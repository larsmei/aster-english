# r7.02.06 Bordet model and Rice and Tracey model

**Summary**

The foundations of the local-approach models of fracture which make it possible to model brittle fracture (Bordet model) and ductile initiation (Rice and Tracey model) are recalled.

In the case of the brittle-fracture model (Bordet), one describes how the fracture probability of a structure is computed from the knowledge of the mechanical fields loading it. Placing oneself in the general case of a non-monotonic thermomechanical loading path and assuming that certain parameters of the models do not depend on the temperature, one establishes the general expression for the cumulative fracture probabilities.

The model leading to the cavity-growth law of Rice and Tracey is then presented, together with the ductile-initiation criterion relating to it. Finally, indications concerning the implementation of these models in *code_aster*  are summarised.

## 1 Introduction

One is interested here in a metallic structure loaded thermomechanically. One seeks to determine fracture criteria for this structure, representative of the two mechanisms encountered on certain steels:

- at low temperature, certain metallic materials (such as reactor-vessel steel) may behave as brittle materials, fracturing suddenly by cleavage,
- at higher temperature, ductile tearing appears.

By contrast with the global approach, the Bordet and Rice–Tracey models presented here rely on knowledge of the mechanical fields in the most highly loaded zones in order to obtain a local fracture criterion representative of the physical mechanisms involved (instability of cleavage microcracks, or growth then coalescence of the porosity).

The foundations of the Beremin model, which likewise makes it possible to model brittle fracture, are given in the documentation [R7.02.04](doc:r7.02.04).

Advice on the use of the Beremin and Bordet models is given in the documentation [U2.05.08](doc:u2.05.08).

## 2 The Bordet model

The Bordet model is presented briefly here. For further details, one may refer to [3], [4] or [5].

The Bordet model is based on the same foundations as that of Beremin. It defines a probability of fracture by local cleavage. However, in the Beremin model, one assumes the creation of microcracks at the moment when the plasticity threshold is reached, and these microcracks remain potentially active throughout the loading which follows. In steels, however, global fracture is mainly related to newly created microcracks. The level of plastic strain reached at each time instant must therefore be taken into account. This is already possible in the Beremin model *via*  the plastic-correction option defined in the document [U4.81.22](doc:u4.81.22).

In the Bordet model, this is taken into account by considering that the probability of fracture by cleavage is the product of the probabilities of nucleation and of propagation at the same time instant.

### 2.1 Local cleavage-fracture probability of the Bordet model

The local criterion is defined here as the statistical event of simultaneously fulfilling the conditions of nucleation and of propagation of the microcracks. In the model, nucleation and propagation of the microcracks are considered as independent events; nucleation denotes the fracture of a carbide leading to the formation of a microcrack, whereas propagation is defined as the local instability of cleavage driven solely by the local stresses.

The local cleavage-fracture probability is then written:

$$
{P}_{\mathit{cliv}}={P}_{\mathit{nucl}}{P}_{\mathit{propa}}
$$

By plastic strain ${\varepsilon}_{p}$ one means the equivalent plastic strain defined by:

$$
{\epsilon}_{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}{\epsilon}_{p}:{\epsilon}_{p}}
$$

This definition is valid only for elastoplastic constitutive laws with a von Mises criterion. In *code_aster* , only constitutive laws of this type are therefore suited to this model.

#### 2.1.1 Local nucleation probability

With the definition given above, the probability of nucleation of a microcrack during an increment of plastic strain $d{\epsilon}_{p}$ is proportional to the yield stress ${\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p})$ at the temperature $T$ and the plastic strain rate ${\dot{\varepsilon}}_{p}$ :

$$
{P}_{\mathit{nucl}}\propto {N}_{\mathit{unc}}({\epsilon}_{p}){\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p})d{\epsilon}_{p}
$$

This remains true so long as the number of microcracked carbides remains small compared with the number of sound carbides ${N}_{\mathrm{unc}}({\varepsilon}_{p})$ . The microcracking rate of the carbides is constant for a given plastic strain; the number of sound sites varies exponentially with ${\varepsilon}_{p}$ . Denoting by ${\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}$ the yield stress at a reference temperature and plastic strain rate and by ${\varepsilon}_{p,0}$ a reference plastic strain, the probability ${P}_{\mathrm{nucl}}$ may be expressed as follows:

$$
{P}_{\mathit{nucl}}\propto \frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p})}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}}\exp\left(\frac{-{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p})}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}}\frac{{\epsilon}_{p}}{{\epsilon}_{p,0}}\right)
$$

If the plastic strain is small, and therefore if the cracking of carbides is fairly limited, i.e. $\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}{\epsilon}_{p,0}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p})}\gg {\epsilon}_{p}$ , then the nucleation probability reduces to ${P}_{\mathit{nucl}}\propto \frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p})}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}}$ .

#### 2.1.2 Local propagation probability

As in the Beremin model [R4.02.04](doc:r4.02.04), the size density of the carbide microcracks is assumed to be distributed according to an inverse power law (with $\alpha$ and $\beta$ material parameters independent of the temperature and of the strain rates, and $l$ the size of the microcrack):

$$
f(l)=\frac{\alpha}{{l}^{\beta}}
$$

The cracking of a carbide will propagate into the ferrite (by virtue of the propagation inertia alone, by a dynamic effect) only on condition that a sufficiently high local stress is present. An upper bound on the size of a nucleated ferritic microcrack ${l}_{max}=l\left({\sigma}_{\mathit{th}}\right)$ is therefore introduced; it makes it possible to define the minimum local stress necessary for the propagation of the largest possible ferritic microcrack. The propagation probability is therefore written:

$$
{P}_{\mathit{propa}}({\sigma}_{I})={\int}_{{l}_{c}({\sigma}_{I})}^{{l}_{max}}f(l)\mathit{dl}
$$

With ${\sigma}_{I}$ the maximum principal stress, ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{th}}$ the threshold below which propagation cannot take place and ${l}_{c}$ the critical size of the ferritic microcrack, obeying the Griffith relation for an elliptical microcrack:

$$
{l}_{c}\left(\sigma \right)=\frac{\pi E{\gamma}_{p}}{2\left(1-{\nu}^{2}\right){\sigma}^{2}}
$$

With $E$ Young's modulus, $\nu$ Poisson's ratio and ${\gamma}_{p}$ the surface energy density. One finally obtains:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{P}_{\mathit{propa}}({\sigma}_{I})& =0& {\sigma}_{I}<{\sigma}_{\mathit{th}}\\ {P}_{\mathit{propa}}({\sigma}_{I})& =\left({\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{I}}{{\sigma}_{u}}\right)}^{m}-{\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{th}}}{{\sigma}_{u}}\right)}^{m}\right)& {\sigma}_{I}⩾{\sigma}_{\mathit{th}}\end{array}
$$

With $m$ a parameter independent of the temperature, like $\alpha$ and $\beta$ , and ${\sigma}_{u}$ which may depend on it (if Young's modulus depends on it).

As for the Beremin model, the effects of the orientation of the microcracks relative to the direction of the maximum principal stress are taken into account only *via* the parameter ${\sigma}_{u}$ .

#### 2.1.3 Local cleavage probability

As stated previously, the local cleavage probability is assumed to be the product of the nucleation probability and the propagation probability; for this one considers that, during an infinitesimal increment of plastic strain, the active stress is constant. Whence, if ${\sigma}_{I}⩾{\sigma}_{\mathit{th}}$ :

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}{P}_{\mathit{cliv}}\left({\sigma}_{I},d{\epsilon}_{p}\right)& \propto & {P}_{\mathit{nucl}}\left(d{\epsilon}_{p}\right).{P}_{\mathit{propa}}\left({\sigma}_{I}\right)\\ \text{}& \propto & \frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}\left(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p}\right)}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}}\exp\left(\frac{-{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}\left(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p}\right)}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}}\frac{{\epsilon}_{p}}{{\epsilon}_{p,0}}\right)\left({\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{I}\left({\epsilon}_{p}\right)}{{\sigma}_{u}}\right)}^{m}-{\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{th}}}{{\sigma}_{u}}\right)}^{m}\right)d{\epsilon}_{p}\end{array}
$$

This equation does not indicate that the nucleated microcracks remain active during the increment of plastic strain, but rather that the propagation condition for each of these microcracks is determined by the value of the local stress field at the moment of creation.

The probability that a ferritic microcrack is created and propagates over a plastic-strain interval $\left[0,{\epsilon}_{p,u}\right]$ is then:

$$
{P}_{\mathit{cliv}}\left({\sigma}_{I},{\epsilon}_{p,u}\right)\propto {\int}_{0}^{{\epsilon}_{p,u}}\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}\left(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p}\right)}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}}\exp\left(\frac{-{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}\left(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p}\right)}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}}\frac{{\epsilon}_{p}}{{\epsilon}_{p,0}}\right)\left({\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{I}\left({\epsilon}_{p}\right)}{{\sigma}_{u}}\right)}^{m}-{\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{th}}}{{\sigma}_{u}}\right)}^{m}\right)d{\epsilon}_{p}
$$

This probability does indeed reduce to $0$ if the stress remains below ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{th}}$ throughout the loading path.

### 2.2 Global cleavage-fracture probability of Bordet

The previous paragraph has made it possible to determine the local cleavage-fracture probability. Following the weakest-link principle, the global cleavage-fracture probability over ${n}_{c}$ potential initiation sites is written:

$$
{P}_{\mathit{Bordet}}=1-\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{{n}_{c}}{P}_{\mathit{cliv}}\left({\sigma}_{I,i},{\epsilon}_{p,u,i}\right)\right)
$$

This equation may be expressed as a function of the volume of the process zone by introducing an infinitesimal volume $\mathrm{dV}$ over which the strains and the stresses are constant (in the numerical case, the Gauss point). In order to compare simply the Beremin and Bordet probabilities on a given example, one may define a Bordet stress of the same type as the Weibull stress:

$$
{\sigma}_{\mathit{Bordet}}={\left\lbrace {\int}_{{V}_{p}}\left({\int}_{0}^{{\epsilon}_{p,u}}\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}\left(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p}\right)}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}}\left({\left({\sigma}_{I}\left({\epsilon}_{p},\mathit{dV}\right)\right)}^{m}-{\left({\sigma}_{\mathit{th}}\right)}^{m}\right)\exp\left(\frac{-{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}\left(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p}\right)}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}}\frac{{\epsilon}_{p}}{{\epsilon}_{p,0}}\right)d{\epsilon}_{p}\right)\frac{\mathit{dv}}{{V}_{0}}\right\rbrace }^{\frac{1}{m}}
$$

and the global cleavage-fracture probability is written with a Weibull distribution:

$$
{P}_{\mathrm{Bordet}}=1-\exp(-{(\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{Bordet}}}{{\sigma}_{u}})}^{m})
$$

If the number of microcracked carbides is small compared with the number of sound carbides, the exponential term is close to $1$ and the Bordet stress is thereby simplified.

### 2.3 Discussion

#### 2.3.1 Bordet or Beremin?

The Bordet model is slightly more complex and finer than the Beremin model.

One of its advantages is that it considers the maximum principal stress at each time instant, and not the maximum principal stress during the loading; consequently nothing prevents the Bordet probability from decreasing, unlike that of Beremin.

Moreover, the Bordet model accounts for the fact that a region with a lower stress but a larger plastic strain may be more critical than a zone in which the stresses are higher but the level of plastic strain is lower.

However, the Bordet model requires knowledge of additional material parameters, as will be described in the paragraph below.

#### 2.3.2 Material parameters

The usual Beremin model requires knowledge of three material parameters: the two shape parameters of the Weibull law, $m$ and ${\sigma}_{u}$ , as well as the elementary volume of the plastic zone ${V}_{0}$ ; only the parameter ${\sigma}_{u}$ depends on the temperature. To these three parameters, one may add the threshold plastic strain making it possible to define the plastic zone over which the integration is carried out.

The first three parameters are formally retained by the Bordet model.

**Note:**  they are only **formally** retained; they may be different and require a calibration of the same type as that carried out for Beremin and presented in [R7.02.09](doc:r7.02.09).

Other parameters are added to them.

The plasticity threshold ${\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}(T,{\dot{\epsilon}}_{p})$ is a function *at least* of the temperature and potentially of the plastic strain rate, and its reference value ${\sigma}_{\mathit{ys},0}$ .

The critical stress below which propagation of the ferritic microcracks cannot take place, ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{th}}$ , independent of the loading conditions.

In the complete version only (with the exponential term taken into account), a reference plastic strain ${\varepsilon}_{p,0}$ whose identification method is not given and which seems fairly delicate. One will note that the author himself (cf. [8], [9]) seems to use, for certain studies and validation, the version of the model in which this parameter does not appear (for the user of code_aster, it is sufficient to specify PROBA_NUCL='NON')

### 2.4 Implementation in code_aster

The computation of the Bordet probability and stress is carried out by the operator POST_BORDET. It requires a prior elastoplastic thermomechanical analysis *via*  the operator STAT_NON_LINE. Advice on the use of this model is given in the documentation [U2.05.08](doc:u2.05.08).

In the version currently implemented, the temperature of the medium on which the analysis is carried out must be uniform (but may evolve in the course of time); this limitation does not seem prohibitive inasmuch as the plastic zone at the crack tip is as a rule fairly small.

It is possible to carry out the analysis with or without the exponential term; if this term is requested, the material parameter ${\varepsilon}_{p,0}$ must be supplied.

In all cases, one computes at each time instant the quantities such as the maximum principal stress and the equivalent plastic strain. One determines the value of the parameters ${\sigma}_{u}(T)$ and ${\sigma}_{\mathit{ys}}(T,\dot{{\epsilon}_{p}})$ as a function of the material data supplied by the user, then one computes the global Bordet stress by summation over the Gauss points of the elements of the mesh-cell group indicated by the user, and finally the global Bordet fracture probability over all the time instants up to the one requested. The analysis carried out is finally written as follows:

$$
{\sigma}_{\mathrm{Bordet}}={\left\lbrace \sum_{o\text{=1}}^{{n}_{\mathrm{inst}}}\sum_{\mathrm{el}\text{=1}}^{{n}_{\mathrm{elem}}}\sum_{\mathrm{pg}=1}^{{n}_{\mathrm{pg}}}(\begin{array}{}\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{ys}}(T(o),{\dot{\stackrel{ˉ}{\varepsilon}}}_{p,\mathrm{el},\mathrm{pg}}(o))}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{ys},0}}\\ \mathrm{Max}({\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}_{1,\mathrm{el},\mathrm{pg}}^{m}(o)-{\sigma}_{\mathrm{th}}^{m},0)\\ \exp(\frac{-{\sigma}_{\mathrm{ys}}(T(o),{\dot{\stackrel{ˉ}{\varepsilon}}}_{p,\mathrm{el},\mathrm{pg}}(o))}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{ys},0}}\frac{{\stackrel{ˉ}{\varepsilon}}_{p,\mathrm{el},\mathrm{pg}}(o)}{{\varepsilon}_{p,0}})\\ \Delta {\varepsilon}_{p,\mathrm{el},\mathrm{pg}}(o)\frac{{V}_{\mathrm{el},\mathrm{pg}}}{{V}_{0}}\end{array})\right\rbrace }^{1/m}
$$

With ${n}_{\mathrm{inst}}$ the number of time instants over which the analysis is carried out, ${n}_{\mathrm{elem}}$ the number of elements contained in the mesh-cell group requested by the user and ${n}_{\mathrm{pg}}$ the number of Gauss points of each of these elements. Moreover, for any scalar $a$ , $\stackrel{ˉ}{a}(o)=\frac{a(o)+a(o-1)}{2}$ , $\dot{a}(o)=\frac{a(o)-a(o-1)}{t(o)-t(o-1)}$ and $\Delta a(o)=a(o)-a(o-1)$ .

The result is a table containing the global values of the Bordet stress and of the Bordet fracture probability.

For the analysis to be correct, the user must supply the keyword COEF_MULT as recommended in the user's documentation of POST_BORDET.

## 3 The Rice and Tracey model

One is now interested in the case of ductile initiation. Considering a volume element initially sound, the ductile tearing of this element results from the following elementary mechanisms:

- nucleation of cavities caused by the decohesion of inclusions present in the material,
- growth then coalescence of these cavities.

### 3.1 Isolated cavity in an infinite rigid-plastic matrix

In an approach of analytical understanding, Rice and Tracey studied the behaviour of a cavity, initially spherical (surface ${S}_{v}$ ), isolated in an infinite isotropic medium (volume $V$ ), with rigid-plastic von Mises behaviour (yield stress ${\sigma}_{0}$ ), incompressible, subjected at infinity to an arbitrary strain rate ${\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\infty}$ (stress denoted ${\sigma}^{\infty}$ at infinity). They show that the displacement-velocity field, solution of the mechanical problem posed, minimises the functional:

$$
Q(\dot{u})=\underset{V}{\int}\left[{s}_{ij}(\dot{\epsilon})-{s}_{ij}^{\infty}\right]{\dot{\epsilon}}_{ij}\text{dV}-{\sigma}_{ij}^{\infty}\underset{{S}_{v}}{\int}{n}_{i}{\dot{u}}_{j}\text{dS}
$$

### 3.2 Approximate law of cavity growth

By succeeding in minimising this functional in various situations, Rice and Tracey then showed the predominant influence of the triaxiality ratio $\frac{{\sigma}_{m}^{\infty}}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{\infty}}$ ( with ${\sigma}_{m}^{\infty}$ the trace and ${\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{\infty}$ the von Mises equivalent of the stress imposed on the volume element considered) on the cavity growth rate.

They even exhibit a cavity-growth law which, although approximate, is very close to the results of the previous model. Thus, in each of the principal directions $(K)$ associated with the strain rate ${\dot{\epsilon}}^{\infty}$ , the elongation rate of a cavity amounts to:

$$
{\dot{R}}_{K}=\left\lbrace \mathrm{\chi }{\dot{\epsilon}}_{K}^{\infty}+{\dot{\epsilon}}_{\text{eq}}^{\infty}D\right\rbrace {R}_{K}
$$

For a principal direction $K$ , the parameter ${R}_{K}$ is the radius of the cavity, ${\dot{\varepsilon}}_{K}^{\infty}$ the principal value of the strain rate imposed at infinity and ${\dot{\varepsilon}}_{\text{eq}}^{\infty}$ the von Mises equivalent value of the strain rate imposed at infinity. This relation, in which the coefficients $\khi$ and $D$ depend on the situation envisaged:

- $\khi =\frac{5}{3}$ for a linearly hardening matrix or a perfectly plastic matrix at low triaxiality ratio, or $\khi =2$ in the case of a perfectly plastic matrix at high triaxiality ratio,
- $D=\alpha \exp(\frac{3{\sigma}_{m}^{\infty}}{2{\sigma}_{0}})$ for a perfectly plastic matrix or $D=\frac{{\sigma}_{m}^{\infty}}{4{\sigma}_{{}^{\text{eq}}}^{\infty}}$ for a linearly hardening matrix.

$\alpha =0,283$ is the value given by Rice and Tracey, whereas more precise computations (cf. [bib4]) have shown that this coefficient is higher ($\alpha =1,28$ ).

Mudry then proposed to apply these theoretical results to the case of reactor-vessel steel, *i.e.* :

- behaviour intermediate between the extreme behaviour cases studied by Rice and Tracey, with non-zero but reasonable hardening,
- cracked structures (high triaxiality ratio).

He deduced from this the following approximate law, valid for sufficiently high triaxiality ratios (greater than 0.5):

$$
\dot{R}=\alpha {\dot{\varepsilon}}_{\text{eq}}^{{p}^{\infty}}\exp(\frac{3{\sigma}_{m}^{\infty}}{2{\sigma}_{{}_{\text{eq}}}^{\infty}})R
$$

In which:

- ${\dot{\varepsilon}}_{\text{eq}}^{\infty}$ has been replaced by ${\dot{\varepsilon}}_{\text{eq}}^{{p}^{\infty}}$ (von Mises equivalent of the plastic part of the strain rate) in order to extend the Rice and Tracey law to the elastoplastic case,
- the yield stress ${\sigma}_{0}$ has been replaced by ${\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{\infty}$ in order to take account of the hardening of the matrix around the cavity.

Experimental measurements of porosity growth for various triaxiality ratios have made it possible to validate this expression. These results show that, when the initial porosity ratio remains low, the exponential character of the relation between the cavity radius and the triaxiality ratio is indeed confirmed. On the other hand, the coefficient $\alpha$ depends on the material considered as well as on the initial porosity fraction.

### 3.3 Ductile-initiation criterion

${R}_{0}$ and $R(t)$ denoting the radius of the cavities initially and at the time instant $t$ considered, the ductile-initiation criterion adopted here is:

$$
\frac{R(t)}{{R}_{0}}={(\frac{R}{{R}_{0}})}_{c}
$$

The left-hand side of this expression results from the integration of the growth law, in accordance with the indications of the previous paragraph.

Several arguments of principle may be raised against the direct use of this Rice and Tracey cavity-growth law as a ductile-initiation criterion. Thus:

- the inclusions, and therefore the cavities, are not in reality isolated. Worse, they are often grouped in clusters,
- the coalescence of the cavities doubtless results from interactions which, likewise, are not described in the model established,
- in a cracked structure, the presence of gradients at the crack tip makes the previous analysis, which concerns an infinite medium subjected to homogeneous boundary conditions, less directly applicable.

Nevertheless, by using the previous criterion, one hopes that this law remains realistic, on average, even in clusters or in zones of strong gradients (average over an element of dimensions comparable to that of the Beremin model). Furthermore, one makes the hypothesis that the critical size retained, generally recalibrated on given geometries (CT specimen, for example), represents the coalescence, which amounts to assuming that the coalescence does not depend too much on the nature of the mechanical loadings imposed on the volume element (triaxiality, shear, ...).

Let us note finally that the Rice and Tracey model is only an approximate law, valid for high triaxiality ratios ( *i.e* . greater than 0.5).

### 3.4 Implementation in code_aster

Consider a domain ${\Omega}_{c}$ of the structure studied, which may be the whole of the mesh studied, a mesh-cell group or a mesh cell. Following an elastoplastic thermomechanical analysis, one knows the evolution of the stress, strain and plastic-strain fields in this domain and one wishes to determine the spatial and temporal variations of the cavity growth rate in this domain.

To do this, one uses the keyword RICE_TRACEY of the command POST_ELEM.

At each Gauss point of the domain ${\Omega}_{c}$ , one identifies the stresses and strain rates computed at each time instant with the quantities applied to the infinite medium considered previously. The Rice and Tracey growth law is thus integrated step by step with the aid of the following approximate formula:

$$
\text{Log}(\frac{R({t}_{n})}{{R}_{0}})=\text{Log}(\frac{R({t}_{n-1})}{{R}_{0}})+0,283\text{signe}(\frac{{\sigma}_{m}({t}_{n})}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}({t}_{n})})\text{Exp}(1,5.\mid \frac{{\sigma}_{m}({t}_{n})}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}({t}_{n})}\mid )({\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}^{p}({t}_{n})-{\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}^{p}({t}_{n-1}))
$$

One thus obtains at each time instant the values of the ratio $\frac{R}{{R}_{0}}$ at each Gauss point of the domain ${\Omega}_{c}$ , the sign of the triaxiality ratio making it possible to take account of evolutions both in tension and in compression. Two functionalities are then offered in *code_aster* : the maximum value and the mean value of the growth rate.

#### 3.4.1 Search for the maximum value of the growth rate

At each time instant, one seeks over the whole of the domain ${\Omega}_{c}$ the Gauss point (and the volume of the associated sub-cell) maximising $\frac{R}{{R}_{0}}$ .

#### 3.4.2 Computation of the mean value of the growth rate

By quadrature on each mesh cell and then averaging over the target domain ${\Omega}_{c}$ , one deduces at each time instant the mean value of $\frac{R}{{R}_{0}}$ over ${\Omega}_{c}$ .

As in the case of the Weibull model, a variant is introduced: the previous time integration is then carried out from the stress and the plastic strain averaged per mesh cell.
