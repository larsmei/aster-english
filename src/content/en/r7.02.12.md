# r7.02.12 eXtended Finite Element Method: General remarks

**Summary:**

This document presents the X-FEM method (eXtended Finite Element Method), which makes it possible mainly to consider cracks which do not conform to the mesh in order to treat 2D and 3D crack problems. The crack is defined by the command DEFI_FISS_XFEM [u4.82.08](doc:u4.82.08) and can be used for linear and non-linear static analyses.

Other documents dedicated to specific problems are available:

- frictional contact on the lips of the crack in large sliding with X-FEM [r5.03.53](doc:r5.03.53),

## 1 Introduction

Numerical simulation in fracture mechanics is essentially based on the use of the finite element method (FEM). Classically, for a crack-propagation problem, a first analysis is carried out on an initial mesh, then a new mesh is determined, taking into account the advance of the crack (according to the chosen propagation law). A new analysis follows from it, and the procedure is iterated for each propagation step.

A major and immediate drawback is the necessity of remeshing at each propagation step. The remeshing process can easily be automated in 2D, and in certain cases in 3D, but a quality 3D remeshing proves costly in time (human supervision) and in money. Indeed, with an automatic mesher, a local refinement appropriate to the cracking zone often leads to an excessive number of elements everywhere else on the rest of the structure. A layer-by-layer refinement process is generally useful, such as the introduction of a torus at the crack front, an efficient solution (also facilitating the introduction of Barsoum elements) but requiring a costly human intervention; and all the more so as the geometrical shape of the crack is very complex (helical cracks for example). The problem becomes almost inconceivable in 3D multiple cracking. In addition to these practical difficulties, the projection of quantities (stresses, internal variables) from one mesh onto another poses fundamental theoretical problems (verification of the conservation equations of energy, of momentum, of mass).

In parallel with the difficulties linked to propagation, methods with a mesh prove rather ineffective for parametric studies in which one is interested in the influence of the position and of the shape of the crack.

The so-called “Meshless” methods have been proposed in order to free oneself from the constraints linked to the mesh. Meshless methods are based on a purely nodal discretisation, without connectivity, and the shape functions are constructed from the nodal configuration. Initially introduced at the end of the 1970s for problems without boundaries (Smoothed Particle Hydrodynamics method in the field of astrophysics), Meshless methods were subsequently extended to mechanical problems and today come in various variants: MLS (Moving Least Square) methods, Diffuse Element Method (DEM), Element Free Galerkin (EFG), Reproducing Kernel Particle Method (RKPM), hp clouds, and many others. The common element of all these methods is the notion of Partition of Unity, which is a set of functions whose sum is equal to one at each point of the domain considered. The main drawback of these methods is that they require a greater numerical effort than those based on a mesh (CPU time in particular). In particular, the evaluation of the shape functions is far from being as trivial, the numerical integration schemes are generally richer and therefore more costly, and the resulting global system of equations has a larger bandwidth compared with an FEM. Even in the recent versions of Meshless methods combining physical and mathematical supports, the imposition of boundary conditions remains problematic.

Other methods are based on a partition of unity within the framework of standard finite elements, where only the definition of the shape functions differs. This choice of partition of unity avoids the problems of integration of the stiffness, which is considerably expensive for Meshless methods (EFG, DEM, RKPM in particular). Moreover, the use of a partition of unity based on finite elements allows an easy implementation of Dirichlet-type boundary conditions (unlike techniques using a partition of unity based on least squares). These methods, which are first found in their meshed versions under the name of Partition of Unity Finite Element Method (PUFEM) or of Generalized Finite Element Method (GFEM), make it possible to enrich easily the space of the shape functions, thanks to the *a priori* knowledge of the properties of the solution of the problem. Combining the GFEM and the *hp-cloud*, Duarte *et al.* proposes a mixed partition of unity (finite elements and Shepard), which makes it possible to consider a non-meshed crack, with enriched shape functions.

Even closer to the classical finite-element framework, the extended finite element method (X-FEM) has aroused one of the keenest interests if one refers to the evolution of the number of publications on this subject since its appearance and to the place reserved for it in international conferences. This method uses the partition of unity to enrich the basis of the shape functions in order to represent a jump of the displacement field at the lips of the crack, as well as the singularity at the crack front. Two enrichments are then introduced: an enrichment by a jump function which makes it possible to handle the discontinuity across the lips of the crack, and an enrichment by asymptotic functions, which allows a faithful representation of the physical phenomena taking place at the crack front. Historically, the precursor signs are due to Belytschko and Black, who apply the partition of unity to fracture mechanics by incorporating the analytical formulae of the asymptotic fields into the displacement approximation. The addition of the generalised Heaviside function makes it possible to write the enriched approximation of the displacement in its final form, an expression which gives birth to the extended finite element method. Compared with GFEM, it offers less dependence with respect to the knowledge of the form of the solutions, which offers greater flexibility. The field of application of X-FEM continues to widen, this approach having been used in very varied frameworks: Reissner-Mindlin plates, shells, fracture in 3D, multiple cracking, cohesive zones, modelling of holes and of bi-materials, incompressible formulations and large transformations, nucleation of cracks, cracking under contact, dynamic fracture, plasticity…

Moreover, the use of the level set method coupled with X-FEM has greatly facilitated the treatment of cracks in 2D (, and ) and in 3D. Initially introduced within the framework of fluid mechanics in order to represent the evolution of interfaces, the level set method considers the interface as the zero iso-value of a distance function. This method proves particularly effective for the propagation of a crack in 3D, coupled with the use of the Fast Marching Method.

This document is organised around 4 sections, of which this introduction serves as section r7.02.12-calcul-energetique-facteurs-intensite-contraintes.

r7.02.12-representation-fissure is devoted to the use of the level set method for cracking. After a brief theoretical reminder, the computation of the level sets is specified; they prove practical for determining the position of the crack in 3D and of the crack front, and they make it possible to define a local basis at the crack front.

r7.02.12-probleme-fissuration presents the cracking problem treated with X-FEM. At r7.02.12-enrichissement-approximation-deplacement one introduces the approximation of the displacement written in a basis of enriched shape functions. The addition of functions discontinuous across the interface leads to a subdivision procedure detailed at r7.02.12-sous-decoupage, prior to the phase of integration of the stiffness terms and of the right-hand side, the implementation of which is explained at r7.02.12-integration-rigidite.

r7.02.12-calcul-energetique-facteurs-intensite-contraintes is concerned with the post-processing of the energy release rate and of the stress intensity factors in linear fracture mechanics. The $G$ -theta method makes it possible to compute the local energy release rate. For this purpose, a theta field is introduced, representing a virtual extension of the crack. The local $G$ is then the solution of a variational equation, using the $J$ integral in the form of domain integrals. The choices of discretisations of $G$ and of the theta field lead to a linear system, the solution of which yields the values of $G$ along the crack front. The G-theta method also makes it possible to determine the stress intensity factors along the crack front. Instead of using the $J$ integral, one uses the bilinear form of $G$, which leads to domain integrals mixing solution fields and analytical asymptotic fields (also called interaction integrals). Thus, in the same way as for $G$, the $K$ are then solutions of a variational equation, using interaction integrals. The choices of discretisations of the $K$ and of the theta field lead to a linear system, the solution of which yields the values of the $K$ along the crack front.

## 2 Representation of the crack by “level sets”

The “Level set” method was introduced within the framework of fluid mechanics in order to represent the evolution of interfaces. The main idea is to consider the interface as the zero iso-value of a distance function. The choice of the distance function matters little here, because only the knowledge of the zero iso-value is useful and important.

### 2.1 Theoretical aspect of the level sets

Let an interface $\Gamma$ delimiting an open set $\Omega$ of ${\Re }^{n}$ . The idea is to define a function $\varphi (x,t)$ which is regular (at least Lipschitz) such that the subspace $\varphi (x,t)=0$ represents the interface.

The level set has the following properties:

$\begin{array}{c}\varphi (x,t)>0\text{pour}x\in \Omega \\ \varphi (x,t)<0\text{pour}x\mathrm{\notin }\overline{\Omega}\\ \varphi (x,t)=0\text{pour}x\in \partial \Omega =\Gamma (t).\end{array}$

This method applies easily to 2D cracking problems, in particular within the framework of approaches in which the crack is not meshed. (, in 2D). The extension is possible for the treatment of cracks in 3D.

Thus, in the case of cracking, it is necessary to introduce two level sets (in 2D and in 3D):

- a normal level set ($\text{lsn}$ ) which represents the distance to the surface of the crack (surface extended by prolongation to the whole domain),
- a tangent level set ($\text{lst}$ ) which represents the distance to the crack front.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/10000000000003790000025496805F42B791E8A4.png)

*Level sets and distance to the crack*

The zero iso-value of the normal level set defines the surface of the crack, extended by continuity to the whole domain. The intersection of the zero iso-values of the two level sets defines the crack front. Moreover, the sign of the tangent level set is chosen in such a way that the surface of the crack ${\Gamma}_{\text{cr}}$ corresponds to the space generated by $(\text{lsn}=0)\cap (\text{lst}<0)$ . The sign of the normal level set is chosen arbitrarily by means of the orientation convention of the normal to the crack plane, defined explicitly at r7.02.12-calcul-level-set-normale-lsn. The points $x$ for which $\text{lsn}(x)$ is negative are said to be “below” the crack, and those for which $\text{lsn}(x)$ is positive are said to be “above” the crack (see r7.02.12-fig-2-2 and r7.02.12-fig-2-3).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/1000000000000319000001C907D464CF7118A12B.png)

*Level sets for the representation of a 3D crack*

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/100000000000023E0000013148BE9D8FA25A0FC2.jpg)

*Level sets for the representation of a 2D crack*

The crack-propagation phase is translated simply by the propagation of the level sets. The propagation of a level set requires three successive steps:

- extension of the velocity known on the zero iso-value towards the entire domain,
- propagation of the level set from this velocity field,
- reinitialisation of the level set function in order to retain a signed distance function.

The propagation of a crack represented by 2 level sets presents a few particularities. The velocity vector is known only on the crack front, that is to say a curve. The crack can only grow, not move. Two level set functions must be propagated and one wishes their gradients to remain orthogonal. The sequence of the steps can be summarised as follows:

- propagation of $\text{lsn}$ and reinitialisation of $\text{lsn}$ ,
- propagation of $\text{lst}$ ,
- orthogonalisation of the gradient of $\text{lst}$ versus the gradient of $\text{lsn}$ ,
- reinitialisation of $\text{lst}$ .

All these steps reduce to the solution of an equation of Hamilton-Jacobi type:

$\frac{\partial \dots }{\partial t}+F\mid \nabla \dots \mid =f$

where $F$ and $f$ are functions which depend on the step.

The Fast Marching Method is an alternative technique well suited to the strictly monotone propagation of fronts. This method separates the nodes of the mesh according to their distance from the interface, and at each iteration the propagation equation is solved for the only nodes immediately adjacent to the interface, using a finite-difference scheme of order 2.

This part is more detailed in the document [r7.02.13](doc:r7.02.13).

### 2.2 Computation of the level sets

The computation of the level sets (scalar fields) is carried out for each crack. It can be done in two ways. Either by the data of their analytical expressions and in this case, a simple evaluation of these functions at the nodes of the mesh provides the scalar fields sought. Or the crack is meshed and in this case, it is necessary to give surface mesh cells corresponding to one lip (GROUP_MA_FISS) and the line mesh cells corresponding to the crack front (GROUP_MA_FOND). ). In the 2D case, one will give line mesh cells (for GROUP_MA_FISS) and point mesh cells (for GROUP_MA_FOND). The distances are then computed by an algorithm of orthogonal projections inspired by the one used for contact, made explicit at r7.02.12-calcul-level-set-normale-lsn and r7.02.12-calcul-level-set-tangente-lst.

#### 2.2.1 Computation of the normal level set (lsn)

For each node of the mesh, one seeks the mesh cell of GROUP_MA_FISS closest to this node. For this purpose, one uses the algorithms of projection onto a triangle (see paragraph §2.3.2 of ) and onto a quadrangle (see paragraph §2.3.3 of ). The value of $\text{lsn}$ is then the normal distance from this point to the mesh cell.

##### 2.2.1.1 Computation of the normal level set in 3D

In 3D, one must pay attention to the sense of the normal. Indeed, the mesh cells of GROUP_MA_FISS being interior to the structure, they are not boundary mesh cells, and the automatic orientation of the normals (keyword ORIE_PEAU_3D of the operator MODI_MAILLAGE) is then impossible. In order to make sure of always choosing the same sense for the normals, one takes the normal of the first mesh cell of GROUP_MA_FISS as reference and one “propagates” the direction of this normal to all the other mesh cells of the group for each of the adjacent elements. For each mesh cell one stores an *orientation indicator* of the mesh cell which is equal to “+1” if the orientation of its normal is consistent with that of the reference mesh cell constructed step by step and “-1” otherwise. This makes it possible to assign the correct sign of the value of the normal level set by multiplying its uncorrected value by the orientation indicator (see the algorithm for the computation of $\text{lsn}$ in 3D below).

Algorithm for the computation of the *orientation indicator* of the mesh cell:

- creation of the vector $I$ “orientation indicator” and of the vector $C$ containing the number of the layer to which each element of GROUP_MA_FISS belongs. The size of the two vectors is equal to the number of elements $n$ of GROUP_MA_FISS
- one sets to zero all the elements of the two vectors $I$ and $C$ , except the first element to which one assigns the value +1
- loop $i$ on the number of the layer (vector $C$ ), from 1 to $n$

  - loop $j$ on the elements of the vector $C$ , from 1 to $n$

    - one retrieves the number of the layer to which the element $j$ belongs: ${n}_{\mathit{couche}}=C(j)$
    - if ${n}_{\mathit{couche}}=i$ , that is to say the element $j$ belongs to the current layer $i$ :

      - one computes the normal $\vec{{n}_{j}}$ to the element $j$ (one computes the normal to the triangle formed by the first three nodes defining the element)
      - loop ${n}_{\mathit{el}}$ on the elements which have at least one node in common with the element $j$

        - if the element ${n}_{\mathit{el}}$ does not belong to any layer ($C({n}_{\mathit{el}})=0$ ), one assigns to it the number of the current layer+1 ($C({n}_{\mathit{el}})=i+1$ ), one computes its normal $\vec{{n}_{{n}_{\mathit{el}}}}$ and the orientation indicator: $I({n}_{\mathit{el}})=\mathit{sign}\left(I(j)\cdot \vec{{n}_{{n}_{\mathit{el}}}}\cdot \vec{{n}_{j}}\right)$
      - end loop
    - end if
  - end loop
- end loop

Algorithm for the computation of $\text{lsn}$ in 3D:

- loop on all the nodes $P$ of the mesh
  initialisation of $\mathit{dmin}$

  - loop on the triangular mesh cells of GROUP_MA_FISS (with subdivision of the quadrangles into triangles)

> let $A$ , $B$ and $C$ be the vertices of the triangle
> computation of the normal to the mesh cell: $\overrightarrow{N}=\overrightarrow{\text{AB}}\wedge \overrightarrow{\text{AC}}$
> computation of $M$ , projection of $P$ into the plane $\mathit{ABC}$
> if $M$ is outside the triangle $\mathit{ABC}$ , one brings $M$ back onto one of the straight lines $(\mathit{AB})$ $(\mathit{AC})$ $(\mathit{BC})$
> if M is still outside the triangle $\mathit{ABC}$ , one brings $M$ back to $A$ $B$ or $C$
> one retrieves the *orientation indicator* $I$ of the normal of the mesh cell
> if $\mathrm{PM}<\mathrm{dmin}$ then $\mathrm{dmin}=\mathrm{PM}$ and $\text{lsn}(P)=I\cdot \overrightarrow{\text{PM}}\cdot \overrightarrow{N}$

  - end loop
- end loop

The subdivision of a quadrangle into triangles makes it possible to reduce the problem to an approximate computation of projection in a linear case (because the shape functions of the quadrangle are bilinear whereas those of the triangle are linear). Moreover, there exist 2 ways of carrying out such a cutting (according to the chosen diagonal). One therefore generates all the possible cases, as is done in paragraph §2.3.3 of .

The computation of the projection within the framework of the search for pairing of the contact nodes for the continuous method does not use this kind of algorithm, but solves the non-linear problem of projection onto a quadrangle by the Newton method. One could draw inspiration from it for the computation of the level sets by projection.

In 2D, one begins by reorganising the mesh cells of GROUP_MA_FISS in order to have a series of contiguous mesh cells. Two vectors will be stored, the list of the ordered mesh cells and the list of their orientation, which one will call here $\mathit{LIMA}$ and $\mathit{ORI}$ .

Algorithm for sorting GROUP_MA_FISS:

Initialisation of the first mesh cell M, first mesh cell of GROUP_MA_FISS. One has $\mathit{LIMA}(1)=M$ and $\mathit{ORI}(1)=1$ . One initialises the Boolean $\mathit{FINFIS}$ which tests whether one is at the end of the crack, $\mathit{FINFIS}$ initialised to $\mathit{FALSE}$ .

- loop searching for the following mesh cell $\mathit{LIMA}(k)$ while $\mathit{FINFIS}=\mathit{FALSE}$

> if $\mathit{ORI}(k-1)=1$ , computation of $N$ node 2 of $\mathit{LIMA}(k-1)$
> if $\mathit{ORI}(k-1)=0$ , computation of $N$ node 1 of $\mathit{LIMA}(k-1)$
> $\mathit{FINFIS}=\mathit{TRUE}$

- loop on the mesh cells $M$ of GROUP_MA_FISS, while $\mathit{FINFIS}=\mathit{TRUE}$

> Computation of node 1 and of node 2 of $M$ , $\mathit{N1}$ and $\mathit{N2}$

> if $\mathit{N2}=N$ , $\mathit{ORI}(k)=0$ and $\mathit{LIMA}(k)=M$ , $\mathit{FINFIS}=\mathit{FALSE}$
> (exit from the loop when one finds a following mesh cell)

- end loop

> (exit from the loop if one has not found a following mesh cell because one is at the end of the crack)

- end of loop

#### 2.2.2 Computation of the tangent level set (lst)

For each node of the mesh, one seeks the mesh cell of GROUP_MA_FOND closest to this node. For this purpose, one uses the algorithm of projection onto a segment (see paragraph §2.3.1 of ). The value of $\text{lst}$ is then the normal distance from this point to the segment.

As previously, the determination of the normal to the segment is not obvious a priori. In order to compute it, one must first find the surface mesh cell of GROUP_MA_FISS which borders it.

Algorithm for the computation of $\text{lst}$ in 3D:

- loop on all the nodes $P$ of the mesh

> initialisation of $\mathit{dmin}$

- loop on the segments of GROUP_MA_FOND

> let $A$ and $B$ be the two ends of the segment

- loop on all the mesh cells of GROUP_MA_FISS

  - if $A$ and $B$ belong to this mesh cell then

> let $C$ be a node of the mesh cell other than $A$ and $B$
> computation of the normal to this mesh cell
> (that is to say the normal to the crack plane): $\overrightarrow{N}=\overrightarrow{\text{AB}}\wedge \overrightarrow{\text{AC}}$
> computation of the normal to the crack front in the plane of the crack:
> $\overrightarrow{N'}=\overrightarrow{\text{AB}}\wedge \overrightarrow{N}$
> Verification of the sense of $\overrightarrow{N'}$
> Let $M$ be the projection of $P$ onto $(\mathit{AB})$
> If $M$ is outside $[\mathit{AB}]$ , one brings it back to $A$ or $B$
> If $\mathit{PM}<\mathit{dmin}$ then $\mathit{dmin}=\mathit{PM}$ and $\text{lst}(P)=\overrightarrow{\text{PM}}\cdot \overrightarrow{N'}$

  - end if
- end loop

- end loop

- end loop

*Problem of cancellation of the tangent level-set*

The mesh cell ABC successively sees its three vertex nodes have the tangent level-set cancelled:

- because of the fit-to-vertex criterion for the nodes A and B as their support is traversed (cf. r7.02.12-reajustement-level-sets)
- because of the criteria specific to quadratic elements for the node C.

Once the tangent level-set of all the vertex nodes has been cancelled, one has:

$$
{\mathit{lst}}_{max}=max\lbrace {\mathit{lst}}_{\mathit{ABC}}\rbrace =0
$$

Therefore, when one computes the quantity $d=\mathit{lstm}/{\mathit{lst}}_{max}$ , one has a problem.

This situation can be frequent in 2D with a “segment” crack (FORM_FISS='SEGMENT') and a mesh characterised by a strong gradient of mesh-cell size (and all the more so if the mesh is triangular and free).

In order to correct this, before computing $d$ , one looks at whether ${\mathit{lst}}_{max}$ is zero.

If yes, and if the particular conditions are satisfied:

- all the vertex nodes must satisfy $\left|\mathit{lst}\right|<\epsilon$ ;
- the level-sets must have been computed from the catalogue of geometrical shapes of DEFI_FISS_XFEM in the 2D case with FORM_FISS='SEGMENT'
- for each edge of the current mesh cell, one compares the length of its orthogonal projection onto the segment (which constitutes the crack) with the length of this segment. At least one of these projections must have a length comparable (relative criterion) to that of the segment. In this way one makes sure that the mesh cell is “far” from the crack (unless the mesh is extremely coarse)

Then, one short-circuits the end of the current iteration of the loop on the edges (that of the block relating to the adjustments specific to the quadratic case). Otherwise, one stops in fatal error.

#### 2.2.3 Approximation of the level sets

Whatever the computation method used (by analytical functions or by projection), the level set fields are interpolated by the linear shape functions used for the approximation of the displacement field:

$\begin{array}{c}\text{lsn}(x)=\sum_{i}{\varphi}_{i}(x){\text{lsn}}_{i}\\ \text{lst}(x)=\sum_{i}{\varphi}_{i}(x){\text{lst}}_{i}\end{array}$

where ${\phi }_{i}$ are the classical linear shape functions and ${\text{lsn}}_{i}$ and ${\text{lst}}_{i}$ the nodal values of the level set fields. On a tetrahedron with 4 nodes the surface of the crack will therefore be a plane, but on a hexahedron with 8 nodes, it may be slightly curved.

The discretisation error of the level sets is directly linked to the size of the mesh and to the curvature of the level set. Let us examine the following example, involving a curved crack in 2D:

Let the ellipse level set of equation:

${x}^{2}+{(\frac{y}{0.5})}^{2}=1$

The distance to the ellipse is computed at each of the 4 nodes of the quadrangle defined by $(x,y)\in \left[0.4,0.9\right]\times \left[0,0.5\right]$ . Let $P$ be a given point of the space of coordinates $({x}_{p},{y}_{p})$ . Let $H$ be its projection onto the ellipse previously defined. $H$ has coordinates $(a\cos\theta ,b\sin\theta )$ . The equation of the straight line $(\text{HP})$ is the following:

$y=\frac{a\sin\theta }{b\cos\theta }x+b\sin\theta (1-\frac{{a}^{2}}{{b}^{2}})$

One solves numerically this equation in the unknown $\theta$ for each node of the quadrangle and one deduces from it the distance to the ellipse:

$\text{dist}=\sqrt{{({x}_{p}-a\cos\theta )}^{2}+{({y}_{p}-b\sin\theta )}^{2}}$

The approximation of the level set is then written on this quadrangle:

${\mathit{lsn}}^{h}\left(x,y\right)=-0.44347{\varphi}_{1}\left(x,y\right)-0.1{\varphi}_{2}\left(x,y\right)+0.22227{\varphi}_{3}\left(x,y\right)+0.040806{\varphi}_{4}\left(x,y\right)$

#### 2.2.4 Readjustment of the level sets

In order to limit the integration problems when the crack passes “close” to a node, a procedure of readjustment of the normal level set is put in place. If on an edge of the mesh, $\text{lsn}$ vanishes too “close” to an end node, the value of $\text{lsn}$ at this node is set to zero. The criterion used for the moment is 1% of the length of the edge. This value is the one used by the software developed by the team of Nicolas Moës at the GeM (see paragraph §1.3.2.3 of ).

Algorithm for the readjustment of the normal level set:

- loop on the mesh cells

- loop on the edges of the mesh cell, of ends $A$ and $B$

> if $\text{lsn}\left(A\right)\ne \text{lsn}(B)$

> $d=\frac{\text{lsn}(A)}{\text{lsn}(A)-\text{lsn}(B)}$
> if $\mid d\mid \le 0.01$ then $\text{lsn}(A)=0$ end if
> if $\mid d-1\mid \le 0.01$ then $\text{lsn}(B)=0$ end if

> end if
> if quadratic element

> if $\text{lsn}(A)=0$ and $\text{lsn}(B)=0$ then

> $d=\text{lsn}(M)$
> if $\mid d\mid \le 0.0001$ then $\text{lsn}(M)=0$
> end if

> end if

> end if

- end loop

- end loop

In the case of a quadratic interpolation, a complementary readjustment is necessary in order to limit and control the situations of multiple cancellation of the level set along an edge. Indeed, in order not to multiply the configurations of cutting of the enriched elements into integration sub-elements, one authorises only two situations of multiple cancellation along the edge of an enriched element. The first corresponds to the case where the level set is zero all along the edge (therefore zero at its two end nodes and at its mid-side node). The second is the situation where the level set is zero at one of the end nodes and at the mid-side node (see r7.02.12-fig-2-11). In all the other situations, the level set must vanish only once along an edge. It will then be easy to detect the cancellations of the level set along an edge in order to form the integration sub-elements. In order to reduce the problem to these situations, a readjustment is carried out when one is in one of the situations represented on the figure r7.02.12-fig-2-12.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/100000000000027F000000F8A2791354392D4B4A.jpg)

*Multiple cancellation of the level set along an edge*

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/reajustement_complementaire_level_sets.png)

*Complementary readjustment of the level sets*

In the two configurations of r7.02.12-fig-2-12 where one adjusts the level set of one of the end nodes to zero, it is the end of the edge which has the smallest level set in absolute value which is chosen to be adjusted. Also, when the adjustment to be carried out is judged too large, which reveals a significant curvature of the level set relative to the size of the element, an alarm message recommending the use of a finer mesh is issued. The criterion chosen is the following: the alarm message is issued as soon as:

$\mid \mathit{lsn}à\mathit{ajuster}\mid >0.01\ast \underset{\mathit{noeuds}\mathit{de}l'\mathit{élément}}{max}\mid \mathit{lsn}\mid$

This procedure is applied both for the tangent level set and for the normal level set.

#### 2.2.5 Notion of true signed distance

If the level sets are computed by analytical functions, the choice of the functions is not unique for a given crack geometry. Indeed, any function with positive values on one side and negative values on the other is valid. However, if one wishes the level set to represent the true signed distance, then the choice is unique. This notion is important subsequently when one defines the coordinates in the local basis at the crack front by means of the values of the level sets. One must therefore take care to give the exact formula of the distance to the crack (an expression which is not easy for complex crack geometries).

One of the interests of the projection method is that it provides a field which is the true signed distance. One could therefore also envisage combining the two methods. The data of a simple analytical function would make it possible in a first stage to determine the zero iso-value; then to create a simplified mesh of this surface, which would serve as a support for the projection method. The drawback is to create a virtual 2D mesh of the crack which is disjoint from the real 3D mesh.

Otherwise, one could also envisage an orthogonalisation phase which transforms an arbitrary level set into a true distance.

*Note:*

*If the shape of the level-set represented is not regular enough (for example, possesses a sharp angle or a bifurcation), the definition of the distance by orthogonal projection onto the zero iso-value may be ambiguous* r7.02.12-fig-2-13 *.*

*In the neighbourhood of the singularity, the projection onto the surface of the level-set is not possible: the gradient of the zero iso-value of the level-set is discontinuous, therefore the normal to the zero iso-value of the level-set does not exist. One then designates by “shadow zone” the region where the computation of the distance by orthogonal projection onto the zero iso-value is to be ruled out.*

*In the “shadow zone”, one computes the distance by returning to its fundamental definition: the distance of a point* $M$ *to the zero iso-value is the minimum length connecting the point* $M$ *to a point of the zero iso-value.*

*Let* $C$ *be the point corresponding to the vertex of the right angle on the zero iso-value.*

*For every point* $M$ *in the “shadow zone”* r7.02.12-fig-2-13 *,* $C$ *satisfies the minimum length, that is to say:* $d(M,\text{iso\_zero})=min(\parallel \vec{\mathit{MN}}\parallel ,N\in \text{{iso\_zero}})=\parallel \vec{\mathit{MC}}\parallel$ *.*

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/1000020100000195000001503A8C82C9D2E4BAF8.png)

*Difficulty of the definition of a distance by orthogonal projection in the case of a singularity of the zero iso-value*

*Moreover, let us note that the interpolation of the level-set regularises the singularity. As the non-regular level-set is interpolated by polynomial functions* r7.02.12-approximation-level-sets *, the discretised level-set becomes a regular function within the element.*

During this phase, “concatenated” fields are created. For example, one creates a global normal level set field for all the cracks. For each node (nodal fields) or each mesh cell (element fields), one goes and seeks the information associated with the closest crack.

- Those linked to the subdivision (see r7.02.12-sous-decoupage),

- Those linked to the data structures for contact (see document R5.03.54).

As regards propagation [r7.02.13](doc:r7.02.13), one can define several cracks on the model and one can give the list of the cracks which propagate: at each call of the operator PROPA_FISS, all the cracks given propagate.

The cracks must be sufficiently spaced from one another (3 mesh cells without cracks must at least separate them). *A fortiori*, the cracks must not intersect either. Otherwise, the introduction of special enrichments is necessary.

### 2.3 Local basis at the crack front

The gradients of the level sets can be used to define the local basis at the crack front.

The gradients are determined thanks to the derivatives of the shape functions and of the nodal values of the level sets.

$\left \lbrace \begin{array}{}\nabla {\mathrm{lsn}}_{j}^{\mathrm{elt}}=\sum_{i}{\phi }_{i,j}{\mathrm{lsn}}_{i}\\ \nabla {\mathrm{lst}}_{j}^{\mathrm{elt}}=\sum_{i}{\phi }_{i,j}{\mathrm{lst}}_{i}\end{array}j=1,3 \right.$

where the ${\phi }_{i,j}$ are the derivatives of the shape functions with respect to the direction $j$ .

One thus determines a field of gradients per element. The values are computed at the nodes of the elements, for each element independently of the others; then in order to compute the nodal value one averages over the values obtained by elements at the nodes.

The local basis at the crack front $\left\lbrace {e}_{1},{e}_{2},{e}_{3}\right\rbrace$ is then computed at every point thanks to the nodal gradient fields:

${e}_{1}=\frac{\sum_{i}{\varphi}_{i}\nabla {\mathit{lst}}_{i}}{\parallel \sum_{i}{\varphi}_{i}\nabla {\mathit{lst}}_{i}\parallel },{e}_{2}=\frac{\sum_{i}{\varphi}_{i}\nabla {\mathit{lsn}}_{i}}{\parallel \sum_{i}{\varphi}_{i}\nabla {\mathit{lsn}}_{i}\parallel },{e}_{3}={e}_{1}\times {e}_{2}$

where $\nabla {\mathrm{lsn}}_{i}$ and $\nabla {\mathrm{lst}}_{i}$ are the nodal values of the gradients.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/1000000000000183000000EB1194BFE8886C739E.png)

*Local basis at the crack front*

In 2D one will have only:

${e}_{1}=\frac{\sum_{i}{\phi }_{i}\nabla {\mathrm{lst}}_{i}}{\parallel \sum_{i}{\phi }_{i}\nabla {\mathrm{lst}}_{i}\parallel },{e}_{2}=\frac{\sum_{i}{\phi }_{i}\nabla {\mathrm{lsn}}_{i}}{\parallel \sum_{i}{\phi }_{i}\nabla {\mathrm{lsn}}_{i}\parallel }$

### 2.4 Determination of the crack front

The crack front is defined by the intersection of the zero iso-values of the two level sets. For the computation of the stress intensity factors (Stress Intensity Factors in English), it is practical to define points belonging to the crack front, which will serve as a basis for the interpolation of the SIFs (see r7.02.12-discretisations).

The points chosen are the intersections of the faces of the elements with the curve $\text{lsn}=0\cap \text{lst}=0$ . These points will then be ordered so as to define a curvilinear abscissa along the crack front.

#### 2.4.1 Search for the points of the crack front

The search for the points of the crack front is carried out in the following manner. One restricts oneself to the elements where the normal level set changes sign and where all the nodes are of status “Crack-Tip” (this notion is defined at r7.02.12-enrichissement-statut-nœuds).

Let a face of such an element; if this face is a quadrangle, one reduces it to two triangles.

Let the triangle $\mathit{ABC}$

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/1000000000000407000001EEE765862376F98182.png)

*Face of an element intersected by the crack front*

One seeks the point $M$ solution of the following system:

$\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\text{lsn}(M)=0\\ \text{lst}(M)=0\end{array} \right.$

One writes the point $M$ in the frame $\left(A,\vec{\text{AB}},\vec{\text{AC}}\right)$

$M=A+{\xi}_{1}\overrightarrow{\text{AB}}+{\xi}_{2}\overrightarrow{\text{AC}}$

The system is then written

$\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\left(1-{\xi}_{1}-{\xi}_{2}\right)\mathit{lsn}(A)+{\xi}_{1}\mathit{lsn}(B)+{\xi}_{2}\mathit{lsn}(C)=0\\ \left(1-{\xi}_{1}-{\xi}_{2}\right)\mathit{lst}(A)+{\xi}_{1}\mathit{lst}(B)+{\xi}_{2}\mathit{lst}(C)=0\end{array} \right.$ $\mathrm{\iff }\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\xi}_{1}\\ {\xi}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace ={\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathit{lsn}(B)-\mathit{lsn}(A)& \mathit{lsn}(C)-\mathit{lsn}(A)\\ \mathit{lst}(B)-\mathit{lsn}(A)& \mathit{lst}(C)-\mathit{lsn}(A)\end{array}\right]}^{-1}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}-\mathit{lsn}(A)\\ -\mathit{lst}(A)\end{array}\right\rbrace$

The point $M$ is retained provided that it belongs to the triangle $\mathit{ABC}$ .

In 2D, one uses the same procedure but directly on the mesh cells and not on the faces of the mesh cells (always triangles or else quadrangles cut into triangles).

The search for the points of the crack front is accompanied by a strategy of elimination of the redundant points $M$ . Indeed, if the face is not a face of the geometrical boundary of the structure, it is then common to two mesh cells of the list of the mesh cells of the front, and a point of the front can thus be detected (at least) twice. In order to check whether the new point $M$ detected does not already belong to the list of the points $P$ of the front, one proceeds to a characterisation of the point $M$ according to whether it is situated on a vertex node of the face, an edge of the face or else inside the face. One then carries out a check according to the cases, via a loop on the points $P$ of the same status as $M$ :

- if $M$ is situated on a vertex node of number ${N}^{M}$ and ${N}^{M}={N}^{P}$ ,
- if $M$ is situated on an edge characterised by the vertex numbers ${N}_{1}^{M}$ and ${N}_{2}^{M}$ and         $\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{N}_{1}^{M}+{N}_{2}^{M}={N}_{1}^{P}+{N}_{2}^{P}\\ {N}_{1}^{M}.{N}_{2}^{M}={N}_{1}^{P}.{N}_{2}^{P}\end{array}\right.$ ,
- if $M$ is situated inside a face characterised by $\mathit{NNF}$ vertices and

$\left \lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\sum}_{i=1}^{\mathit{NNF}}{N}_{i}^{M}={\sum}_{i=1}^{\mathit{NNF}}{N}_{i}^{P}\\ {\prod}_{i=1}^{\mathit{NNF}}{N}_{i}^{M}={\prod}_{i=1}^{\mathit{NNF}}{N}_{i}^{P}\\ {\sum}_{i,j\in (1,\mathit{NNF}),i\ne j}{N}_{i}^{M}.{N}_{j}^{M}={\sum}_{i,j\in (1,\mathit{NNF}),i\ne j}{N}_{i}^{P}.{N}_{j}^{P}\end{array} \right.$ ,

then the point $M$ is not retained. In the opposite case, it is added to the list of the points $P$ of the front. In order to free oneself from the numerical errors which could cause a point $M$ normally situated on a vertex node to be characterised as a point interior to the surface, a “fit to vertex” procedure is associated with the previous strategy. Thus, if $M$ is detected as a point situated on an edge or inside a face, the following algorithm is used:

- computation of the distance from each vertex node of the face to the point $M$ ,
- each of these values is placed in the vector $\mathit{Dist}$ which is then sorted from the smallest value to the largest,
- if $\mathit{Dist}(1)<{10}^{-4}.\mathit{Dist}(2)$ then $M$ is replaced onto the vertex node. Otherwise, it retains its previous status.

An equivalent procedure is put in place in order to replace (if need be) a point interior to the face onto an edge.

**Note**

Even when one restricts oneself to the elements where the normal level set changes sign and where all the nodes are of type “Crack-Tip”, one cannot be sure that the system is invertible. One could already limit oneself to the triangular faces where the level sets change sign in the broad sense (0 included), but that would not eliminate all the cases of a zero determinant. Indeed, when the trace of the crack front on the triangular face is a curve, the system admits an infinity of solutions. This case is not detectable a priori, and only a test on the non-nullity of the determinant makes it possible to free oneself from such cases. Thus, if on a face the determinant is numerically zero, one does not determine a point of the crack front on this face. If some exist (necessarily an infinity), the two solution points on the edges of the face are then determined by another face of the element in question on which the determinant is non-zero.

#### 2.4.2 Orientation of the crack front

The orientation of the crack front is necessary only in 3D.

##### 2.4.2.1 Explanation of the method of orientation of the crack front

During the search for the points of the crack front by the method mentioned at r7.02.12-recherche-points-fond-fissure, the points are not necessarily found in an order allowing the immediate definition of an ordered path and of a curvilinear abscissa. Yet, the definition of an ordered crack front and of a curvilinear abscissa along the crack front is indispensable to the computation of G by the G-theta method.

The orientation method used is based on the fact that two consecutive points of the front necessarily belong to a same 3D mesh cell.

In order to explain this method, one will rely on the example of a crack front defined by a straight line crossing a parallelepiped structure. One has illustrated on the following figure the top view of the mesh of this structure in 3D:

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/cadre1.png)

*Top view of a mesh of a parallelepiped structure*

The numbering of the mesh cells is arbitrary.

One can see on r7.02.12-fig-2-16 the crack front in red constituted of 4 points.

All the mesh cells of this structure have their normal level set which changes sign. The 14 mesh cells represented are said to be connected to the crack front and each possesses at least one of the four points of the front.

The ordered list of the points of this front is 1-2-3-4.

In order to search for the points of the front, one makes a loop on the 14 mesh cells connected to the front. This loop being made in the increasing order of the indices of the mesh cells, one finds first the point 1 then the points 3 and 4 (or 4 then 3) and finally the point 2. One therefore obtains the following list of points: 1-4-3-2. It remains to order this list.

*Step 1* **: Search for the points of the front by mesh cell**

The principle of the orientation is based on the fact that a mesh cell connected to the front necessarily contains 1 or 2 points of the front.

If for example the front passes through a single vertex of a mesh cell, the latter will contain only one point of the front. On entering a 3D mesh cell through a face, the front must necessarily leave through another point of this same mesh cell. The latter then contains two points of the front.

**Note** :

*If one or several mesh cells possess more than two points of the front, the procedure fails and the front is not oriented. A hexahedron element* HEXA8 can contain for example three points of the front if the latter *cuts cleanly two opposite faces and grazes another face.*

*If the orientation of the front is necessary for the remainder of the computations, one must refine the mesh in order to have only mesh cells containing at most two points of the front.*

The first step of this orientation consists in associating, for each mesh cell connected to the front, a pair of indices of points of the front belonging to them.

In the example, mesh cell 1 possesses only the point 1, one associates with it the pair $(1,0)$ . Mesh cell 2 has the points 3 and 4, one associates with it the pair $(4,3)$ , etc.

Let $\mathit{NMAFON}$ be the number of mesh cells connected to the front.

One creates a list $\mathit{LISTPT}$ of size $2\times \mathit{NMAFON}$ , containing the indices of the points of the front for each of the $\mathit{NMAFON}$ mesh cells connected to the front.

In our example, the list $\mathit{LISTPT}$ contains 14 pairs of indices and could be described as follows:

| Mesh-cell index | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | ... |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pair associated with the mesh cell | $(1,0)$ | $(4,3)$ | $(3,0)$ | $(3,4)$ | $(2,0)$ | $(2,0)$ | $(2,1)$ | $(3,2)$ | ... |

**Notes** :

- *The pairs* $(4,3)$ *and* $(3,4)$ *are equivalent.*
- *The pairs containing a 0 will not be considered. Indeed, a point will always belong to at least one pair of non-zero indices. For example the point 1 belongs to the pair* $(2,1)$ *.*
- *One detects the end points by looking at how many pairs of non-zero indices the points belong to. The point 3 is found in the two different pairs of non-zero indices* $(3,4)$ *and* $(2,3)$ *. Thus, one knows automatically that the point 3 is situated between the point 2 and the point 4.* *The point 1 is part only of the pair of non-zero indices* $(2,1)$ *. The point 1 is therefore necessarily an end of the front.*

*Step 2* **: Search for the ends of the front**

As has just been mentioned, the ends of the crack front or fronts are identifiable because a single pair of indices all non-zero in $\mathit{LISTPT}$ contains their index. Thanks to this indication, one creates the list $\mathit{PTEXTR}$ comprising the set of the end points.

In the example, the point 1 belongs only to the pair $(2,1)$ and the point 4 only to the pair $(4,3)$ . They are then the two ends of our front.

*Step 3* **: Ordering of the points of the front**

This step corresponds to the orientation of the front proper.

In order to begin the ordering one starts from an end point. The latter is the first point of the list $\mathit{PTEXTR}$ . If the front is closed (front without end), the first point is the one having the index 1.

In our example, the first point is the point 1. In order to know the following point, it suffices to seek the pair possessing a 1 and a second non-zero value. In our case, there exists only one pair with the 1, namely $(2,1)$ , therefore the second point of the front is 2. Then, the point 2 is found only in the pair $(2,3)$ , therefore the following point is 3. One continues this approach until all the points are ordered.

One has then obtained the list of the points 1-2-3-4.

**Note**

If the first end found had been the point 4, one would have ordered the front in the other sense. One would have obtained the ordered list of the points 4-3-2-1.

*Case of a continuous crack front*

In this case, one still speaks of **the** crack front, as of the set of the pieces of the front. One says that the crack front is a multiple front. On the example of r7.02.12-fig-2-18, the crack front is composed of the curved lines $(\mathit{BC})$ , $(\mathit{DE})$ , $(\mathit{FG})$ and $(\mathit{HA})$ .

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/100000000000022D000001D655FEAE8C3A121874.png)

*Multiple crack front*

In the case of a multiple front, the first two steps of ordering of the points are unchanged relative to the case of a continuous front.

During the third step, the cracks having a multiple front are detected when one reaches a second end, without having ordered the set of the points of the front. One then searches for the start of the following front by taking a point of the list $\mathit{PTEXTR}$ .

In the case illustrated on r7.02.12-fig-2-19, $\mathit{PTEXTR}$ contains the points 1, 5, 6 and 10. One begins by ordering the points from 1 to 5. Then, as one knows that five points remain to be ordered, one takes the point 6 of $\mathit{PTEXTR}$ in order to continue the orientation of the front.

**Note**

In 2D, each point of the crack front represents by itself a front.

##### 2.4.2.3 Algorithm used for the orientation of the front

Let:

- $\mathit{NFON}$ be the number of points of the front,
- $\mathit{NMAFON}$ the number of mesh cells connected to the front,
- $\mathit{LISTPT}$ the list of the pairs of indices of the points of the front associated with the mesh cells.

One initialises to 0 an integer vector $\mathit{TABPT}$ of length $\mathit{NFON}$ , which will contain the indices of the ordered points.

The pair of indices associated with a mesh cell of index $\mathit{IMA}$ is

$(\mathit{LISTPT}(2\times \mathit{IMA}),\mathit{LISTPT}(2\times \mathit{IMA}+1))$ .

# the first point of the list of the ordered points:

$\mathit{TABPT}(1)=\mathit{PTEXTR}(1)$

## 3 Cracking problem with X-FEM

### 3.1 General problem

In this part, one recalls the equations of the general problem of a cracked structure. One considers a crack ${\Gamma}_{c}$ in a domain $\Omega \in {\Re }^{3}$ delimited by $\partial \Omega$ of outward normal ${n}_{\mathrm{ext}}$ . The lips of the crack are denoted ${\Gamma}^{1}$ and ${\Gamma}^{2}$ of outward normals ${n}^{1}$ and ${n}^{2}$ . The stress and displacement fields are denoted respectively $\sigma$ and $u$ .

A quasi-static loading is imposed on the structure by means of a body-force density $f$ , of a surface-force density $t$ on ${\Gamma}_{t}$ and of a surface-force density $g$ on the lips. The solid is clamped on ${\Gamma}_{u}$ .

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/10000000000002A60000013A99BCFF470FEBFCB8.png)

*Notations of the general problem*

The strong form of the equilibrium equations and of the boundary conditions is written:

$$
\begin{array}{}\nabla \cdot \sigma =f\text{dans}\Omega \\ \sigma \cdot {n}_{\mathrm{ext}}=t\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{t}\\ \sigma \cdot {n}^{1}=g\text{sur}{\Gamma}^{1}\\ \sigma \cdot {n}^{2}=g\text{sur}{\Gamma}^{2}\\ u=0\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{u}\end{array}
$$

We place ourselves within the framework of small strains and small displacements, for which the strain-displacement relation is written:

$$
\varepsilon =\varepsilon (u)={\nabla}_{s}u
$$

where ${\nabla}_{s}$ is the symmetric part of the gradient and $\varepsilon$ the strain tensor.

One considers a linear elastic material 1. The constitutive law of the solid $\Omega$ is written:

$$
\sigma =C:\varepsilon \text{dans}\Omega
$$

where $C$ is the Hooke tensor.

The principle of virtual work is written:

$$
{\int}_{\Omega}\sigma (u):\epsilon (v)d\Omega ={\int}_{\Omega}f\cdot \mathrm{vd}\Omega +{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{t}}t\cdot \mathrm{vd\Gamma }+{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}g\cdot \mathrm{vd\Gamma }\forall v\in {H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega )
$$

where ${H}_{0}^{1}(O)$ is the Sobolev space of the functions whose derivative is square-integrable, vanishing on $\partial \Omega$ .

### 3.2 Enrichment of the displacement approximation

The main idea is to enrich the basis of the interpolation functions thanks to the partition of unity. One recalls the classical finite-element approximation:

${u}^{h}(x)=\sum_{i\in {N}_{n}(x)}{a}_{i}{\phi }_{i}(x)$

where the ${a}_{i}$ are the displacement degrees of freedom at node i and ${\phi }_{i}$ the shape functions associated with node i. ${N}_{n}(x)$ is the set of the nodes whose support contains the point $x$ . One identifies the support of a node i with the support of the shape functions associated with this node, that is to say with the set of points $x$ such that ${\phi }_{i}(x)\ne 0$ .

The enriched approximation is written:

${u}^{h}(x)=\sum_{i\in {N}_{n}(x)}{a}_{i}{\varphi}_{i}(x)+\sum_{j\in {N}_{n}(x)\cap K}{b}_{j}{\varphi}_{j}(x){H}_{j}\left(\mathit{lsn}(x)\right)+\sum_{k\in {N}_{n}(x)\cap L}\sum_{\alpha =1}^{4}{c}_{k}^{\alpha}{\varphi}_{k}(x){F}^{\alpha}\left(\mathit{lsn}(x),\mathit{lst}(x)\right)$

This expression is composed of 3 terms. The 1st term is the classical continuous term. The 2nd and 3rd terms are enriched terms. Being at the heart of the X-FEM method, these terms are made explicit in the following paragraphs.

#### 3.2.1 Enrichment with a domain-selection function (2nd term)

Suppose that the interface ${\Gamma}_{c}$ partitions the domain such that $\Omega ={\Omega}_{+}\cup {\Omega}_{-}$ . If ${\Gamma}_{c}$ is a crack, one partitions in the same manner the domain $\Omega$ by virtually extending ${\Gamma}_{c}$ .

In order to represent the displacement jump across ${\Gamma}_{c}$ , one introduces the domain-selection function or characteristic function of the domain ${H}_{j}\left(x\right)$ defined by:

$\mathit{Si}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{x}_{j}\in {\Omega}_{+},\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{H}_{j}(x)=\left \lbrace \begin{array}{c}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}0\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\text{si}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}x\in {\Omega}_{+}\\ -2\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\text{si}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}x\notin {\Omega}_{+}\end{array} \right.$

$\mathit{Si}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{x}_{j}\in {\Omega}_{-},\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{H}_{j}(x)=\left \lbrace \begin{array}{c}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}0\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\text{si}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}x\in {\Omega}_{-}\\ +2\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\text{si}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}x\notin {\Omega}_{-}\end{array} \right.$

By making use of the normal level set, the quantity ${H}_{j}\left(\text{lsn}\left(x\right)\right)$ is equal to $0$ if the point $x$ and the node ${x}_{j}$ are on the same side of the crack and $\pm 2$ otherwise. The coefficient “2” is introduced in order to have a simpler writing of the mean displacement jump along the interface.

The ${b}_{j}$ are the enriched degrees of freedom. $K$ is the set of the nodes whose support is entirely cut by the crack (nodes represented by a circle on r7.02.12-fig-3-2).

**Note**
By abuse of language, one will also call the domain-selection functions “Heaviside” functions. But one will have to refer to the definition above, in order to represent the approximation of the discontinuity of the displacement field.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/100000000000053E00000208882E36DD6745FDF5.png)

*On the left, the “circle” nodes are enriched by the Heaviside function and the “square” nodes by the singular functions (topological enrichment). On the right, the “square” nodes are enriched by the singular functions (geometrical enrichment).*

#### 3.2.2 Enrichment with a domain-selection function for a branching of cracks

Suppose a branching of two discontinuities, which partitions the space into three domains such that $\Omega ={\Omega}_{1}\cup {\Omega}_{2}\cup {\Omega}_{3}$ . If it is a branching of two cracks, one partitions in the same manner the domain $\Omega$ by virtually extending the three crack branches.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/Object_1095.png)

*Partitioning of the domain by a crack junction*

For a simple branching, the generalisation of the domain-selection functions leads to the following writing:

$\begin{array}{c}{u}^{h}(x)=\sum_{i\in {N}_{n}(x)}{a}_{i}{\varphi}_{i}(x)+\sum_{j\in K\cap {\Omega}_{1}}{b}_{j,1}{\varphi}_{j}(x){\mathrm{\chi }}_{{\Omega}_{2}}+{b}_{j,2}{\varphi}_{j}(x){\mathrm{\chi }}_{{\Omega}_{3}}\\ +\sum_{j\in K\cap {\Omega}_{2}}{b}_{j,1}{\varphi}_{j}(x){\mathrm{\chi }}_{{\Omega}_{1}}+{b}_{j,2}{\varphi}_{j}(x){\mathrm{\chi }}_{{\Omega}_{3}}\\ +\sum_{j\in K\cap {\Omega}_{3}}{b}_{j,1}{\varphi}_{j}(x){\mathrm{\chi }}_{{\Omega}_{1}}+{b}_{j,2}{\varphi}_{j}(x){\mathrm{\chi }}_{{\Omega}_{2}}\end{array}$

Where ${\mathrm{\chi }}_{{\Omega}_{i}}$ are domain-selection functions defined by:

${\mathrm{\chi }}_{{\Omega}_{i}}(x)=\left \lbrace \begin{array}{c}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}2\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\text{si}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}x\in {\Omega}_{i}\\ \phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}0\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\text{si}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}x\notin {\Omega}_{i}\end{array} \right.$

However, the topological description of the three connected domains (Ω1, Ω2 and Ω3) is not trivial, given the only information provided by the level-sets. The level-sets make it possible to represent a scalar change of sign across a discontinuity. Whereas we wish to represent the partitioning of the space in the neighbourhood of the discontinuity.

From an “elementary” point of view, we observe that the sign field of the level-sets read in vector form sticks approximately to the partitioning that we wish to carry out. This partitioning, thanks to the vectorisation of the scalar level-sets, then makes it possible to reuse advantageously the data structures of Code_Aster. However, the elementary partitioning alone is insufficient to represent the “domain dof” (χΩϕi).

**Note**

In fact, the “domain dof” corresponds to the collection of the partitions of the domain Ω over the whole support of the node i. On the support of each node, it is therefore indispensable to concatenate the elementary partitions corresponding to a given “domain dof”. This operation is not trivial since the sign fields evolve from one element to another (as the scalar sign fields are not extended onto all the elements, in order to localise on a few elements of interest the definition of a crack, also called the “near band” of enrichment). An algorithm dedicated to the concatenation of the elementary partitioning has been developed in order to solve this fundamental difficulty.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/1000020100000282000001D6A229EAD52242D64E.png)

*Partitioning of a multi-cracked element by means of the “vectorised” sign field. For each domain, the first scalar component of the sign vector corresponds to the characterisation of the discontinuity of crack no. 1 and the second scalar component of the sign vector corresponds to the discontinuity of crack no. 2.*

*Construction of the sign functions or junctions:*

Let us recall the construction of the Heaviside sign functions or junction-type functions. It is a Heaviside function which is “truncated” at the branching. The enriched nodes are represented on r7.02.12-fig-3-5.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/Object_1383.png)

*Enrichment for the second crack. The circle nodes are enriched by the junction function, which is equal to +1, -1, 0.*

The value of this function depends on the normal level sets of the 2 cracks. One considers the sign of the normal level set of crack 1, on the side where crack 2 is defined $\mathrm{sign}({\mathrm{lsn}}_{1}({\mathrm{fiss}}_{2}))$ (in practice, one looks at the sign of a point belonging to the domain of ${\mathrm{lsn}}_{1}$ in which crack 2 is found, cf. operand JONCTION of the document [u4.82.08](doc:u4.82.08)). One then has the junction enrichment function for crack 2 which is written:

${J}_{2}(x)=\left \lbrace \begin{array}{}H({\mathrm{lsn}}_{2}(x))\text{si}\mathrm{sign}({\mathrm{lsn}}_{1}({\mathrm{fiss}}_{2}))H({\mathrm{lsn}}_{1}(x))\ge 0\\ 0\text{sinon}\end{array} \right.$

It is possible to branch a third crack onto the first (for example in order to model an intersection):

${J}_{3}(x)=\left \lbrace \begin{array}{}H({\mathrm{lsn}}_{3}(x))\text{si}\mathrm{sign}({\mathrm{lsn}}_{1}({\mathrm{fiss}}_{3}))H({\mathrm{lsn}}_{1}(x))\ge 0\\ 0\text{sinon}\end{array} \right.$

If one wishes to branch the third crack onto the second, one must take into account the domain of definition of the first; one will therefore have:

${J}_{3}(x)=\left \lbrace \begin{array}{}H({\mathrm{lsn}}_{3}(x))\text{si}\forall i\in [1,2],\mathrm{sign}({\mathrm{lsn}}_{i}({\mathrm{fiss}}_{3}))H({\mathrm{lsn}}_{i}(x))\ge 0\\ 0\text{sinon}\end{array} \right.$

If one generalises the approach to a crack $N$ which branches onto the cracks of a set $K$ , one then defines the set of cracks $P$ , which contains both all the cracks of $K$ and all the cracks onto which the cracks of $K$ possibly branch. The junction function is then written in a general manner:

${J}_{N}(x)=\left \lbrace \begin{array}{}H({\mathrm{lsn}}_{N}(x))\text{si}\forall i\in P,\mathrm{sign}({\mathrm{lsn}}_{i}({\mathrm{fiss}}_{n}))H({\mathrm{lsn}}_{i}(x))\ge 0\\ 0\text{sinon}\end{array} \right.$

An example of configuration is presented on r7.02.12-fig-3-6. On this example one constructs a connectivity tree of the cracks. One deduces from this tree that for $N=3$ , one has $K=2$ and $P=[1,2]$ . Likewise for $N=5$ , one deduces $K=[3,4]$ and $P=[1,2,3,4]$ . Thus one has ${J}_{5}(x)=H({\mathrm{lsn}}_{5}(x))$ on the hatched domain of the figure, and zero elsewhere.

#### 3.2.3 Enrichment with the singular functions (3rd term)

In order to represent the singularity at the crack front, one enriches the approximation with functions based on the asymptotic expansions of the displacement field in linear elastic fracture mechanics. These expressions have been determined for a plane crack in an infinite medium.

$$
\begin{array}{}{u}_{1}=\frac{1}{2\mu }\sqrt{\frac{r}{2\pi }}({K}_{1}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}(\kappa -\cos\theta )+{K}_{2}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}(\kappa +2+\cos\theta ))\\ {u}_{2}=\frac{1}{2\mu }\sqrt{\frac{r}{2\pi }}({K}_{1}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}(\kappa -\cos\theta )+{K}_{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}(\kappa -2+\cos\theta ))\\ {u}_{3}=\frac{1}{2\mu }\sqrt{\frac{r}{2\pi }}{K}_{3}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\\ \mu =\frac{E}{2(1+\nu )}\text{et}\kappa =3-4\nu \text{en}\text{déformations}\text{planes}.\end{array}
$$

The plane-stress assumption cannot be retained because no cracked plate is in a situation of plane stress in the neighbourhood of the singularity, when one places oneself at a finite distance from the skin of the shell.

The basis making it possible to describe these fields comprises 4 functions:

$\left\lbrace \sqrt{r}\cos\frac{\theta}{2},\sqrt{r}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\theta ,\sqrt{r}\sin\frac{\theta}{2},\sqrt{r}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\theta \right\rbrace$ .

As:

$\left \lbrace \begin{array}{c}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\theta =-\sin\theta \sin\frac{\theta}{2}+\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\\ \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\theta =\sin\theta \cos\frac{\theta}{2}-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\end{array} \right.$

one then chooses the following basis 2:

$F=\left\lbrace \sqrt{r}\sin\frac{\theta}{2},\sqrt{r}\cos\frac{\theta}{2},\sqrt{r}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\theta ,\sqrt{r}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\theta \right\rbrace$ .

where $(r,\theta )$ are the polar coordinates in the local basis at the crack front (see r7.02.12-fig-2-14 and r7.02.12-fig-3-8).

These coordinates can be expressed easily thanks to the level sets, since:

$r=\sqrt{\text{lsn}\mathrm{²}+\text{lst}\mathrm{²}},\theta =\arctan(\frac{\text{lsn}}{\text{lst}}),\theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]$

In practice, one rather uses the computer function $\text{atan2}(\text{lsn},\text{lst})$ which returns the principal value of the argument of the complex number $(\text{lst},\text{lsn})$ expressed in radians in the interval $\left[-\pi ,\pi \right]$ .

For points situated exactly on the lower lip $(\text{lsn}=0)$ , the function $\text{atan2}(0,\text{lst})$ gives an angle equal to $\pi$ . In theory, $\text{atan2}(-0,\text{lst})$ makes it possible to obtain $-\pi$ as expected, but this is not numerically always the case. In order to remedy this drawback, one rather uses the following expression for the angle $\theta$ :

$\theta =H(\text{lsn})\mid \text{atan}2(\text{lsn},\text{lst})\mid ,\theta \in \left[-\pi ,\pi \right]$

where $H(\text{lsn})$ is the value of the Heaviside function. Thus, when one is on the lower lip, the value $-\pi$ is indeed reached.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/10000000000002610000016142F13C1B42502444.png)

*Polar coordinates in the local basis*

One notes that only the 1st function of the basis is discontinuous across the crack. The other functions are added only in order to improve the precision. These functions are the Westergaard solutions, analytical asymptotic solutions of an elastic fracture problem in 2D. This basis is well suited to the 3D cases, at least for cracks whose front is fairly regular. These functions are said to be “singular” because their derivatives are singular at $r=0$ .

The ${c}_{k}^{\alpha}$ are the enriched degrees of freedom. $L$ is the set of the nodes whose support is partially cut by the crack front (nodes represented by a square on r7.02.12-fig-3-2). This means that a single layer of elements is enriched around the crack front. This enrichment is called “topological”.

#### 3.2.4 Geometrical enrichment

From the very first papers on X-FEM, it is stated that a geometrical criterion ${r}_{\max}$ can be defined in order to determine the nodes enriched by the singular functions (see r7.02.12-fig-3-2):

$L=\left\lbrace \text{noeuds}\text{tels}\text{que}r<{r}_{\max}\right\rbrace$

The first convergence studies were carried out in 2000 within the framework of the GFEM, with account taken of several layers of elements enriched at the crack front.

When one studies the convergence, one is interested in the evolution of the error with respect to the degree of refinement of the mesh. Generally, one denotes by $h$ a characteristic length of the elements of the mesh, and one seeks to determine the parameter $\alpha$ called the rate (or speed or order) of convergence, such that the relative error ${e}_{\text{rel}}$ is written in the form:

${e}_{\mathrm{rel}}={\parallel u-{u}_{h}\parallel }_{{H}^{1}}\underset{h\to 0}{\approx}{\mathrm{Ch}}^{\alpha}$

where $C$ is a constant independent of $h$ .

Since

$\log{e}_{\text{rel}}\approx \log{C}+\alpha \log{h}$

the parameter $\alpha$ appears as the slope of the straight line $\log{e}_{\text{rel}}$ as a function of $\log{h}$ when $h$ tends towards 0.

Stazi *et al.* studies the convergence of the error in energy for an infinite plate with a straight crack, in mode I, for linear and quadratic formulations. He notes that the quadratic formulation improves the error, but not the rate of convergence. Béchet *et al.* confirms this observation and shows that a fixed enrichment zone makes it possible to recover an almost optimal rate of convergence.

In parallel, Laborde *et al.* goes deeper into the question, and tests the rates of convergence for the polynomial formulations of higher order. Moreover, he brings improvements in order to recover an optimal rate, or even a super-convergence. r7.02.12-tab-3-1 gathers the results obtained by Laborde for different variants of X-FEM, and this for polynomial approximations of degree $k=1,2,3$ .

|  | FEM | X-FEM | X-FEM (f. a.) | X-FEM (d. g.) | X-FEM (p. m.) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| P1 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.9 | 0.5 | 1.1 |
| P2 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 1.8 | 1.5 | 2.2 |
| P3 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 2.6 | 2.6 | 3.3 |

The first column corresponds to the orders of convergence of the classical finite element method for a cracking problem. Given the singularity, the speed of convergence is in $\sqrt{h}$ whatever the degree $k$ . The 2D simulations carried out on a test problem: rectilinear crack on a square in opening mode I show that X-FEM does not improve the rate of convergence. This can be explained by the fact that the topological enrichment concerns only a single layer of elements at the crack front. The zone of influence of this enrichment is therefore strongly linked to $h$ . Thus, when $h$ tends towards 0, the size of the zone of influence of the enrichment also tends towards 0. The idea which seems natural is then no longer to limit the enrichment zone to a single layer of elements, but to widen it to a zone of fixed size, independent of the refinement of the mesh. The 3rd column of r7.02.12-tab-3-1 presents the results obtained with this method called X-FEM f. a. (for Fixed enrichment Area). One recovers almost the hoped-for rates of convergence ($\alpha =k$ for a $\mathit{Pk}$ approximation). However, the conditioning is degraded relative to the usual X-FEM method. In order to recover an acceptable conditioning, Laborde proposes to gather the degrees of freedom enriched by the singular functions. In short, instead of having different enriched dofs for each enriched node, they are globalised so as to have only one per singular function and per crack tip (but in 3D, one does not clearly see how that works). With this arrangement, the conditioning is greatly improved, but the rates of convergence are lower (X-FEM d. g.). The problem comes from the transition elements between the enriched zone and the non-enriched zone. According to Laborde, the phenomenon is explained by the effect of the partition of unity which cannot be used on these partially enriched elements. In order to remedy this defect, a final version is proposed: X-FEM p.m. (for Pointwise Matching). The displacements of the nodes on the boundary between the enriched zone and the non-enriched zone are imposed equal. Thanks to this joining, one obtains the hoped-for rates of convergence (or even a slight super-convergence).

**Note**

For the polynomial approximations of degree* $k=2,3$ , Laborde et al. like many others uses shape functions of degree* $k$ for the classical terms and the terms enriched by the discontinuity functions (2nd term), so that the jump is of degree $k$ . On the other hand, for the terms enriched by the singular functions (3rd term), it suffices to use the linear shape functions in order to capture the singularity at the crack front and not to deteriorate too much the conditioning of the matrices.

At present, in *code_aster*, only an approximation of degree 1 is possible, and the fact of supplying or not a value of radius for the enrichment zone (keyword RAYON_ENRI) makes it possible to place oneself respectively within the framework of X-FEM (f. a.) or of classical X-FEM.

#### 3.2.5 Enrichment in code_aster

##### 3.2.5.1 Enrichment (status) of the nodes

In order to know whether a node is enriched by the Heaviside function (node of type “Heaviside”) or by the singular functions (node of type “crack-tip”), one computes the min and the max of the normal level set on all the nodes belonging to the support of the node (the notion of “support” is defined at r7.02.12-enrichissement-approximation-deplacement), and one computes the min and max of the tangent level set on all the points belonging to the support of the node considered where the normal level set vanishes.

$\begin{array}{c}j\in K\iff \left(\underset{x\in {N}_{n}(j)}{\min}(\text{lsn}(x))\underset{x\in {N}_{n}(j)}{\max}(\text{lsn}(x))<0\right)\text{et}\left(\underset{x\in {N}_{n}(j)\cap \text{lsn}(x)=0}{\max}(\text{lst}(x))<0\right)\\ k\in L\iff \left(\underset{x\in {N}_{n}(j)}{\min}(\text{lsn}(x))\underset{x\in {N}_{n}(j)}{\max}(\text{lsn}(x))\le 0\right)\text{et}\left(\underset{x\in {N}_{n}(j)\cap \text{lsn}(x)=0}{\min}(\text{lst}(x))\underset{x\in {N}_{n}(j)\cap \text{lsn}(x)=0}{\max}(\text{lst}(x))\le 0\right)\end{array}$

Similar ideas appear in , but it would seem that certain cases of figures have not been taken into account. These expressions are the outcome of the first efforts which aimed at determining the types of enrichment solely by means of the level sets. The reader will find there the corresponding explanatory figures.

In the case of a geometrical enrichment at the crack front, the criterion for selection of the nodes is the following:

$k\in L\iff \sqrt{\text{lsn}(x)\mathrm{²}+\text{lst}(x)\mathrm{²}}\le {r}_{\max}$

Concerning the choice of the value of the enrichment radius, nothing is clearly indicated in the literature. It would seem however that a radius equal to between $1/5$ and $1/10$ of the length of the crack is a relevant choice.

Recent studies have shown that the geometrical enrichment strongly degrading the conditioning of the stiffness matrix, it had to be limited in a restricted zone around the crack front, pending a treatment making it possible to improve the conditioning. One proposes an alternative, which is halfway between the topological and the geometrical enrichment: an enrichment on $n$ layers. In this case, one computes an ${r}_{max}$ (unique for each crack) according to the user data of the number of layers, then one applies the previous formula.

Let $\mathit{MAFIS}$ be the set of the mesh cells on which the normal level set vanishes

- loop on all the nodes $P$ of the mesh

- end loop
- loop on the vertex nodes of the mesh cell

- end loop

> if $(\text{minlsn}.\text{maxlsn}<0)$ and $(\text{maxlst}\le 0)$ then $P\in K$

In order to obtain the equation, as well as the value of the tangent level set at the point $C$ , one determines beforehand the curvilinear abscissa $s$ such that

$C=A+s(B-A)$

thanks to the fact that the normal level set vanishes at $C$ , that is

$\text{lsn}(C)=\text{lsn}(A)+s(\text{lsn}(B)-\text{lsn}(A))=0$

One deduces from it the expression of the point $C$ as well as the value of the tangent level set at $C$ :

$\begin{array}{c}\text{lst}\left(C\right)=\text{lst}\left(A\right)+s\left(\text{lst}\left(B\right)-\text{lst}\left(A\right)\right)\\ =\text{lst}\left(A\right)-\frac{\text{lsn}\left(A\right)}{\text{lsn}\left(B\right)-\text{lsn}\left(A\right)}\left(\text{lst}\left(B\right)-\text{lst}\left(A\right)\right)\end{array}$

In order to obtain, in the quadratic case, the equation, as well as the value of the tangent level set at the point $C$ , one determines beforehand the coordinate in the reference space $\xi$ of the point $C$

The $\mathit{lsn}$ is interpolated quadratically along the edge. It is therefore a matter of solving a polynomial of the second degree:

$0=\frac{\mathit{lsn}(A)\ast \xi \ast (\xi -1)}{2}+\frac{\mathit{lsn}(B)\ast (\xi +1)\ast \xi }{2}-\mathit{lsn}(M)\ast (\xi -1)\ast (\xi +1)$

The equation $\mathit{lsn}=0$ possesses a unique solution on the edge because $\text{lsn}(A)\text{lsn}(B)<0$

Once the coordinate $\xi$ of the point $C$ has been obtained, one interpolates the $\mathit{lst}$ of the points $A$ , $B$ and $M$ in order to obtain the $\mathit{lst}$ of the point $C$ :

$\mathit{lst}(C)=\frac{\mathit{lst}(A)\ast \xi \ast (\xi -1)}{2}+\frac{\mathit{lst}(B)\ast (\xi +1)\ast \xi }{2}-\mathit{lst}(M)\ast (\xi -1)\ast (\xi +1)$

##### 3.2.5.2 Enrichment (status) of the mesh cells

In *Code_Aster*, one must define precise types of finite elements, and in order not to multiply the number of possibilities, the choice was made to define 3 types of X-FEM finite elements: the “Heaviside” elements, the “crack-tip” elements and the mixed “Heaviside and crack-tip” elements.

If the mesh cell possesses at least one node of type “Heaviside”, then it is a “Heaviside” mesh cell.

If the mesh cell possesses at least one node of type “crack-tip”, then it is a “crack-tip” mesh cell.

If the mesh cell possesses at least one “Heaviside” node and at least one “crack-tip” node, or if the mesh cell contains at least one “Heaviside and crack-tip” node, then it is a “Heaviside and crack-tip” mesh cell.

Let us denote $\mathit{GRMAEN1}$ the “Heaviside” mesh cells, $\mathit{GRMAEN2}$ the “crack-tip” mesh cells and $\mathit{GRMAEN3}$ the “Heaviside and crack-tip” mesh cells.

Let the mesh cell $i$ , and $\text{Ni}$ the set of the nodes $j$ of the mesh cell $i$ .

$\begin{array}{c}\text{si}\left(\exists j\in \text{Ni},\text{tel}\text{que}j\in K\right)\text{alors}i\in \text{GRMAEN}1\\ \text{si}\left(\exists j\in \text{Ni},\text{tel}\text{que}j\in L\right)\text{alors}i\in \text{GRMAEN}2\\ \text{si}\left(\exists (j,k)\in {\text{Ni}}^{2},\text{tels}\text{que}j\in K\text{et}k\in L\right)\text{ou}\left(\exists j\in \text{Ni},\text{tel}\text{que}j\in K\cap L\right)\text{alors}i\in \text{GRMAEN}3\end{array}$

One notes that all the nodes of an element will be assigned the same characteristics and the same enrichment, whereas this is not necessarily what is wanted. One must therefore cancel the enriched degrees of freedom “in excess”.

##### 3.2.5.3 Cancellation of the enriched degrees of freedom “in excess”

One has seen in the preceding paragraph that a mesh cell of type “Heaviside” can comprise for example only a single node of type “Heaviside”, the other nodes of the mesh cell being classical nodes requiring no enrichment. Now these nodes will be assigned the degrees of freedom of the “Heaviside” mesh cell, therefore enriched degrees of freedom. Consequently, one must proceed to a cancellation of these degrees of freedom enriched wrongly. The cancellation makes it possible in practice to pass continuously from an enriched zone to a non-enriched zone and makes it possible to have two types of elements coexist (in the sense that they share a common boundary), one enriched, the other not enriched. The non-enriched variable is the same on the common boundary and the degrees of freedom corresponding to the enrichment are set to zero for the element which is enriched on this same boundary (but not elsewhere in this same element). This way of proceeding avoids having to solve the question of the “blending elements”, a treatment of which can be found in .

Several cases present themselves:

- Heaviside degrees of freedom to be cancelled at the classical nodes of a Heaviside or mixed mesh cell
- crack-tip degrees of freedom to be cancelled at the classical nodes of a crack-tip or mixed mesh cell
- Heaviside degrees of freedom to be cancelled at the crack-tip nodes of a mixed mesh cell
- crack-tip degrees of freedom to be cancelled at the Heaviside nodes of a mixed mesh cell.

The technique of cancellation of these degrees of freedom is explained in detail in [r5.03.54](doc:r5.03.54) §4.4.

#### 3.2.6 Conditioning linked to the enrichment

##### 3.2.6.1 General remarks on the XFEM conditioning

The conditioning, denoted ${10}^{\delta}$ , corresponds to the ratio between the largest and the smallest eigenvalue of a system to be inverted. For a computation in double precision with a numerical error of ${10}^{-15}$ , the relative error obtained on the computation is of the order of ${10}^{-15+\delta }$ . One must therefore verify the condition $\delta <9$ in order to guarantee a numerical precision of the order of ${10}^{-6}$ .

The geometrical enrichment strongly degrades the conditioning of the stiffness matrix. Béchet et al. propose a technique of orthogonalisation of the degrees of freedom during the computation of the elementary stiffness matrices in order to improve the conditioning of the assembled matrix. Laborde et al. explain that the poor conditioning is due to the fact that the chosen enrichment basis does not form a linearly independent family locally. They therefore propose to put a single degree of freedom for these functions over the whole enrichment zone and to connect the displacements at the limit between enriched and non-enriched zones in order to recover optimal rates of convergence. The conditioning problem is moreover such that with quadratic elements it becomes impossible to obtain results, without putting in place one of the techniques. Indeed, for these elements, the poor conditioning is due not only to the singular part of the enrichment, but also to the Heaviside enrichment, when the crack passes very close to a node. One presents the evolution of the conditioning number as the interface approaches the nodes of the mesh on r7.02.12-fig-3-11, for linear and quadratic elements respectively. The values of this figure are very approximate. On the one hand one does not take into account the elements of the neighbourhood. On the other hand these values are obtained in a coarse manner. In the case of the cube cut in a corner for example (on the right of the figure), one considers the stiffness of the small cube of the corner of side ́ ${10}^{-\gamma }$ instead of the small tetrahedron of the corner. Finally the conditioning of a global problem is generally larger than the local conditioning and increases when one refines the mesh. Taking into account all these considerations, one obtains in practice conditionings of an order 2 to 4 times higher than those of r7.02.12-fig-3-11.

##### 3.2.6.2 The estimation criteria for improving the conditioning

The technique of readjustment of the level set mentioned at r7.02.12-reajustement-level-sets makes it possible to guard against this poor conditioning. Denoting by ${10}^{-\gamma }$ the distance from the intersection point of the level set to the closest vertex node normalised by the length of the side, the readjustment to 1% of the edge length carried out at r7.02.12-reajustement-level-sets corresponds to $\gamma =2$ . This readjustment must act sufficiently quickly so that the conditioning is not too deteriorated, but not for values of $\gamma$ that are too small so as not to disturb the system by displacing in a non-realistic manner the cracking surface. For quadratic hexahedron elements, if ${10}^{-15+9\gamma +2}$ must be of the order of ${10}^{-5}$ , one obtains $9\gamma +2=10$ that is a readjustment to 13% of the edge length. It is therefore not possible in this case to activate reasonably the readjustment of the level sets at the vertex, so that the conditioning is not deteriorated.

Under these conditions, one puts in place a criterion making it possible to detect whether a Heaviside degree of freedom poses a problem. If the criterion is verified, the degree of freedom is eliminated by setting it to zero, as at r7.02.12-annulation-degres-liberte.

The principle is the same as the volume criterion proposed by Daux for the junctions. The idea is to look, for each node whose support is cut by the level set, at the ratio of the sizes of the zones on either side of the level set on this support (zones assigned a Heaviside value equal to ±1). If:

$$
\frac{min({V}_{-1},{V}_{+1})}{{V}_{\mathit{support}}}\le {10}^{-\alpha }
$$

the Heaviside degrees of freedom of the node concerned are set to zero in all the directions.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/Object_937.png)

*Illustration of the implementation of the volume criterion. The support of the node cut by the level set is illustrated. One compares the grey and white volumes relative to the total volume of the support of the node (union of the grey and white volumes) in the case of a crack (left) and of a junction (right).*

If this criterion is relevant for the linear elements (triangles, tetrahedra) with a value of $\alpha$ of 4, it is not satisfactory for the multi-linear elements (quadrangles, pyramids, pentahedra, hexahedra) and quadratic elements. Indeed the value of $\alpha$ of 4 leads to eliminating degrees of freedom which should not be eliminated, which disturbs the solution, whereas higher values of $\alpha$ degrade the conditioning. In order to take these elements into account, one uses a stiffness criterion, which is based on a comparison of the stiffnesses of the support and no longer simply of the volumes. For each node whose support is cut by the level set, one looks for this support at the ratio of the stiffnesses of the zones on either side of the level set (zones assigned a Heaviside value equal to ±1). If at a node $n$ where $\mathit{se}$ are the sub-elements of its support, one has:

$$
\frac{min({\sum}_{{\mathit{se}}_{-1}}{\int}_{{\Omega}_{\mathit{se}}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{\phi }_{n,X}\mathrm{\parallel }}^{2}d{\Omega}_{\mathit{se}},{\sum}_{{\mathit{se}}_{+1}}{\int}_{{\Omega}_{\mathit{se}}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{\phi }_{n,X}\mathrm{\parallel }}^{2}d{\Omega}_{\mathit{se}})}{{\sum}_{\mathit{se}}{\int}_{{\Omega}_{\mathit{se}}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{\phi }_{n,X}\mathrm{\parallel }}^{2}d{\Omega}_{\mathit{se}}}\le {10}^{-\delta }
$$

where ${\phi }_{n,X}$ is the derivative of the shape function at the node $n$ in the global direction $X$ , then the Heaviside degrees of freedom of the node $n$ are set to zero in all the directions. One will note that the constitutive behaviour is not present in the stiffness criterion of , the criterion having been normalised. This makes it possible to discriminate in advance the degrees of freedom to be eliminated, without knowledge of the problem to be solved (non-linearities, plasticity, contact, etc). This criterion, very close to a conditioning criterion, leads us to choose values of $\delta$ comprised between 8 and 10. In practice, we take a value of $\delta$ of 9.

The criterion described in quantifies, in order of magnitude, the compromise between quality of the solution and conditioning. As this criterion is valid only in order of magnitude, it is not necessarily relevant to compute exactly all the integrals. In the Aster programming, one therefore approximates the integral computation on the sub-elements:

$$
{\int}_{{\Omega}_{\mathrm{se}}}{\parallel {\phi }_{n,X}\parallel }^{2}d{\Omega}_{\mathrm{se}}\approx \text{}{\parallel {\phi }_{n,X}({G}^{\mathrm{se}})\parallel }_{\infty}^{2}{V}^{\mathrm{se}}
$$

where ${G}^{\mathrm{se}}$ designates the barycentre of the sub-element, ${V}^{\mathrm{se}}$ the volume of the sub-element.

Instead of computing the integral on the sub-element, one evaluates the derivatives of shape functions at a point. This strategy makes it possible to capture in order of magnitude the elimination criterion of , with a low computation cost.

This approximation proves robust and inexpensive with linear elements.

On the other hand, with quadratic elements, the derivatives of the shape functions admit roots. The computation at a point is risky: the barycentre may be a root of the derivatives of shape functions, where the estimate will be zero, but not the integral on the sub-element. This poor estimate can randomly generate eliminations, which disturbs the solution and is not acceptable.

One therefore reinforces the estimate for the quadratic elements, by adding other evaluation points, in addition to the barycentre:

$$
{\int}_{{\Omega}_{\mathrm{se}}}{\parallel {\phi }_{n,X}\parallel }^{2}d{\Omega}_{\mathrm{se}}\approx \text{}{\max}_{{P}_{i}\in {\Omega}_{\mathrm{se}}}({\parallel {\phi }_{n,X}({P}_{i})\parallel }_{\infty}^{2}){V}^{\mathrm{se}}
$$

So that the evaluation is relevant, the points are sufficiently well distributed: one considers the points equidistant between the vertex nodes and the barycentre (for example, 3 points for a tri6).

$$
\lbrace {P}_{i}\rbrace =\lbrace {G}^{\mathrm{se}}\rbrace \cup \lbrace \frac{{\mathrm{Node}}_{i}+{G}^{\mathrm{se}}}{2}\rbrace
$$

However the following criticisms can be made with respect to these estimation criteria:

- on the one hand, they do not guarantee the control of the conditioning just before the elimination. As long as the elimination is not carried out, the conditioning increases as ${10}^{n\times \gamma }$ (r7.02.12-fig-3-11), which can lead to the stopping of the direct solver, in an arbitrary manner. This phenomenon is exacerbated with the quadratic elements, see r7.02.12-fig-3-11.
- on the other hand, an analysis of these different criteria is made in . It shows that the eliminations lead to an absence of convergence of the error in energy on a simple example of homogeneous compression of a cube crossed by an inclined interface. The solution proposed consists in replacing the elimination of the Heaviside degrees of freedom by an orthogonalisation of the local stiffness matrices, an idea which comes from the X-FEM preconditioner of . In order to remove this last difficulty it is necessary to estimate local stiffness matrices at least in triple precision.

In order to address the first criticism, that is to say to stem the exponential increase of the conditioning, we have put in place in Code_Aster the preconditioner proposed by Béchet et al.

##### 3.2.6.3 The XFEM preconditioner for the matrices

It is an automatic and algebraic procedure, in order to “orthogonalise” the degrees of freedom associated with an XFEM node. Indeed, the XFEM enrichment functions rely on the nodal functions ${\Phi}_{i}$ in order to describe, either the discontinuity of the displacement by the jump functions $H{\Phi}_{i}$ , or the crack front by the singular functions ${F}^{\alpha}{\Phi}_{i}$ . The functions introduced by the XFEM enrichment are not orthogonal to the shape functions at each XFEM node. Moreover, the XFEM enrichment functions and the nodal functions share the same support: situations arise where the XFEM functions approach the nodal functions, to the point of becoming almost collinear.

The information on the collinearity is transported into the stiffness matrix $K$ : the conditioning of the stiffness matrix increases if the collinearity increases at least at one XFEM node. From a more formal point of view, the stiffness matrix (cf. r7.02.12-integrande-rigidite-mecanique derives from the discretisation of a symmetric positive bilinear form. Therefore, there exists a scalar product ${\langle .,.\rangle }_{K}$ such that the term of the stiffness matrix ${K}_{i,x}$ representing the collinearity between the nodal function ${\Phi}_{i}$ and the enrichment function ${F}_{x}{\Phi}_{i}$ is rewritten:

$$
{K}_{i,x}={\langle {\Phi}_{i},{F}_{x}{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{K}
$$

Béchet proposes the construction of a preconditioner such that:

$$
K\to \stackrel{̃}{K}={P}_{c}^{T}K{P}_{c}
$$

One then transforms the original matrix system, in the following manner:

$$
Ku=f\to \lbrace \begin{array}{c}\stackrel{̃}{K}\stackrel{̃}{u}=\stackrel{̃}{f}\\ \stackrel{̃}{f}={P}_{c}^{T}f\\ u={P}_{c}\stackrel{̃}{u}\end{array}
$$

Thus, Béchet constructs the new matrix $\stackrel{̃}{K}$ in order to respect the following orthogonality condition:

$$
{\stackrel{̃}{K}}_{i,x}={\langle {\Phi}_{i},{F}_{x}{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{\stackrel{̃}{K}}={\langle {P}_{c}{\Phi}_{i},{P}_{c}{F}_{x}{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{K}=0
$$

In other words, the new functional basis $\left({P}_{c}{\Phi}_{i},{P}_{c}{F}_{x}{\Phi}_{i}\right)$ (by the change of basis ${P}_{c}$ ) is **orthogonal,** on the support of each XFEM node. Consequently, the new matrix system to be inverted $\stackrel{̃}{K}\stackrel{̃}{u}={P}_{c}^{T}f$ is better conditioned independently of the position of the interface, cf. r7.02.12-criteres-estimatifs-conditionnement.

The construction of $\stackrel{̃}{K}$ is not unique. proposes to use the Cholesky factorisation of the local stiffness matrices, associated with each XFEM node:

$$
{K}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{\langle {\Phi}_{i},{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{K}& {\langle {\Phi}_{i},H{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{K}& {\langle {\Phi}_{i},{F}^{\alpha}{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{K}\\ {\langle {\Phi}_{i},H{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{K}& {\langle H{\Phi}_{i},H{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{K}& {\langle H{\Phi}_{i},{F}^{\alpha}{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{K}\\ {\langle {\Phi}_{i},{F}^{\alpha}{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{K}& {\langle H{\Phi}_{i},{F}^{\alpha}{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{K}& {\langle {F}^{\alpha}{\Phi}_{i},{F}^{\alpha}{\Phi}_{i}\rangle }_{K}\end{array}\right]
$$

The matrix ${K}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}$ is symmetric positive definite. It therefore admits a Cholesky factorisation, that is to say, there exists an upper triangular matrix ${S}_{i}$ such that:

$$
{K}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}={S}_{i}^{T}{S}_{i}
$$

In a configuration of optimal contiguous storage of the degrees of freedom, then chooses a block-diagonal preconditioner, such that:

$$
{P}_{c}=\left[\begin{array}{cccccc}{P}_{c}^{1}& 0& 0& \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}\\ 0& {P}_{c}^{2}& 0& \mathrm{...}& 0& \mathrm{...}\\ 0& 0& {P}_{c}^{3}& \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}\\ \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}& \ddots & \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}\\ \mathrm{...}& 0& \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}& {P}_{c}^{i}& \mathrm{...}\\ \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}& \mathrm{...}& \ddots \end{array}\right]
$$

where

$$
{P}_{c}^{i}=\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{S}_{i}^{-1}\text{si}i\text{est un noeud XFEM}\\ {I}_{d}\text{sinon}\end{array}
$$

with ${S}_{i}^{-1}$ the inverse of the Cholesky factorised matrix. The new stiffness matrix $\stackrel{̃}{K}$ verifies at the local scale of the support of the XFEM node:

$$
{\stackrel{̃}{K}}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}={\left({P}_{c}^{i}\right)}^{T}{K}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}{P}_{c}^{i}={\left({S}_{i}^{-1}\right)}^{T}{K}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}{S}_{i}^{-1}={\left({S}_{i}^{-1}\right)}^{T}\left({S}_{i}^{T}{S}_{i}\right){S}_{i}^{-1}={I}_{d}
$$

${I}_{d}$ designates the identity matrix. ${I}_{d}$ therefore respects the orthogonality condition of the equation.

In practice, it is necessary to scale the new matrix ${\stackrel{̃}{K}}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}$ with the rest of the stiffness matrix. One therefore prefers the following preconditioner to the preconditioner:

$$
{P}_{c}^{i}=\sqrt{\mathit{scal}}\times {S}_{i}^{-1}
$$

where $\mathit{scal}$ is a scaling coefficient such that: $\mathit{scal}=\frac{max(∣\mathit{diag}(K)∣)+min(∣\mathit{diag}(K)∣)}{2}$

Consequently, the new local preconditioned matrix respecting the orthogonality condition, is:

$$
{\stackrel{̃}{K}}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}={\left({P}_{c}^{i}\right)}^{T}{K}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}{P}_{c}^{i}=\mathit{scal}\times {I}_{d}
$$

Moreover, the use of a Cholesky factorisation is risky if the conditioning of the local stiffness matrix ${K}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}$ deteriorates. In the event of failure of the Cholesky factorisation, one orthogonalises the local matrix by means of an SVD (singular value decomposition):

$$
{K}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}={U}_{i}{D}_{i}{U}_{i}^{T}
$$

where ${U}_{i}$ is an orthogonal matrix and ${D}_{i}$ is a diagonal matrix with strictly positive values.

One then carries out alternatively the choice of preconditioner, as follows:

$$
{P}_{c}^{i}=\sqrt{\mathit{scal}}\times {U}_{i}\sqrt{{D}_{i}^{-1}}
$$

It is easy to verify that this choice makes it possible to respect the orthogonality condition:

${\stackrel{̃}{K}}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}={\left({P}_{c}^{i}\right)}^{T}{K}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}{P}_{c}^{i}={\left(\sqrt{\mathit{scal}}\times {U}_{i}\sqrt{{D}_{i}^{-1}}\right)}^{T}\left({U}_{i}{D}_{i}{U}_{i}^{T}\right)\left(\sqrt{\mathit{scal}}\times {U}_{i}\sqrt{{D}_{i}^{-1}}\right)=\mathit{scal}\times {I}_{d}$

In summary, the construction of the Béchet preconditioner proceeds in 4 steps:

1. the extraction of the local matrices ${K}_{\mathit{loc}}^{i}$ in the stiffness matrix (that is to say of the block matrices associated with the dofs carried by the XFEM nodes no. $i$ ).
2. the computation of the local preconditioning matrices ${P}_{c}^{i}$ (cf. equation or ),
3. the assembly of the preconditioner (cf. equation ),

### 3.3 Subdivision

Particular attention must be paid during the numerical integration of the stiffness terms and of the right-hand side of an element crossed by the crack. Indeed, on an element crossed by a crack, the gradients of the displacements can be discontinuous, and in this case the Gauss-Legendre numerical integration over the whole of the element is not applicable. In order to return to classical conditions of regularity, one must proceed to an integration over domains where the integrand is at least continuous. For an element crossed by a crack, one must therefore integrate separately on either side of the crack (this appears for the 1st time in for the 2D and in for the 3D). Several procedures are conceivable, and easily implemented in 2D. The difficulties appear with the 3D.

One seeks to subdivide into sub-tetrahedra an arbitrary volume element (tetrahedron, pentahedron, hexahedron) cut by a surface. One recalls that this cutting serves only for integration purposes, it is purely virtual and no node is added to the mesh. The mesh is in no way modified.

For the pentahedra and the hexahedra, the number of possibilities being too large and the configurations too complicated, one prefers to reduce the problem to the cutting of tetrahedra. One thus “condenses” the large number of cutting possibilities to a few configurations. In other words, we formulate the “clustering” of a set of large size (number of intersection configurations) towards a set of small size (reduced number of cutting configurations in a tetrahedron).

In fact, the “condensation” algorithm constructs a bijective mapping, from the parent polyhedron, towards a few reference cutting configurations. This mapping can be decomposed into two bijective operations:

1 – A (explicit) bijection towards the cutting of a tetrahedron: One therefore carries out a preliminary phase which consists in cutting systematically the quadrangles, pyramids, pentahedra and hexahedra into tetrahedra. This bijection is not unique. As one can see on r7.02.12-fig-3-14, there exist two bijections for dividing a quadrangle into two triangles (likewise for a pyramid), 6 bijections for dividing a pentahedron into 3 tetrahedra and 6 bijections for dividing a hexahedron into 2 pentahedra which are then divided into tetrahedra. In the case of the hexahedron, one thus obtains twice each cutting configuration. The maximum number of distinct bijections for dividing a hexahedron into 6 tetrahedra is therefore $\frac{{6}^{3}}{2}=108$ .

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/100000000000053B000001C54E599DDD1ABDD260.jpg)

*Division of a quadrangle into two triangles (on the left), division of a pentahedron into 3 tetrahedra (in the middle) and division of a hexahedron into 2 pentahedra (on the right)*

In r7.02.12-phase-prealable-decoupe-hexaedres to r7.02.12-phase-prealable-decoupe-pyramides, one presents the bijection retained by default for the division of the non-simplicial elements into tetrahedra (or triangles for the 2D case).

It is a bijection, because one constructs a (invertible) mapping which associates the relative numbering of the vertex nodes in a sub-tetrahedron with the absolute numbering of the nodes in the mesh. The direct mapping is stored in the connectivity table (see [d4.10.02](doc:d4.10.02): XFEM data structures).

2 – An (implicit) bijection towards a reference cutting configuration: on the one hand, one identifies the reference cutting configuration corresponding to the tetrahedron to be cut, on the other hand, one turns the tetrahedron “geometrically”, in order to “superimpose” it on this reference cutting configuration (for example r7.02.12-fig-3-15). In short, one identifies the nodes of the sub-tetrahedron with the nodes of the reference cutting element (this is the direct mapping of the bijection). One then “condenses” the multiple cutting possibilities, linked to the different possibilities of ordering of the edges and of the intersection points.

Subsequently, this operation facilitates the ordering of the mid-side nodes of the quadratic sub-tetrahedra for the computer storage. Indeed, the ordering of the new mid-side nodes no longer depends on the faces or on the edges where they are computed, but on a single reference cutting configuration. Consequently, the new mid-side nodes computed are necessarily ordered in the same manner, in the reference cutting sub-tetrahedron. Then, these mid-side nodes are positioned on the edges of the tetrahedron by the inverse mapping (of the identification of the nodes above). The bijection used is therefore tacit.

The procedures of ordering and of computation of the mid-side points are made explicit below.

#### 3.3.4 Subdivision of a tetrahedron into sub-tetrahedra

The reference tetrahedron is defined on r7.02.12-fig-3-20. One determines the intersection points $Pi$ between the surface ${\mathrm{lsn}}^{h}=0$ and the edges of the tetrahedron.

Let $n$ be the number of intersection points $Pi$ .

With each intersection point $Pi$ , one associates two integers: $\mathit{Ai}$ and $\mathit{NSi}$

- $\mathit{Ai}$ is the number of the edge on which $Pi$ is found (for example if $Pi$ is found on the edge of ends $\mathit{N2}-\mathit{N3}$ , then $\mathit{Ai}=4$ ). In the case where $Pi$ coincides with a vertex node of the tetrahedron, Ai is equal to 0,
- $\mathit{NSi}$ is the number of the vertex node in the case where $Pi$ coincides with a vertex node of the tetrahedron (for example if $Pi$ coincides with $\mathit{N3}$ , then $\mathit{NSi}=3$ ). In the case where $Pi$ is found on an edge, $\mathit{NS}$ is equal to 0.

**Note**

The product of $\mathit{Ai}$ by $\mathit{NSi}$ is always equal to 0.

The intersection points are then sorted in the increasing order of the $\mathit{Ai}$ . The intersection points coinciding with vertex nodes will therefore be found at the beginning of the list.

#### 3.3.5 Multi-cutting

When one wishes to model junctions, intersections or simply that two cracks are close enough to cut the same element, one must be able to divide the element into domains which respect all the discontinuities introduced.

The strategy retained consists in cutting the element several times sequentially. It is a strategy which has the merit of being fairly quick to implement, because the cutting of a reference tetrahedron by a crack generates sub-tetrahedra which can in their turn be considered as reference tetrahedra for the cutting by the following crack.

The problem is that one does not optimise the total number of sub-elements generated, which risks being very high if one recuts more than 3 times in 3D. In order to solve this problem, one would have to be able to cut directly any type of element (hexahedron, pentahedron, pyramid, tetrahedron) into a combination of all these types of elements. This requires fairly heavy developments and is therefore not conceivable. Another strategy would consist in grouping the sub-elements by zone and in finding the optimal number of Gauss points (and the weights) per zone. One would no longer store the sub-elements but directly the zones with the associated Gauss points.

*Example of multi-cutting in 2D*

#### 3.3.6 Maximum number of sub-elements

In order to dimension correctly the data structures relating to the subdivision, one must determine the maximum number of sub-elements generated by the subdivision phase, according to the type of the initial mesh cell.

One considers in this paragraph that the element is cut by only a single crack.

##### 3.3.6.1 Case of the tetrahedron

The case generating the largest number of sub-elements is the one described at r7.02.12-quatre-points-intersection, which leads to 6 sub-elements.

##### 3.3.6.2 Case of the pentahedron

A pentahedron being beforehand divided into three tetrahedra, one might think that the largest number of sub-elements generated is the one where each of these three tetrahedra is in its turn subdivided into 6 sub-elements; which would lead to a final cutting into 18 sub-elements. However, such a case of figure is impossible. At most, of the three tetrahedra, two will be cut into 6 sub-elements and only one will be cut into 4 sub-elements, leading to 16 sub-elements.

##### 3.3.6.3 Case of the hexahedron

As previously, the case of figure where all six tetrahedra are each subdivided into 6 sub-elements is impossible. The maximum case is the one which corresponds to the case mentioned in the preceding paragraph: the two pentahedra deduced from the hexahedron (see r7.02.12-fig-3-14) are each subdivided into 16 sub-elements. The number of sub-elements is therefore $(6+6+4)+(6+6+4)=32$ .

##### 3.3.6.4 Case of the multi-cutting

An XFEM finite element can be cut by 4 cracks at most in *code_aster.* The maximum number of sub-elements generated is then difficult to evaluate and an upper bound of the maximum number of sub-elements would be too high. Indeed, if one takes the maximum number of sub-elements generated by a single crack, namely 32, and if one considers that the 32 tetrahedra can give rise to 6 tetrahedra for each of the following cracks, one obtains $32\ast \mathrm{6³}=6912$ . This number of integration sub-elements for a hexahedron is quite unattainable because it goes without saying that the tetrahedra arising from the 1st cutting will not all be cut by the following cracks, but it is difficult to exhibit a better upper bound. One chooses to fix the maximum number of sub-elements for the multi-cracked elements at 4 times the maximum number of sub-elements for a single crack, namely $32\ast 4=128$ in 3D and $4\ast 6=24$ in 2D. These numbers can easily be exceeded for elements cut by 3 or 4 cracks. In this case, the cutting procedure returns an error and the user is invited to refine the mesh used in order to avoid finite elements being streaked with cracks, which would moreover lead to important conditioning problems (see r7.02.12-conditionnement-enrichissement).

If the triangle is quadratic, one determines the mid-side points of the edge of the 2nd point $\mathit{Q1}$ and $\mathit{Q2}$ and that of the crack $\mathit{Q3}$ .

One obtains the triangles $\mathit{N1}\mathit{P1}\mathit{P2}$ , $\mathit{P1}\mathit{P2}\mathit{N3}$ and $\mathit{P1}\mathit{N2}\mathit{N3}$ . $\mathit{N1}$ being the point close to the 2 intersection points according to the sorting carried out. If the triangle is quadratic, one determines the mid-side points of the edge of the 1st point $\mathit{Q1}$ and $\mathit{Q2}$ , those of the 2nd point $\mathit{Q3}$ and $\mathit{Q4}$ , that of the crack $\mathit{Q5}$ and that of the new edge $\mathit{Q6}$ .

#### 3.3.8 Subdivision algorithms

In r7.02.12-phase-prealable-decoupe-hexaedres to r7.02.12-sous-decoupage-2d, we have explained the geometrical cutting of the elements into integration sub-cells (Tetra10 in 3D, Tri6 in 2D), without explaining how the coordinates of the intersection points and of the new mid-side nodes are computed in the virtual mesh. In this paragraph, we shall therefore describe the algorithms for the computation of the coordinates of the new points, generated by the cutting.

For each type of points, there exists a specific positioning algorithm:

- The positioning of the intersection points makes it possible to delimit an explicit boundary, although the interface/crack is defined by an implicit equation ${\mathrm{lsn}}^{h}=0$ . Precisely, the locus of the intersection points is computed by taking the intersection between the edges of the mesh and the zero iso-value of the level-set. The intersection points are then stored in an auxiliary data structure, in completion of the points of the mesh [d4.10.02](doc:d4.10.02).
- The positioning of the mid-side points makes it possible to improve the description of a curved level-set, in the case of quadratic elements. In the presence of elements with curved edges, the curvature of the edges of the quadratic element is preserved, by positioning mid-side nodes rigorously on the edges during the cutting. On the other hand, a cutting with elements with straight edges would alter the curvature of the original element. In order to capture the curvature of the level-set and of the curved edges of quadratic elements, one therefore computes several types of mid-side points (see r7.02.12-calcul-points-milieu). Several algorithms are implemented, in order to capture the different types of mid-side points conceivable. These new mid-side points are then stored in an auxiliary data structure, in completion of the points of the mesh [d4.10.02](doc:d4.10.02).

Subsequently, one adopts the following notation:

- $\xi ={\left({\xi}_{i}\right)}_{i\in [1,\mathit{dim}]}$ designates the reference coordinates in the parent element. These coordinates generally vary between $\left[0,1\right]$ or between $\left[-1,1\right]$ . Let us recall that the shape functions or interpolation polynomials are parameterised in Aster, thanks to the reference coordinates [r3.01.01](doc:r3.01.01),
- $X={\left({X}_{i}\right)}_{i\in [1,\mathit{dim}]}$ designates the coordinates of a point in the real space. If the current point is a node, these are the geometrical coordinates of the node in the mesh. If the current point is not a node, these are the geometrical coordinates interpolated in the parent element $X(\xi )=\sum_{1⩽j⩽\mathit{nnop}}{X}^{j}{\Phi}_{j}(\xi )$ .

##### 3.3.8.1 Computation of the intersection points

1. Moës proposes to pose the problem of the search for the intersection points in the parent reference space. In short, in order to compute the real coordinates of an intersection point, one first searches for the coordinates in the reference space $\xi$ . One then deduces the real coordinates thanks to the interpolation of the geometrical coordinates in the parent element.

In order to compute the intersection point $P$ between an edge $\mathit{AB}$ and the zero iso-value, one solves the following problem:

$\begin{array}{c}P\in \mathit{AB}\\ {\mathit{lsn}}^{h}\left(P\right)=0\end{array}$

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/10000000000002D9000002B580288E4465E9E80B.jpg)

*Search for the intersection point ${I}_{1}$ on the edge ${I}_{2}{I}_{3}$ which is curved in the reference space. One then takes into account the curvature of ${I}_{2}{I}_{3}$ with the mid-side node ${M}_{2}$*

*Exception:* For the multi-cracked quadratic elements, in the parent reference element, the edge $\mathit{AB}$ is not necessarily straight. The mid-side node $M$ of the edge $\mathit{AB}$ is not necessarily on the segment $[A,B]$ if the side $\mathit{AB}$ is the side of an integration sub-element generated by the cutting in conformity with a first crack (see r7.02.12-fig-3-41). One must then take into account the possible curvature of the edge; one thus has $P\in \mathit{AB}\iff {\xi}_{P}=(\mathrm{2t}-1)(t-1){\xi}_{A}+t(\mathrm{2t}-1){\xi}_{B}+\mathrm{4t}(1-t){\xi}_{M}$ with $t\in [0,1]$ .

Consequently, the problem posed amounts to searching for $t\in [0,1]$ such that ${\mathit{lsn}}^{h}\left({\xi}_{P}=(\mathrm{2t}-1)(t-1){\xi}_{A}+t(\mathrm{2t}-1){\xi}_{B}+\mathrm{4t}(1-t){\xi}_{M}\right)=0$ .

For the linear modellings or the single-crack quadratic modellings, the point $P$ necessarily belongs to the edge $[A,B]$ and therefore: ${\xi}_{P}=(1-t){\xi}_{A}+t{\xi}_{B}$ with $t\in [0,1]$ .

Consequently, the problem posed amounts to searching for $t\in [0,1]$ such that ${\mathit{lsn}}^{h}\left({\xi}_{P}=(1-t){\xi}_{A}+t{\xi}_{B}\right)=0$ .

One then seeks the root of a polynomial equation since the interpolation functions of the level-set are polynomials. This equation is then solved by means of the Newton-Raphson algorithm (detailed below).

As $f:t\to {\mathit{lsn}}^{h}(t)$ is ${C}^{2}$ and strictly monotone in the neighbourhood of the intersection point (otherwise, the level-set becomes parallel to the edge $\mathit{AB}$ and does not intersect it), the convergence of the Newton algorithm is **quadratic**, therefore optimal.

**Newton-Raphson algorithm for the computation of an intersection point of the zero iso-value with the edge** $\mathit{AB}$ **:**

- Reading of the reference coordinates of the vertex nodes $A$ and $B$ and of the possible mid-side node $M$ : by construction, these nodes are nodes of the child element that one is cutting and their coordinates are therefore known.
- Initialisation of the Newton-Raphson:

  - For the linear modellings, one initialises the search with the linear approximation along the edge:

${t}_{0}=\frac{\mathit{lsn}(A)}{\mathit{lsn}(A)-\mathit{lsn}(B)}$

  - For the quadratic modellings, one initialises the search with the exact solution of the problem along the edge of the child element. This solution is given by the solution of a polynomial equation of the second degree. The coefficients of this polynomial of the second degree are given by the values of $\mathit{lsn}$ at the end nodes and at the mid-side node of the edge of the child sub-element. Given that the $\mathit{lsn}$ changes sign strictly between the ends of this edge, there exists a unique solution of the equation $\mathit{lsn}=0$ on the edge. The equation to be solved has the following form:

$\mathit{lsn}(A)+(4\ast \mathit{lsn}(M)-3\ast \mathit{lsn}(A)-\mathit{lsn}(B))\ast {t}_{0}+2\ast (\mathit{lsn}(A)+\mathit{lsn}(B)-2\ast \mathit{lsn}(M))\ast {t}_{0}\mathrm{²}=0$

Among the two possible solutions of this equation of the second degree one retains only the one which satisfies: ${t}_{0}\in [0,1]$ . This value constitutes a good initialisation and it is even the final solution of the problem when the edge of the child element on which one carries out the search coincides with an edge of the parent element. Indeed, the l $\mathit{lsn}$ is then interpolated only by the end nodes and the mid-side node of the edge of the parent element. This interpolation being quadratic, the initialisation provides the exact solution. On the other hand, when the edge of the child element does not coincide with an edge of the parent element (internal edge r7.02.12-fig-3-42), the initialisation does not provide the exact solution of the problem because the $\mathit{lsn}$ is interpolated by all the nodes of the parent element along the edge of the child element.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/10000000000004C0000003A4D57C30E0A28D4D76.jpg)

*Intersection of the curve *lsn=0* with an edge of the child element*

- While $n⩽\mathit{itermax}$ do:

  - ${\xi}_{n}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}(1-{t}_{n}){\xi}_{A}+{t}_{n}{\xi}_{B}\text{}\mathit{en}\mathit{linéaire}\mathit{et}\mathit{mono}-\mathit{fissuré}\\ ({\mathrm{2t}}_{n}-1)({t}_{n}-1){\xi}_{A}+{t}_{n}({\mathrm{2t}}_{n}-1){\xi}_{B}+{\mathrm{4t}}_{n}(1-{t}_{n}){\xi}_{M}\text{}\mathit{en}\mathit{quadratique}\mathit{multi}-\mathit{fissuré}\end{array}\right.$
  - Computation of the function: ${\mathit{lsn}}^{h}({\xi}_{n})=\sum_{1⩽j⩽\mathit{nnop}}{\mathit{lsn}}_{j}{\Phi}_{j}({\xi}_{n})$
  - Computation of the derivative: ${\mathit{dlsn}}^{h}({\xi}_{n})/\mathit{dt}=\sum_{1⩽j⩽\mathit{nnop}}{\mathit{lsn}}_{j}\nabla {\Phi}_{j}({\xi}_{n})\cdot \vec{v}$
  - Verification of the slope: if $∣{\mathit{dlsn}}^{h}({\xi}_{n})/\mathit{dt}∣<{10}^{-16}$ then exit with error code.
  - Computation of the increment: ${\Delta}_{n}=\frac{{\mathit{lsn}}^{h}({\xi}_{n})}{{\mathit{dlsn}}^{h}({\xi}_{n})/\mathit{dt}}$
  - Computation of the new position: ${t}_{n+1}={t}_{n}-{\Delta}_{n}$
  - Verification of the assumption $\mathit{AB}$ : if ${t}_{n}\notin \left[0,1\right]$ then exit with error code.
  - Stopping criterion on the increment: if $∣{\Delta}_{n}∣<{10}^{-8}$ then end of the loop.
- Computation of the reference coordinates of the intersection point: ${\xi}_{\infty}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}(1-{t}_{\infty}){\xi}_{A}+{t}_{\infty}{\xi}_{B}\text{}\mathit{en}\mathit{linéaire}\mathit{et}\mathit{mono}-\mathit{fissuré}\\ ({\mathrm{2t}}_{\infty}-1)({t}_{\infty}-1){\xi}_{A}+{t}_{\infty}({\mathrm{2t}}_{\infty}-1){\xi}_{B}+{\mathrm{4t}}_{\infty}(1-{t}_{\infty}){\xi}_{M}\text{}\mathit{en}\mathit{quadratique}\mathit{multi}-\mathit{fissuré}\end{array}\right.$
- Verification of the assumption $P\in \mathit{ELREFP}$ : if ${\xi}_{\infty}\notin \mathit{ELREFP}$ then exit with error code.
- Computation of the real coordinates by interpolation of the geometrical coordinates:

$X({\xi}_{\infty})=\sum_{1⩽j⩽\mathit{nnop}}{X}^{j}{\Phi}_{j}({\xi}_{\infty})$

**Note on the stopping criterion:**

*One tests the increment* ${\Delta}_{n}$ *in order to evaluate the convergence of the Newton method, because the solution and the increment vary on the scale of unity. The value of the increment relative to unity is therefore a robust indicator of the convergence. If the stopping criterion bore on the value of the function* ${\mathit{lsn}}^{h}({\xi}_{n})$ *, one would have to scale the result.*

*Very concretely, when the increment tends towards zero relative to unity, the solution* ${t}_{n+1}={t}_{n}-{\Delta}_{n}$ *becomes stationary relative to unity. From a numerical point of view, this guarantees the convergence on the interval* $[0,1]$ *, given the machine precision.*

By way of example, let us now consider the interpolation of a level-set in the quadratic element (QUAD8) of r7.02.12-approximation-level-sets. Let us envisage an arbitrary segment $\mathit{AB}$ in the parent element (see r7.02.12-fig-3-43). By plotting the evolution of the interpolated level-set $f:t\to {\mathit{lsn}}^{h}(t)$ along the segment $A-B$ (see r7.02.12-fig-3-44), one observes that the level-set varies in a strictly monotone manner in the neighbourhood of the intersection point $P$ . As long as one remains in the neighbourhood of the point $P$ , the Newton algorithm will respect the conditions of optimal convergence.

The Newton algorithm proposed above is initialised by a linear interpolation of the level-set along the segment $A-B$ r7.02.12-fig-3-45. The initialisation point ${P}_{0}$ is then sufficiently close to the convergence point $P$ . The Newton algorithm will have an optimal behaviour.

On the other hand, if the algorithm had been initialised at the point $A$ , the proximity of a stationarity point (where the first derivative of $f:t\to {\mathit{lsn}}^{h}(t)$ vanishes) would have caused the failure of the Newton method. Several scenarios would be conceivable:

- either the Newton method would not converge after a given maximum number of iterations,
- or the Newton method would leave the segment $A-B$ .

The presence of the stationarity point has a geometrical justification: the zero iso-value of the level-set is practically parallel to the segment $A-B$ in the neighbourhood of the point $A$ (see r7.02.12-fig-3-43). Subsequently, the distance between the segment $A-B$ and the zero iso-value decreases progressively. Consequently, in the neighbourhood of the intersection point the variation of the level-set becomes strictly monotone again.

In order to prevent this configuration, the Newton method is initialised sufficiently close to the intersection point r7.02.12-fig-3-45. Moreover, at each iteration of the algorithm, one controls the persistence of the solution on the segment $A-B$ that is to say $\forall t,t\in [0,1]$ . This then brings an additional guarantee on the robustness of the solution computed by the Newton method.

*Search for the intersection point between the zero iso-value of the level-set and an arbitrary segment A-B in a QUAD8 element*

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/10000000000002200000016C7C0275C7B9CB01A5.png)

*Evolution of the distance function (level-set) along the segment A-B*

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/100002010000026200000182FB80E918BA42C582.png)

##### 3.3.8.2 Computation of the mid-side points

During the cutting of a quadratic tetrahedron, the new mid-side points can be grouped according to 3 categories r7.02.12-fig-3-46:

- the mid-side points of the first type ${\mathit{MP}}_{1}$ : situated on the edges of the tetrahedron (triangle), between the intersection points and the vertex nodes,
- the mid-side points of the second type ${\mathit{MP}}_{2}$ : situated on the zero iso-value, in the quadratic face (SE3, TRI6 or QUAD8) delimited by the intersection points. The mid-side points on the edges of the face and the mid-side point at the centre of the face (if quadrangle face) are computed with slightly different procedures,
- the mid-side points of the third type ${\mathit{MP}}_{3}$ : situated on the quadrangle faces of the child sub-polyhedra (pentahedron or pyramid), generated by the cutting of the tetrahedron.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/100002010000037D00000365B9D7BF0EBFEBB781.png)

*The different types of mid-side nodes ${\mathit{MP}}_{i}$ to be computed during the cutting of the quadratic elements*

**Definition of the mid-side points**

One proposes to define systematically the mid-side points in the reference frame of the parent element. As the reference element has straight edges and plane faces, this greatly facilitates the computation of the mid-side points, in particular the mid-side nodes of the first type. Let us emphasise further this essential point: the computations to come will often rely implicitly on the assumption that the faces of the tetrahedra to be cut are plane in the parent reference element.

However, with this definition, it is possible to compute a mid-side node which is not “in the middle” of the edge in the real coordinate system. This is not troublesome, provided that one chooses a suitable integration technique.

In the reference element, we propose the following definition of the mid-side points:

- for a mid-side point of the **first type**, situated between the vertex node $\mathit{XX}$ and the intersection point $\mathit{IP}$ : ${\xi}_{{\mathit{PM}}_{1}}={\xi}_{\mathit{Milieu}(\mathit{XX}-\mathit{IP})}=\frac{{\xi}_{\mathit{XX}}+{\xi}_{\mathit{IP}}}{2}$

As represented on r7.02.12-fig-3-47.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/100002010000014B00000144CD1929717E3C417E.png)

*Computation in the reference frame of a mid-side point of the second type, situated between 2 intersection points*

*Exception:* For the multi-cracked quadratic elements, in the parent reference element, the intersected edge on which one wishes to position the mid-side points is not necessarily straight. If the intersected edge is the side of an integration sub-element generated by the cutting in conformity with a first crack (see r7.02.12-fig-3-48), one must take into account the possible curvature of the edge in order to position the mid-side points of the first type:

$\begin{array}{c}{\xi}_{{M}_{6}}=\frac{{\xi}_{{I}_{4}}+{\xi}_{{I}_{2}}}{2}\\ {M}_{6}=\frac{{\xi}_{{M}_{6}}({\xi}_{{M}_{6}}-1)}{2}{I}_{2}+\frac{{\xi}_{{M}_{6}}({\xi}_{{M}_{6}}+1)}{2}{I}_{3}+(1-{\xi}_{{M}_{6}})(1+{\xi}_{{M}_{6}}){I}_{4}\end{array}$

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/10000000000002F0000002B21D9F1EB6BFE86B00.jpg)

*Determination of the mid-side points ${M}_{6}$ *and* ${M}_{7}$ on the edge ${I}_{2}{I}_{3}$*

- for a mid-side point of the **second type**, situated between 2 intersection points on a face of the tetrahedron r7.02.12-fig-3-49, one makes the following choice:

$\begin{array}{c}{\xi}_{{\mathit{PM}}_{2}}={\xi}_{M}+t\vec{v}\\ {\mathit{lsn}}^{h}\left({\xi}_{{\mathit{PM}}_{2}}\right)=0\end{array}$

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/100002010000015C000001478FA0FF788ED2CE5E.png)

*Computation in the reference frame of a mid-side point of the second type, situated between 2 intersection points*

$\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\vec{v}\text{}\in \text{}(A,B,C)\\ \vec{v}\text{}\perp \text{}\vec{{\mathit{IP}}_{1}-{\mathit{IP}}_{2}}\\ \parallel \vec{v}\parallel \text{}=\text{}1\end{array}\right\rbrace \Rightarrow \left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\vec{v}\text{}=\text{}{\alpha}_{1}\vec{\mathit{AB}}\text{}+\text{}{\alpha}_{2}\vec{\mathit{AC}}\\ \vec{v}\text{}\cdot \text{}\vec{{\mathit{IP}}_{1}-{\mathit{IP}}_{2}}\text{}=\text{}0\\ \parallel \vec{v}\parallel \text{}=\text{}1\end{array}\right\rbrace$

After solution:

$\vec{v}\text{}=\text{}\pm {\alpha}_{1}\left({k}_{2}\vec{\mathit{AB}}\text{}-\text{}{k}_{1}\vec{\mathit{AC}}\right)$ with $\begin{array}{c}{\alpha}_{1}\text{}=\text{}1/\sqrt{{n}_{1}^{2}{k}_{2}^{2}+{n}_{2}^{2}{k}_{1}^{2}-2k{k}_{1}{k}_{2}}\\ {n}_{1}\text{}=\text{}\parallel \vec{\mathit{AB}}\parallel \text{et}{n}_{2}\text{}=\text{}\parallel \vec{\mathit{AC}}\parallel \\ {k}_{1}=\vec{\mathit{AB}}\text{}\cdot \text{}\vec{{\mathit{IP}}_{1}-{\mathit{IP}}_{2}}\\ {k}_{2}=\vec{\mathit{AC}}\text{}\cdot \text{}\vec{{\mathit{IP}}_{1}-{\mathit{IP}}_{2}}\\ k=\vec{\mathit{AB}}\text{}\cdot \text{}\vec{\mathit{AC}}\end{array}$

*Notes:*

1. *If* ${k}_{2}=0$ *that is to say* $\vec{\mathit{AC}}\text{}\perp \text{}\vec{{\mathit{IP}}_{1}-{\mathit{IP}}_{2}}$ *, then,* $\vec{v}\text{}=\text{}\pm \frac{\vec{\mathit{AC}}}{\parallel \vec{\mathit{AC}}\parallel }$
2. *There are 2 possible unit direction vectors for a given straight line, which explains the 2 solutions computed for the vector* $\vec{v}$ *. One chooses arbitrarily one of the 2 solutions. This choice has no influence on the convergence of the Newton algorithm.*

- for a mid-side point of the **second type**, situated at the centre of a quadrangle face, one makes the following choice:

$\begin{array}{c}{\xi}_{{\mathit{PM}}_{2}}={\xi}_{M}+t\vec{v}\\ {\mathit{lsn}}^{h}\left({\xi}_{{\mathit{PM}}_{2}}\right)=0\end{array}$

- $M$ is the mid-side point of the edge $\left[{\mathit{IP}}_{1}-{\mathit{IP}}_{4}\right]$ that is to say ${\xi}_{M}=\frac{{\xi}_{\mathit{IP1}}+{\xi}_{\mathit{IP4}}}{2}$ . If the zero iso-value is plane the cutting then generates sub-tetrahedra with straight edges.
- $\vec{v}$ is the gradient of the lsn at the point M: $\vec{v}=\vec{{\mathit{grad}}_{\mathit{lsn}}}(M)$ . It is the “best” search direction because the gradient of the lsn is by definition orthogonal to the surface $\mathit{lsn}=0$ that one seeks to reach.

- **Computation of the mid-side points of the second type:**

> The mid-side points of the second type satisfy the equation of intersection of a straight line with the zero iso-value following:
> $\begin{array}{c}{\xi}_{{\mathit{PM}}_{2}}={\xi}_{M}+t\vec{v}\\ {\mathit{lsn}}^{h}\left({\xi}_{{\mathit{PM}}_{2}}\right)=0\end{array}$

According to the localisation of the mid-side point (either situated between 2 intersection points, or situated inside the quadrangle face on the zero iso-value), we have made explicit the exact coordinates of ${\xi}_{M}$ and $\vec{v}$ in the reference element.

One solves the search for the zero of the equation ${\mathit{lsn}}^{h}\left({\xi}_{M}+t\vec{v}\right)=0$ by a Newton method as at r7.02.12-approximation-level-sets. In order to make sure that the Newton algorithm “remains” respectively in the TRIA face for a mid-side point of the second type situated between two intersection points and in the TETRA sub-element for a mid-side point of the second type situated at the centre of a quadrangle face, one reinforces the Newton algorithm by the Dekker method. If an iteration of the Newton algorithm sends us outside the authorised search zone, a dichotomy will be preferred in order to approach the solution. Indeed, it happens that the equation $\begin{array}{c}{\xi}_{{\mathit{PM}}_{2}}={\xi}_{M}+t\vec{v}\\ {\mathit{lsn}}^{h}\left({\xi}_{{\mathit{PM}}_{2}}\right)=0\end{array}$ possesses several solutions, but there is only one which is in the TRIA face of the search or in the TETRA sub-element of the search. This solution may prove difficult to capture by the Newton algorithm, especially when the curve $\mathit{lsn}=0$ has a significant curvature or “grazes” an edge of the TRIA face or a face of the TETRA sub-element (see r7.02.12-fig-3-51 and r7.02.12-fig-3-52).

**Newton-Raphson algorithm for the computation of a mid-side point situated on the zero iso-value of the level-set:**

- Definition of ${\xi}_{M}$ the starting point of the search and of $\vec{v}$ the direction of the search.
- Initialisation of the Newton-Raphson: ${t}_{0}=0$
- Determination of the lower and upper bounds ${t}_{min}$ and ${t}_{max}$ of the search space (see r7.02.12-fig-3-53 and r7.02.12-fig-3-54)
- While $n⩽\mathit{itermax}$ do:

- ${\xi}_{n}={t}_{n}\vec{v}+{\xi}_{M}$
- Computation of the function: ${\mathit{lsn}}^{h}({\xi}_{n})=\sum_{1⩽j⩽\mathit{nnop}}{\mathit{lsn}}_{j}{\Phi}_{j}({\xi}_{n})$
- Computation of the derivative: ${\mathit{dlsn}}^{h}({\xi}_{n})/\mathit{dt}=\sum_{1⩽j⩽\mathit{nnop}}{\mathit{lsn}}_{j}\nabla {\Phi}_{j}({\xi}_{n})\cdot \vec{v}$
- Verification of the slope: if $∣{\mathit{dlsn}}^{h}({\xi}_{n})/\mathit{dt}∣<{10}^{-16}$ then exit with error code.
- Computation of the increment: ${\Delta}_{n}=\frac{{\mathit{lsn}}^{h}({\xi}_{n})}{{\mathit{dlsn}}^{h}({\xi}_{n})/\mathit{dt}}$
- Computation of the new position: ${t}_{n+1}={t}_{n}-{\Delta}_{n}$
- If ${t}_{n+1}\in [{t}_{min},{t}_{max}]$ , then one continues, otherwise:
- If ${t}_{n+1}>{t}_{max}$ , then ${t}_{n+1}=\frac{{t}_{n}+{t}_{max}}{2}$
- If ${t}_{n+1}<{t}_{min}$ , then ${t}_{n+1}=\frac{{t}_{n}+{t}_{min}}{2}$
- Stopping criterion on the increment: if $∣{\Delta}_{n}∣<{10}^{-8}$ then end of the loop.

- Computation of the reference coordinates of the intersection point: ${\xi}_{\infty}={t}_{\infty}\vec{v}+M$
- Verification of the assumption ${\xi}_{\infty}\in \mathit{ELREFP}$ : if ${\xi}_{\infty}\notin \mathit{ELREFP}$ then exit with error code.
- Computation of the real coordinates by interpolation of the geometrical coordinates:

$X({\xi}_{\infty})=\sum_{1⩽j⩽\mathit{nnop}}{X}^{j}{\Phi}_{j}({\xi}_{\infty})$

For the non-simplicial elements, there exists a situation for which the search for the mid-side point of type 2 is vain in the authorised search space (between the bounds $inf$ and $\sup$ ). As indicated at r7.02.12-phase-prealable-decoupe-hexaedres, the cutting into sub-elements is based on a reduction of the non-simplicial elements into simplicial elements (TRIA in 2D and TETRA in 3D). Now this reduction can lead to situations which make the search for the mid-side points of type 2 impossible within the subdivision of the non-simplicial elements. An example is presented on r7.02.12-fig-3-56 on the left. One searches for the mid-side point of type 2 between the intersection points ${\mathit{IP}}_{1}$ and ${\mathit{IP}}_{2}$ . Now in the authorised search interval (between the bounds $inf$ and $\sup$ ), the field of $\mathit{lsn}$ is of constant sign. The Newton-Raphson algorithm is therefore put into failure. The internal diagonal edge is indeed intersected by the curve $\mathit{lsn}=0$ whereas the latter was detected as not cut because the value of the $\mathit{lsn}$ at each of its 3 nodes is strictly of the same sign. But unlike an edge of the parent element, the field $\mathit{lsn}$ along this internal edge depends on the value of the $\mathit{lsn}$ at each of the 8 nodes of the parent element QUAD8, and not only on the value of the $\mathit{lsn}$ at its 3 nodes. That is why the field of $\mathit{lsn}$ changes sign along this internal edge. This difficulty can be circumvented by choosing another bijection for the division of the quadrangle into two triangles (confer r7.02.12-fig-3-13). On r7.02.12-fig-3-56 on the right, one uses the other bijection for the cutting of the quadrangle into two triangles so as to avoid a change of sign of the field $\mathit{lsn}$ along the internal edge. One then determines the mid-side points of the second type ${M}_{1}$ and ${M}_{2}$ in the authorised intervals. Therefore each time that the search for a mid-side point of the second type in the interval $[inf,\sup]$ is put into failure within a simplicial element, one relaunches the cutting procedure of the element by choosing a different bijection for the division into triangular (or tetrahedral in 3D) sub-cells. ### 3.4 Retrieval of the contact facets

#### 3.4.1 XH elements

The retrieval of the contact facets is useful both for the contact and also for applying a mechanical pressure on the lips of cracks. In order to obtain these facets, one relies on the work of cutting into sub-elements described at r7.02.12-sous-decoupage. Indeed, the topology of the integration sub-elements already contains all the information on the contact facets, which are none other than the sides (in 2D) or the faces (in 3D) of the integration sub-elements which satisfy $\mathit{lsn}=0$ . It therefore suffices simply to traverse the sides or the faces of the integration sub-elements and to select those which coincide with the discontinuity (see ). Now the vertex nodes of the integration sub-elements which are intersection points are identified by their connectivity comprised between 1000 and 1999. In the case of an intersection point coinciding with a node of the parent element, one will have directly $\mathit{lsn}=0$ for this node.

The facets that one retrieves are SEG2 in 2D linear, SEG3 in 2D quadratic, TRIA3 in 3D linear and TRIA6 in 3D quadratic. In order to retrieve the facets only once and once only, one carries out the search only on the sides or the faces of the integration sub-elements such that $\mathit{lsn}<0$ .

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/10000000000001E9000001935D558D2EF83BC516.png)

*Integration sub-elements and contact facets in a QUAD8*

In the example above, the QUAD8 is divided into 6 TRIA6. One carries out the search for the contact facets on the sub-elements 2, 3 and 4 which satisfy $\mathit{lsn}<0$ . One then retrieves two SEG3 (in red).

**Algorithm for the retrieval of the contact facets for the XH elements:**

- Loop on the $\mathit{nse}$ sub-elements of the parent XH element

  - If the sub-element satisfies $\mathit{lsn}<0$ , then

    - Loop on the sides (in 2D) or the faces (in 3D) of the sub-element

      - Loop on the vertex nodes of the current side or face

- if the connectivity of the node is comprised between 1000 and 1999, it is an intersection point with the discontinuity, one selects it.
- if the connectivity of the vertex node is strictly less than 1000, then it is a vertex node of the parent element, one selects it only if it satisfies $\mathit{lsn}=0$ .

      - End of the loop on the vertex nodes of the current face
      - If all the vertex nodes of the current side or face have been selected, it is a contact facet, of which one stores the connectivity and the coordinates of its nodes
    - End of the loop on the sides (in 2D) or the faces (in 3D) of the sub-element
  - End of the condition
- End of the loop on the $\mathit{nse}$ sub-elements of the parent XH element

#### 3.4.2 XHT and XT elements

When the element is XHT or XT, an additional piece of work is necessary relative to the XH elements. Indeed, the cutting into sub-elements is carried out in conformity with the $\mathit{lsn}$ , but not in conformity with the $\mathit{lst}$ . The contact facets that one retrieves therefore indeed satisfy $\mathit{lsn}=0$ , but not necessarily $\mathit{lst}<0$ , because the XHT and XT elements are liable to contain the crack front. A recutting in conformity with the $\mathit{lst}$ is then necessary.

Once the contact facets such that $\mathit{lsn}=0$ have been obtained, one recuts them if necessary according to the curve $\mathit{lst}=0$ (see )

In order to do this, one uses exactly the same approach as during the cutting of the principal elements into integration sub-elements (cutting in conformity with the surface $\mathit{lsn}=0$ ). In the example below, the SEG2 on the right is cut along the curve $\mathit{lst}=0$ .

#### 3.4.3 The multi-Heaviside elements

For the multi-cracked elements, the procedure is the same. Indeed, the cutting into sub-elements is carried out sequentially, crack after crack, so that the final integration sub-elements are in conformity with each of the cracks (see ). One then uses, for each crack, the same procedure as for the XH elements. However, for the cracks onto which another crack branches, one does not necessarily carry out the search for the contact facets in the elements such that $\mathit{lsn}<0$ . In order to make sure that the contact facets are in conformity with the junction, one carries out the search for the contact facets on the side where the crack branches (see ).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/recuperation_facettes_contact.png)

*Retrieval of the contact facets for a multi-cracked element. The facets of crack 1 are retrieved on the side where crack 2 branches.*

When several cracks branch onto a same crack within an element, if all the branchings are not done on the same side, there do not exist in general contact facets in conformity with each crack (see ). The cutting algorithm then issues an alarm message which invites the user to refine the mesh so as no longer to have multiple junctions within a same element.

There is no recutting necessary because the multi-cracked elements cannot contain a crack front; they are interface elements only.

**Algorithm for the retrieval of the contact facets for the multi-cracked elements:**

- Loop on the $\mathit{nfiss}$ cracks which cross the element

> ${\mathit{signe}}^{\mathit{ref}}=-1$
> If one or several cracks branch onto $\mathit{ifiss}$ , one determines the sign corresponding to the side where the cracks branch ${\mathit{signe}}^{\mathit{ref}}=\mathit{signe}(\mathit{côté})$ . If the cracks do not all branch on the same side, one issues the following alarm message:
> *“Several cracks are branched on either side of a same crack within the same element. The retrieval of contact facets in conformity with each junction risks failing. Refine the mesh so that this situation no longer occurs”*

- Loop on the $\mathit{nse}$ sub-elements of the parent XH element

- If the sub-element satisfies ${\mathit{signe}}^{\mathit{sous}\mathit{élément}}(\mathit{ifiss})={\mathit{signe}}^{\mathit{ref}}$ , then

- Loop on the sides (in 2D) or the faces (in 3D) of the sub-elements

- Loop on the vertex nodes of the current side or face

- if the connectivity of the node is comprised between 1000 and 1999, it is an intersection point with one of the cracks, one preselects it. One selects it definitively if $∣{\mathit{lsn}}^{\mathit{ifiss}}∣<\mathit{loncar}\ast {10}^{-8}$ with $\mathit{loncar}$ the characteristic length of the parent element. This part of the programming will evolve so as to get rid of this criterion. Indeed, two nodes of a same side, of connectivities comprised between 1000 and 1999 realise the minimum of a single level set which it is therefore easy to identify;
- if the connectivity of the vertex node is strictly less than 1000, then it is a vertex node of the parent element, one selects it only if it satisfies ${\mathit{lsn}}^{\mathit{ifiss}}=0$ .

- End of the loop on the vertex nodes of the current face
- If all the vertex nodes of the current side or face have been selected, it is a contact facet for the crack $\mathit{ifiss}$ , of which one stores the connectivity and the coordinates of its nodes

- End of the loop on the sides (in 2D) or the faces (in 3D) of the sub-elements

- End of the condition

- End of the loop on the $\mathit{nse}$ sub-elements of the parent XH element

- End of the loop on the $\mathit{nfiss}$ cracks which cross the element

##### 3.4.4.3 Multi-Heaviside case

In the multi-cracked case, the maximum number of contact facets per crack is higher than the maximum number of contact facets for an element crossed by a single crack. Indeed, the sequential cutting generates more sub-elements for each new crack and therefore more contact facets because the latter rely on the sub-elements. In 2D, one fixes the maximum number of contact facets per crack at 7. A 2D element crossed by 4 cracks can therefore have a total of $4\ast 7=28$ facets.

### 3.5 Integration of the stiffness

#### 3.5.1 Integrand of the mechanical stiffness term

The X-FEM approximation of the displacement field is written in vector form:

$\left\lbrace {u}^{h}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {\varphi}_{i}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{\varphi}_{j}{H}_{j}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{\varphi}_{k}{F}^{1}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{\varphi}_{k}{F}^{2}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{\varphi}_{k}{F}^{3}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{\varphi}_{k}{F}^{4}\right\rbrace \cdot \left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{a}_{i}\\ {b}_{j}\\ {c}_{k}^{1}\\ {c}_{k}^{2}\\ {c}_{k}^{3}\\ {c}_{k}^{4}\end{array}\right\rbrace$

Or again:

$\left\lbrace {u}^{h}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace N\right\rbrace \cdot \left\lbrace u\right\rbrace$

where $\left\lbrace N\right\rbrace$ is the vector of the enriched basis of the shape functions and $\left\lbrace u\right\rbrace$ the vector of the nodal displacement dofs.

The strains are written:

$\left\lbrace \epsilon \right\rbrace =\left[B\right]\cdot \left\lbrace u\right\rbrace$

where $\left[B\right]$ is the matrix of the derivatives of the shape functions

$\left[B\right]=\left\lbrace {\left(\varphi \right)}_{{i}^{\prime }}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{\left({\varphi}_{j}\right)}^{\prime }{H}_{j}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{\left({\varphi}_{k}\right)}^{\prime }{F}^{\alpha}+{\varphi}_{k}{\left({F}^{\alpha}\right)}^{\prime }\right\rbrace ,\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\alpha =1,4$

On an X-FEM finite element occupying a domain ${O}_{e}$ , the expression of the elementary stiffness matrix is (according to the expression of the PVW):

$\left[{K}_{e}\right]={\int}_{{\Omega}_{e}}{\left[B\right]}^{t}\left[D\right]\left[B\right]d{\Omega}_{e}$

One subdivides the integral into a sum of integrals on the sub-elements:

$\left[{K}_{e}\right]=\sum_{\text{sous-éléments}}{\int}_{{\Omega}_{\text{se}}}{\left[B\right]}^{t}\left[D\right]\left[B\right]d{\Omega}_{\text{se}}$

Let us note in passing that the quantities to be integrated do not change, and always refer to the X-FEM finite element ${O}_{e}$ (called “parent element”) and not to the sub-elements ${O}_{\text{se}}$ .

On each sub-element, the integrand is continuous. Indeed, the sub-elements respect the discontinuity of the crack, therefore the function ${H}_{j}\left(x\right)$ is constant there, and the functions ${\phi }_{{i}^{\prime }}$ , ${F}^{\alpha}$ and ${({F}^{\alpha})}^{\prime }$ are continuous there.

#### 3.5.2 Integrand of the geometrical stiffness term

When one places oneself within the framework of large rotations or of large strains, one must take into account the geometrical stiffness. The geometrical stiffness is also useful in the case of the modal analysis.

In the case of large rotations for example, the virtual work of the internal forces can be written on the initial configuration in the form:

$\delta \pi_{int} = \int_{\Omega} ( S(E) : \delta E^{\ast} ) \, d\Omega$

where $E$ and $S$ are the vectors of Green-Lagrange strain and of second Piola-Kirchhoff stress respectively.

The iterative variation of the virtual work is then written:

$\Delta \delta \pi_{int} = \int_{\Omega} \big( \Delta S(E) : \delta E^{\ast} + S(E) : \Delta \delta E^{\ast} \big) \, d\Omega$

and by using the fact that:

$[E]=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{Ñu}\text{+Ñ}{u}^{T}\text{+Ñ}{u}^{T}\mathrm{Ñu})$

$[\Delta E]=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla \Delta u+\nabla \Delta {u}^{T}+\text{}\nabla \Delta {u}^{T}\nabla {u}^{T}+\text{}\nabla {u}^{T}\nabla \Delta u)$

and:

$[\delta \Delta E]=\frac{1}{2}(\text{}\nabla \Delta {u}^{T}\nabla \delta u\text{}+\text{}\nabla \delta {u}^{T}\nabla \Delta u)$

the variation of the virtual work becomes:

$\Delta \delta \pi_{int} = \int_{\Omega} \Big( \Delta S(E) : \delta E^{\ast} + \mathrm{tr}\big( \nabla^{T} u^{\ast} \cdot S \cdot \nabla \delta u \big) \Big) \, d\Omega$

The integral on an X-FEM element of the second term of this equation which is written (in 2D):

$\int_{\Omega_e} \mathrm{tr}\big( \nabla^{T} u^{\ast} \cdot S \cdot \nabla \delta u \big) \, d\Omega_{se} =\sum_{\text{se}} \int_{\Omega_{se}} \sum_{m,n}\Big( \begin{array}{cccccc}a_X^{\ast} & a_Y^{\ast} & b_X^{\ast} & b_Y^{\ast} & c_X^{\alpha \ast} & c_Y^{\alpha \ast}\end{array} \Big)_m\cdot [K_{se}^{\mathrm{geom}}]_{m,n} \cdot\left( \begin{array}{c}\delta a_X \\ \delta a_Y \\ \delta b_X \\ \delta b_Y \\ \delta c_X^{\beta} \\ \delta c_Y^{\beta}\end{array} \right)_n \, d\Omega_{se}$

brings out the geometrical stiffness matrix of the sub-element:

$$
[{K}_{\mathit{se}}^{\mathit{geom}}]_{m,n} =   \left(   \begin{array}{cccccc}   {\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & H{\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & {\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}\big({\phi }_{,j}^{n}{F}^{\beta}+{\phi }_{j}^{n}{F}_{,j}^{\beta}\big) & 0 \\   0 & {\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & H{\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & {\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}\big({\phi }_{,j}^{n}{F}^{\beta}+{\phi }_{j}^{n}{F}_{,j}^{\beta}\big) \\   H{\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & {H}^{2}{\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & H{\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}\big({\phi }_{,j}^{n}{F}^{\beta}+{\phi }_{j}^{n}{F}_{,j}^{\beta}\big) & 0 \\   0 & H{\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & {H}^{2}{\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & H{\phi }_{,i}^{m}{S}_{ij}\big({\phi }_{,j}^{n}{F}^{\beta}+{\phi }_{j}^{n}{F}_{,j}^{\beta}\big) \\   \big({\phi }_{,i}^{m}{F}^{\alpha}+{\phi }_{i}^{m}{F}_{,i}^{\alpha}\big){S}_{ij}{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & \big({\phi }_{,i}^{m}{F}^{\alpha}+{\phi }_{i}^{m}{F}_{,i}^{\alpha}\big){S}_{ij}H{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & \big({\phi }_{,i}^{m}{F}^{\alpha}+{\phi }_{i}^{m}{F}_{,i}^{\alpha}\big){S}_{ij}\big({\phi }_{,j}^{n}{F}^{\beta}+{\phi }_{j}^{n}{F}_{,j}^{\beta}\big) & 0 \\   0 & \big({\phi }_{,i}^{m}{F}^{\alpha}+{\phi }_{i}^{m}{F}_{,i}^{\alpha}\big){S}_{ij}{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & \big({\phi }_{,i}^{m}{F}^{\alpha}+{\phi }_{i}^{m}{F}_{,i}^{\alpha}\big){S}_{ij}H{\phi }_{,j}^{n} & 0 & \big({\phi }_{,i}^{m}{F}^{\alpha}+{\phi }_{i}^{m}{F}_{,i}^{\alpha}\big){S}_{ij}\big({\phi }_{,j}^{n}{F}^{\beta}+{\phi }_{j}^{n}{F}_{,j}^{\beta}\big)   \end{array}   \right)
$$

where $n$ and $m$ correspond to the numbers of the nodes in the parent element.

#### 3.5.3 Computation of the derivatives of the singular functions:

One seeks the derivatives of the singular functions with respect to the global coordinates $(x,y,z)$ . The singular functions are given as a function of the polar coordinates $(r,\theta )$ .

Derivatives of the singular functions in the polar basis $(r,\theta )$ :

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}\frac{\partial {F}^{1}}{\partial r}& \frac{\partial {F}^{1}}{\partial \theta }\\ \frac{\partial {F}^{2}}{\partial r}& \frac{\partial {F}^{2}}{\partial \theta }\\ \frac{\partial {F}^{3}}{\partial r}& \frac{\partial {F}^{3}}{\partial \theta }\\ \frac{\partial {F}^{4}}{\partial r}& \frac{\partial {F}^{4}}{\partial \theta }\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}& \frac{\sqrt{r}}{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\\ \frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}& -\frac{\sqrt{r}}{2}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\\ \frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\theta & \frac{\sqrt{r}}{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\theta +\sqrt{r}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\theta \\ \frac{1}{2\sqrt{r}}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\theta & -\frac{\sqrt{r}}{2}\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\theta +\sqrt{r}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\theta \end{array}\right]$

where ${\left[P\right]}_{\text{Ee}}$ is the passage matrix from the global basis to the local basis, such that

$\begin{array}{cccc}& & \begin{array}{ccc}\phantom{[}{e}_{1}& {e}_{2}& {e}_{3}\phantom{]}\end{array}& \\ {[P]}_{\mathrm{Ee}}& =& \left[\begin{array}{ccc}\times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times \\ \times & \times & \times \end{array}\right]& \begin{array}{c}{E}_{1}\\ {E}_{2}\\ {E}_{3}\end{array}\end{array}$

#### 3.5.4 Integration schemes

##### 3.5.4.1 Integration of the non-singular terms

For the non-singular terms (classical terms and Heaviside enrichment), the functions to be integrated are polynomials. The numerical integration by a Gauss-Legendre scheme on each sub-tetrahedron is then well suited. Let $\text{npg}$ be the number of Gauss points. These points have coordinates in the reference sub-tetrahedron $({s}_{g},{t}_{g},{u}_{g})$ and the associated weight is denoted ${w}_{g}$ . Let $J$ be the Jacobian of the transformation sub-tetrahedron $\to$ reference sub-tetrahedron. Its determinant is constant per sub-element. The classical procedure is written:

${\int}_{{\Omega}_{\mathrm{se}}}fd{\Omega}_{\mathrm{se}}={\int}_{{\Omega}_{\mathrm{se}}^{\mathrm{ref}}}f\mid \det(J)\mid d{\Omega}_{\mathrm{se}}\approx \sum_{g=1}^{\text{npg}}f({s}_{g},{t}_{g},{u}_{g}){w}_{g}\mid \det(J)\mid$

The function $f$ being known only in the frame linked to the reference parent element, it is necessary to express the coordinates of the Gauss point $({s}_{g},{t}_{g},{u}_{g})$ in this frame. First, one transports them into the real frame (simple use of the shape functions of the sub-tetrahedron), then one transports them into the frame linked to the reference parent element (solution of non-linear equations by the Newton-Raphson algorithm):

$({s}_{g},{t}_{g},{u}_{g})\stackrel{{t}_{1}}{\to }({x}_{g},{y}_{g},{z}_{g})\stackrel{{t}_{2}}{\to }({\xi}_{g},{\eta}_{g},{\zeta}_{g})$

**Note** :

*Indeed, one should rather write* ${\int}_{{\Omega}_{\text{se}}^{\text{ref}}}f°{t}_{2}°{t}_{1}({s}_{g},{t}_{g},{u}_{g})\mid \det(J)\mid d{\Omega}_{\text{se}}$ . *The transformation* ${t}_{1}$ *is linear. On the other hand, the transformation* ${t}_{2}$ *is non-linear. It is the inverse of a linear transformation. It is a combination of rational fractions and of square roots in several variables. Because of this, one does not know the effect of* $f°{t}_{2}°{t}_{1}$ *on the maximum order of the monomials in the general case. On the other hand, for a real element with parallel edges (rhombus in 2D),* ${t}_{2}$ *being linear, one considers that* $f°{t}_{2}°{t}_{1}({s}_{g},{t}_{g},{u}_{g})$ *are monomials in* $({s}_{g},{t}_{g},{u}_{g})$ *of the same order as those defined on the reference parent element.*

The shape functions of the iso-parametric elements are monomials of type ${\xi}^{i}{\eta}^{j}{\zeta}^{k}$ with $i+j+k\le p$ (see r7.02.12-tab-3-8). The derivatives of the shape functions are then monomials of the same type with $i+j+k\le p-1$ . The quantities to be integrated are therefore monomials of order $m={(p-1)}^{2}$ . One refers to in order to determine the scheme suited to an exact integration 3 of all the terms. For the elements with non-parallel edges, the integration will then be approximate.

| pentahedron | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| tetrahedron | 1 | 0 | 1 |

##### 3.5.4.2 Integration of the singular terms

The Gauss-Legendre numerical integration schemes were designed for the integration of polynomials. Now the presence of monomials in $1/\sqrt{r}$ coming from the enrichment with the asymptotic fields leads to a very slow convergence of the precision of these schemes: a considerable number of Gauss points is then necessary in order to obtain an acceptable error. When the singularity is found on the boundary of the integration domain, well-chosen successive changes of variables make it possible to reduce the problem to the integration over a unit domain of a non-singular function, polynomial (or quasi-polynomial), on which a classical Gauss-Legendre integration proves effective and rapidly convergent. About ten Gauss points in each direction suffice to reach the limits of the numerical precision of a computer. When the singularity is not found on the boundary, one must cut the domain into sub-domains, in such a way that the singularity is found on the boundaries of the sub-domains. Despite very satisfactory results in terms of convergence of the relative integration error on each term, the implementation effort to be provided for the 3D structures seems to us considerable. All the more so as the difference at the level of the global errors (after solution) between a classical integration with a number of Gauss points that is fairly high but reasonable and a modified singular integration is not really significant.

For the moment, one has put in place the same classical Gauss-Legendre scheme as the one which is used for the integration of the non-singular terms.

### 3.6 Integration of the surface right-hand sides

#### 3.6.1 Integrand of the right-hand side of the surface forces

The right-hand sides due to the surface forces are written in discretised manner:

${\int}_{{\Gamma}^{t}}t\cdot {u}^{\ast }{\mathrm{d\Gamma }}^{t}$

the discretised surface ${\Gamma}^{t}$ corresponds to the faces of the 3D elements. a possible subdivision for these 2D faces is the one corresponding to the trace of the subdivision of the 3D elements. In Aster, one chooses to take the generic 2D subdivision, corresponding to the one described at r7.02.12-sous-decoupage-2d.

As for the integration of the stiffness, one must determine the coordinates of the Gauss points of the reference sub-triangle in the reference parent element.

Let the Gauss point of coordinates $({s}_{g},{t}_{g})$ in the reference sub-triangle. The problem is that the computation environment is 3D. One therefore creates a real 2D frame local to each sub-triangle. Let the sub-triangle $\text{ABC}$ , of normal $n=\frac{\overrightarrow{\text{AB}}\wedge \overrightarrow{\text{AC}}}{\parallel \overrightarrow{\text{AB}}\wedge \overrightarrow{\text{AC}}\parallel }$ . The local basis is written:

${x}_{l}=\frac{\overrightarrow{\text{AB}}}{\parallel \overrightarrow{\text{AB}}\parallel },{y}_{l}=n\wedge {x}_{l}$ .

The Gauss point then has local coordinates:

$({x}_{g}{y}_{g})=(\sum_{i=1}^{3}{\psi}_{i}({s}_{g},{t}_{g}){x}^{i},\sum_{i=1}^{3}{\psi}_{i}({s}_{g},{t}_{g}){y}^{i})$

where ${\psi}_{i}$ are the shape functions of the sub-triangles and $({x}^{i},{y}^{i})$ the coordinates of the vertices of the sub-triangle in the local 2D frame $({x}_{l},{y}_{l})$ .

As for the integration of the stiffness terms, one must now use a Newton-Raphson method in order to determine the coordinates of the Gauss point in the reference frame of the parent element (face element). For this purpose, all the real coordinates are expressed in the real local 2D frame.

- the lip ${\Gamma}^{1}$ is such that the Heaviside function is negative and the polar angle is equal to $-\pi$ ,
- the lip ${\Gamma}^{2}$ is such that the Heaviside function is positive and the polar angle is equal to $\pi$ ,

## 4 Linear thermal analysis with X-FEM

### 4.1 Introduction

The X-FEM method has been introduced into the linear thermal operator in order to be able to carry out chained thermo-mechanical analyses taking the crack into account in the thermal solution: the temperature field is then discontinuous across the crack if no thermal loading is applied on its lips (with a natural condition of zero flux). It is also possible to define a condition of thermal exchange between the lips of the crack by the data of an exchange coefficient.

**Note** :

*The solution of the linear thermal problems with X-FEM relies on an algorithmics almost identical to that implemented for the solution of mechanical problems of cracked structures with X-FEM, detailed in* r7.02.12-probleme-fissuration *. In particular the procedures making it possible:*

- *to enrich the approximation of the field of the nodal unknowns (status of the nodes, status of the mesh cells, cancellation of the degrees of freedom enriched wrongly);*
- *to subdivide the elements crossed by a crack;*
- *the procedures of integration of the matrices and of the elementary vectors on this subdivision*;

*are identical to those which have been previously detailed; they will therefore not be so in this part.*

### 4.2 Restrictions

The set of the modellings and of the types of loading available for the transient linear thermal algorithm in the case of classical models (non X-FEM) has not been integrally carried over for the thermal X-FEM finite elements. All these functionalities of course remain available on the non-enriched part of the model, and are documented in . Forthcoming developments will make it possible to complete progressively the list of the functionalities available to date.

*Type of problems treated*

Solution of linear thermal transients (independence with respect to temperature of the material parameters), for an isotropic material (scalar conductivity). The branching of cracks (multi-Heaviside) is excluded.

*Finite elements available*

For the moment, only the X-FEM elements of the 3D, axisymmetric and plane modellings have been introduced for the following linear support mesh cells:

| *modelling* | *support mesh cells of the principal elements* | *support mesh cells of the boundary elements* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 3D | TETRA4, HEXA8, PENTA6, PYRA5 | TRIA3, QUAD4 |
| plane / axisymmetric | TRIA3, QUAD4 | SEG2 |

There therefore do not exist lumped thermal X-FEM elements, and the mesh from which one defines the enriched model is obligatorily linear.

*Thermal loadings available on the enriched part of the model*

For the moment, the only loading available on the enriched part of the model is the condition of thermal exchange between the lips of the crack. No other boundary condition can be imposed on boundary thermal X-FEM elements, or on nodes belonging to thermal X-FEM elements. Likewise, no source term can be imposed on principal thermal X-FEM elements. The user must therefore make sure of applying his loadings sufficiently “far” from the crack, that is to say on non-enriched elements (or nodes whose support is formed only of non-enriched elements).

### 4.3 Problem treated

In order not to weigh down the expression of the variational formulation, one considers the simplified problem of a cracked solid, constituted of a linear and isotropic material, of which a part of the boundary (sufficiently far from the crack) is subjected to an imposed temperature. The rest of the boundary (crack excluded) is subjected to a condition of zero flux. Finally, one imposes a condition of thermal exchange between the lips of the crack.

One therefore considers a crack ${\Gamma}^{c}$ in a domain $\Omega \subset {ℝ}^{{n}_{\mathit{dim}}}$ , with ${n}_{\mathit{dim}}=2\text{ou}3$ . One denotes $n$ the outward normal to its boundary $\partial \Omega$ , ${\Gamma}_{1}^{c}$ and ${\Gamma}_{2}^{c}$ the lips of the crack of respective outward normals ${n}_{1}$ and ${n}_{2}$ , and ${\Gamma}^{d}$ the part of $\partial \Omega$ subjected to an imposed temperature (see Figure ). One denotes $x$ the space variable, $t$ the time, and $T$ the temperature. The interval $\left]{t}_{0},{t}_{f}\right]$ corresponds to the time interval considered for the solution of the problem.

denoting ${T}_{1}={T}_{\text{|}{\Gamma}_{1}^{c}}$ , ${T}_{2}={T}_{\text{|}{\Gamma}_{2}^{c}}$ , and $h$ the coefficient of thermal exchange between the lips of the crack, the boundary conditions are written:

${T}^{+}=T(x,{t}_{n+1})$ , and ${T}^{-}=T(x,{t}_{n})$

${V}^{+}=\left\lbrace v\in {H}^{1}(\Omega ),{v}_{\text{|}{\Gamma}^{d}}=\stackrel{ˉ}{{T}^{+}}\right\rbrace$ and ${V}^{-}=\left\lbrace v\in {H}^{1}(\Omega ),{v}_{\text{|}{\Gamma}^{d}}=\stackrel{ˉ}{{T}^{-}}\right\rbrace$

### 4.4 X-FEM approximation of the temperature field

One uses the notations previously adopted for the mechanics at r7.02.12-enrichissement-approximation-deplacement. One recalls the classical finite-element approximation:

${T}^{h}(x)=\sum_{i\in {N}_{n}(x)}{T}_{i}{\mathrm{\phi }}_{i}(x)$

The enriched approximation is written for its part:

This expression is composed of 3 terms. The 1st term is the classical term where ${T}_{i}^{C}$ designate the classical degrees of freedom, the second term is the term enriched by the domain-selection function ${H}_{j}$ with ${T}_{j}^{H}$ the corresponding enriched degrees of freedom, and the third term is the term enriched by the singular function ${F}^{1}$ (“Crack-Tip” enrichment) with ${T}_{k}^{\mathit{CT}}$ the corresponding enriched degrees of freedom.

One assumes that the domain is partitioned $\Omega ` par l'interface de la fissure de la fissure :math:`{\Gamma}^{c}$ into two domains ${\Omega}_{-}$ on the side of ${\Gamma}_{1}^{c}$ and ${\Omega}_{+}$ on the side of ${\Gamma}_{2}^{c}$ .

$\mathit{Si}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{x}_{j}\in {\Omega}_{+},\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{H}_{j}(x)=\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}0\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\text{si}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}x\in {\Omega}_{+}\\ -2\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\text{si}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}x\notin {\Omega}_{+}\end{array}$ $\mathit{Si}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{x}_{j}\in {\Omega}_{-},\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}{H}_{j}(x)=\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}0\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\text{si}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}x\in {\Omega}_{-}\\ +2\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}\text{si}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}x\notin {\Omega}_{-}\end{array}$

## 5 Energy computation of the Stress Intensity Factors

This part recalls the $G$ -theta method used in *Code_Aster* for the computation of the energy release rate in linear fracture mechanics, following a classical finite-element analysis (in 2D or 3D). This method is also used for the computation of the stress intensity factors, by using the bilinear form of $g$ but only in 2D. Because in order to be able to use it in 3D, one must know the local basis at the crack front in order to express the asymptotic fields as a function of the polar coordinates $(r,\theta )$ .

Then, one presents the contribution of the level sets in classical finite elements, which has been to be able to construct a local basis at the crack front in 3D. As one has seen at r7.02.12-base-locale-fissure, the gradients of the level sets give a local basis at the crack front. Moreover, the polar coordinates in this local basis are expressed easily as a function of the level sets (see r7.02.12-fig-3-8). Thus thanks to the level sets, the $G$ -theta method has been applicable to the computation of the stress intensity factors in 3D with classical finite elements. In this context, the crack is meshed, a classical analysis is carried out. In post-processing, one determines the two level set fields from the meshed crack and one deduces from them a discretisation of the crack front. The theta field is constructed from this discretised crack front, and the $G$ -theta method is applied.

The last point is the computation of the energy release rate and of the stress intensity factors, following an X-FEM analysis. In this case, the crack is not meshed, but the procedure followed is almost the same: the discretisation of the crack front is determined from the level sets, a theta field is constructed and the $G$ -theta is applied. The only difference is situated at the level of the numerical integrations: with X-FEM, the numerical integrations require a subdivision of the elements to be integrated. In fact, one uses the subdivision carried out for the computation of the tangent matrices and of the right-hand sides.

### 5.1 G-theta method for the computation of G

#### 5.1.1 Equilibrium relations

In linear elasticity, one defines the free-energy density $\Psi$ , in the absence of initial strains and stresses, by a positive definite quadratic form of the components of the strain tensor:

$\Psi (\varepsilon ,T)=\frac{1}{2}(\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}):C:(\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{\text{th}})$

$C$ is the Hooke tensor (tensor of the 4th order), and the two dots designate the tensor product contracted on two indices.

The stress tensor $\sigma$ derives from the potential $\Psi$ in order to give the state law (or constitutive law of the material):

$\sigma =\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial \varepsilon }(\varepsilon ,T)=C:(\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{\text{th}})$

The equilibrium relations in weak formulation are obtained by minimising the global potential energy of the system:

$W(v)=\underset{\Omega}{\int}\Psi (\varepsilon (v))d\Omega -\underset{\Omega}{\int}{f}_{i}{v}_{i}d\Omega -\underset{S}{\int}{g}_{i}{v}_{i}d\Gamma$

This functional being minimal for the displacement field $u$ :

$\begin{array}{c}\delta W=\underset{\Omega}{\int}\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial {\varepsilon}_{ij}}\delta {\varepsilon}_{ij}d\Omega -\underset{\Omega}{\int}{f}_{i}\delta {v}_{i}d\Omega -\underset{S}{\int}{g}_{i}\delta {v}_{i}d\Gamma \\ =\underset{\Omega}{\int}{\sigma}_{ij}\frac{1}{2}(\delta {v}_{i,j}+\delta {v}_{j,i})d\Omega -\underset{\Omega}{\int}{f}_{i}\delta {v}_{i}d\Omega -\underset{S}{\int}{g}_{i}\delta {v}_{i}d\Gamma \\ =\underset{\Omega}{\int}{\sigma}_{ij}\delta {v}_{i,j}d\Omega -\underset{\Omega}{\int}{f}_{i}\delta {v}_{i}d\Omega -\underset{S}{\int}{g}_{i}\delta {v}_{i}d\Gamma =0\end{array}$

The equilibrium relations in weak formulation are therefore written in the following manner:

Find $u\in V$ such that

$\underset{\Omega}{\int}{\sigma}_{ij}{v}_{\text{i,j}}d\Omega =\underset{\Omega}{\int}{f}_{i}{v}_{i}d\Omega +\underset{S}{\int}{g}_{i}{v}_{i}d\Gamma ,\forall v\in {V}_{0}$

#### 5.1.2 Lagrangian expression of the energy release rate

By definition, the local energy release rate $G$ is defined by the opposite of the derivative of the potential energy with respect to the domain:

$G=-\frac{\partial W}{\partial \Omega }$

The method of virtual extensions used here is a Lagrangian method of derivation of the potential energy. It consists in introducing transformations:

${F}^{\eta}:P\in \Omega \to M=P+\eta \theta (P)\in {\Omega}^{\eta}$

which, to each material point $P$ of the reference domain $O$ , associate a spatial point $M$ of the transformed domain ${\Omega}^{\eta}$ . These transformations representing propagations of the crack, must modify only the position of the crack front ${\Gamma}_{0}$ . The fields $\theta$ must be tangent to the surface of the crack.

Let $n$ be the normal to the surface of the crack, the fields $\theta$ must satisfy:

${\theta}_{j}{n}_{j}=0\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{\text{cr}}$

Let $m$ be the unit normal to the crack front, situated in the tangent plane of the crack.

According to , the local energy release rate $G$ is the solution of the following variational equation:

$$
\underset{{\Gamma}_{0}}{\int}G\theta \cdot m=G(\theta ),\forall \theta \in \theta
$$

where $G(\theta )$ is defined by the opposite of the derivative of the potential energy $W(u(\eta ))$ at equilibrium with respect to the initial evolution of the crack front:

$G(\theta )=-{\frac{\text{dW}(u(\eta ))}{d\eta }\mid }_{\eta =0}$ (particle derivative)

In order to derive the potential energy with respect to its support (in the neighbourhood of the reference support), one uses the Reynolds transport theorem:

For a transformation ${F}^{\eta}$ of class ${C}^{1}$ and a function $\varphi$ (tensor of order 0, 1 or 2) of class ${C}^{1}$ , denoting $\dot{\varphi}$ the Lagrangian derivative linked to the variation of domain, one has the following relation:

${\frac{\partial}{\partial \eta }(\underset{\Omega}{\int}\varphi d\Omega )\mid }_{\eta =0}=\underset{\Omega}{\int}\dot{\varphi}+\varphi div{(\frac{\partial {F}^{\eta}}{\partial \eta })}_{\eta =0}d\Omega$

In fact, in practice, the transformation ${F}^{\eta}$ is not always of class ${C}^{1}$ because the field $\theta$ is only piecewise ${C}^{1}$ .

Thus,

$\begin{array}{rcl}-G(\theta) & = & \left. \frac{\partial}{\partial \eta} \left( \int_{\Omega} \Psi - f_i u_i \, d\Omega - \int_{S} g_i u_i \, d\Gamma\right) \right|_{\eta = 0} \\[6pt]& = & \int_{\Omega} \overbrace{\Psi - f_i u_i}^{\dot{\phantom{a}}} + (\Psi - f_i u_i)\,\operatorname{div}(\theta)\, d\Omega \\[6pt]& & - \int_{S} \overbrace{g_i u_i}^{\dot{\phantom{a}}} + g_i u_i \left( \operatorname{div}(\theta) - \frac{\partial \theta}{\partial n_k} n_k \right) d\Gamma\end{array}$

where:

$\Psi (\varepsilon ,T)=\frac{1}{2}\lambda {({\epsilon}_{ii})}^{2}+\mu {\varepsilon}_{ij}{\varepsilon}_{ij}-3K\alpha (T-{T}_{\mathrm{réf}})$

$\dot{\Psi}(\epsilon )=\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial {\epsilon}_{ij}}{\dot{\epsilon}}_{ij}+\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial T}\dot{T}={\sigma}_{ij}{\dot{\epsilon}}_{ij}+\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial T}\dot{T}$

therefore:

$\begin{array}{c}-G(\theta )=\underset{\Omega}{\int}{\sigma}_{ij}{\dot{\varepsilon}}_{ij}+\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial T}\dot{T}-{\dot{f}}_{i}{u}_{i}-{f}_{i}{\dot{u}}_{i}+(\Psi -{f}_{i}{u}_{i}){\theta}_{k,k}d\Omega \\ -\underset{S}{\int}{\dot{g}}_{i}{u}_{i}-{g}_{i}{\dot{u}}_{i}+{g}_{i}{u}_{i}({\theta}_{k,k}-\frac{\partial \theta }{\partial {n}_{k}}{n}_{k})d\Gamma \end{array}$

Firstly, let us use the relation giving the Lagrangian derivative of a field $\varphi$ as a function of its Eulerian derivative $\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial \eta }$ :

$\dot{\varphi}=\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial \eta }+\nabla \varphi \cdot \theta$

According to this relation, the body force $f$ being assumed independent of $\eta$ , that is to say being the restriction to $O$ of fields defined on ${ℝ}^{3}$ , one can write that $\frac{\partial {f}_{i}}{\partial \eta }=0$ . As it is the same for $g$ and $T$ , one has the following relations:

$\begin{array}{c}\dot{T}={T}_{,k}{\theta}_{k}\\ {\dot{f}}_{i}={f}_{i,k}{\theta}_{k}\\ {\dot{g}}_{i}={g}_{i,k}{\theta}_{k}\end{array}$

Secondly, let us use the relation giving the Lagrangian derivative of the gradient of a field as a function of the gradient of the field and of the gradient of the derivative of the field:

$\dot{\nabla}\varphi =\nabla \dot{\varphi}-\nabla \varphi \cdot \nabla \theta$

that is in indicial notations,

$\stackrel{\cdot}{\overbrace{{\varphi}_{i,j}}} = {\dot{\varphi}}_{i,j} - {\varphi}_{i,p}{\theta}_{p,j}$

therefore

${\dot{\varepsilon}}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{2}({\dot{u}}_{i,j}+{\dot{u}}_{j,i})-\frac{1}{2}({u}_{i,p}{\theta}_{p,j}+{u}_{j,p}{\theta}_{p,i})$

One replaces the 2 expressions in the 1st and one obtains:

$\begin{array}{c}-G(\theta )=\underset{\Omega}{\int}{\sigma}_{ij}{\dot{u}}_{i,j}+\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial T}{T}_{,k}{\theta}_{k}-{\sigma}_{ij}{u}_{i,p}{\theta}_{p,j}-{f}_{i,k}{\theta}_{k}{u}_{i}-{f}_{i}{\dot{u}}_{i}+(\Psi -{f}_{i}{u}_{i}){\theta}_{k,k}d\Omega \\ -\underset{S}{\int}{g}_{i,k}{\sigma}_{k}{u}_{i}-{g}_{i}{\dot{u}}_{i}+{g}_{i}{u}_{i}({\theta}_{k,k}-\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial {n}_{k}}{n}_{k})d\Gamma \end{array}$

One can eliminate the terms in $\dot{u}$ by noting that the field $\dot{u}$ is kinematically admissible and satisfies the equilibrium equation:

$\underset{\Omega}{\int}{\sigma}_{ij}{\dot{u}}_{i,j}d\Omega =\underset{\Omega}{\int}{f}_{i}{\dot{u}}_{i}d\Omega +\underset{S}{\int}{g}_{i}{\dot{u}}_{i}d\Gamma$

Which gives the final expression of $J(\theta )$ :

$$
\begin{array}{c}G(\theta )=\underset{\Omega}{\int}{\sigma}_{ij}{u}_{i,p}{\theta}_{p,j}-\Psi {\theta}_{k,k}+\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial T}{T}_{,k}{\theta}_{k}d\Omega \\ +\underset{\Omega}{\int}{f}_{i,k}{\theta}_{k}{u}_{i}+{f}_{i}{u}_{i}{\theta}_{k,k}d\Omega \\ +\underset{S}{\int}{g}_{i,k}{\theta}_{k}{u}_{i}+{g}_{i}{u}_{i}({\theta}_{k,k}-\frac{\partial \theta }{\partial {n}_{k}}{n}_{k})d\Gamma \end{array}
$$

**Note**

This form of the $J$ integral does not use contour integrals but domain integrals.

#### 5.1.3 Discretisations

One denotes $s$ the curvilinear abscissa at the crack front. The computation of the local value of $G$ requires solving the following variational equation for several fields ${\theta}^{i}$ :

${\int}_{{\Gamma}_{0}}G(s){\theta}^{i}(s)\cdot m(s)\text{ds}=G({\theta}^{i})\forall i\in [1,P]$

The scalar field $G(s)$ is discretised on a basis denoted ${({p}_{j}(s))}_{1\le j\le N}$ :

$G(s)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}{G}_{j}{p}_{j}(s)$

Likewise, the fields ${\theta}^{i}$ are discretised on a basis denoted ${({q}_{k}(s))}_{1\le k\le M}$ . Let ${\stackrel{ˉ}{\theta}}^{i}$ be the trace of the field ${\theta}^{i}$ on the crack front ${\Gamma}_{0}$ : ${\stackrel{ˉ}{\theta}}^{i}(s)={\theta \mid }_{{\Gamma}_{0}}(s)$ and let ${\theta}_{k}^{i}$ be the components of ${\stackrel{ˉ}{\theta}}^{i}(s)$ in this basis:

$$
{\stackrel{ˉ}{\theta}}^{i}(s)=\sum_{k=1}^{M}{\theta}_{k}^{i}{q}_{k}(s)
$$

By injecting these expressions into the variational equation, one obtains:

$\underset{{\Gamma}_{0}}{\int}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{G}_{j}{p}_{j}(s)\sum_{k=1}^{M}({\theta}_{k}^{i}{q}_{k}(s))\cdot m(s)\mathit{ds}=G({\theta}^{i}),\forall i\in [1,P]$

The ${G}_{j}$ can therefore be determined by solving the linear system with $P$ equations and $N$ unknowns:

$\left\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{a}_{ij}{G}_{j}={b}_{i},i=1,P\\ \quad\text{avec}\quad {a}_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{M}{\theta}_{k}^{i}\underset{{\Gamma}_{0}}{\int}{p}_{j}(s){q}_{k}(s)\cdot m(s)\mathit{ds}\\ \phantom{\mathit{avec}}{b}_{i}=G({\theta}^{i})\end{array}\right.$

This system has a solution if one chooses $P$ independent fields ${\theta}^{i}$ such that: $P\ge N$ and if $M\ge N$ . It can comprise more equations than unknowns, in which case it is solved in the least-squares sense.

### 5.2 G-theta method for the computation of KI, KII and KIII with the level sets

This paragraph presents the contribution of the level sets for the computation of the stress intensity factors in 3D. It is based on the separation of the mixed modes thanks to the bilinear form of $g$ . This bilinear form is used in *Code_Aster* for the computation by the $G$ -theta method of ${K}_{I}$ , ${K}_{\mathit{II}}$ in 2D.

#### 5.2.1 Bilinear form of g

The computation of the stress intensity factors can also be done by the theta method, by defining a symmetric bilinear form $g$ with:

$$
g(u,v)=\frac{1}{4}(G(u+v)-G(u-v))
$$

Thus, let two displacement fields $u$ and $v$ , the computation of $g(u,v)$ is carried out thanks to the expression of $G$ given by the equation, in which the strains linked to $u$ and $v$ derive from the fields $u$ and $v$ , and the stresses linked to $u$ and $v$ are deduced by the constitutive law:

$\begin{array}{c}\varepsilon (u)={\nabla}_{s}u,\varepsilon (v)={\nabla}_{s}v\\ \sigma (u)=C:\varepsilon (u),\sigma (v)=C:\varepsilon (v)\end{array}$

Taking up the notations of , the classical term of $J(\theta )$ is:

$\begin{array}{c}\text{TCLA}=\sigma (u):(\nabla u\nabla \theta )-\Psi (\varepsilon (u))div\theta \\ =(\mathit{S2}-\mathit{S2}\text{TH})-\frac{1}{2}(\mathit{S1}-\mathit{S1}\text{TH})div\theta \end{array}$

One recalls that in elasticity, the free energy is expressed as a function of the Lamé coefficients $\lambda$ and $\mu$ :

$\begin{array}{c}\Psi =\frac{1}{2}\lambda {({\varepsilon}_{ii})}^{2}+\mu {\varepsilon}_{ij}{\varepsilon}_{ij}-{\Psi}^{\text{th}}\\ \phantom{\Psi}=\frac{1}{2}\left[(\lambda +2\mu )({\varepsilon}_{11}^{2}+{\varepsilon}_{22}^{2}+{\varepsilon}_{33}^{2})+2\lambda ({\varepsilon}_{11}{\varepsilon}_{22}+{\varepsilon}_{11}{\varepsilon}_{33}+{\varepsilon}_{22}{\varepsilon}_{33})+2\mu (2{\varepsilon}_{12}^{2}+2{\varepsilon}_{13}^{2}+2{\varepsilon}_{23}^{2})\right]-{\Psi}^{\text{th}}\end{array}$

with ${\Psi}^{\text{th}}=\mathrm{3K}\alpha (T-{T}^{\text{ref}})\text{tr}\varepsilon$

For each term $\mathrm{S1}$ and $\mathrm{S2}$ , one will first write their usual expression, then afterwards, one will pass to the bilinear form.

##### 5.2.1.1 Expression of S1 and S1TH

One writes $\mathrm{S1}$ with the following expression:

$\mathit{S1}=\mathit{C1}.S11+\mathit{C2}.S12+\mathit{C3}.S13$

by setting:

$\left\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\mathit{C1}=\lambda +2\mu \\ \mathit{C2}=\lambda \\ \mathit{C3}=\mu \end{array}\right.$     and    $\begin{array}{c}S11={u}_{1,1}^{2}+{u}_{2,2}^{2}+{u}_{3,3}^{2}\\ S12=2({u}_{1,1}{u}_{2,2}+{u}_{1,1}{u}_{3,3}+{u}_{2,2}{u}_{3,3})\\ S13={({u}_{1,2}+{u}_{2,1})}^{2}+{({u}_{1,3}+{u}_{3,1})}^{2}+{({u}_{2,3}+{u}_{3,2})}^{2}\end{array}$

Let us now write the associated bilinear form. The free energy then depends on two fields $u$ and $v$ . The material coefficients $\mathrm{C1}$ , $\mathrm{C2}$ and $\mathrm{C3}$ are obviously unchanged, and the new coefficients $S11$ , $S12$ and $S13$ have for expressions:

$\begin{array}{}S11={u}_{1,1}{v}_{1,1}+{u}_{2,2}{v}_{2,2}+{u}_{3,3}{v}_{3,3}\\ S12=({u}_{1,1}{v}_{2,2}+{u}_{2,2}{v}_{1,1})+({u}_{1,1}{v}_{3,3}+{u}_{3,3}{v}_{1,1})+({u}_{2,2}{u}_{3,3}+{u}_{3,3}{v}_{2,2})\\ S13=({u}_{1,2}+{u}_{2,1})({v}_{1,2}+{v}_{2,1})+({u}_{1,3}+{u}_{3,1})({v}_{1,3}+{v}_{3,1})+({u}_{2,3}+{u}_{3,2})({v}_{2,3}+{v}_{3,2})\end{array}$

The expression of the thermal part is:

$\text{SITH}=3K\alpha (({T}_{u}-{T}_{\text{réf}})\text{tr}\epsilon (v)+({T}_{v}-{T}_{\text{réf}})\text{tr}\epsilon (u))$

For the computation of ${K}_{i}$ , $v$ is the asymptotic field in mode i, obtained for ${T}_{v}={T}_{\text{réf}}$ , therefore the terms in $({T}_{v}-{T}_{\text{réf}})$ simplify:

$\text{SITH}=3K\alpha (({T}_{u}-{T}_{\text{réf}})\text{tr}\epsilon (v))$

##### 5.2.1.2 Expression of S2 and S2TH

As for $\mathrm{S1}$ , one writes $\mathrm{S2}$ with the following expression:

$\mathit{S2}=\mathit{C1}.S21+\mathit{C2}.S22+\mathit{C3}.S23$

The material coefficients $\mathrm{C1}$ , $\mathrm{C2}$ and $\mathrm{C3}$ are those defined in the preceding paragraph.

The coefficients $S21$ , $S22$ and $S23$ have for expressions:

$\begin{array}{c}\mathit{S21}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{p=1}^{3}{u}_{k,k}{u}_{k,p}{\theta}_{p,k}\\ \mathit{S22}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{l\mathrm{\ne }k}^{}\sum_{p=1}^{3}{u}_{l,l}{u}_{k,p}{\theta}_{p,k}\\ \mathit{S23}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{l\mathrm{\ne }k}\sum_{\begin{array}{c}m\mathrm{\ne }k\\ m\mathrm{\ne }l\end{array}}\sum_{p=1}^{3}{u}_{l,m}{u}_{l,p}{\theta}_{p,m}+{u}_{l,m}{u}_{m,p}{\theta}_{p,l}\end{array}$

This writing makes it easy to pass to the associated bilinear form. It suffices to replace the terms ${u}_{i,j}{u}_{k,l}$ by $\frac{1}{2}({u}_{i,j}{v}_{k,l}+{u}_{k,l}{v}_{i,j})$ . If one introduces the following notation:

$B({u}_{i,j},{v}_{k,l})=\frac{1}{2}({u}_{i,j}{v}_{k,l}+{u}_{k,l}{v}_{i,j})$

then the new coefficients $S21$ , $S22$ and $S23$ have for expressions:

$\begin{array}{c}\mathit{S21}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{p=1}^{3}B({u}_{k,k},{v}_{k,p}){\theta}_{p,k}\\ \mathit{S22}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{l\mathrm{\ne }k}^{}\sum_{p=1}^{3}B({u}_{l,l},{v}_{k,p}){\theta}_{p,k}\\ \mathit{S23}=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\sum_{l\mathrm{\ne }k}\sum_{\begin{array}{c}m\mathrm{\ne }k\\ m\mathrm{\ne }l\end{array}}\sum_{p=1}^{3}B({u}_{l,m},{v}_{l,p}){\theta}_{p,m}+B({u}_{l,m},{v}_{m,p}){\theta}_{p,l}\end{array}$

The expression of the thermal part is:

$\mathit{S2}\text{TH}=\frac{\text{TH1}}{2}3K\alpha ({T}_{u}-{T}_{\text{ref}})(\frac{\partial {v}_{i}}{\partial {x}_{j}}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{i}})+\frac{\text{TH1}}{2}3K\alpha ({T}_{v}-{T}_{\text{ref}})(\frac{\partial {u}_{i}}{\partial {x}_{j}}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{i}})$

where $\text{TH1}=1$ in D_PLAN, AXIS and in 3D and $\text{TH1}=\frac{1-2\nu }{1-\nu }$ in C_PLAN.

For the computation of $\mathit{Ki}$ , $v$ is the asymptotic field in mode $i$ , obtained for ${T}_{v}={T}_{\text{réf}}$ , therefore the terms in $({T}_{v}-{T}_{\text{réf}})$ simplify:

$\mathit{S2}\text{TH}=\frac{\text{TH}1}{2}\mathrm{3K}\alpha ({T}_{u}-{T}_{\text{réf}})(\frac{\partial {v}_{i}}{\partial {x}_{j}}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{i}})$

##### 5.2.1.3 Surface term

The additional term in the expression of $G$ *,* due to the imposition of a surface force $g$ on ${\Gamma}_{c}$ of outward normal $n$ is the following:

$\begin{array}{c}\text{TSUR}=(\nabla g\cdot \theta )u+g\cdot u(div\theta -n\frac{\partial \theta }{\partial n})\\ ={g}_{i,k}{\theta}_{k}{u}_{i}+{g}_{i}\cdot {u}_{i}({\theta}_{k,k}-{n}_{k}\frac{\partial \theta }{\partial {n}_{k}})\end{array}$

The term $n\frac{\partial \theta }{\partial n}$ is zero because the gradient of the field $\theta$ is orthogonal to $n$ .

There therefore remains:

$\begin{array}{c}\text{TSUR}=(\nabla g\cdot \theta )u+g\cdot udiv\theta \\ ={g}_{i,k}{\theta}_{k}{u}_{i}+{g}_{i}\cdot {u}_{i}{\theta}_{k,k}\end{array}$

The associated bilinear form $g(u,v)$ for the computation of $G$ and of ${K}_{I}$ *,* ${K}_{\mathit{II}}$ *and* ${K}_{\mathit{III}}$ is:

$\text{TSUR}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}\left[((\nabla {g}_{u}\cdot \theta )v+{g}_{u}\cdot vdiv\theta )+((\nabla {g}_{v}\cdot \theta )u+{g}_{v}\cdot udiv\theta )\right]$

For the computation of ${K}_{i}$ , $v$ is the asymptotic field in mode $i$ , obtained for ${g}_{v}=0$ , therefore the terms in ${g}_{v}$ and $\nabla {g}_{v}$ simplify:

$\text{TSUR}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}\left[(\nabla {g}_{u}\cdot \theta )v+{g}_{u}\cdot vdiv\theta \right]$

##### 5.2.1.4 Thermal term

The additional term in the expression of $G$ *,* due to the temperature field $T$ is the following:

$\begin{array}{ccc}\mathit{TTHE}=\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial T}{T}_{,k}{\theta}_{k}& =& \frac{1}{2}3K\alpha \text{tr}\varepsilon (v)(\frac{\partial {T}_{u}}{\partial x}{\theta}_{x}+\frac{\partial {T}_{u}}{\partial y}{\theta}_{y}\frac{\partial {T}_{u}}{\partial z}{\theta}_{z})\\ & +& \frac{1}{2}3K\alpha \text{tr}\varepsilon (u)(\frac{\partial {T}_{v}}{\partial x}{\theta}_{x}+\frac{\partial {T}_{v}}{\partial y}{\theta}_{y}\frac{\partial {T}_{v}}{\partial z}{\theta}_{z})\end{array}$

For the computation of ${K}_{i}$ , $v$ is the asymptotic field in mode i, obtained for ${T}_{v}={T}_{\text{réf}}=\text{cste}$ , therefore the terms in $\mathit{TTHE}=\frac{-\partial \Psi }{\partial T}(\nabla T\cdot \theta )=\frac{1}{2}3K\alpha \text{tr}\varepsilon (\frac{\partial T}{\partial x}{\theta}_{x}+\frac{\partial T}{\partial y}{\theta}_{y})$ simplify:

$\mathit{TTHE}=\frac{1}{2}3K\alpha \text{tr}\varepsilon (v)(\frac{\partial {T}_{u}}{\partial x}{\theta}_{x}+\frac{\partial {T}_{u}}{\partial y}{\theta}_{y}+\frac{\partial {T}_{u}}{\partial z}{\theta}_{z})$

#### 5.2.2 Separation of the mixed modes

The symmetric bilinear form $g(u,v)$ has as an interesting property that of separating the three opening modes of the crack. Indeed, in the form $g(u,v)$ , if the field on the left is the solution displacement field and if the term on the right is a field of singular displacements at the crack front ${u}_{I}^{S}$ , ${u}_{\text{II}}^{S}$ or ${u}_{\text{III}}^{S}$ , the stress intensity factors are expressed in the following manner:

$$
{K}_{I}=\frac{E}{(1-{\nu}^{2})}g(u,{u}_{I}^{S})
$$

$$
{K}_{\text{II}}=\frac{E}{(1-{\nu}^{2})}g(u,{u}_{\text{II}}^{S})
$$

$$
{K}_{\text{III}}=2\mu g(u,{u}_{\text{III}}^{S})
$$

Certain authors use the concept of interaction integrals, in place of the bilinear form of $g$ . These two vocabularies designate in fact the same quantities. Indeed, denoting ${I}^{u,{u}_{I}^{S}}$ the interaction integral between the solution field and the singular field in mode $I$ , the expression of ${K}_{I}$ in terms of interaction integral is:

${K}_{I}=\frac{E}{2(1-{\nu}^{2})}{I}^{u,{u}_{I}^{S}}$

This expression for ${K}_{I}$ is identical to the one given by the equation up to a multiplicative term. If one applies the method of virtual extensions to the bilinear form of $g$ , the equivalent expression of the variational equation for the local ${K}_{i}$ is:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\frac{(1-{\nu}^{2})}{E}\underset{{\Gamma}_{0}}{\int}{K}_{I}\theta \cdot m\text{ds}=g{(u,{u}_{I}^{S})}_{\theta},\forall \theta \in \Theta \\ \frac{(1-{\nu}^{2})}{E}\underset{{\Gamma}_{0}}{\int}{K}_{\text{II}}\theta \cdot m\text{ds}=g{(u,{u}_{\text{II}}^{S})}_{\theta},\forall \theta \in \Theta \\ \frac{1}{2\mu }\underset{{\Gamma}_{0}}{\int}{K}_{\text{III}}\theta \cdot m\text{ds}=g{(u,{u}_{\text{III}}^{S})}_{\theta},\forall \theta \in \Theta \end{array}
$$

One finds the analogue of these expressions written with interaction integrals in and .

One recalls that the right-hand side of the equations is the bilinear form of $g$ (see ) in which the term on the left $u$ is the solution displacement field (coming from the solution of the problem by the finite element method) and the term on the right ${u}_{i=I,\text{II},\text{III}}^{S}$ is the asymptotic field (in mode $i=I,\text{II},\text{III}$ ) whose analytical expression is given by the equation. These analytical expressions are given in the local basis at the crack front $({e}_{1},{e}_{2},{e}_{3})$ arising from the gradients of the level sets, whereas the derivatives intervening in $g{(u,{u}_{I,\text{II},\text{III}}^{S})}_{\theta}$ are taken with respect to the global frame $({E}_{1},{E}_{2},{E}_{3})$ (see the expressions of the terms $\mathit{S1}$ and $\mathrm{S2}$ in the preceding paragraph). One must therefore proceed to a change of basis.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.12/10000000000002AA00000109FF4DA0A096E86CAA.png)

*Polar coordinates, local basis and global basis*

Let $\text{DPODL}$ be the matrix of the derivatives of the polar coordinates in the local basis:

$\text{DPODL}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}& \frac{\partial r}{\partial y}& \frac{\partial r}{\partial z}\\ \frac{\partial \theta }{\partial x}& \frac{\partial \theta }{\partial y}& \frac{\partial \theta }{\partial z}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\cos\theta & \sin\theta & 0\\ -\frac{\sin\theta }{r}& \frac{\cos\theta }{r}& 0\end{array}\right]$

Let $u$ be an auxiliary field expressed as a function of the polar coordinates $(r,\theta )$ . The derivatives of $u$ with respect to the variables $(r,\theta )$ are determined analytically. One writes them in matrix form:

$\text{DUDPO}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\frac{\partial {u}_{1}}{\partial r}& \frac{\partial {u}_{1}}{\partial \theta }\\ \frac{\partial {u}_{2}}{\partial r}& \frac{\partial {u}_{2}}{\partial \theta }\\ \frac{\partial {u}_{3}}{\partial r}& \frac{\partial {u}_{3}}{\partial \theta }\end{array}\right]$

Then, one writes the derivatives in the local basis $({e}_{1},{e}_{2},{e}_{3})$ :

$\text{DUDL}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\frac{\partial {u}_{1}}{\partial x}& \frac{\partial {u}_{1}}{\partial y}& \frac{\partial {u}_{1}}{\partial z}\\ \frac{\partial {u}_{2}}{\partial x}& \frac{\partial {u}_{2}}{\partial y}& \frac{\partial {u}_{2}}{\partial z}\\ \frac{\partial {u}_{3}}{\partial x}& \frac{\partial {u}_{3}}{\partial y}& \frac{\partial {u}_{3}}{\partial z}\end{array}\right]=\text{DUDPO}.\text{DPODL}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\frac{\partial {u}_{1}}{\partial r}& \frac{\partial {u}_{1}}{\partial \theta }\\ \frac{\partial {u}_{2}}{\partial r}& \frac{\partial {u}_{2}}{\partial \theta }\\ \frac{\partial {u}_{3}}{\partial r}& \frac{\partial {u}_{3}}{\partial \theta }\end{array}\right].\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\frac{\partial r}{\partial x}& \frac{\partial r}{\partial y}& \frac{\partial r}{\partial z}\\ \frac{\partial \theta }{\partial x}& \frac{\partial \theta }{\partial y}& \frac{\partial \theta }{\partial z}\end{array}\right]$

It now remains to write the derivatives of $u$ in the global basis $({E}_{1},{E}_{2},{E}_{3})$ . For this purpose, one denotes $R$ the matrix of the vectors of the global basis written in the local basis (passage matrix):

${R}_{\alpha m}=({e}_{\alpha}\cdot {E}_{m})$

Let *i* and *j* be global indices, the $j$ th derivative of the $i$ th component of $u$ is then written:

${u}_{i,j}={(\sum_{\alpha =1}^{3}{u}_{\alpha}{R}_{\alpha i})}_{,j}=\sum_{\alpha =1}^{3}({u}_{\alpha ,j}{R}_{\alpha i}+{u}_{\alpha}{R}_{\alpha i,j})=\sum_{\alpha =1}^{3}(\sum_{\beta =1}^{3}({u}_{\alpha ,\beta }{R}_{\beta j}){R}_{\alpha i}+{u}_{\alpha}{R}_{\alpha i,j})$

In this expression, the terms ${u}_{\alpha ,\beta }$ are the components of the matrix $\text{DUDL}$ . The term ${R}_{\mathrm{\alpha i},j}$ appears as the curvature of the local basis. It can be neglected if the curvature of the crack front is small. In practice, taking it into account hardly changes the results.

#### 5.2.3 Discretisations

As previously, one denotes *s* the curvilinear abscissa at the crack front. The computation of the local value of ${K}_{I}$ , ${K}_{\text{II}}$ and ${K}_{\text{III}}$ requires solving the following variational equation for several fields ${\theta}^{i}$ :

$$
\begin{array}{c}\frac{(1-{\nu}^{2})}{E}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{0}}{K}_{I}(s){\theta}^{i}(s)\cdot m(s)\text{ds}=g{(u,{u}_{I}^{S})}_{{\theta}^{i}}\forall i\in [1,P]\\ \frac{(1-{\nu}^{2})}{E}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{0}}{K}_{\text{II}}(s){\theta}^{i}(s)\cdot m(s)\text{ds}=g{(u,{u}_{\text{II}}^{S})}_{{\theta}^{i}}\forall i\in [1,P]\\ \frac{1}{2\mu }{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{0}}{K}_{\text{III}}(s){\theta}^{i}(s)\cdot m(s)\text{ds}=g{(u,{u}_{\text{III}}^{S})}_{{\theta}^{i}}\forall i\in [1,P]\end{array}
$$

The scalar fields ${K}_{\text{I}}(s)$ , ${K}_{\text{II}}(s)$ and ${K}_{\text{III}}(s)$ are discretised on the basis denoted ${({p}_{j}(s))}_{1\le j\le N}$ :

$\begin{array}{ccc}{K}_{I}(s)& =& \sum_{j=1}^{N}{K}_{Ij}{p}_{j}(s)\\ {K}_{\mathit{II}}(s)& =& \sum_{j=1}^{N}{K}_{\mathit{II}j}{p}_{j}(s)\\ {K}_{\mathit{III}}(s)& =& \sum_{j=1}^{N}{K}_{\mathit{III}j}{p}_{j}(s)\end{array}$

For the theta fields, the approximation is retained.

As for $G$ , by injecting the expressions of the discretisations into the variational equation, one obtains the linear system:

$\begin{array}{cccc}\underset{{\Gamma}_{0}}{\int}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{K}_{Ij}{p}_{j}(s)\sum_{k=1}^{M}({\theta}_{k}^{i}{q}_{k}(s))\cdot m(s)\mathit{ds}& =& g{(u,{u}_{I}^{S})}_{{\theta}^{i}},& \forall i\in [1,P]\\ \underset{{\Gamma}_{0}}{\int}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{K}_{\mathit{II}j}{p}_{j}(s)\sum_{k=1}^{M}({\theta}_{k}^{i}{q}_{k}(s))\cdot m(s)\mathit{ds}& =& g{(u,{u}_{\mathit{II}}^{S})}_{{\theta}^{i}},& \forall i\in [1,P]\\ \underset{{\Gamma}_{0}}{\int}\sum_{j=1}^{N}{K}_{\mathit{III}j}{p}_{j}(s)\sum_{k=1}^{M}({\theta}_{k}^{i}{q}_{k}(s))\cdot m(s)\mathit{ds}& =& g{(u,{u}_{\mathit{III}}^{S})}_{{\theta}^{i}},& \forall i\in [1,P]\end{array}$

### 5.3 G-theta method with X-FEM

Up to now, the G-theta method has been presented within the framework of the finite element method (FEM). In order to adapt the G-theta method to the X-FEM framework, there are very few modifications to be made. The only difference resides at the level of the numerical integrations, in order to compute the domain integrals. As for the integration of the tangent matrices and of the right-hand sides, one carries out the integration on the sub-elements.
