# r7.02.14 Elements with an internal discontinuity, behaviour CZM_EXP and piloting of the loading

**Summary:**

Relying on an energetic formulation set out briefly in a first part, we present a numerical model of initiation and propagation of two-dimensional cracks. It relies on a finite element with an internal displacement discontinuity, close to the models known under the name “embedded discontinuity finite element” in the literature.

For this model, we detail the constitutive law used (of exponential Cohesive Zone Model type: keyword CZM_EXP), the properties of the finite element as well as the solution of the problem of minimisation of the total energy. We furthermore describe a technique of load piloting making it possible to follow possible instabilities in the global response of the structure.

These elements can be used in 2D plane or axisymmetric conditions only with the modellings PLAN_ELDI and AXIS_ELDI. They are validated on the test cases ssnp128a (documentation [V6.03.128](doc:v6.03.128)) and ssna115a (documentation [V6.01.115](doc:v6.01.115)).

## 1 Energetic model of cracking

The aim of this part is to set the general framework of a model of crack initiation and propagation based on a principle of energy minimisation. This model is inspired by those developed in the literature under the name “Cohesive Force Models” in the sense that one seeks to take into account a residual interaction between the faces of the crack. However, the major difference of our approach is the formulation of the problem in a broader framework by adopting the energetic point of view introduced by Francfort and Marigo [3].

The idea consists in taking into account the process of energy dissipation in the course of cracking by means of an energy defined on a surface of discontinuity and depending on the displacement jump across the latter. We shall content ourselves here with posing the problem in its form continuous in space and semi-discretised in time without seeking to make explicit the form of the surface energy.

The following part will be devoted to the implementation of the finite element with an internal discontinuity relying on this energetic formulation.

### 1.1 General principle

One considers an *elastic* structure defined by the domain $\Omega$ and a crack defined as a surface of discontinuity denoted $\Gamma$ across which the displacement $u$ admits a jump $\delta$ :

$$
\delta ={〚u〛}_{\Gamma}
$$

One defines the total energy ${E}_{T}$ of this structure as the sum of its elastic energy denoted $\Phi$ , of a surface energy denoted $\Psi$ and of ${W}^{\text{ext}}$ the potential of the external forces:

$$
{E}_{T}=\Phi +\Psi -{W}^{\text{ext}}
$$

One writes $\Phi ={\int}_{\Omega}\phi .d\Omega$ and $\Psi ={\int}_{\Gamma}\psi .d\Gamma$ , where $\phi$ and $\psi$ denote respectively the elastic energy density and the surface energy density. The total energy is a function of the displacement $u$ and of the displacement jump $\delta$ ; the **minimisation problem** is written:

$$
\text{Chercher}({u}^{\text{*}},{\delta}^{\text{*}})\text{minimum local de l'énergie totale}{E}_{T}(u,\delta )
$$

Let us recall that one carries out a search for a *local* minimum because the total energy is not bounded from below in the presence of external forces. Let us now see what hypotheses one can formulate on the surface energy in order to take into account the condition of non-interpenetration of the crack faces and according to whether one considers cracking as a reversible process or not.

### 1.2 Reversible model

Let us present a first simple model, but not very realistic from a physical point of view at the macroscopic scale, in which one assumes that the cracking of a material is a reversible process. One considers that the total reclosing of the faces of a crack makes it possible to recover a sound material. The surface energy can be written as the sum of a function of the Euclidean norm of the displacement jump and of an indicator function accounting for the condition of non-interpenetration of the faces:

$$
\Psi (\delta )={\Psi}_{\mathrm{rev}}(\parallel \delta \parallel )+{I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}({\delta}_{n})
$$

with ${\delta}_{n}=\delta \cdot n$ where $n$ is the normal to the discontinuity and ${I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}({\delta}_{n})$ is the indicator function:

$$
{I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}({\delta}_{n})=\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}+\infty & \text{si}{\delta}_{n}<0\\ 0& \text{si}{\delta}_{n}\ge 0\end{array}
$$

The model developed in *Code_Aster* makes it possible, for its part, to take into account the irreversibility of cracking; it is presented in the following section.

### 1.3 Model with memory

Relying on the writing of the previous reversible model, one introduces an internal variable denoted $\kappa$ making it possible to memorise the state of cracking at a given time instant and thus to translate its irreversible character. One defines its evolution in the following way:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{ccc}\kappa (0)& =& {\kappa}_{0}\\ \kappa (t)& =& \underset{\tau \le t}{\sup}\lbrace  \mathrm{\parallel }\delta (\tau )\mathrm{\parallel },{\kappa}_{0}\rbrace  \forall t>0\end{array}
$$

One can equivalently express this evolution law by means of a threshold function $f$ defined by:

$$
f(\kappa ,\delta )=\mathrm{\parallel }\delta \mathrm{\parallel }-\kappa
$$

The evolution law then takes the form of a classical consistency condition:

$$
f\le 0,\dot{\kappa}\ge 0,\mathrm{f.}\kappa =0
$$

One defines the surface energy $\Psi$ as a function of the displacement jump and of the variable $\kappa$ :

$$
\Psi (\delta ,\kappa )=H(\parallel \delta \parallel -\kappa ).{\Psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}(\parallel \delta \parallel )+\left[1-H(\parallel \delta \parallel -\kappa )\right].{\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}(\parallel \delta \parallel ,\kappa )+{I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}({\delta}_{n})
$$

with $H$ the Heaviside function defined by:

$$
H(x)=\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}1& \text{si}& x\ge 0\\ 0& \text{si}& x<0\end{array}
$$

The surface energy $\Psi$ will therefore be equal to ${\Psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}$ if the threshold is positive, translating the dissipation when the crack evolves. And it will be equal to ${\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}$ , translating an evolution without dissipation of energy, if the threshold is negative. ${\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}$ takes into account the case where the crack recloses; one makes the choice of a linear unloading, so the corresponding surface energy density ${\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}$ will be of the form:

$$
{\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}(\parallel \delta \parallel ,\kappa )=\frac{1}{2}.R(\kappa ).{\parallel \delta \parallel }^{2}+{C}_{0}
$$

where $R(\kappa )$ and ${C}_{0}$ are chosen in order to ensure the continuously differentiable character of the energy. The surface energy density ${\Psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}$ when cracking evolves can take many forms; we shall give an example for the finite-element model of chapter 5. From the surface energy densities ${\Psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}$ and ${\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}$ one can define the stress vector $\overrightarrow{\sigma}$ across the discontinuity:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\overrightarrow{\sigma}=\frac{\partial {\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}}{\partial \delta }\text{si}\parallel \delta \parallel <\kappa \\ \overrightarrow{\sigma}=\frac{\partial {\Psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}}{\partial \delta }\text{si}\parallel \delta \parallel >\kappa \end{array}
$$

This is the interface law which we shall adopt.

### 1.4 Time discretisation

Let us now present the time discretisation. Let us first specify that only quasi-static evolutions are envisaged; time is therefore parametrised by loading increments. By carrying out a semi-discretisation in time, one defines the surface energy $\Psi$ at a time instant $i$ as a function of the displacement jump and of ${\kappa}^{i-1}$ , the internal variable at the time instant $i-1$ :

$$
\Psi (\delta ,{\kappa}^{i-1})=H(\parallel \delta \parallel -{\kappa}^{i-1}).{\Psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}(\parallel \delta \parallel )+\left[1-H(\parallel \delta \parallel -{\kappa}^{i-1})\right].{\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}(\parallel \delta \parallel ,{\kappa}^{i-1})+{I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}({\delta}_{n})
$$

The minimisation problem is then written at the time instant

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.14/Object_49.svg)

:

$$
\underset{u,\delta }{\min}{E}_{T}(u,\delta ;{\kappa}^{i-1})
$$

The internal variable at the time instant

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.14/Object_51.svg)

is updated once the jump at the time instant

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.14/Object_52.svg)

is known:

$$
{\kappa}^{i}=\max(\parallel \delta \parallel ,{\kappa}^{i-1})
$$

## 2 Model with an internal discontinuity

This model makes it possible to take into account the initiation and the propagation of cracks in a structure for a given direction. It is based on particular finite elements which one calls *elements with an internal discontinuity* (from the English embedded discontinuity finite elements). The main idea rests on the introduction of a discontinuity included in the element and of an internal threshold variable governing the dissipative process as well as the irreversible character of cracking. Moreover, the displacement jump will be considered constant per element, which will facilitate the numerical solution. One will be able to adopt a static-condensation technique in which the computation of the jump during the minimisation will be done at the element level.

The discontinuity element has been developed in order to do without the regularisation of the energy at zero (penalised adhesion) of the law CZM_EXP_REG developed for the joint element (see documentation [R3.06.09](doc:r3.06.09) for the element and [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11) for the law). Indeed, the latter is detrimental for models based on a principle of minimum energy since it leads to cancelling the derivative of the surface energy density at zero, that is the critical stress of the model. In such a situation, the initiation of a crack occurs as soon as the loading is applied, however small it may be (let us note however that this does not prevent one from obtaining interesting results with such a law, as soon as one fixes the cracking path).

In order to take the discontinuity into account one carries out an enrichment of the displacements and of the strains which makes it possible to ensure their compatibility.

In this part one will first present the constitutive law which governs the discontinuity element. One will be led to define a stress vector in the element which will be used during the minimisation of the energy. One will then detail the properties of the finite element and then the numerical solution of the energy-minimisation problem.

### 2.1 Constitutive law of the discontinuity element: CZM_EXP

The discontinuity elements have a mixed behaviour: elastic and dissipative. The total energy of the element is written as the sum of an elastic energy and of a surface energy of CZM type. The following parts will be devoted to the presentation of the latter as well as of the stress which derives from it.

#### 2.1.1 Total energy in the discontinuity element

One chooses the total energy ${E}_{T}$ as the sum of an elastic energy defined on the element denoted ${\Omega}_{e}$ and of a surface energy defined on the discontinuity of the element denoted ${\Gamma}_{e}$ :

$$
{E}_{T}(U,\delta ;\kappa )=\Phi (U,\delta )+\Psi (\delta ,\kappa )
$$

where $\Phi$ corresponds to the elastic energy. Let us note that the latter depends on the nodal displacement $U$ and on the displacement jump

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.14/Object_60.svg)

constant (in the element), which one will see induces an additional strain. The surface energy $\Psi$ depends on the displacement jump and on $\kappa$ , an internal variable making it possible to treat the irreversibility of cracking.

**Note**: The cracking path is known *a priori*; the normal to the discontinuity will therefore be fixed by the orientation of the elements in the mesh (see figure ).

##### 2.1.1.1 Elastic energy

In order to define the elastic energy we shall need to introduce right away the interpolation of the displacement field in the discontinuity element; the properties of the latter will however be detailed in a part to follow (see § 2.2). One represents in the figure the discontinuity element denoted ${\Omega}_{e}$ ; it is a four-node quadrangle with an internal discontinuity which one denotes ${\Gamma}_{e}$ .

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.14/Cadre12.gif)

Figure 1: Discontinuity element

The interpolation of the displacement field $u(x)$ in the discontinuity element is written:

$$
u(x)=N(x).\left[\begin{array}{c}{U}_{A}\\ {U}_{B}\\ {U}_{C}-\delta \\ {U}_{D}-\delta \end{array}\right]+{H}_{{\Gamma}_{e}}(x).\delta
$$

with $N$ the matrix of the classical bilinear shape functions of type $\mathrm{Q1}$ , ${H}_{{\Gamma}_{e}}$ the Heaviside function on the discontinuity of the element ${\Gamma}_{e}$ equal to 1 if $x\in {\Omega}_{e}^{+}$ and 0 if $x\in {\Omega}_{e}^{-}$ . The vector $\delta$ corresponds to the displacement jump on the discontinuity. One does indeed have $〚u〛=\delta$ since $N$ is a continuous function and ${H}_{{\Gamma}_{e}}=1$ on ${\Gamma}_{e}$ . Moreover, the $U$ (indexed by the vertices) correspond to the nodal displacements; indeed one can write:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{U}_{A}\\ {U}_{B}\\ {U}_{C}\\ {U}_{D}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{U}_{A}\\ {U}_{B}\\ {U}_{C}+\delta -\delta \\ {U}_{D}+\delta -\delta \end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

From the approximate field () one can define the associated strain on the element **outside the discontinuity**:

$$
\epsilon ={\nabla}^{\text{s}}N(x).\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{U}_{A}\\ {U}_{B}\\ {U}_{C}-\delta \\ {U}_{D}-\delta \end{array}\right\rbrace +\underset{=0}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\nabla}^{\text{s}}{H}_{{\Gamma}_{e}}(x)}}\cdot \delta
$$

${\nabla}^{\text{s}}$ denotes the symmetrised gradient. One can rewrite () in the synthetic form:

$$
\epsilon =B.U-D.\delta
$$

where $B$ is the matrix of the symmetrised gradients of the shape functions, $U={({U}_{A},{U}_{B},{U}_{C},{U}_{D})}^{T}$ and $D.\delta =B{(0,0,\delta ,\delta )}^{T}$ . In the expression () one distinguishes a part of the strain linked to the displacements $B.U$ and another part $-D.\delta$ linked to the jump. The stress for an elastic constitutive law is written:

$$
\sigma =E.\epsilon
$$

With $E$ the elasticity tensor. The elastic energy in the element is therefore defined by:

$$
\Phi (U,\delta )=\frac{1}{2}.{\int}_{{\Omega}_{e}}{(B.U-D.\delta )}^{T}.E.(B.U-D.\delta ).d\Omega
$$

##### 2.1.1.2 Surface energy

As we presented it in the theoretical model (see § 1), one chooses a surface energy depending on the norm of the displacement jump $\parallel \delta \parallel$ , on $\kappa$ a positive internal variable (its initial value ${\kappa}_{0}$ is zero) and on the orientation of the crack (only for the contact condition). One takes:

$$
\Psi (\delta ,\kappa )=H(\parallel \delta \parallel -\kappa ).{\Psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}(\parallel \delta \parallel )+\left[1-H(\parallel \delta \parallel -\kappa )\right].{\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}(\parallel \delta \parallel ,\kappa )+{I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}(\delta \cdot n)
$$

The indicator function ${I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}$ makes it possible to take into account the condition of non-interpenetration of the crack faces:

$$
{I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}({\delta}_{n})=\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}+\infty & \text{si}\delta \cdot n<0\\ 0& \text{si}\delta \cdot n\ge 0\end{array}
$$

Depending on the value of the threshold, the surface energy will be equal to ${\Psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}$ or to ${\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}$ (plus the indicator function). In the first case one will speak of a dissipative regime, in the second case of a linear regime. One can write the two energies in the form:

$$
{\Psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}(\parallel \delta \parallel )={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{e}}{\psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}(\parallel \delta \parallel ).d\Gamma
$$

with ${\psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}$ and ${\psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}$ surface energy densities. Let us present in detail the values of these densities.

**Surface energy density in the linear regime**

In the case where an existing crack evolves (opening or reclosing) without dissipating energy, i.e. $\parallel \delta \parallel <\kappa$ , the element will be in a linear phase (loading or unloading). One therefore chooses an energy density which is a quadratic function of the norm of the jump:

$$
{\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}(\parallel \delta \parallel ,\kappa )=\frac{1}{2}.P(\kappa ).{\parallel \delta \parallel }^{2}+{C}_{0}
$$

where $P(\kappa )=\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{\kappa}.\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.\kappa )$ is chosen in order to ensure the continuity of the derivative of $\Psi$ at $\kappa$ (i.e. the continuity of the stress in the element from one regime to the other) and ${C}_{0}$ a constant making it possible to ensure the continuity of $\Psi$ at $\kappa$ .

**Energy density in the dissipative regime**

> Drawing inspiration from the idea of Barenblatt [1] of taking into account the process of rupture of the inter-atomic bonds, one assumes that the surface energy is zero at zero and grows towards the Griffith energy when the value of the jump becomes large compared with the characteristic length of the atomic scale. Moreover, relying on the form of the inter-atomic potentials, one chooses an increasing concave energy with a convex derivative. One knows in particular that concavity plays an essential role in dimension 1 in limiting the number of cracks (see Charlotte *et al.* [2]). Thus, in the case where $\parallel \delta \parallel <\kappa$ , one chooses a surface energy density of the following form, illustrated in the figure:

$$
{\Psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}(\parallel \delta \parallel )={G}_{\mathrm{c.}}\left[1-\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.\parallel \delta \parallel )\right]
$$

${G}_{c}$ represents the critical energy-release rate (or toughness) of the material and ${\sigma}_{c}={\Psi}_{\mathrm{rev}}^{'}(0)$ the critical stress.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.14/Cadre2.gif)

Figure 2: Surface energy density as a function of the norm of the jump

#### 2.1.2 Stress vector in the discontinuity element

Let $\overrightarrow{\sigma}=({\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{t})$ be the stress vector in the element. When the element is sound $(\kappa =0)$ , the surface energy is zero. The stress vector is defined from the stress tensor of the elastic constitutive law and from the normal to the line of discontinuity:

$$
\overrightarrow{\sigma}=\sigma \cdot n
$$

One will see later (see § 2.3.1.1 on the initiation criterion) that the jump remains zero as long as the norm of the stress vector does not reach the critical stress ${\sigma}_{c}$ . On the other hand, if the threshold $\kappa$ is not zero, the stress vector in the element is given by the derivative of the surface energy density with respect to the jump (the stress vector remains equal to $\sigma \cdot n$ since the element ensures the continuity of the normal stress, see § 2.2.2).

##### 2.1.2.1 Linear regime

In the linear regime ($\parallel \delta \parallel <\kappa$ ), the stress vector is equal to:

$$
\overrightarrow{\sigma}=\frac{\partial {\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}}{\partial \delta }=P(\kappa ).\delta
$$

And its derivative with respect to the jump:

$$
\frac{\partial \overrightarrow{\sigma}}{\partial \delta }=\mathrm{Id}.P(\kappa )
$$

##### 2.1.2.2 Dissipative regime

In the dissipative regime ($\parallel \delta \parallel >\kappa$ ), the stress vector is equal to:

$$
\overrightarrow{\sigma}=\frac{\partial {\Psi}_{\mathit{dis}}}{\partial \delta }={\sigma}_{c}.\frac{\delta}{\mathrm{\parallel }\delta \mathrm{\parallel }}.\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.\mathrm{\parallel }\delta \mathrm{\parallel })
$$

And its derivative with respect to the jump:

$$
\frac{\partial \overrightarrow{\sigma}}{\partial \delta }={\sigma}_{c}.\left[\frac{\mathrm{Id}}{\parallel \delta \parallel }-\frac{\delta}{\parallel \delta \parallel }\otimes \frac{\delta}{\parallel \delta \parallel }.(\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}+\frac{1}{\parallel \delta \parallel })\right].\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.\parallel \delta \parallel )
$$

### 2.2 Properties of the discontinuity element

The discontinuity element is a four-node quadrangle with an internal displacement jump. A first part will be devoted to the geometric description of the element as well as to the presentation of a parametrisation relying on the reference element. Next, we shall see that the element ensures the continuity of the normal stress across the displacement jump. Then, we shall show the uniqueness of the displacement jump provided that the size of the element is sufficiently small. Finally, we shall see that the choice of a constant jump introduces a parasitic surface energy which tends towards zero when one refines the mesh.

#### 2.2.1 Geometry of the element and parametrisation

##### 2.2.1.1 Geometry

Let $(X,Y)$ be the Cartesian basis constituting a global frame of ${ℝ}^{2}$ . The discontinuity element, denoted ${\Omega}_{e}$ , is a four-node quadrilateral finite element. It is made up of an elastic region and of a discontinuity ${\Gamma}_{e}$ passing through the centre of the element (a segment passing through the mid-points of the sides $[\mathrm{AD}]$ and $[\mathrm{BC}]$ ) and of length $l$ (see figure ).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.14/Cadre16.gif)

Figure 4: Discontinuity element
The orientation of the discontinuity defines a local frame of the element $(n,t)$ . The corresponding reference element is a square $\stackrel{ˆ}{{\Omega}_{e}}$ defined by the domain $[-1,1]\times [-1,1]$ (see figure ). Each of the elements has four Gauss points. Those of the reference element have coordinates $(\pm \sqrt{(3)}/3,\pm \sqrt{(3)}/3)$ . Let us denote ${\omega}_{g}$ and $\stackrel{ˆ}{{\omega}_{g}}$ the weights of the Gauss points respectively in the real configuration and in the reference configuration. Finally, let us recall that the approximation of the displacement field in the element was presented in section § 2.1.1.1 .

##### 2.2.1.2 Parametrisation

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.14/Cadre1.gif)

Figure 6: Parametrisation of the discontinuity element
Let $T$ be the geometric transformation which associates with a point $(x,y)$ of the discontinuity element a point $(\eta ,\xi )$ of the reference element. Let us seek to parametrise the position of an arbitrary point $M$ of the discontinuity element by $(\eta ,\xi )$ , its coordinates in the frame of the reference element.

Let $P$ , $Q$ and $M$ be the points (see figure ) such that:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AP}}=\frac{1}{2}.(1+\xi ).\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AB}}\\ \overrightarrow{\mathrm{DQ}}=\frac{1}{2}.(1+\xi ).\overrightarrow{\mathrm{DC}}\\ \overrightarrow{\mathrm{PM}}=\frac{1}{2}.(1-\eta ).\overrightarrow{\mathrm{PQ}}\end{array}
$$

Using the sum of the vectors, one has:

$$
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AM}}=\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AP}}+\overrightarrow{\mathrm{PM}}=\frac{1}{2}.(1+\xi ).\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AB}}+\frac{1}{2}.(1-\eta ).\overrightarrow{\mathrm{PQ}}
$$

One rewrites the vector $\overrightarrow{\mathrm{PQ}}$ :

$$
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{PQ}}=\overrightarrow{\mathrm{PA}}+\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AD}}+\overrightarrow{\mathrm{DQ}}
$$

Expanding with the expressions of ():

$$
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{PQ}}=\frac{1}{2}.(1+\xi ).\overrightarrow{\mathrm{BA}}+\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AD}}+\frac{1}{2}.(1+\xi ).\overrightarrow{\mathrm{DC}}
$$

Grouping the terms, one finally obtains:

$$
\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AM}}=\frac{1+\eta }{2}.\frac{1+\xi }{2}.\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AB}}+\frac{1-\eta }{2}.\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AD}}+\frac{1-\eta }{2}.\frac{1+\xi }{2}.\overrightarrow{\mathrm{DC}}
$$

And therefore:

$$
dM=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\mathrm{dx}\\ \mathrm{dy}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\frac{N(\xi )}{2}.d\eta +\frac{T(\eta )}{2}.d\xi
$$

With the two vectors of the frame on the discontinuity:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}N(\xi )=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{N}_{x}\\ {N}_{y}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\frac{(1+\xi )}{2}.\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AB}}-\frac{(1-\xi )}{2}.\overrightarrow{\mathrm{DC}}-\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AD}}\\ T(\eta )=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{T}_{x}\\ {T}_{y}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\frac{(1+\eta )}{2}.\overrightarrow{\mathrm{AB}}+\frac{(1-\eta )}{2}.\overrightarrow{\mathrm{DC}}\end{array}
$$

One then has the parametrisation:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\mathrm{dx}\\ \mathrm{dy}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left[J\right].\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}d\eta \\ d\xi \end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

Where $\left[J\right]$ is the Jacobian matrix of the transformation:

$$
\left[J\right]=\frac{1}{2}.\left[\begin{array}{cc}{N}_{x}& {T}_{x}\\ {N}_{y}& {T}_{y}\end{array}\right]
$$

**Note**: The discontinuity being situated at the centre of the element, one can define the vectors of the local frame attached to it as well as its length from the vector $T$ at $\eta =0$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}n={R}_{-\pi /2}T(0)/\parallel T(0)\parallel \\ t=T(0)/\parallel T(0)\parallel \\ l=\parallel T(0)\parallel \end{array}
$$

#### 2.2.2 Continuity of the normal stress

Relying on the previous parametrisation, one shows in [6] how the discontinuity element ensures the continuity of the normal stress across the displacement jump when the stress is homogeneous in the element. In other words, one shows that the stress vector $\overrightarrow{\sigma}={\psi}^{'}(\delta )$ of the constitutive law CZM_EXP (see § 2.1.2) is equal to the normal stress in the element $\sigma .n$ .

#### 2.2.3 Condition of existence and uniqueness of the jump in the element

From a numerical point of view it is important to place oneself in a situation in which the search for the jump in the element leads to a unique solution. In other words it is necessary that the solution of the minimisation problem () possess a single solution for $U$ and $\kappa$ fixed.

$$
\underset{\delta \in {ℝ}^{2}}{\min}{E}_{T}(U,\delta ,\kappa )
$$

In [6] one shows that the existence and the uniqueness of the jump are ensured as soon as the following condition, bearing on the geometry as well as on the material parameters of the element, is ensured:

$$
\frac{\mu}{16}.\sum_{g}(\frac{1}{{\omega}_{g}}.\underset{{\eta}_{g}}{\min}{\parallel T({\eta}_{g})\parallel }^{2})>l.\frac{{\sigma}_{c}^{2}}{{G}_{c}}
$$

**Note**: In the particular case where the discontinuity element is rectangular, the weights of the Gauss points are equal to a quarter of the area of the element. Moreover, the length of the element is equal to the length of the discontinuity $l$ . If one denotes by $e$ the width of the element, one has for every $g$ :

$$
{\omega}_{g}=\frac{l.e}{4}
$$

Moreover $\parallel T\parallel =l$ at each Gauss point, so the uniqueness condition becomes a condition on the width of the element:

$$
e<\mu .\frac{{G}_{c}}{{\sigma}_{c}^{2}}
$$

It is this last condition which makes it possible to ensure the uniqueness of the jump for the discontinuity elements in *Code_Aster*. One must therefore handle the latter with care when they are not of rectangular shape and make sure that the condition given by () is indeed satisfied.

#### 2.2.4 Parasitic energy

The constant jump in the discontinuity element leads to the introduction of a parasitic energy at the interface between two adjacent elements. One shows in [6] that this energy tends towards zero when one refines the mesh.

### 2.3 Minimisation of the total energy

Adopting the principle of energy minimisation, the aim of this part is to present the computation of the displacement jump in the discontinuity elements as well as that of the displacement field. The total energy (see § 2.1.1) is not convex with respect to the pair $(U,\delta )$ . The search for a global minimum for such a functional is not possible with the numerical method which we shall use. Moreover, at imposed force, the total energy not being bounded from below, the global minimum does not exist. These two arguments lead us to make the choice of seeking a local minimum. The problem of minimisation of the total energy is written:

$$
\text{Chercher}({U}^{\text{*}},{\delta}^{\text{*}})\text{minimum local de l'énergie totale}{E}_{T}(U,\delta ,\kappa )
$$

In a first section (§ 2.3.1) one will seek to compute ${\delta}^{\text{*}}$ , a local minimum of the total energy at $U$ fixed:

$$
{\delta}^{\text{*}}(U)=\underset{\delta}{\text{argmin}}{E}_{T}(U,\delta ,\kappa )
$$

In the second section (§ 2.3.2), one will content oneself with seeking a necessary condition for ${U}^{\text{*}}$ to be a local minimum of the total energy with $\delta ={\delta}^{\text{*}}(U)$ :

$$
{U}^{\text{*}}=\underset{U}{\text{argmin}}{E}_{T}(U,{\delta}^{\text{*}}(U),\kappa )
$$

This second stage amounts to solving the equilibrium equations taking into account the possible work of the external forces and the displacements imposed on the structure.

**Note**: The numerical treatment of the indicator ${I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}(\delta \cdot n)$ translating the condition of non-interpenetration of the crack faces will be carried out by adding the constraint $\delta \cdot n\ge 0$ to the minimisation problem.

#### 2.3.1 Minimisation of the total energy with respect to the jump

The object of this section is to compute the displacement jumps, on each of the discontinuity elements of a mesh, which minimise the total energy. If one indexes by $i$ the discontinuity elements and if one denotes by ${\delta}_{i}$ the displacement jumps on each of these elements, the minimisation problem is written:

$$
\mathrm{Chercher}\mathrm{les}{\delta}_{i}\text{minima locaux de l'énergie totale, solutions de}\underset{{\delta}_{i},{\delta}_{i}\cdot n\ge 0}{\min}\sum_{i}{E}_{T}(U,{\delta}_{i},\kappa )
$$

Two important points will make it possible to simplify the problem appreciably. First of all, the choice of a displacement jump independent from one element to another makes it possible to minimise the total energy with respect to the jump at an element level (static-condensation technique). One has the following equality:

$$
\underset{{\delta}_{i},{\delta}_{i}\cdot n\ge 0}{\min}\sum_{i}{E}_{T}(U,{\delta}_{i},\kappa )=\sum_{i}\underset{{\delta}_{i},{\delta}_{i}\cdot n\ge 0}{\min}{E}_{T}(U,{\delta}_{i},\kappa )
$$

Moreover, one has seen in § 2.2.3 that, for sufficiently small elements, the displacement jump is unique. Thus, the problem reduces to a search for a global minimum on each element:

$$
\text{Sur chaque élément chercher}\delta \in S\text{minimum global de}{E}_{T}(U,{\delta}_{i},\kappa )
$$

with $S$ the set of the admissible jumps:

$$
S=\lbrace s/s\text{constant par élément et}s\cdot n\ge 0\rbrace
$$

The equilibrium condition, called the first-order optimality condition, becomes necessary and sufficient in order to determine the solution of the problem. It is written in the form:

$$
{E}_{T}(U,\delta ,\kappa )\le {E}_{T}(U,\delta +h\phi ,\kappa )
$$

For every $\phi$ constant per element and for $h$ sufficiently small so that $\delta +h\phi \in S$ . Passing to the limit when $h$ tends towards zero, it becomes a condition on the directional derivative:

$$
\underset{h\to 0}{\lim}\frac{1}{h}.({E}_{T}(U,\delta +h\phi ,\kappa )-{E}_{T}(U,\delta ,\kappa ))\ge 0
$$

For every $\phi$ constant per element such that $\delta +h\phi \in S$ . In general, the directional derivative is a function positively homogeneous of degree one in $\phi$ .

In what follows we shall present the computation of the displacement jump on an element, relying on the optimality condition. One will begin by bringing out a necessary and sufficient condition, which one will call the initiation criterion, for the zero jump to be the solution of the problem. Then we shall detail the computation of the jump after initiation, for the two types of behaviour of the element: linear and dissipative.

##### 2.3.1.1 Initiation criterion

In this part we shall seek to determine under what condition the zero displacement jump is the solution of the problem (). Let us note that the choice of making the surface energy depend on the norm of the jump implies that the total energy does not admit a derivative at zero. Consequently the derivative of the energy in the direction $\phi$ will not be a linear function but positively homogeneous of degree one in $\phi$ . Using the definition of the total energy (see § 2.1.1) when the element is sound (i.e. when $\kappa =0$ ), one computes its derivative in the direction $\phi$ at zero:

$$
\underset{h\to 0}{\lim}\frac{1}{h}.({E}_{T}(U,h\phi ,\kappa )-{E}_{T}(U,0,\kappa ))=-\sum_{g}{\omega}_{g}.{\phi }^{t}.{D}_{g}^{t}.E.{B}_{g}.U+l.{\sigma}_{c}.\parallel \phi \parallel
$$

The optimality condition () therefore leads to the inequality:

$$
-\sum_{g}{\omega}_{g}.{\phi }^{t}.{D}_{g}^{t}.E.{B}_{g}.U+l.{\sigma}_{c}.\parallel \phi \parallel \ge 0
$$

For every $\phi \in S$ . Now, in order to show the continuity of the normal stress (see [6]), one has seen that:

$$
\sum_{g}{\omega}_{g}.{D}_{g}^{t}.E.{B}_{g}.U=\mathrm{l.}\overrightarrow{\sigma}
$$

Moreover $\phi$ is constant on the element, so () becomes:

$$
-\overrightarrow{\sigma}\cdot \phi +{\sigma}_{c}.\parallel \phi \parallel \ge 0
$$

Setting $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\phi }_{n}=\rho .\cos\theta \\ {\phi }_{t}=\rho .\sin\theta \end{array}$ with $\rho >0$ and $\frac{-\pi }{2}\le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$ so that ${\phi }_{n}\ge 0$ , and using the definition $\overrightarrow{\sigma}=({\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{t})$ , one obtains the condition:

$$
{\sigma}_{n}.\cos\theta +{\sigma}_{t}.\sin\theta \le {\sigma}_{c}\forall \theta \in \left[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}\right]
$$

A study of the function $f(\theta )={\sigma}_{n}.\cos\theta +{\sigma}_{t}.\sin\theta$ gives:

$$
\underset{-\frac{\pi}{2}\le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}}{\sup}f(\theta )=\sqrt{\text{<}{\sigma}_{n}{\text{>}}_{+}^{2}+{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}
$$

With the definition of the positive part of a quantity $\text{<}.{\text{>}}_{+}=\max(.,0)$ . The inequality () therefore leads to the following initiation criterion in stress:

$$
\sqrt{\text{<}{\sigma}_{n}{\text{>}}_{+}^{2}+{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}\le {\sigma}_{c}
$$

##### 2.3.1.2 Computation of the jump

Let us seek to solve the minimisation problem in the case where the jump is not zero. In this case, the directional derivative is a linear function of $\phi$ ; the optimality condition () is written:

$$
\underset{h\to 0}{\lim}\frac{1}{h}.({E}_{T}(U,\delta +h\phi ,\kappa )-{E}_{T}(U,\delta ,\kappa ))=\frac{\partial {E}_{T}}{\partial \delta }\cdot \phi \ge 0
$$

For every $\phi$ constant per element such that $\delta +h\phi \in S$ . By playing on the admissible directions one deduces the conditions:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}\frac{\partial {E}_{T}}{\partial {\delta}_{n}}.{\phi }_{n}\ge 0& \forall {\phi }_{n}\ge 0& \text{si}{\delta}_{n}=0\\ \frac{\partial {E}_{T}}{\partial {\delta}_{n}}.{\phi }_{n}=0& \forall {\phi }_{n}& \text{si}{\delta}_{n}>0\\ \frac{\partial {E}_{T}}{\partial {\delta}_{t}}.{\phi }_{t}=0& \forall {\phi }_{t}& \end{array}
$$

Moreover, by approximating the integral by a discrete sum on the Gauss points, the derivative of the total energy in the direction $\phi$ is written:

$$
\frac{\partial {E}_{T}}{\partial \delta }\cdot \phi =-\sum_{g}{\omega}_{g}.{\phi }^{t}.{D}_{g}^{t}.E.({B}_{g}.U-{D}_{g}\delta ).\phi +l.\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial \delta }\cdot \phi
$$

With $\psi$ the surface energy density, equal to ${\psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}$ in the linear regime and to ${\psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}$ in the dissipative regime. In order to lighten the notations one defines $S$ in the following way:

$$
S=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{S}_{n}\\ {S}_{t}\end{array}\right\rbrace =-\sum_{g}{\omega}_{g}.{D}_{g}^{t}.E.{B}_{g}.U
$$

et $Q$ :

$$
Q=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{Q}_{\mathrm{nn}}& {Q}_{\mathrm{nt}}\\ {Q}_{\mathrm{nt}}& {Q}_{\mathrm{tt}}\end{array}\right]=-\sum_{g}{\omega}_{g}.{D}_{g}^{t}.E.{D}_{g}
$$

One therefore has the simplified writing of ():

$$
\frac{\partial {E}_{T}}{\partial \delta }\cdot \phi =(S+Q.\delta )\cdot \phi +l.\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial \delta }\cdot \phi
$$

Let us now seek to compute the displacement jump from the conditions using the derivative of the energy written in the form (). One will distinguish the computation in the linear regime from that in the dissipative regime and for each of them one will distinguish the case where the normal jump is zero from that where it is not.

**Computation of the jump in the linear regime**

In the linear regime the surface energy density is given by:

$$
\psi ={\psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}=\frac{1}{2}.P(\kappa ).\delta \cdot \delta
$$

The derivative of the total energy with respect to the jump () becomes:

$$
\frac{\partial {E}_{T}}{\partial \delta }\cdot \phi =(S+Q.\delta )\cdot \phi +l.P(\kappa ).\delta \cdot \phi
$$

In the case of a zero normal jump $(0,{\delta}_{t})$ , the optimality conditions () lead to:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{S}_{n}+{Q}_{\mathrm{nt}}.{\delta}_{t}\ge 0\\ {\delta}_{t}=-\frac{{S}_{t}}{{Q}_{t}+l.P(\kappa )}\end{array}
$$

The tangential jump is given explicitly by the second condition. The first imposes that the normal stress in the element be negative or zero. If this were not the case, there would necessarily be a normal jump. In other words, the jump in the element will be zero as soon as the element is put in compression. This translates the taking into account of the non-interpenetration of the crack faces.

In the case of a strictly positive normal jump $({\delta}_{n}>0,{\delta}_{t})$ , the optimality conditions () give directly the displacement jump:

$$
\delta =-{(Q+\mathrm{Id}.l.P(\kappa ))}^{-1}.S
$$

In the linear regime the internal variable $\kappa$ does not evolve.

**Computation of the jump in the dissipative regime**

In the dissipative regime the surface energy density is given by:

$$
\psi ={\psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}={G}_{c}.\left[1-\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.\parallel \delta \parallel )\right]
$$

The derivative of the total energy with respect to the jump () becomes:

$$
\frac{\partial {E}_{T}}{\partial \delta }\cdot \phi =(S+Q.\delta )\cdot \phi +l.{\sigma}_{c}.\frac{\delta \cdot \phi }{\parallel \delta \parallel }.\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.\parallel \delta \parallel )
$$

In the case of a zero normal jump $(0,{\delta}_{t})$ , the optimality conditions () lead to:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{S}_{n}+{Q}_{\mathrm{nt}}.{\delta}_{t}\ge 0\\ {S}_{t}+{Q}_{\mathrm{tt}}.{\delta}_{t}+l.{\sigma}_{c}.\text{sign}({\delta}_{t}).\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.∣{\delta}_{t}∣)=0\end{array}
$$

The first condition implies that the normal jump is zero when the normal stress in the element is negative. This translates the taking into account of the non-interpenetration of the crack faces. From the second condition, one deduces that $\text{sign}({\delta}_{t})=-\text{sign}({S}_{t})$ , so ${\delta}_{t}=-\text{sign}({S}_{t}).\beta$ with $\beta >0$ is the solution of the following non-linear scalar equation:

$$
∣{S}_{t}∣+{Q}_{n}.\beta +l.{\sigma}_{c}.\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.\beta )=0
$$

which one solves by a Newton algorithm.

In the case of a strictly positive normal jump $({\delta}_{n}>0,{\delta}_{t})$ , the optimality conditions () lead to the following non-linear equation:

$$
S+Q.\delta +l.{\sigma}_{c}.\frac{{\delta}_{t}}{\parallel \delta \parallel }.\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.\parallel \delta \parallel )=0
$$

One writes $\delta =r.\tilde{\delta}$ with $r>0$ and $\tilde{\delta}=(\cos\theta ,\sin\theta )$ where $\frac{-\pi }{2}\le \theta \le \frac{\pi}{2}$ so that $\tilde{\delta}\cdot n\ge 0$ . According to () one has:

$$
\tilde{\delta}=-{(r.Q+l.{\sigma}_{c}.\mathrm{Id}.\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.r))}^{-1}.S
$$

It remains to compute $r$ . For this it suffices to solve the non-linear scalar equation $\parallel \tilde{\delta}\parallel =1$ by a Newton algorithm. In the dissipative regime the internal variable $\kappa$ evolves; it corresponds to the norm of the computed jump $\kappa =\parallel \tilde{\delta}\parallel$ .

#### 2.3.2 Minimisation of the total energy with respect to the displacements

After having computed the displacement jumps ${\delta}^{\text{*}}(U)$ on each of the elements with a discontinuity, the objective is to compute the displacement field which minimises the total energy. The problem is formulated in the following way:

$$
\text{Chercher}{U}^{\text{*}}\text{minimum local de l'énergie totale}{E}_{T}(U,{\delta}^{\text{*}}(U),\kappa )
$$

The functional ${E}_{T}(U,{\delta}^{\text{*}}(U),\kappa )$ is not convex in $U$ . In order to be a local minimum, the solution must satisfy the conditions of equilibrium and of stability. The second condition concerns the second derivative of the functional and requires the use of a method specifically devoted to minimisation. The Newton algorithm used does not make it possible to check this second condition. One will therefore content oneself with checking the equilibrium condition, a necessary condition for the displacement to be a solution of the problem ().

Consider a structure $\Omega$ with the following loading:

- $f$ body-force density on $\Omega$ *;* 
- $F$ surface-force density on ${\Gamma}_{N}$ ;
- ${U}^{d}$ prescribed displacements on ${\Gamma}_{D}$

${\Gamma}_{D}$ and ${\Gamma}_{N}$ are disjoint parts of the boundary of $\Omega$ . The work of the external forces is written:

$$
{W}^{\mathrm{ext}}(U)={\int}_{\Omega}f.U.d\Omega +{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{N}}F.U.d{\Gamma}_{N}
$$

The total energy of the structure is then written:

$$
{E}_{T}(U,{\delta}^{\text{*}}(U),\kappa )=\Phi (U,{\delta}^{\text{*}}(U))+\Psi ({\delta}^{\text{*}}(U),\kappa )-{W}^{\mathrm{ext}}(U)
$$

With $U$ belonging to the space of kinematically admissible displacement fields. The first-order optimality condition, a necessary condition for $U$ to be a local minimum, is written:

$$
\frac{\partial {E}_{T}}{\partial U}(U,{\delta}^{\text{*}}(U),\kappa ).V\ge 0
$$

For every admissible test field $V$ . One knows that:

$$
\frac{\partial {E}_{T}}{\partial \delta }(U,{\delta}^{\text{*}}(U),\kappa )=0
$$

Which gives:

$$
{\int}_{\Omega}{B}^{t}.E.(B.U-D.{\delta}^{\text{*}}(U)).V.d\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}f.V.d\Omega -{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{N}}F.V.d{\Gamma}_{N}\ge 0\forall V
$$

This inequality becomes an equality by taking well-chosen $V$ , and, since this equality holds for every admissible $V$ , one obtains the equilibrium between the internal and external forces:

$$
{F}^{int}(U)={F}^{\text{ext}}
$$

With:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{F}^{int}(U)={\int}_{\Omega}{B}^{t}.E.(B.U-D.{\delta}^{\text{*}}).d\Omega \\ {F}^{\text{ext}}={\int}_{\Omega}f.d\Omega +{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{N}}F.d{\Gamma}_{N}\end{array}
$$

Let $C$ denote the linear operator expressing the prescribed-displacement conditions. The dualisation of these conditions leads to the following system:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{F}^{int}(U)+{C}^{t}.\lambda ={F}^{\text{ext}}\\ C.U={U}^{d}\end{array}
$$

The unknowns are henceforth at every time instant the pair $(U,\lambda )$  where $\lambda$ represents the Lagrange multipliers associated with the Dirichlet conditions. The method used to solve is a Newton method (see the documentation of STAT_NON_LINE [R5.03.01}). The computation of the tangent matrix is explained in the appendix.

## 3 Method of piloting of the loading

The method of piloting of the loading is intended to take into account the instabilities of the structure, and thus to follow possible “snap-backs” of the global force–displacement response. A particular case of this method, initially developed by Lorentz and Badel  [7] , has been adapted to the preceding cracking model.

The principle of the piloting method as well as the solution of the new global system which follows from it is explained in detail in [R5.03.80](doc:r5.03.80). We shall detail the part of the piloting specific to the law CZM_EXP by explaining the choice of the piloting control equation as well as the method for solving it.

### 3.1 Piloting control equation

The aim of this part is to present the piloting control equation as well as its solution for the internal-discontinuity model CZM_EXP. The unknown of this equation is the intensity of the loading ${\eta}_{i}^{n}$ at the computation time instant $i$ and at the Newton iteration $n$ which one will henceforth denote

\overrightarrow{\sigma}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{n}\\ {\sigma}_{t}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\frac{1}{l}.\sum_{g}{\omega}_{g}.{D}_{g}^{t}.E.({B}_{g}.U-{D}_{g}\delta )

And $R(\kappa )$ the stress-threshold variable defined by ():

With ${\delta}_{n}\ge 0$ and $\kappa$ the jump threshold. We therefore place ourselves in the case where ${\kappa}^{-}=0$ .The aim here is to explain the solution of () for a given element, without taking into account the $max$ over all the elements (taking the $max$ into account is explained in [R5.03.80](doc:r5.03.80)). One therefore seeks to solve:

$$

Let us first make $\eta$ appear explicitly in the expression of the criterion before $\tilde{{F}_{\mathrm{el}}}(\eta )$ explaining the solution of the equation. At the time instant $i$ one seeks the displacement ${U}_{i}$ which is expressed at the Newton iteration $n$ :

And the piloted part:

Directly:

$$
{\mathrm{U}}_{i}={\mathrm{U}}_{\text{impo}}+\eta .{\mathrm{U}}_{\text{pilo}}

The displacement jump being zero one has:

By using the decomposition ():

\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{n}\\ {\sigma}_{t}\end{array}\right\rbrace ={\mathrm{S}}_{\text{impo}}+\eta .{\mathrm{S}}_{\text{pilo}}

With:

#### 3.1.2 Solution of the equation with the jump criterion

The aim here is to solve the piloting control equation for a given element in the case where ${\kappa}^{-}>0$ , still without taking into account the $max$ over all the elements. One seeks to solve:

The solution of the piloting control equation therefore amounts to finding the intersection between a straight line and an arc of a circle or the intersection between a straight line and a segment.

In summary, one solves:

- The polynomial (), of degree two in $\eta$ (straight-line/circle intersection): one accepts the $\eta$ which satisfy $({\delta}_{\text{impo}}+\eta .{\delta}_{\text{pilo}})\cdot n\ge -{\kappa}^{-}.\Delta \tau$ ;
- The polynomial $({\delta}_{\text{impo}}+\eta .{\delta}_{\text{pilo}})\cdot n=-{\kappa}^{-}.\Delta \tau$ , of degree one in $\eta$ : $({\delta}_{p}+\eta {\delta}_{d})\cdot n=-{\kappa}^{-}\Delta \tau$ (straight-line/segment intersection); one accepts the $\eta$ which satisfy $∣({\delta}_{\text{impo}}+\eta .{\delta}_{\text{pilo}})∣\cdot t\le d$ with $d={\kappa}^{-}.\sqrt{1+2.\Delta \tau }$ .

One will denote ${\eta}_{k=0,1,2}$ the accepted solutions. As regards the choice among these solutions one will refer to [R5.03.80](doc:r5.03.80).

## 4 APPENDIX: Computation of the tangent matrix for the element with a discontinuity

In this part we shall detail the computation of the derivative of the internal forces with respect to the displacement: $\frac{\partial {F}^{int}}{\partial U}$ which appears in ${K}_{T}$ the tangent matrix of the system involved in the Newton algorithm. One has:

$$
{F}^{int}(U)={\int}_{\Omega}{B}^{t}.E.(B.U-D.{\delta}^{\text{*}}).d\Omega
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{\partial {F}^{int}(U)}{\partial U}={\int}_{\Omega}{B}^{t}.E.(B-D.\frac{\partial {\delta}^{\text{*}}}{\partial U}).d\Omega
$$

One must compute the term $\frac{\partial {\delta}^{\text{*}}}{\partial U}$ on each element with a discontinuity ${\Omega}_{e}$ (static condensation). The minimisation with respect to $\delta$ of the total energy has led us to compute ${\delta}^{\text{*}}$ solution of the equation:

$$
{\int}_{{\Omega}_{e}}{D}^{t}.E.(B.U-D.{\delta}^{\text{*}}).d\Omega =l.{\psi}^{'}({\delta}^{\text{*}})
$$

Let us differentiate this equation with respect to $U$ :

$$
{\int}_{{\Omega}_{e}}{D}^{t}.E.(B.U-D.\frac{d{\delta}^{\text{*}}}{dU}).d\Omega =l.{\psi}^{\text{''}}({\delta}^{\text{*}}).\frac{d{\delta}^{\text{*}}}{dU}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{d{\delta}^{\text{*}}}{\mathrm{dU}}={\left[l.{\Psi}^{''}(\delta \text{*})+{\int}_{{\Omega}_{e}}{D}^{t}.E.D.d\Omega \right]}^{-1}.{\int}_{{\Omega}_{e}}{D}^{t}.E.B.d\Omega
$$

It then suffices to compute ${\Psi}^{''}$ ; one distinguishes three cases:

1. If $\delta =0$ , then ${\Psi}^{''}=0$ ;
2. If the element is in the linear regime, ${\Psi}^{''}={\Psi}_{\mathrm{lin}}^{''}=\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{\kappa}.\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.\kappa ).{\mathrm{Id}}_{2\times 2}$ ;
3. If the element is in the dissipative regime, ${\Psi}^{''}={\Psi}_{\mathrm{dis}}^{''}=C.{\mathrm{Id}}_{2\times 2}+\tilde{C}.\delta \otimes \delta$ with

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}C=\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{\parallel \delta \parallel }.\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.\parallel \delta \parallel )\\ \tilde{C}=-(\frac{1}{\parallel \delta \parallel }+\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}).\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{\parallel \delta \parallel }^{2}}.\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}.\parallel \delta \parallel )\end{array}
$$
