# u2.06.13 General advice on the use of the operator DYNA_NON_LINE

**Summary:**

This document presents the use of transient solution methods (implicit or explicit) for the numerical simulation of non-linear dynamic problems on the physical basis.

The general-purpose reference operator for this type of analysis is called DYNA_NON_LINE and its correct use will be made easier by respecting a few rules of good practice described in this document.

This advice on use covers:

- the correct definition of the model in the dynamic sense (including the initial conditions and the boundary conditions),
- the definition of the discretisation, including the choice of the time scheme ([R5.05.05](doc:r5.05.05), and also see u2.06.13-Bibliographie),
- the choice of the damping models,
- a few pieces of advice on post-processing.

Given the great diversity of non-linear problems, the user will find it very useful to complete this reading with other, more specific references:

- [U2.06.10](doc:u2.06.10): on the specific features of civil-engineering studies under seismic loading,

- [U2.04.07](doc:u2.04.07): use of DYNA_NON_LINE to solve problems in slow evolution which are strongly non-linear and which have difficulty converging with STAT_NON_LINE (see the bibliography),

## 1 Introduction

In order to have as fine an evaluation as possible of the response of a mechanical system to a given set of loadings, it can prove indispensable to take into account the non-linearities and the dynamic phenomena.

Usually, one can distinguish two lines of evolution of the numerical methods in order to arrive at this:

- on the one hand, the classical linear dynamic analyses (often on a modal basis) are called into question by the possible appearance of non-linearities (material, contact/friction or large transformations) which require the use of the operator `DYNA_NON_LINE` (the operator `DYNA_VIBRA` in transient analysis on a modal basis accepts only local non-linearities, for example of shock-node type),
- on the other hand, one may question the validity and the limits of application of the non-linear quasi-static approaches (operator [STAT_NON_LINE](doc:u4.51.03)), when the evolution of the solution can be subjected to transient phenomena whose time scale becomes small compared with the natural characteristics of the structure.

In the first case, the user therefore starts from a model which is relevant for linear dynamics and which must be enriched correctly for the taking into account of the non-linearities.

In the second case, it is rather the reverse: the user starts from a model which is relevant in non-linear quasi-statics and which it is appropriate to adapt to dynamics.

This documentation will attempt to guide users who are potentially faced with these two situations.

Given the very great variety of problems liable to be addressed, the rules proposed here are necessarily fairly general and it is highly probable that case-by-case adjustments for specific problems will be indispensable. It is totally illusory to think that, even if all the specifications of this document are respected, the non-linear analysis will proceed without any surprise… The work of expert assessment remains unavoidable!

As a preliminary to reading this documentation it is strongly recommended to have read the reference documentations of the operators `STAT_NON_LINE` and `DYNA_NON_LINE`: [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01) and [R5.05.05](doc:r5.05.05). Indeed, the theoretical aspects will be little detailed here because they are already addressed in these two reference documents.

This documentation is completed by other, more specific references:

## 2 Definition of a problem suited to transient dynamics

In this chapter, advice will be proposed for the setting up of the modelling of a problem in mechanics whose non-linear transient numerical simulation one wishes to carry out with *Code_Aster*. The following chapter will deal with the discretisation in space and in time, whereas in this chapter only the continuous model will be addressed.

The non-linearities in transient dynamics with DYNA_NON_LINE are:

* the large displacements and the large transformations,
* the non-linear constitutive relations (plasticity, damage, viscoplasticity...),
* the interface non-linearities: frictional contact, collisions...

### 2.1 Model

The first step is the definition of a model consistent with the hypothesis of transient evolution.

Thus, since any mechanical system must not have a mode with zero kinetic energy, one should make sure that the density is defined at every point of the continuous model. Likewise, if one wants to introduce discrete elements in order to simulate solid bodies, one must associate a mass with them.

Likewise, for finite elements of shell or plate type, the user may have to make sure that all the degrees of freedom, in particular the rotations, have an associated mass term. In order to check this condition, it is advised to specify the option INER_ROTA='OUI' when calling the command [AFFE_CARA_ELEM](doc:u4.42.01) for all the COQUE models.

Certain modellings which have no mass matrix (this computation has not been programmed) cannot be used directly with DYNA_NON_LINE, such as the non-local models. This difficulty can be got round by superimposing on them a classical model making it possible to represent the kinetic energy (mass matrix): the two models being supported by the same nodes.

The use of certain devices which are current in quasi-statics, such as very stiff zones (a fictitious material having a very large Young's modulus) in order to take into account reinforcements which one does not wish to represent finely from the geometric point of view, for example, can generate disturbances in dynamics. Indeed, this very stiff material can generate high-frequency oscillations and wave propagations whose speed is non-physical. Moreover, with an explicit time scheme, these very stiff zones risk causing the value of the critical time step to drop (CFL stability condition, *cf.* [R5.05.05](doc:r5.05.05)).

In addition to the inertial contribution, the system will have a dissipative contribution, and therefore a damping one. In the non-linear regime, dissipation can be due to the constitutive relation (plasticity…), to friction in the links…

Usually, the lack of detailed knowledge of all the dissipative mechanisms in the system is compensated by a simplified representation which makes it possible to define a global damping. In *Code_Aster*, one has available two models of global viscous damping (which one can associate in the modelling with dampers on discrete elements, *cf.* command [AFFE_CARA_ELEM](doc:u4.42.01) (factor keyword DISCRET or DISCRET_2D, etc...): the Rayleigh model and modal damping.

In practice, in both cases, it is indispensable to have carried out a prior modal analysis. Indeed, the modal damping is defined on the modes, which are then arguments of DYNA_NON_LINE. For Rayleigh damping, the only way of grasping its physical meaning simply is to recalibrate it on values of modal damping (which can come from experimental results). Rayleigh damping will be returned to in more detail in the following chapter, u2.06.13-Discretisation_du_probleme_continu.

In a general manner, one must keep in mind that all the global damping models were designed for the linear cases and that they then serve to take into account certain dissipative phenomena such as friction or imperfect links. Therefore, if one adds to the model the taking into account of these non-linearities, while keeping the global damping values recalibrated on a linear approach, this can lead to having too much dissipation in the non-linear system, in particular at low frequencies.

Ideally, the taking into account of all the non-linearities, associated with a fine discretisation of the system, ought to make it possible not to have to use in addition a blanket global damping. In reality, one is obliged to neglect certain non-linear aspects for reasons of CPU cost and/or of lack of knowledge of the mechanisms brought into play, and in this case the global damping has its role to play. Documentation [U2.06.03](doc:u2.06.03) presents its use in more detail.

### 2.2 Boundary conditions and excitations

The first decision to be taken in order to solve the transient is the choice of the configuration frame (the displacement field): the general case is a Galilean frame (absolute frame). In frequent cases where the system is driven in a rigid translation motion, one will be able to solve the transient in the relative frame. The analysis will be carried out with a fixed base; see u2.06.13-Mono_appui.
If the system is loaded by various supports excited differently, one is in the case u2.06.13-Multi_appuis and one will have to solve the transient in the absolute frame (unless the constitutive behaviour is entirely linear elastic under the small-perturbation hypothesis, HPP).

In quasi-statics, it is current to impose boundary conditions whose evolutions in time are simple functions to define, such as ramps or continuous functions which are piecewise affine.

It is frequent that in dynamics the excitations are described by the product of a function of space and of a ramp or a function of time (or a signal in time). The factor keyword EXCIT will thus be used.

It is recommended to define this signal in time so that it starts and finishes at zero. It can also be useful for the list of time instants chosen for the transient with DYNA_NON_LINE to last longer than the imposed signal, so as to let the system under study return to rest through the effect of the damping which has been chosen.

In dynamics, if one wants to avoid parasitic oscillations of the solution, one must absolutely impose quantities which are sufficiently regular in time: therefore at least continuously differentiable, piecewise. In order to arrive at this simply, one can choose to define for oneself polynomial functions of time in place of the affine functions. Possible irregularities in the imposed loadings can be partially compensated by the use of a suitable damping model (or in particular thanks to a dissipative time-integration scheme of HHT type; see u2.06.13-Schema_integration_temps). Nevertheless, excessive oscillations in the non-linear regime can irremediably compromise the rest of the analyses, because the solution (including the internal variables) depends on its history.

On the diagram u2.06.13-lissage a possible regularisation of an affine curve (in dashed lines) by a curve suited to the dynamic analysis is shown. One can note the necessity, on this example, of having to make the dynamic evolution start at a negative time, if one wants to keep comparable values for the positive time instants (outside the regularisation zones).

![Figure](/figures/u2.06.13/Shape1.gif)

*Smoothing of an imposed evolution in order to adapt it to a transient analysis*

In all the cases where one can compute the spectrum of the imposed signals, the Shannon rule for defining the sampling is not strict enough for the non-linear analyses. In practice, it is advised to have between 4 and 10 time steps over the shortest period of all the imposed signals.

Any insufficient discretisation in time risks being reflected by high-frequency over-oscillations of the solution. There then exists a palliative for this in choosing certain time schemes, but at the risk of introducing too much damping into the system.

#### 2.2.1 Single support

Loadings of single-support type (driving by a rigid translation motion), which are currently employed in seismic analyses, are characterised by particular features which it is appropriate to recall here.

First of all, the solution, under the single-support hypothesis, is carried out natively in the relative frame linked to the support (basemat...). In post-processing, one must therefore recombine with the driving motion in order to obtain the absolute kinematic fields. For the acceleration, this is in general immediate because the driving acceleration is the known input datum in the form of the accelerograms employed.

On the other hand, in order to reconstruct the absolute displacement, one must have available the driving displacement. In the classical case where one has available only accelerograms, one is obliged to integrate twice in time in order to compute the corresponding displacement (this integration can be done with the operator [CALC_FONCTION](doc:u4.32.04), keyword INTEGRE). The user must then be very attentive to the problems of the numerical integration of sampled signals, such as integration constants which can induce a drift or oscillations at very low frequency. The operator [CALC_FONCTION](doc:u4.32.04) has options for correcting these drifts (keyword CORR_DEPL and `CORR_ACCE`), but it is very strongly recommended to check carefully the quality of the final integrated result because, in certain cases, these corrections are insufficient, including the correction by high-pass filtering of [CALC_FONCTION](doc:u4.32.04) (with `CORR_ACCE`, `METHODE = 'FILTRAGE'`). A complementary specific correction is then indispensable. Going through the FFT in order to carry out the integration in frequency can then constitute a good alternative, making it possible to arrive at integrated signals which are more correct or at least easier to correct (just a shift by a constant, for example, but without drift).

#### 2.2.2 Multiple supports

Among the types of loading which can require an adaptation to DYNA_NON_LINE, one can mention the case of multiple supports (in imposed accelerations on the supports) for seismic studies.

This method, originally developed for transient analyses under the linear hypothesis, relies on the definition of elastic static modes at the supports. In the non-linear regime, as long as these modes remain relevant, that is to say that the non-linearities are weak enough not to require their updating, the usual multi-support approach remains acceptable.

On the other hand, in the general case, one must call into question the use of elastic static modes computed initially. In this case, in order to avoid their use, the multi-support loading must be taken into account by imposing at the supports the displacements corresponding to the integration of the accelerograms which are input data of the usual linear multi-support method.

The method is then rigorous, whatever the type of non-linearity, but the integration of the accelerograms must be carried out with care, so as not to arrive at incorrect signals.

Apart from the sampling, it is indispensable, among other things, to check the absence of drift of the signal (a correction can be done with the option `CORR_ACCE` of [CALC_FONCTION](doc:u4.32.04)). In *Code_Aster*, one can carry out this integration easily by making use of the `FFT` which is available in [CALC_FONCTION](doc:u4.32.04). It is also the occasion to analyse the frequency content of the signal. One then has the relation, in the frequency domain: $U=-A/{\omega}^{2}$ .

One can also make use of the option `INTEGRE` of [CALC_FONCTION](doc:u4.32.04). For more information on the implementation of loadings of single- or multi-support type, reading documentations [U2.06.09](doc:u2.06.09) and [U2.06.10](doc:u2.06.10) is entirely appropriate.

#### 2.2.3 Contact and collisions

The loadings coming from contact by penalisation can also disturb the solution by high-frequency oscillations, which are linked to the value of the penalisation coefficient. These artefacts can be reduced with the use of a time-integration scheme which is dissipative at high frequencies.

One can also provide oneself with a list of analysis time instants which manages the difficulties by subdivision, *cf.* the command [DEFI_LIST_INST](doc:u4.34.03) with the factor keyword `ECHEC= _F(EVENEMENT = 'DELTA_GRANDEUR', ...)`, of “event-driven” type, by testing whether the increment of a component of a field (of velocity, for example, at the contact point) exceeds a fixed threshold.

If one wants to use an explicit time-integration scheme, too large a penalisation coefficient will lead to a drop in the critical time step. Compared with statics, it can prove obligatory to lower the penalisation coefficient, with the drawback of increasing the interpenetration of the solids during contact.

One may consult the test cases:

* sdnv100, impact of a beam on a rigid wall [V5.03.100](doc:v5.03.100),
* sdnv105, rocking of a block on a table [V5.03.105](doc:v5.03.105),
* sdnv301, Hertzian collision of two elastic spheres [V5.03.301](doc:v5.03.301).

**Note**

For the explicit time schemes ():ref:u2.06.13-Schema_integration_temps), if one imposes displacement boundary conditions which evolve in the course of time, one must take into account the fact that these conditions are in fact imposed in acceleration in the explicit case (because it is the primal unknown). This means that one must enter into DYNA_NON_LINE the second derivative of the displacement signal that one wants to impose. This evolution of the imposed displacement must therefore be differentiable at least twice in time

### 2.3 Initial conditions

In the same logic as for the boundary conditions, it is recommended to avoid singular initial conditions, such as those which can be generated by imposed loadings having evolutions in time of Dirac or Heaviside type.

Regular conditions will be favoured, therefore with derivatives (first and if possible second) in time which are zero at ${t}_{0}$.

If one wants to start from a prestressed (non-virgin) state, it is entirely possible and recommended to chain a dynamic analysis onto a quasi-static analysis. In this case, the initial state (factor keyword ETAT_INIT) must then be perfectly equilibrated (displacements, stresses, internal variables, forces applied at this time instant) and, subject to regularity in the conditions imposed at the moment of the passage from statics to dynamics, the dynamic excitation having to start with a zero signal, the transient solution must not present non-physical oscillations. This method of chaining from quasi-statics to dynamics allows the easy taking into account of all the preloads, such as for example gravity.
For example, in a release simulation, with a non-zero imposed initial displacement, one must then supply the stresses and internal variables corresponding to this time instant.

If one had wanted to take this prestressed initial state into account with only a dynamic solution, it would have been necessary to introduce a strong initial damping and to wait until the oscillations had dissipated in order to have the prestressed static state. One can then continue the analysis with the physical damping. One is obliged to proceed in this way when one uses an analysis code which does not treat non-linear quasi-statics (*cf.* Europlexus, see [bib2]_).

In certain situations, one must chain a simulation in statics, with blocked boundary conditions, and then in dynamics under the action of a field of incident waves with absorbing and released boundary conditions. One will consult [U2.04.08](doc:u2.04.08).

During the initialisation of the integration scheme for the non-linear transient analysis, one therefore seeks to invert the mass matrix. If it is singular, then a message warns the user and the initial acceleration is arbitrarily set to zero. One can then fear the presence of parasitic oscillations at the beginning of the transient; one can reduce them by adopting a smaller time step right at the beginning of the transient. In a general manner the non-invertibility of the mass matrix must lead the user to check the model, unless it is intentional.

During restarts, two aspects are sensitive.

On the one hand, it is better to avoid having too sudden variations of the time step: the consistency of the solution at the passage of the restart would be affected by it. A strong change of time step can be seen as a filter. A large time step constitutes a low-pass filter of a solution initially computed with a fine time step.

On the other hand, if one wants to change the type of time scheme, certain rules are to be respected. If one wants to pass from an implicit time-integration method (quasi-static or dynamic) to an explicit scheme, the restart will be mathematically valid because the initial state will be equilibrated (to within the residual). On the other hand, the direct reverse switch introduces an error because the initial state, coming from an explicit analysis, will not respect equilibrium in the implicit sense. Indeed, this operator will seek to recheck equilibrium: one solves the equilibrium by inverting the mass matrix, which gives the initial acceleration. If this field is non-zero, this reflects the failure to verify, in the static sense, the equilibrium, at the initial time instant. In practice, this inaccuracy can generate oscillations of the solution (overshooting phenomenon). In a more subtle manner, the switch from the $\theta$-scheme to a second-order scheme such as the complete average acceleration `NEWMARK` or `HHT` will introduce a small error on the acceleration terms, which can disturb the numerical solution. If, despite everything, one wants to make these switches which induce errors, one can minimise the inaccuracies by choosing to make these switches during phases in which the excitation, and therefore the solution, evolves little. In summary:

| Origin | Destination | Possible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| STAT_NON_LINE | DYNA_NON_LINE | yes |
| DYNA_NON_LINE with an implicit time scheme | DYNA_NON_LINE | yes |
| DYNA_NON_LINE with an explicit time scheme | DYNA_NON_LINE with an explicit time scheme | yes |
| DYNA_NON_LINE with an explicit time scheme | DYNA_NON_LINE with an implicit time scheme | no |

## 3 Discretisation of the continuous problem

In addition to the advice previously given for the continuous model, this chapter will list the most important aspects to be respected in order to obtain a discretised model suited to [DYNA_NON_LINE](doc:u4.53.01).

### 3.1 Mesh

As a preliminary to the transient analysis, it is strongly recommended to carry out a modal analysis (with [CALC_MODES](doc:u4.52.02)), in order to obtain modal information which will make it possible to qualify the quality of the model in dynamics and to adjust certain parameters. The objective not being to go into the details of modal analysis, a few rules can nevertheless be recalled.

In general, one can define for oneself a cut-off frequency for the problem to be studied, and therefore an associated modal truncation. The proper representation of all the modes of this truncated basis can give indications of the mesh-cell sizes to be employed, in addition to the considerations already taken into account for the quasi-static analyses. Roughly, about ten mesh cells per smallest wavelength is sufficient (to be adjusted according to the richness of the finite elements, of course).

The modal analysis will also make it possible to check that the model is free of problems such as contributions to the inertia or to the stiffness that are not defined.

Finally, it is indispensable for the use of modal damping in DYNA_NON_LINE or for recalibrating the Rayleigh damping, as will be seen in what follows (*cf.* u2.06.13-Mod_lisation_de_l_amortissement).

In a manner much more marked than for the quasi-static analyses, the dynamic solution will cope rather badly with meshes presenting sudden variations in finite-element sizes. Indeed, these zones can be likened to interfaces which will disturb the propagation of the waves. One can then see reflected waves appear which are superimposed on the “physical” wave trains. Likewise, if one wants a good representation of the waves, the mesh must be fine over the whole path of the waves: one cannot be content with refining only in certain non-linear zones. If a wave leaves a finely meshed zone in order to go towards a more coarsely meshed zone, it will undergo a filter and the wave reflected by the opposite boundary risks being strongly disturbed, or even disappearing. If one is interested only in short durations, therefore before any return of a wave onto the finely meshed zone, then this error on the reflected wave is not penalising. On the other hand, if one wants to compute solutions over longer durations, the error committed may be non-negligible.

Finally, unlike the quasi-static case, time has a physical meaning and the time scales that one wants to analyse are strongly coupled to the spatial scales of the discretised problem. Thus, the time step is linked to the mesh-cell size, which one can perceive immediately with the notion of the CFL stability condition for the explicit schemes.

### 3.2 Time-integration scheme

Time having a physical meaning in dynamics, the quality of its discretisation is all the more sensitive for that. An integration scheme has the role of expressing by an approximate expression two relations between the quantities (`DEPL`, `VITE`, `ACCE`) at the previous time instant and those at the following time instant. The equations of motion constitute the third set of equations which makes it possible to close the system to be solved. According to the principal quantity (`DEPL`, `VITE`, `ACCE`) which one chooses as the driver in order to express these equations, one adopts a type of `FORMULATION`: `DEPLACEMENT`, `VITESSE`, `ACCELERATION`.

A few rules can be stated for the choice of the time step:

- the evolution of the imposed loadings must be sampled sufficiently finely (between 5 and 10 time steps per shortest period of the signals considered),
- the modal behaviour of the structure must be well represented (as above, one must have between 5 and 10 time steps per lowest period of the modes considered up to the cut-off frequency); in particular, the frequency distortion caused by the time-integration scheme must be limited: the choice of taking between 5 and 10 time steps per lowest period of the modes considered up to the cut-off frequency ensures a frequency distortion of less than one per cent.

Given the low-frequency character, or at best medium-frequency character, of most of the problems that one can address here, these two rules are, in general, not very penalising.

The choice of the time-integration scheme and of its parameters is made with the factor keyword: `SCHEMA_TEMPS =_F(…)`.

Test cases SDLD31 [V2.01.031](doc:v2.01.031) and SDLD32 [V2.01.032](doc:v2.01.032) offer a comparison of the time-integration schemes proposed in DYNA_NON_LINE and in [DYNA_VIBRA](doc:u4.53.03) on simple linear systems under harmonic excitation.

#### 3.2.1 Explicit integration scheme

With an explicit integration scheme, conditionally stable, dedicated to problems of “fast dynamics” in the presence of transients with a “high-frequency” content, one must in addition respect the Courant stability condition (known as “CFL”, *cf.* [R5.05.05](doc:r5.05.05) and u2.06.13-Bibliographie) on pain of numerical divergence (“explosion” of the kinetic energy). In *Code_Aster*, these schemes are treated in `FORMULATION ='ACCELERATION'` (one wants to solve by inverting the mass matrix). The mass matrix, which must be invertible, is preferentially chosen in diagonal form (*lumped*) for reasons of performance; this is obtained by the keyword: `MASS_DIAG =  'OUI'`. The *lumped* mass matrix makes it possible to correct part of the frequency drift over long durations coming from the error in time induced by the integration scheme. For the structural finite elements, for example DKT/DKTG/DST, it is necessary to specify in AFFE_CARA_ELEM, under the factor keyword `COQUE`, the simple keyword `INER_ROTA = 'OUI'`, so as to have non-zero inertia terms over the whole diagonal of the mass matrix, even if they are small, [R3.07.03](doc:r3.07.03).

However, this option not being available for all the finite elements, nor with all the discrete elements, one must then solve with the consistent mass matrix, as in the implicit case. For the finite elements of HM modelling, the mass matrix has zero terms for certain specific degrees of freedom, for example the pressure. In this case too, one must solve with the consistent mass matrix, to which one adds a small contribution of the stiffness matrix with the keyword `COEF_MASS_SHIFT`.

Two explicit schemes are available:

- central differences (`DIFF_CENT`), which is non-dissipative,
- Tchamwa-Wielgosz (`TCHAMWA`), which is dissipative, in a manner comparable to HHT.

It is recommended to begin by using a non-dissipative scheme.

The automatic computation of the Courant condition on a modal basis is exact and will always be valid whatever the type of finite element used.

For the explicit integration scheme of central differences `DIFF_CENT`, the critical time step is worth $2/{\omega}_{max}$ where ${\omega}_{max}$ is the highest natural angular frequency of the discretised system. The presence of shock stiffnesses associated with the penalised modelling of frictional or frictionless contact significantly increases this highest natural angular frequency. In the presence of physical damping (reduced damping coefficient $\xi$) the critical time step has the expression: $2 \left(\sqrt{1+\xi^2}-\xi\right)/{\omega}_{max}$.

One can compute this angular frequency with `CALC_MODES` by choosing the option `'PLUS_GRANDE'` (or with the option `PLUS_PETITE` by inverting the roles of the mass matrix and of the stiffness matrix).

For the `TCHAMWA` scheme of dissipative explicit integration of Tchamwa-Wielgosz (see [bib11]_), the critical time step is slightly smaller than that of the `DIFF_CENT` scheme and decreases when one increases the numerical damping linked to the scheme (parameter `PHI`, whose default value is $1.05$).

The Courant stability condition can also be approximated, at least on models with solid finite elements, by $\Delta t={l}_{\min}/c$ where ${l}_{\min}$ is the smallest length of the discretised model and $c$ the speed of the tension waves at the point considered.

The operator `DYNA_NON_LINE` makes use of this formula in order to give an approximation of the Courant stability condition. There are however certain limitations:

1. one does not know how to compute automatically the Courant stability condition associated with the presence of discrete springs (it is not programmed),
2. one does not correct the formula for the structural finite elements (shells, plates and beams).

There can therefore exist cases where the value returned is not a lower bound of the true Courant condition. Therefore, in the event of divergence, it is appropriate to reduce the time step.

Moreover, the computation of the Courant stability condition is not updated and is done only at the beginning of the analysis, being based on the speed of the elastic waves for the initial state. If the elastic modulus decreases (damage), the initial Courant stability condition can become too severe. There is no risk of divergence (except for materials whose elastic modulus could increase), but the CPU time could be reduced a little by updating the Courant stability condition (as is done in the codes dedicated to fast dynamics, such as Europlexus [bib2]_).

For most structures, the Courant stability condition is very penalising: the wave speed often being of the order of a few thousand $m/s$ , one arrives at time steps of less than ${10}^{-5}s$ , for finite-element dimensions chosen for usual structures. This therefore imposes a very high total number of time instants in order to describe the transient, and therefore produces a considerable volume of results.

If one solves the problem on a reduced modal basis (factor keyword `PROJ_MODAL =_F()`), then the drawback of the very small critical time step for an explicit scheme disappears. Indeed, the limiting time step will be directly proportional to the smallest natural period of the truncated modal basis. One has the relation: $\Delta t=2/\omega$ where $\omega$ is the highest natural angular frequency of the system. The more the modal basis is truncated, the larger the associated critical time step will be.

Moreover, the computation of the Courant condition is then immediate because one knows explicitly all the angular frequencies of the basis, and therefore the highest one in particular.

With an explicit scheme, it is recommended to place oneself slightly below the Courant condition: between 0.5 and 0.7 times the Courant stability condition. There will be no subdivision of the time step on account of non-convergence at the scale of the global equilibrium: the user remains the sole master of the time step throughout the analysis. On the other hand, via the factor keyword COMPORTEMENT, *cf.* [U4.51.11](doc:u4.51.11), the user indicates the requirements for the integration of the non-linear constitutive law, which can lead to the automatic subdivision defined in the command [DEFI_LIST_INST](doc:u4.34.03).

- if one imposes displacement boundary conditions which evolve in the course of time, one must take into account the fact that these conditions are in fact imposed in acceleration in the explicit case (because it is the primal unknown). This means that one must enter into `DYNA_NON_LINE` the second derivative of the displacement signal that one wants to impose. This evolution of the imposed displacement must therefore be differentiable at least twice in time
- The use of quadratic finite elements is not recommended in the explicit case: indeed, parasitic oscillations can appear on the solution fields (displacement or stresses). This phenomenon can also be amplified by the joint use of the consistent mass matrix
- For the verification of the plane-stress hypothesis, if one uses the de Borst method, the associated global iterative process is then blocked at the first iteration (unlike the implicit case). This can lead to a slight inaccuracy of the results, tempered by the fact that, the time step being in any case very small, the non-planarity of the stresses can only be very moderate. However, it is entirely possible to use the local version of the de Borst algorithm by means of the keyword `ITER_CPLAN_MAXI` under the factor keyword `COMPORTEMENT`, *cf.* [Non-linear constitutive behaviours](doc:u4.51.11). Indeed, these local iterations do take place in the explicit case, with an associated extra computational cost.

**Notes**

#### 3.2.2 Implicit integration scheme

With an implicit integration scheme, which makes it possible to respect as well as possible the non-linear dynamic equilibrium conditions, dedicated to problems of “slow dynamics” in the presence of transients with a “low-frequency” content, in a phase of setting up the transient simulation, it is recommended to make a comparison of the results obtained for two choices of values of the time step.
In the implicit case, the schemes proposed are unconditionally stable, but this does not mean that one can take an arbitrary time step! Too large a step will not lead to a divergence, but the error on the solution, such as the frequency distortion, will obviously be large.

These schemes are accessible in `FORMULATION="DEPLACEMENT"`, `FORMULATION ="VITESSE"`, `FORMULATION ="ACCELERATION"`. The `FORMULATION ="DEPLACEMENT"` is entirely natural and is favoured in the case of material non-linearities, because the internal forces result from the treatment of the constitutive relation expressed from the strains, themselves obtained with the displacement field. It is not possible to use a *lumped* mass matrix with these schemes.

It is recommended to ensure a uniform and regular sampling of the time step as far as possible. However, in the presence of constitutive non-linearities, it is neither possible nor desirable to avoid the temporal resubdivision in the event of non-convergence of the Newton algorithm, driven by the step-subdivision directives which one will have defined in the command [DEFI_LIST_INST](doc:u4.34.03).

One can classify the implicit schemes in two categories:

- average acceleration (`NEWMARK`), of order 2 and which brings no numerical dissipation: to be used first,
- complete HHT (`MODI_EQUI = 'OUI'`, the default option), which remains of order 2, unlike the case of the modified average acceleration. If one desires more damping in the medium-frequency range, then the modified average-acceleration scheme can be employed with `MODI_EQUI = 'NON'`, and the scheme becomes of order 1. The complete HHT scheme (`MODI_EQUI = 'OUI'`) is specifically developed in order to introduce a high-frequency numerical damping and therefore not to disturb the low-frequency physical response. The damping is driven directly by the parameter ALPHA of the scheme,

If one observes high-frequency oscillations in the numerical solution (roughly, oscillations whose period is of the order of a few time steps), one will prefer to choose the complete HHT scheme, beginning with a value of the order of $–0,1$ for the parameter `ALPHA`. A value of $–0,3$ constitutes an upper limit that is still usable. It is strongly recommended to carry out a parametric study on the value of the parameter `ALPHA` in order to choose the value closest to zero which makes it possible to obtain a correct solution without numerical over-damping.

One will refer to paragraph §5.4 of [R5.05.05](doc:r5.05.05).

The implicit schemes are to be used, as a priority, with a formulation in displacements: `FORMULATION = 'DEPLACEMENT'`.

#### 3.2.3 Summary for the choice of the integration scheme

The following table summarises the essential points.

| name | `NEWMARK` | `HHT` | `DIFF_CENT` | `TCHAMWA` |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| type | implicit | implicit | explicit | explicit |  |
| applications | simulations of “long duration” for low-frequency excitations | likewise | short-duration simulations | likewise |  |
| FORMULATION | `"DEPLACEMENT"` | `"DEPLACEMENT"` | `"ACCELERATION"` | `"ACCELERATION"` |  |
| mass matrix | consistent | consistent | diagonal preferentially | diagonal preferentially |  |
| stability | unconditional | unconditional | conditional | conditional |  |
| order | order 2 except if $\gamma \neq 1/2$ | order 2 | order 2 | order 1 |  |
| numerical dissipation | no if $\gamma=1/2$ and $\beta=1/4$ | yes at HF if $\alpha<0$ | no | yes at HF if $\varphi>1$ |  |
| frequency distortion | in $-{(\omega_{max} \Delta t)}^2$ | in $-(\omega_{max} \Delta t)^2$ | in $+(\omega_{max} \Delta t)^2$ |  |  |
| amplitude error | in $\omicron (\Delta t )^2$ |  | in $\omicron (\Delta t )^4$ |  |  |
| choice of the time step | limit the frequency distortion | limit the frequency distortion | guarantee stability | guarantee stability |  |

To finish, it should be pointed out that the analytical results on the characteristics (convergence, frequency distortion, error, numerical damping, overshooting…) of the time schemes are obtained for a linear framework. Demonstrations in the non-linear regime are very rare and are confined to particular cases. In practice, certain characteristics of the schemes can deteriorate in the non-linear regime. This can explain why it is not necessarily indispensable to use the scheme which, in the linear regime, will have exceptional performance (order 4…), but that it is better to favour simpler and more robust schemes, in particular with high-frequency numerical dissipation.

Likewise, in the non-linear regime, the precise evaluation of the Courant stability condition of an explicit scheme requires an updating of its computation. Indeed, the Courant stability condition computed initially may not be conservative (for example if certain finite elements see their size decrease, or if shocks occur, with a modelling by penalisation). This computation is not updated and, in the event of divergence, it is recommended to reduce the time step and to re-run the analysis.

The results of linear analysis on the schemes nevertheless constitute a solid basis for their analysis (*cf.* [R5.05.05](doc:r5.05.05) and u2.06.13-Bibliographie), while knowing that the non-linearities can disturb the behaviour of the schemes.

The time step to be imposed can judiciously be bounded:

- from above, by the time step to be respected in order to discretise well the evolutions of the imposed loadings and in order to represent well the highest natural frequency of the system which one wants to take into account,
- from below, by the Courant stability condition, in the sense that it has of the shortest time for which a piece of information can pass from one node of the mesh cell to another.

Between these bounds, it is indispensable to carry out a parametric study in order to make sure of the proper convergence of the numerical solution.

The lower bound gives crucial information as to the maximum subdivisions of the time step which must be authorised when the algorithm does not converge. Letting the time step subdivide until it goes markedly below the Courant stability condition may be of no use, because the computed solution then risks undergoing a numerical pollution which will not facilitate convergence.

Still in connection with the iterations for the verification of equilibrium at each step, one can note that, in most cases, if the time step is sufficiently fine, the maximum number of iterations to convergence remains moderate: often of the order of 10, whereas in quasi-statics one can currently exceed these values.

Therefore, the idea consists in saying that the time step is of a good order of magnitude if the number of iterations to convergence remains moderate. If this number increases, one can attempt to reduce the step slightly, while always respecting the bounds defined above. There nevertheless exist cases where one can, at certain points, need to authorise more iterations during a few steps.

To finish, if one is obliged to change time scheme in order to use a dissipative scheme, such as HHT, it is indispensable to carry out a parametric study on this damping. Indeed, the risk of introducing too great a dissipation is not negligible, especially with the modified average-acceleration scheme. The following paragraph will return to this point.

### 3.3 Initial conditions

Regular initial conditions will be favoured: displacements and velocities differentiable at $t=t_0$, a state of stresses in equilibrium at $t=t_0$, for example resulting from a quasi-static analysis with [STAT_NON_LINE](doc:u4.51.03). Indeed, many time schemes evaluate a predictor of the initial acceleration from the initial state, by inverting the mass matrix (if this can be done): if this initial acceleration is excessive, the disturbance can propagate over the transient, especially if the damping is weak, and generate parasitic oscillations of the solution (“overshooting”).
If the mass matrix is not invertible, see section u2.06.13-Modification_de_la_matrice_de_masse.

### 3.4 Modelling of the damping

The order of introduction and of use of the dissipation in a modelling of a structure discretised by finite elements is the following:

1. intrinsic dissipation linked to the irreversible non-linear constitutive relations of the constituent materials assigned to the support mesh cells of the finite elements, *cf.* factor keyword `COMPORTEMENT=_F(RELATION='...')` *cf.* [Non-linear constitutive behaviours](doc:u4.51.11). These constitutive models can dissipate energy in the course of cycles in strain-stress (for example in elastoplasticity) and/or in strain-rate–stress (for example in non-linear viscoelasticity, in viscoplasticity),
2. intrinsic dissipation linked to the irreversible non-linear constitutive relations of the links on interfaces (collision, friction...): *cf.* factor keyword `COMPORTEMENT` with, as relations: `DIS_CHOC`, `DIS_CONTACT`, `CHOC_ENDO`, `CHOC_ENDO_PENA`, with the help of the parameters AMOR_NOR and AMOR_TAN of the corresponding materials, *cf.* [R5.03.17](doc:r5.03.17) and [AFFE_CARA_ELEM](doc:u4.43.01), which dissipate energy in the course of cycles in displacement-jump–force (for example in elastoplasticity) and/or in displacement-jump-rate–force. Damping forces proportional to the velocity on these interfaces are added to the equations of motion, as a function of the state of activation or otherwise of the frictional contact, without going through a contribution to the damping matrix,
3. dissipation linked to the linear viscous damping assigned to discrete elements, *cf.* command [AFFE_CARA_ELEM](doc:u4.42.01) (factor keyword `DISCRET` or `DISCRET_2D`, keyword `CARA = ’A_T_D_N’`, etc...),
4. global dissipation of structural-damping type (Rayleigh or modal, also called Wilson & Penzien, which one can use simultaneously),
5. dissipation linked to the absorbing boundaries (Sommerfeld condition modelled with paraxial finite elements of order 0) of an elastic domain or of a perfect fluid domain (`3D_ABSO`, `D_PLAN_ABSO`, `3D_FLUI_ABSO`, `FLUI_ABSO`, `2D_FLUI_ABSO`, `AXIS_FLUI_ABSO`), *cf.* [R4.02.05](doc:r4.02.05),
6. numerical dissipation brought by the time scheme.

Ideally, the first categories ought to be sufficient, but in practice, for reasons of simplification of the modelling, it is often indispensable to add structural damping, the damping brought by the time scheme being the last resort.

Only the use of structural damping and that linked to the time-integration scheme will be addressed here (for more information on this point, the reader may refer to documentation [U2.06.03](doc:u2.06.03)).

The global dissipation of structural-damping type is taken into account by modal damping or by Rayleigh damping.

The transient solution with `DYNA_NON_LINE` uses:

- the global damping matrix assembling by summation all the contributions: the global Rayleigh damping, the linear viscous damping assigned to discrete elements, the finite elements of absorbing boundaries (paraxial of order 0), solid and fluid, the constant matrix defined for the condensed substructures (dynamic macro-elements), and the matrix which `DYNA_NON_LINE` assembles from an elementary damping matrix built outside the operator in order to be able to add (accumulate) possibly a damping of another nature, for example `IMPE_MECA` in the U-P formulation of perfect-fluid–structure interaction. It is the purpose of the optional keyword `MATR_ELEM_AMOR` to supply it to `DYNA_NON_LINE`. See test case [zzzz420a](doc:v1.01.420),
- the vector of viscous forces assembling all the contributions: modal damping.

It is possible to use at the same time the global Rayleigh damping, as well as the modal damping and also the numerical damping of the time scheme. In these cases of combinations of several sources of damping (without counting the material dissipation due to the constitutive relation chosen or the frictional contact), the user must check the global consistency of the dissipation and therefore not risk counting the damping several times on one and the same zone. For example, if one wishes to combine global damping (Rayleigh or modal) and material dissipation, in general the regulations impose lowering the value of the global damping compared with a purely elastic analysis.

One will note, *cf.* [R4.02.05](doc:r4.02.05), that one must not employ paraxial finite elements in the case `MULTI_APPUI`.

Another example: the case of a building on seismic isolation bearings. If one wants to represent the dissipation of the bearings by a local model (non-linear law or dampers) and the damping of the building by modal damping, one will have to check that one does not introduce modal damping on the modes which mainly work the bearings, such as the horizontal rigid-body modes (two translations and the rotation about the vertical axis).

Let us finally recall that the more one multiplies the sources of dissipation, the more arduous their control and their physical interpretation will be.

#### 3.4.1 Structural damping

In non-linear transient dynamics, *Code_Aster* offers two types of modellings of this type:

1. modal damping (of Wilson & Penzien), defined in a uniform manner over the whole of the finite-element model, after having established a reduced modal basis over a given representative frequency band, intervening in the form of assembled viscous forces, linear in the velocity vector,
2. global Rayleigh damping, defined according to the assignment of the materials on the mesh, intervening in the form of an assembled matrix carrying out the linear relation between the viscous forces and the velocity vector.

For a linear system with two degrees of freedom these two modellings give the same viscous forces if the calibration of the Rayleigh damping is carried out on the two natural frequencies. In the other cases, the viscous forces differ.

If one solves the equations of motion in the driven frame, considering a rigid translation driving motion, the driving inertia forces being defined with the command [:ref:CALC_CHAR_SEISME<U4.63.01>] (MONO_APPUI= 'OUI'), then the damping is built with the relative velocity vector. One recalls that in non-linear dynamics, *cf.* [R5.05.05](doc:r5.05.05), if the motions imposed at the supports are not a rigid-body motion (case `MULTI_APPUI`), then one must solve the equations of motion in the absolute (Galilean) frame.

##### 3.4.1.1 Modal damping

As regards modal damping, the respect of the three basic rules is insisted upon:

- check that the associated modal basis (`MODE_MECA`) is sufficiently complete (containing at a minimum the whole spectrum of the excitation) so as not to have an absence of damping along certain response modes of the structure,
- check that all the values of modal damping coefficients (`AMOR_REDUIT`/`LIST_AMOR`) are indeed positive for all the modes retained (`MODE_MECA`), because any negative value risks leading to a dynamic instability,
- the modal damping being treated in an explicit manner in `DYNA_NON_LINE` (the viscous forces being expressed with the velocities obtained at the previous step), it may be necessary to reduce the time step so that the time integration remains stable, even with a time scheme which is implicit and unconditionally stable.

Indeed, one does not compute the assembled damping matrix because it would be full and therefore expensive.

This modal basis, serving to define the modal dampings, must imperatively have the same numbering profile as that of the matrices of the dynamic system used during the computation of the response proper. Before use of the modal dampings of the modal basis in the computation of the response, this rule therefore requires, if need be, transforming beforehand this initial modal basis into a new modal basis, whose definition of the boundary conditions is identical to that relating to the computation of the response with `DYNA_NON_LINE`, thus ensuring the same number and the same position of the Lagrange multipliers of dualisation. This is in particular the case when non-zero imposed displacements are applied for the computation of the response. Test case zzzz113a details the corresponding procedure.

If the structure studied is made up of several substructures or subdomains, composed of specific materials, whose reduced damping coefficients differ, one practice consists in the following evaluation of an equivalent modal damping coefficient (for a given mode) according to the rule of RCC-G 1988, ETC-C 2012 (section 1.A.5.4). This modelling is accessible, *cf.* [CALC_AMOR_MODAL](doc:u4.52.13) (AMOR_INTERNE=_F(...), AMOR_SOL=_F(...),...). One will take care not to have provided for dampers on discrete elements, at the risk of damping twice!

##### 3.4.1.2 Rayleigh damping

This modelling uses a global viscous damping matrix ${\bf C}$ as being the assembly, on the finite-element model, of a linear combination of the stiffness and mass matrices. This global damping matrix is diagonalised on the basis of the natural eigenmodes only if the calibration of the Rayleigh parameters is identical throughout the finite-element model. The Rayleigh damping parameters are supplied by the factor keyword ’ELAS’ in the operator [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01).

$$
{\bf C} = \alpha {\bf K} + \beta {\bf M}
$$

Three identification cases are presented here in order to illustrate the effects induced by this modelling:

- damping proportional to the inertia characteristics: $\alpha =0$ $\beta ={\beta}_{i}$ .

This case has been very much used in transient solution in the `ACCELERATION` formulation: if the mass matrix is diagonal, that of the damping still is, and the gain in memory space is obvious. The coefficient $\beta$ can be identified with the experimental reduced damping ${\xi}_{i}$ of the eigenmode $({\rho}_{i},{\omega}_{i})$ which participates the most in the response, whence ${\beta}_{i}=2{\xi}_{i}{\omega}_{i}$ . For any other angular frequency one obtains a reduced modal damping $\xi ={\beta}_{i}\frac{{\omega}_{i}}{\omega}$ . The responses on the modes of high order $\omega \gg {\omega}_{i}$ will be very little damped and those on the low-frequency modes $\omega <{\omega}_{i}$ will be too heavily damped.

![Figure](/figures/u2.06.13/100000000000013C0000007DB8575CA3B4D0F32F.png)

*Appearance of the damping proportional to the mass*

This damping proportional to the mass is the only Rayleigh damping which is easily usable with an explicit integration scheme. It is illustrated on figure u2.06.13-amorPropMass. Indeed, one can show that the introduction of a term proportional to the stiffness leads to a drop in the critical time step (CFL condition).

- damping proportional to the stiffness characteristics: $\alpha ={\alpha}_{j}$ $\beta =0$ .

The coefficient can be identified, as previously, from the modal damping ${\xi}_{j}$ associated with the mode $({\rho}_{j},{\omega}_{j})$ , whence ${\alpha}_{j}=2\frac{{\xi}_{j}}{{\omega}_{j}}$ . For any other angular frequency one obtains a reduced modal damping $\xi ={\alpha}_{j}\frac{\omega}{{\omega}_{j}}$ . The responses on the high modes $\omega \gg {\omega}_{j}$ are therefore very heavily damped. It is illustrated on figure u2.06.13-amorPropRigi

![Figure](/figures/u2.06.13/10000000000001210000007AD759DF3D0B349A54.png)

*Appearance of the damping proportional to the stiffness*

- complete proportional damping: $\alpha ={\alpha}_{j}$ $\beta ={\beta}_{i}$.

From an identification on two different natural frequencies $({\rho}_{i},{\omega}_{i})$ and $({\rho}_{j},{\omega}_{j})$ , one will obtain for any other angular frequency a reduced modal damping $\xi ={\alpha}_{j}\frac{\omega}{{\omega}_{j}}+{\beta}_{i}\frac{{\omega}_{i}}{\omega}$ . In the interval $[{\omega}_{i},{\omega}_{j}]$ the variation of the reduced damping is small and outside it one finds again the combination of the previous drawbacks: the responses on the modes outside the interval are too heavily damped.

![Figure](/figures/u2.06.13/100000000000013D000000775C7AF42F4EDB560B.png)

*Appearance of the complete Rayleigh damping*

The complete Rayleigh damping illustrated on figure u2.06.13-amorPropComplet makes it possible to have a damping value that is almost constant over a given frequency plateau, always greater than $\sqrt{\alpha \beta}$, which makes it possible to control its effect over a frequency range defined consistently with the problem considered. However, when the stiffness is damaged, the transient dynamic responses present a central frequency which decreases. The Rayleigh damping perceived is then slightly stronger at low frequencies and weaker at high frequencies.

**Application to the structure**

The global viscous Rayleigh damping coefficients are defined, at the level of the characteristics of the material (command [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01)), by the parameters AMOR_ALPHA and AMOR_BETA in the factor keyword ELAS. There is no specific keyword in DYNA_NON_LINE for activating the taking into account of this damping: if one does not want to use it, one must remove all the occurrences of the keywords AMOR_ALPHA and AMOR_BETA from all the materials of the finite-element model studied.

The values to be imposed in order to obtain the desired damping $\xi$ in the interval of the natural frequencies ${f}_{1}$ and ${f}_{2}$ are deduced from the following equations:

$$
\alpha =\frac{\xi}{\pi ({f}_{1}+{f}_{2})}
$$

$$
\beta =\frac{4\pi \xi {f}_{1}{f}_{2}}{{f}_{1}+{f}_{2}}
$$

where ${f}_{1}$ and ${f}_{2}$ are the two natural frequencies bounding the interval of study considered. In the framework of this document, low-frequency solutions are sought, so the frequencies ${f}_{1}$ and ${f}_{2}$ will be associated with the first frequencies of the model, whose modes are consistent with the imposed loading.

To give orders of magnitude, the modal damping for steel structures is generally of the order of a few %, whereas for concrete structures, of civil-engineering type, one can go up to 5, or even 7 % of damping, for analyses which are globally linear, intended to represent dissipations of energy coming from diffuse degradations or cracking.

For the discrete elements, the Rayleigh damping parameters not being definable in the command [AFFE_CARA_ELEM](doc:u4.42.01), if one uses the operator DYNA_NON_LINE, the Rayleigh damping therefore does not take into account the contribution of the discrete elements. On the other hand, the global damping matrix assembled for the solution will take into account their contribution and that of the Rayleigh damping coming from the finite elements (volumetric, surfacic or of beam type...).

In the case of models coupled in hydromechanics, THM, for the analysis of geomechanical works (earth dams, soil columns...), *cf.* [U2.04.05](doc:u2.04.05), DYNA_NON_LINE cannot build the Rayleigh damping matrix directly. See [U2.04.05](doc:u2.04.05) and [U2.04.08](doc:u2.04.08).
An example of a non-linear dynamic analysis on a hydromechanical problem of a soil column is provided by test case [wdnp101a](doc:v5.03.107).
This difficulty can be got round by superimposing a classical mechanical model on the same mesh, making it possible to represent the kinetic energy (mass matrix) and the elastic energy (stiffness matrix), the two models being supported by the same nodes. Then by building the elementary damping matrix with the desired parameters $\alpha ={\alpha}_{j}$ $\beta ={\beta}_{i}$ , using the command [CALC_MATR_ELEM](doc:u4.61.01) with OPTION='AMOR_MECA' by giving the elementary mass and stiffness matrices (`MASS_MECA` and `RIGI_MECA`), then by specifying the concept obtained in DYNA_NON_LINE with the keyword `MATR_ELEM_AMOR`.

The command `DYNA_NON_LINE` offers a variant of the Rayleigh damping using the tangent stiffness matrix instead of the initial elastic stiffness matrix. This is the purpose of the optional keyword: `AMOR_RAYL_RIGI=("TANGENTE"/ "ELASTIQUE")`. The default value being `ELASTIQUE`. One will then have a Rayleigh damping matrix which is fixed throughout the analysis, resulting from the chosen values of the material characteristics (parameters `AMOR_ALPHA` and `AMOR_BETA`). Of course, the total assembled damping matrix can nevertheless be variable in the presence of other sources of damping, such as what comes from the relations on interfaces: `DIS_CHOC`, `DIS_CONTACT`, `CHOC_ENDO`, `CHOC_ENDO_PENA`...

With the value `TANGENTE` of the keyword `AMOR_RAYL_RIGI`, the updated stiffness matrix will be the same as that which is used for the computation of the internal forces. The choice of the Rayleigh damping with the `TANGENTE` matrix (built with the parameters `AMOR_ALPHA` and `AMOR_BETA` of the materials) introduces a non-linearity on the damping forces and this can generate additional time-step cuttings compared with the computation of the damping with the `ELASTIQUE` stiffness matrix. Indeed, the parasitic high-frequency disturbances are then less damped than with the latter.
If in addition the material has an irreversible non-linear constitutive behaviour with softening damage, then the `TANGENTE` stiffness matrix can have negative eigenvalues, and therefore produce, if one chooses it in order to build the Rayleigh damping matrix, flutter instabilities at parasitic high frequencies.

If the model contains command variables (temperature, irradiation, etc.) then the elastic stiffness matrix is not constant in the course of time, and one must then adopt the updated `TANGENTE` stiffness matrix in order to build the Rayleigh damping.

For the joint finite elements, one notes that the elastic stiffness matrix does not exist; only the updated `TANGENTE` stiffness matrix is available.

#### 3.4.2 Damping due to the time scheme

Documentation [R5.05.05](doc:r5.05.05) and above all note [bib6]_ present this aspect. One will confine oneself here to recalling the main trends.

On a linear system with one degree of freedom (mass-spring, of natural angular frequency $\omega$ ), one can obtain the following characterisation of the damping induced by the implicit scheme (average acceleration, modified average acceleration and complete HHT), as a function of the time step and for different values of the parameter `ALPHA`. To conclude on this part, given the appearances of the Rayleigh damping, as well as that due to the scheme, one can build a total damping which is relatively varied.

In order to choose in an optimal manner the value of the parameter `ALPHA` of the `HHT` scheme, it is indispensable to carry out a parametric study, in order to find the value closest to zero suited to the problem considered (one can advise beginning with a value of -0.1, for example). In order to validate this choice, one can rely on the two types of analysis presented above: control of the oscillations at high frequencies on the accelerations (*cf.* figure) and absence of numerical over-damping on the displacement response (*cf.* figure). On this last aspect, one can compare with the displacement response obtained with a non-dissipative scheme of `NEWMARK` type.

With the complete HHT scheme, the damping at low and medium frequency being weak, one should not neglect to introduce into the model an *ad hoc* modelling of the real damping (global and/or due to the non-linearities).

The appearance of the damping brought by the Tchamwa scheme is qualitatively close to that of the modified average acceleration.

### 3.5 Modification of the mass matrix

In certain specific cases, such as the analyses on mixed hydromechanical models (HM or THM, *cf.* [U2.04.05](doc:u2.04.05)) or fluid-structure models, *cf.* [R4.02.02](doc:r4.02.02) and [U2.04.08](doc:u2.04.08), the cases having a very poor conditioning of the mass matrix, it can be useful to modify the mass matrix in order to make it invertible. This is also useful in order to establish an initialisation of the acceleration at the start of the time-integration scheme which reduces the overshooting.
It is even indispensable, if this is not the case, for the explicit time schemes.

This modification of the mass matrix can be done with the keyword `COEF_MASS_SHIFT` (*cf.* documentation [R5.05.05](doc:r5.05.05)) in the keyword `SCHEMA_TEMPS`. This makes it possible to introduce a “shift” (or offset) of the mass matrix $M$ which becomes: ${\bf M} '={\bf M} +\mathrm{coef.} {\bf K}$. This positive coefficient mathrm{coef.} has as its dimensions the inverse of an angular frequency squared. This offset of the mass matrix does not modify the modes.

This functionality also makes it possible to improve the convergence of the implicit analyses or to increase the value of the critical time step in the explicit case. It must however be pointed out that this amounts to disturbing the mechanical system, in particular by shifting the natural frequencies of the system downwards (and by decreasing the wave speed): the error exceeds 5% on the natural frequencies as soon as they are above $(\sqrt{\mathrm{coef.}})^{-1}$.
For example, an original natural angular frequency $\omega$ then becomes $\omega '$ such that: $\omega {'}^{2}=\frac{{\omega}^{2}}{1+\mathit{coef}.{\omega}^{2}}$.

One observes from this expression that $\omega '$ tends towards a maximum value ${\mathit{coef}}^{-0.5}$ : the modified system therefore introduces a cut-off frequency driven by the value of the “shift” coefficient. Thus, for example, for a value which is a maximum in practice for $\mathrm{coef}={10}^{-6}$ , the maximum corrected natural angular frequency of the system $\omega '$ is worth $1000r{\mathrm{d.s}}^{-1}$ , and a value for an original natural frequency of $30\mathrm{Hz}$ is corrected by this method to $29,481\mathit{Hz}$ . The natural frequencies situated below the new cut-off frequency are therefore slightly altered.

In practice, it is appropriate not to use too large a value of the “shift”, unless one confines oneself to the computation of solutions at very low frequency, or even of quasi-static type. In this last case, the non-linear transient algorithm can then be seen as a regularised solver for the quasi-static problem, which can be useful when the classical static solution does not converge.

In the same line of thought of using a transient solver in order to obtain a static response whose convergence with a usual implicit method is very difficult, one can also increase strongly the mass of the system (for example by acting on the density) and use an explicit time scheme with a strong structural damping. In this case one combines the convergence capability of the explicit method with a critical stability time step which is not too small, because of the increase in the mass (this stability time step will increase as the square root of the coefficient of increase of the total mass: if one multiplies the total mass by 100, the limiting time step will be multiplied by 10 because the wave speed will be ten times smaller). This type of method has already been used for delicate cases such as the simulation of the excavation of underground galleries, where the surrounding soil can become unstable.

### 3.6 Instability and updated modal analysis

In the course of the solution of the transient problem it is possible to use tools of eigenvalue analysis on the updated global operators. One can carry out two types of analysis.

On the one hand, the computation of the natural frequencies and of the vibratory modes with the updated stiffness matrix. This corresponds to the factor keyword `MODE_VIBR` (this option needing implicitly the mass matrix, it is not available in [STAT_NON_LINE](doc:u4.51.03)). The stiffness matrix can then be the elastic, secant or tangent stiffness, to be chosen with the keyword `MATR_RIGI`. Thanks to this factor keyword one can follow the influence of the non-linearities on the vibratory behaviour of a structure. An example of application would be the case of reinforced-concrete structures for which the damage causes the natural frequencies to decrease (with the tangent or secant stiffness). With an elastoplastic structure, having chosen the tangent stiffness for this purpose, one observes a drop in the natural frequencies during the regimes comprising a plastic excursion, whereas one recovers the initial natural frequencies during the elastic regimes.

The graph u2.06.13-instabiliteChoixMatr presents the possible choices for the stiffness matrix (for a damaging material):

![Figure](/figures/u2.06.13/100021B0000069F0000040B3A4BE5E1BF02D329E.svg)

*Schematic representation of the stiffness operators*

On the other hand, one can, thanks to the factor keyword `CRIT_STAB=_F(TYPE='FLAMBEMENT')`, carry out a stability analysis of the stiffness operator. In the case of small perturbations and where one knows how to compute the geometric stiffness, then this option amounts to a buckling analysis in the Euler sense on the updated stiffness matrix. In the other cases, when one does not know how to compute the geometric stiffness, then one switches to the search for the singularity of the stiffness operator alone. In every case one obtains eigenvalues which will evolve in the course of the analysis.

In the case of Euler buckling, the eigenvalue is directly the multiplier coefficient of the loading which makes it possible to obtain the critical load.

In the second case, the interpretation is less easy (the eigenvalues are not dimensionless). If one observes that an eigenvalue changes sign, this means that the computed solution has passed a bifurcation and therefore that one has lost the uniqueness of the solution.

In every case, these are stability analyses in the “static” sense and, moreover, this option is available in `STAT_NON_LINE`. At present, there exists no operator making it possible to carry out a stability analysis in the dynamic sense (flutter, divergence): therefore, for example, by computing the damping of the system in order to detect when it becomes negative.

As these operations require a certain CPU cost (comparable to a call to `CALC_MODES` on the first few frequencies, with the keyword `NMAX_FREQ`, at each time instant requested), the possibility has been introduced of computing these eigenvalues only for the list of archiving time instants if it exists. In order to reduce the CPU time still further, it is also possible to carry out several restarts and to request the computation of the eigenvalues only over certain time intervals or particular time instants.

One can also recover the eigenmodes (respectively buckling modes) associated with the execution of the keyword `MODE_VIBR`. The resultat concept produced by `DYNA_NON_LINE` is then enriched with a table_container. which bears the name `'ANALYSE_MODALE'`.

In order to recover it in the result concept produced by `DYNA_NON_LINE`, one calls the command [RECU_TABLE](doc:u4.71.02). In this table, one will be able to recover the values of the parameters: time instant of the analysis considered, mode number, frequency (respectively critical load).

One will then be able to print with [IMPR_TABLE](doc:u4.91.03) this table, filtering according to these parameters.

By using the command [EXTR_TABLE](doc:u4.71.05), specifying a `TYPE_RESU='MODE_MECA'`, filtering according to the desired parameters, one obtains a result concept, whose name one has chosen, containing the displacement fields of the modes computed, which one will then be able to visualise. One may consult test case sdnv106 [V5.03.06](doc:v5.03.06).

### 3.7 Archiving and post-processing

The number of time steps being liable to be very large, it is strongly recommended to use the archiving functionalities on a coarser list of time instants (factor keyword `ARCHIVAGE` of `DYNA_NON_LINE`) on pain of having enormous databases and output files. The archiving step can range from a few steps in an implicit scheme to 10 to 100 steps in an explicit scheme. Be careful however of the risk of missing possible extreme values in the analysis of the quantities of interest of the computed solution.

In addition, if one needs to follow precisely in the course of time the evolution of a few quantities of interest on a few `GROUP_NO`, there exists the observation (factor keyword `OBSERVATION` of `DYNA_NON_LINE`) which complements the archiving.

As one has been able to see in paragraph § u2.06.13-Amortissement_du_au_schema_en_temps, if one wants to analyse the responses in velocity or in accelerations in the course of time, one can obtain fairly rough curves. These high-frequency oscillations can be the sign of an insufficient discretisation in time (or of too great an irregularity in time) of the problem. It is also possible, by using a dissipative time scheme of complete HHT type, to smooth these disturbances. A compromise remains to be found between this smoothing and too strong a dissipation of the response. In a general manner, one must fully take in that the instantaneous values of the least smoothed quantities, such as the acceleration, are to be handled with caution. It is better to seek to carry out one's analysis on integrated quantities which are more physically relevant in dynamics, such as the energy.

In addition, all the analysis methods coming from quasi-statics for quantifying the quality of a result (including the different norms of the equilibrium residual) are available and relevant with `DYNA_NON_LINE`.

Finally, one can also recall that if one wishes to carry out frequency analyses, one should take care to respect the domain of validity of the FFT, for example the causality of the signal to be treated. For this, this signal in time must start and finish at zero, with zero derivatives. Without respect for these basic hypotheses, the user risks obtaining inaccurate frequency results.

### 3.8 Energy balance

For any transient analysis, it is very useful to be able to have available the global energy balance, over the duration of the transient, which makes it possible to analyse the response of the system, in particular the contribution of the dissipation of energy by damping, and also to check the quality of the numerical solution. Documentation [R4.09.01](doc:r4.09.01) presents all the energy functionalities available with the factor keyword `ENERGIE=_F(CALCUL='OUI')` of the operator `DYNA_NON_LINE`.

One will use for this the factor keyword `ENERGIE=_F()`. It produces the table of name `PARA_CALC`. The energy balance can be extracted from this table with the help of the command [RECU_TABLE](doc:u4.71.02) and then printed with [IMPR_TABLE](doc:u4.91.03).

One will find in the energy-balance table, in the course of the transient, the quantities accumulated at each time instant:

- `ENER_CIN`, for the kinetic energy, always positive or zero,
- `ENER_TOT`, for the accumulated work of deformation (sum of the stored energy and of the energy dissipated by the irreversible constitutive models, including the case of the absorbing boundaries),
- `TRAV_AMOR`, for the accumulated work of the damping forces coming from the assembled damping matrix (Rayleigh, discrete elements) or from modal damping,
- `TRAV_LIAI`, for the accumulated work including the case of the internal forces of the interface materials (`DIS_CONTACT` and `DIS_CHOC`, `CHOC_ENDO`, CHOC_ENDO_PENA`, `JOINT_MECA_FROT`), cf. R4.09.01, § 1.3.2,
- `TRAV_EXT`, for the accumulated work including the excitation forces on the right-hand side (and the driving inertia forces).

The remainder corresponds to the work dissipated by the numerical integration scheme.

### 3.9 Functionalities available in STAT_NON_LINE and not in DYNA_NON_LINE

Methods of line-search type (mixed or otherwise) are not authorised in dynamics. However, this lack is to be put into perspective, knowing that attempts to apply these methods to studies of reinforced-concrete structures in dynamics have not brought out any significant contribution to convergence, unlike what is observed in quasi-statics. Let us note, nevertheless, that no theoretical argument would forbid the use of these methods in dynamics.

Finally, the piloting techniques available in `STAT_NON_LINE` (arc length, for example) are forbidden in dynamics because they then have no meaning: time has, in dynamics, a physical meaning.

## 4 Specific features of coupled fluid-structure problems

It is possible to use a coupled vibro-acoustic model in `DYNA_NON_LINE`. This model is based on an approach $(u,p,\varphi )$ with the following hypotheses:

- the fluid is of linear acoustic type,
- the structure must be considered as being in small perturbations or in updated Lagrangian.

One can also take into account the free surfaces subjected to gravity (sloshing waves).

Documentation [U2.06.11](doc:u2.06.11) presents in detail the implementation of a non-linear coupled fluid-structure model for a tank analysis. Note [bib5]_ analyses the domain of application of the coupled fluid-structure model.

## 5 Optimisation of the performance

In quasi-statics, it is not rare to have to carry out more than 10 iterations in order to have convergence in the sense of the equilibrium residual. In implicit dynamics this value of 10 iterations constitutes, in general, a good starting value for the parameter `ITER_GLOB_MAXI` of the factor keyword `CONVERGENCE` in `DYNA_NON_LINE`. If one cannot converge in fewer than 10 to 20 iterations, it is then preferable to reduce the time step rather than to increase the maximum number of iterations authorised.

In the explicit case, there are no iterations for equilibrium, so the computational cost of each time step will therefore be constant, whatever the level of non-linearity (apart, possibly, from the local check of the constitutive behaviour).

The use, even current, of the explicit integration schemes therefore seems very attractive in view of the CPU time, which remains under control. One must however temper this optimism by keeping well in mind that one deprives oneself of the safeguard which is the precise verification of equilibrium and that, consequently, the quality of the explicit solution obtained must be analysed with more precautions. The explicit algorithm will not diverge (if one respects the Courant stability condition), but the solution obtained is not guaranteed by an equilibrium-verification criterion. In particular a parametric study on the time step is indispensable because the appearance of the solution can vary strongly as the transient proceeds.

Moreover, code_aster is not a code optimised for explicit analyses and its performance in the explicit case is modest, compared with specialised codes such as Europlexus [bib2]_.

### 5.1 Model reduction and dynamic condensation by substructuring

One solution for reducing the computation time is to project the problem onto a reduced basis (modal basis or Ritz basis). One then greatly reduces the number of degrees of freedom and this type of approach is available in `DYNA_NON_LINE` (the solution also gains by being explicit because the Courant stability condition on a reduced modal basis is not very penalising, since this basis has a low cut-off frequency). To summarise, this type of approach is particularly suited to problems in which the non-linearities remain moderate and localised. As soon as the non-linearities become strong, one can question the updating of the initial reduced basis, which loses its consistency with the current solution. The extra computational cost due to the recomputation of the basis and to the reprojections then reduces the interest of this type of method.
The factor keyword `PROJ_MODAL` makes it possible to carry out this type of analysis with an explicit integration scheme, the degrees of freedom being generalised. An example of an analysis is provided by test case `SDNV107a` [V5.03.107](doc:v5.03.107).
Another example of a non-linear dynamic analysis on a hydromechanical problem of a soil column is provided by test case `WDNP101a` [V5.03.107](doc:v5.03.107).

It is also recommended to use this reduction/condensation approach in a “mixed” manner, for “low-frequency” problems, where the model is made up of several subdomains, by condensing the solution of the subdomains of linear constitutive behaviour onto the degrees of freedom of their interfaces with subdomains of non-linear constitutive behaviour, these being treated with finite elements. Each of these linear subdomains is condensed onto a dynamic macro-element `MACR_ELEM_DYNA` [U4.65.01](doc:u4.65.01) defined on each interface. By means of the operator `-` [U4.63.33](doc:u4.63.33), after solution of the global “mixed” problem, one can reconstitute the fields in each subdomain.
An example of a non-linear dynamic analysis by reduction/condensation is provided by test case `FORMA12g` [V2.08.012](doc:v2.08.012) with an implicit integration scheme.
The methodology is presented in detail in [U2.07.04](doc:u2.07.04).

### 5.2 Parallelism

Parallelism can bring a gain which is all the more notable when the system to be solved comprises a large number of degrees of freedom and the number of time steps is small. Indeed, the solution algorithm imposes significant communications at each step (one is not in a strategy of multi-domain type in time and in space). In practice, on problems comprising a few hundreds of thousands of degrees of freedom, the speed-up remains good up to 16 to 32 processors. In most cases, the iterative solver `PETSC` will bring a gain compared with the solver `MUMPS`. In addition to the parallelism at solver level, the parallelism of the elementary steps (solution of the constitutive behaviour at the Gauss points) will bring an important additional gain if the constitutive integration is costly. In every case, it is very useful to consult in the message file .mess the CPU-time measurements per step, which makes it possible to identify the most costly steps of the transient solution in order to adapt the parallelism accordingly. Documentation [U2.08.06](doc:u2.08.06) gives all the details for the use of parallelism.

## 6 Conclusion

This document presents a few general rules in order to facilitate the use of transient dynamic methods for the simulation of non-linear systems: see [bib3]_, [bib6]_, [bib8]_.

The first step is the adaptation of the model to the methods of dynamic analysis. It is mainly a matter of making sure of the proper regularity of the imposed conditions, of the proper sampling of the excitations, of the correct definition of the density and of the global damping (Rayleigh, modal); it is recommended to begin with a linear transient analysis.

Next, it is recommended to begin by using an implicit scheme (`DYNA_NON_LINE` with a time scheme of type `NEWMARK` for the relatively regular problems or `HHT`, and `THETA_SCHEMA` for the problems with collisions). Indeed, the implicit time schemes are the most efficient and the most general-purpose in *Code_Aster* in so-called “slow” dynamics.

Finally, for certain applications such as fast dynamics, the analysis on a modal basis or certain cases of analyses in slow evolution (*cf.* [U2.04.07](doc:u2.04.07)), the user has the possibility of using explicit time schemes. The CPU-time performance of *Code_Aster* in the explicit case is fairly poor, if one compares it with a dedicated code such as Europlexus [bib2]_. Moreover, not all the functionalities available in the implicit case are available in the explicit case (as, for example, for contact, where only penalisation is authorised).

In order to qualify the quality of the numerical solution obtained, it is indispensable to carry out certain parametric studies:

- as for the quasi-static analyses, by acting on the spatial discretisation,
- by testing different parameter settings of the damping,
- by testing different time steps,
- by testing different time schemes, first non-dissipative and, if need be, with numerical dissipation at high frequencies.

In order to validate this choice of parameters, it is relevant to analyse the responses in displacements and in accelerations, as well as to make use of the energy-balance functionalities.
