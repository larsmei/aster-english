# r7.02.16 Gp method: an energy approach to the prediction of cleavage

**Summary**

The parameter ${G}_{p}$ makes it possible to define an initiation criterion valid in the cleavage domain (intragranular brittle fracture in the presence of plasticity). The foundations of this approach are first recalled: energy framework of the behaviour of structures, modelling of the crack by a notch, principle of minimisation of the energy and definition of the criterion. The link between the global approach and the local approach is discussed before then specifying the elements essential to the implementation of ${G}_{p}$ in *Code_Aster* .

## 1 Introduction

In this chapter one begins by introducing the limits of the classical approach in fracture mechanics (§ 1.1 ), then the objective and the value of the approach ${G}_{p}$ are recalled (§ 1.2 ). Finally the foundations of this method are addressed in (§ 1.4 ).

### 1.1 Limits of the classical approach

In fracture mechanics, the classical parameter making it possible to define an initiation criterion for an already existing crack 1
is the parameter $G$ , the elastic energy-release rate. The parameter $G$ is valid in linear elasticity as well as in non-linear elasticity. That is to say that the framework and the physical assumptions are well defined, and that the calculation formula $G\theta ` is consistent with this framework. On the other hand, the relevance of the fracture criterion :math:`G=\mathit{Gc}$ with respect to the experimental results is another question, not addressed in this document. In elastoplasticity, the parameter $G$ is in general not valid. However, if the loading remains radial and monotonic, then $G$ is valid. This assumption implies that the equations of plasticity are equivalent to the equations of Hencky (which are those of non-linear elasticity [:ref:` **R4.20.01**  < **R4.20.01** >`]). Now, the cases of strictly proportional loadings are rather rare (indeed they do not exist when one is concerned with structures presenting geometric defects such as cracks).

### 1.2 Objective and value of Gp

The objective of the method ${G}_{p}$ is to define an initiation parameter valid in incremental plasticity. This theory assumes a decoupling of plasticity and cracking (the case of fracture by cleavage). Thus the use of ${G}_{p}$ to predict cleavage will make it possible to reduce the conservatisms of the classical approach. Its definition calls upon the free energy as well as upon a few geometric parameters necessary for the definition of the crack, which is modelled by a notch. This parameter ${G}_{p}$ has the unit of a surface energy density and is calculated in the zone adjacent to the notch front.

The value of the method is threefold: ${G}_{p}$ is a deterministic initiation criterion, in other words it makes it possible to assess whether or not there is initiation of a crack. This parameter is consistent with the elastic $G$ [WAD 13, LOR 14], and it is valid in an elastoplastic framework under non-proportional loading and under unloading.

Note:

- ${G}_{p}$ is not a criterion of stability or of instability of the defect.
- ${G}_{p}$ does not predict a propagation length.

### 1.3 History of the development of the Gp approach

The development of this approach, which has its origins at the beginning of the 2000s, stems from the work of Lorentz, Wadier and Debryune [LOR00]. The crack is then modelled by a cut in the plane but the idea of introducing a notch is already mentioned there as a prospect and will rapidly be adopted. The modelling of the crack by a notch is presented in 2003 (CR) in [WAD 03d] and in 2004 (articles) in [LOR 04] and [WAD 04].

The approach has been presented many times at international conferences, presenting first its value for the vessel in loading/unloading situations [WAD 00], [WAD 01a], [WAD 01b], then showing the interpretations of experimental results illustrating the "small-defect" effect [WAD 03a] [WAD 03c] then the effect of warm pre-loading [WAD 03b] [WAD 05] [WAD 09].

A synthesis recalling the foundations of the Gp approach as well as the principal validation results is carried out in [WAD 13]. To this day it is the only article published in an international journal dedicated to the Gp approach. For this reason, it stands as the reference as regards the Gp approach.

It should be noted that attempts to predict crack arrest by means of an approach of Gp type (G-delta) were carried out in the past [WAD 07a], but this path is no longer pursued at the present time.

Since the principal actor of the method (Y. Wadier) went into inactivity, a new generation of engineers at EDF R&D has taken up the torch and continues to promote the Gp approach within the scientific community [GEN 16] [JUL 17]. Recently, the Gp approach has been extended to the configurations for which the zone around the defect tip is in compression [HAB 17].

### 1.4 Foundations of the Gp approach

The method ${G}_{p}$ is set in the context of a global energy formulation of brittle fracture. The approach consists in determining whether a propagation of the defect (preserving the geometry of the defect root and with constant mechanical fields) makes it possible to decrease the total energy of the structure (minimisation principle). In order to achieve this, the cracking path is assumed known *a priori* . Under this assumption, the energy framework proposed by Francfort and Marigo [FRA 93, FRA 98] can be put into practice in order to predict the sudden initiation of cracks. However, this framework presents certain characteristics which limit its scope [LOR 08], notably undesirable scale effects and the incompatibility of the theory with loadings of imposed-force type. This is why two types of modifications have been introduced in order to resolve these difficulties, while preserving the form of the energies introduced. On the one hand, the crack is replaced by a notch whose radius becomes a parameter of the model. On the other hand, one examines whether the absence of propagation is a (global) minimum at fixed mechanical fields; one therefore carries out a global minimisation but in a single direction. The energy nature of the proposed formulation authorises the introduction of plastic mechanisms.

The meaning of the term initiation is here different from the terminology used in fatigue, where initiation means the creation of a crack starting from a sound material. In fracture, one ought rather to say a criterion of "re-initiation", but the term initiation is commonly employed.

## 2 Modelling of defects in the form of a notch

One places oneself in 2D modelling. Within the framework of the approach ${G}_{p}$ , the real crack situated in the domain $\Omega ` is not modelled by a surface of discontinuity of the displacement fields, but by a "notch" in the shape of a U *(H1)* , of thickness :math:`D=2R$ , with $R$ the radius of the notch root (see [Figure 2-a]). It is assumed that the notch may propagate without changing shape *(H2)*  and without bifurcation *(H3)* , in mode I. Thus, the cracking paths are predefined and subject to a continuous evolution of the crack (i.e. no "dotted" cracks along the path).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.16/10000201000006A4000002FC70992AB5FE1A35FD.png)

Figure 2-a – Modelling of the defect by a notch and representation of the virtual propagation zone $C\left(\Delta a\right)$ in grey.

One then defines a virtual propagation zone of the notch called $C(\Delta a)$ , which corresponds to a *damaged* zone, of thickness $D$ and of length $\Delta a$ . The zone $C\left(\Delta a\right)$ illustrated by [Figure 2-b] may be defined as the union of a rectangle ${R}_{1}$ and of a half-disc ${C}_{2}$ , from which the half-disc ${C}_{1}$ is subtracted:

$$
C(\Delta a)=({R}_{1}\cup {C}_{2})-{C}_{1}
$$

The entities ${R}_{1}$ , ${C}_{1}$ and ${C}_{2}$ are defined below, given a notch along the axis $X$ and the coordinates $({X}_{0},{Y}_{0})$ of the centre of the circular root of the notch [BAR 12]:

$$
{R}_{1}=(X,Y)\text{tels que}\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0⩽X-{X}_{0}⩽\Delta a\\ -R⩽Y-{Y}_{0}⩽R\end{array}
$$

$$
{C}_{1}=(X,Y)\text{tels que}\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{(X-{X}_{0})}^{2}+{(Y-{Y}_{0})}^{2}⩽{R}^{2}\\ X-{X}_{0}⩾0\end{array}
$$

$$
{C}_{2}=(X,Y)\text{tels que}\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{(X-{X}_{0}-\Delta a)}^{2}+{(Y-{Y}_{0})}^{2}⩽{R}^{2}\\ X-{X}_{0}-\Delta a⩾0\end{array}
$$

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.16/10000201000003E80000026D06CB57858F0250A3.png)

Figure 2-b – Geometric definition of the notch: the zone $C\left(\Delta a\right)$ hatched, the rectangle ${R}_{1}$ dotted, and the half-discs ${C}_{1}$ and ${C}_{2}$ .

Note: the integral over $C\left(\Delta a\right)$ of a constant $w$ independent of space is written simply:

$$
{\int}_{\textcolor[rgb]{1,0,0}{C(\Delta a)}}wd\Omega =w\times \Delta a\times D
$$

Strictly speaking, one is no longer in the context of fracture mechanics but in that of damage mechanics, considering a model of brutal damage *(assumption* *H4)*  which is total *(assumption* *H5)* . The term brutal, as opposed to progressive, means that only two damage states are possible for the material: sound or damaged. The term total, as opposed to partial, indicates that the damaged material has zero residual stiffness.

A two-valued damage field $\mathrm{\chi }$ is thus defined on the domain $\Omega ` , taking values in :math:$leftlbrace 0,1rightrbrace` . By definition, $\mathrm{\chi }=0$ corresponds to the sound material, and $\mathrm{\chi }=1$ to the damaged material. A damage model makes it possible to fix a critical stress. As a reminder, one fixes the thickness of the zone over which the damage propagates: one considers only the evolutions of the damage which correspond to the growth of the notch $C\left(\Delta a\right)$ , where $\Delta a$ is the length over which the notch propagates, and $D$ its thickness. One thus controls the energy dissipated during the advance of the damaged zone. One denotes $\Omega \setminus C\left(\Delta a\right)$ the set constituted by the difference between the domain $\Omega ` and the virtual propagation zone of the notch :math:`C\left(\Delta a\right)$ . Then one has:

$$
\mathrm{\chi }(\Delta a)(x)=\lbrace \begin{array}{c}1\text{si}x\in C(\Delta a)\\ 0\text{si}x\in \Omega \setminus C\left(\Delta a\right)\end{array}
$$

Note:

One could have distinguished the thickness of the pre-existing notch from that of the damaged zone $C\left(\Delta a\right)$ . This would have led to a more complex model (with more parameters). For the sake of simplicity one therefore does not make this distinction.

## 3 Principle of energy minimisation

### 3.1 Energy formulation in the elastic framework

An energy formulation characterises the response of a structure as a minimum of an energy with respect to the set of the variables which one has chosen in order to describe the mechanical state of the structure [LOR 08]. In the elastic case, the matter is to minimise the potential energy with respect to the displacement field. In order to take the dissipative phenomena into account, the approach ${G}_{p}$ relies on the work of Francfort and Marigo [FRA 93, FRA 98] devoted to a model of brutal *partial* damage. Unlike the elastic case, the energy depends on two fields, the displacement field $u$ and a damage field $\mathrm{\chi }$ with values in $\left\lbrace 0,1\right\rbrace$ , $0$ corresponding to the sound material of volumetric free energy ${\varphi}_{s}$ and $1$ to the damaged material of volumetric free energy ${\varphi}_{d}$ :

$$
{E}_{\mathit{tot}}\left(u,\mathrm{\chi }\right)=\underset{\Omega}{\int}\varphi (\epsilon (u),\mathrm{\chi })+{w}_{c}\mathrm{\chi };\varphi (\epsilon (u),\mathrm{\chi })=\mathrm{\chi }{\varphi}_{d}(\epsilon )+(1-\mathrm{\chi }){\varphi}_{s}(\epsilon )
$$

where one restricts oneself to imposed displacements (the potential of the external forces ${W}_{\mathit{ext}}$ is zero). The additional term ${w}_{c}\mathrm{\chi }$ measures the energy to be supplied in order to pass from the sound state to the damaged state. After discretisation in time, Francfort and Marigo then postulate that the displacement field $u$ and the damage field $\mathrm{\chi }$ realise a minimum of the incremental potential energy:

$$
(u,\mathrm{\chi })=\underset{u,\mathrm{\chi }}{\mathit{argmin}}{E}_{\mathit{tot}}\left(u,\mathrm{\chi }\right)
$$

One subsequently considers the model of brutal damage presented by Francfort and Marigo, except that the residual stiffness is zero for a damaged state (model of brutal *total* damage). One then retains from the theory of Francfort and Marigo that, at a given displacement, the evolution of the damage is governed by the minimisation of the total energy with respect to $\mathrm{\chi }$ . In this case, a total damage or external forces are no longer problematic. Thus, in the elastic framework, the evolution of the damage is obtained by the minimisation of the following total incremental potential energy:

$$
{E}_{\mathit{tot}}\left(u,\mathrm{\chi }\right)={\int}_{\Omega}\left[\left(1-\mathrm{\chi }\right){\Phi}_{\mathit{el}}+\mathrm{\chi }{w}_{C}\right]d\Omega -{W}_{\mathit{ext}}(u)
$$

where ${\Phi}_{\mathit{el}}$ is the free-energy density, ${\Phi}_{\mathit{el}}={\int}_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}[\sigma :{A}^{-1}:\sigma ]d\Omega$ with $A$ the tangent matrix of the constitutive behaviour, and ${w}_{c}$ the volumetric energy dissipated in the damage process at each material point. In this new approach, one may take loadings of imposed-force type: the potential of the external forces ${W}_{\mathit{ext}}$ is not necessarily zero.

### 3.2 Modification of the form of the energy

One of the limitations of the proposed damage model is that it does not distinguish tension from compression, on account of the form of the free-energy density, so that the restitution of a compressive energy contributes just as much to the propagation of the defect as a tensile energy [HAB 16]. One chooses to eliminate artificially the zones in compression from the integral *(assumption* *H6)* . To this end one considers a tension/compression dissymmetry in the formulation of the energy considered, relying on the method developed by Badel in [BAD 01]. Thus, placing oneself in the principal frame of the strains, one will adopt the following elastic energy:

$$
{\Phi}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}({\epsilon}^{\mathit{el}})=\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathit{tr}{({\epsilon}^{\mathit{el}})}^{2}H(\mathit{tr}({\epsilon}^{\mathit{el}}))+\mu \sum_{i}{({\epsilon}_{i}^{\mathit{el}})}^{2}H({\epsilon}_{i}^{\mathit{el}})
$$

where $\lambda ` and :math:$mu ` denote the Lamé coefficients which characterise the stiffness tensor. The eigenvalues of the elastic strain tensor are denoted ${\epsilon}_{i}^{\mathit{el}}$ . $H$ is the Heaviside function such that:

$$
H(x)=\lbrace \begin{array}{c}1\text{si}x⩾0\\ 0\text{si}x<0\end{array}
$$

### 3.3 Extension to the elastoplastic framework

In the case of an elastoplastic constitutive behaviour, the definition of the total incremental energy ${E}_{\mathrm{tot}}$ is extended by Lorentz *et al.* [LOR 00], subject to the definition of new global potentials, free energy and dissipation. One restricts oneself here to the framework of isotropic materials. The state of a material point is described by its strain $\epsilon$ , its damage $\mathrm{\chi }$ , but also by its plastic strain ${\epsilon}^{p}$ and internal variables $\alpha$ characterising the hardening. One assumes:

- that the **plastic dissipation is decoupled from that linked to the damage** (quasi-brittle materials), *(assumption* *H7)* . The plastic dissipation potential ${D}_{\mathrm{pl}}$ therefore depends only on the plastic internal variables.
- that the energy locked by hardening in the dislocations ${E}_{\mathrm{bl}}$ is not recoverable by the cracking mechanism *(phenomenological assumption* *H8)* . One thus considers the following expression of the incremental potential energy:

$$
{E}_{\mathit{tot}}\left(u,{\epsilon}^{p},\alpha ,\mathrm{\chi }\right)={\int}_{\Omega}\left[\left(1-\mathrm{\chi }\right){\Phi}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}+\mathrm{\chi }{w}_{C}\right]d\Omega +{E}_{\mathit{bl}}\left(\alpha \right)+{D}_{\mathit{pl}}\left(\Delta {\epsilon}^{p},\Delta \alpha \right)-{W}_{\mathit{ext}}(u)
$$

where $\Delta$ denotes the variation of a quantity (grandeur) during the increment considered.

## 4 Definition of an initiation criterion in elastoplasticity

By virtue of the results relating to the energy formulations developed by Francfort and Marigo, the solution of the elastoplastic problem with damage minimises the potential energy () *(assumption* *H9)* . The integration of the constitutive equations is written as the minimum of the potential energy with respect to the internal variables $\left({\epsilon}^{p},\alpha \right)$ , the equilibrium equations are expressed as the minimum of the potential energy with respect to the displacement field $u$ . Finally, the evolution of the damage is governed by the minimum of the potential energy with respect to $\mathrm{\chi }$ .

The question of initiation may be formulated in the following manner [LOR 08]: at a given state $\left(u,{\epsilon}^{p},\alpha \right)$ corresponding to a quasi-static evolution without propagation of the notch, is the solution without propagation $\Delta a=0$ always admissible with regard to the minimisation of () ? If so, then one postulates that there is no initiation *(assumption* *H10)* . In the contrary case, that is if the optimum is not attained at $\Delta a=0$ , one postulates that there is propagation.

One thus considers that **there is no initiation of a defect**  for as long as the elastoplastic solution without evolution of the damage is a solution of the problem, that is to say:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}(u,{\epsilon}^{p},\alpha )=\underset{u,{\epsilon}^{p},\alpha }{\mathit{argmin}}{E}_{\mathit{tot}}\left(u,{\epsilon}^{p},\alpha ,\mathrm{\chi }=0\right)\\ \text{et}\\ \forall \Delta a>0\text{}{E}_{\mathit{tot}}(u,{\epsilon}^{p},\alpha ,\mathrm{\chi }=0)⩽{E}_{\mathit{tot}}(u,{\epsilon}^{p},\alpha ,\mathrm{\chi }(\Delta a))\end{array}
$$

Thus, by expressing the second line thanks to equation (), one obtains after simplification that $\forall \Delta a>0$ , there is no initiation of the defect for as long as:

$$
{\int}_{\Omega}{\Phi}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}(\epsilon (u)-{\epsilon}^{p})d\Omega ⩽{\int}_{\Omega}\left[\left(1-\mathrm{\chi }(\Delta a)\right){\Phi}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}(\epsilon (u)-{\epsilon}^{p})+\mathrm{\chi }(\Delta a){w}_{C}\right]d\Omega
$$

In what follows, one will omit the argument of the adopted elastic energy in order to lighten the expression. By expanding on the one hand the integral on the left-hand side and on the right-hand side over the domains $C\left(\Delta a\right)$ and $\Omega \setminus C\left(\Delta a\right)$ , and by using on the other hand the definition of the damage field (), one arrives after simplification at:

$$
{\int}_{\textcolor[rgb]{1,0,0}{\Omega \setminus C(\Delta a)}}{\Phi}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}d\Omega +{\int}_{\textcolor[rgb]{1,0,0}{C(\Delta a)}}{\Phi}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}d\Omega ⩽{\int}_{\textcolor[rgb]{1,0,0}{\Omega \setminus C(\Delta a)}}{\Phi}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}d\Omega +{\int}_{\textcolor[rgb]{1,0,0}{C(\Delta a)}}{w}_{C}d\Omega
$$

Now, according to () :

$$
{\int}_{\textcolor[rgb]{1,0,0}{C(\Delta a)}}{w}_{C}d\Omega ={w}_{c}\times \Delta a\times D
$$

Inequality () is then expressed as below:

$$
\forall \Delta a>0\text{}\frac{{\int}_{\textcolor[rgb]{1,0,0}{C(\Delta a)}}{\Phi}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}d\Omega }{\Delta a}⩽{w}_{c}\times D
$$

One defines the left-hand term $\stackrel{~}{{G}_{p}}(\Delta a)$ such that:

$$
\stackrel{~}{{G}_{p}}(\Delta a)=\frac{{\int}_{\textcolor[rgb]{1,0,0}{C(\Delta a)}}{\Phi}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}d\Omega }{\Delta a}
$$

And one defines the right-hand term ${G}_{\mathit{pc}}={w}_{c}\times D$ as the fracture energy of the material. The non-initiation condition is then written:

$$
\forall \Delta a>0,\text{}\stackrel{~}{{G}_{p}}(\Delta a)⩽{G}_{\mathit{pc}}
$$

One then defines the parameter ${G}_{p}$ as the maximum value of the function $\stackrel{~}{{G}_{p}}(\Delta a)$ :

$$
{G}_{p}=\underset{\Delta a}{max}\stackrel{~}{{G}_{p}}(\Delta a)
$$

The non-re-initiation criterion is written ${G}_{p}⩽{G}_{\mathit{pc}}$ . The initiation criterion may then be written ${G}_{p}>{G}_{\mathit{pc}}$ . This criterion implies knowledge of two material parameters, ${w}_{c}$ and $D$ . For practical reasons, one prefers to choose $D$ and ${G}_{\mathrm{pc}}$ . These parameters therefore require an identification (see paragraph § 6.2 ).

The parameter ${G}_{p}$ is calculated as the maximum (with respect to $\Delta a$ , the virtual propagation length) of the integral over the domain $C\left(\Delta a\right)$ of the modified free energy ${\Phi}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}$ , divided by $\Delta a$ . It is therefore a mean elastic energy density ($\mathrm{kJ}/{m}^{2}$ ). It is not here an energy-release rate because the energy considered for the minimisation is that of the current time instant, and it does not include the re-equilibration of the fields after propagation.

In the manner of ${K}_{J}$ $(\mathit{MPa}.{m}^{1/2})$ , defined from $J$ in a way similar to the Irwin relation $J=\frac{\left(1-{\nu}^{2}\right)}{E}{K}_{J}^{2}$ — valid in 2D plane strain and in 3D —, one may also define a parameter ${K}_{{G}_{P}}$ $(\mathit{MPa}.{m}^{1/2})$ from ${G}_{p}$ by means of this same relation:

$$
{G}_{p}=\frac{\left(1-{\nu}^{2}\right)}{E}{K}_{{G}_{P}}^{2}
$$

## 5 Link between the global approach and the local approach

Lorentz [LOR 08] and Wadier [WAD 07b] position the energy approach ${G}_{p}$ with respect to the global and local approaches to fracture. More recent than the global approach in fracture mechanics, the local approach aims to predict the failure of a structure by relying on the microscopic mechanism of cleavage. It accounts for the scatter of the results by an intrinsically probabilistic modelling. The Beremin model, for example, defines the fracture probability and the Weibull stress ${\sigma}_{w}$ at time $t$ by:

$$
{P}_{R}(t)=1-\exp\left[-{\left(\frac{\underset{\tau \le t}{\max}{\sigma}_{w}(\tau )}{{\sigma}_{c}}\right)}^{m}\right];{\sigma}_{w}(\tau )={\left[\frac{1}{{V}_{0}}\underset{{\Omega}_{p}(\tau )}{\int}{\sigma}_{I}{(\tau )}^{m}d\Omega \right]}^{\frac{1}{m}}
$$

with ${\sigma}_{I}(t)$ and ${\Omega}_{p}(t)$ the maximum principal stress and the domain undergoing yielding at time respectively. ${V}_{0}$ is a reference volume, ${\sigma}_{c}$ the critical stress and $m$ a dimensionless exponent. Thus, the expression of the fracture probability is based in particular on the weakest-link assumption, i.e. failure is associated with the initiation of the most penalising microscopic defect. For the sake of comparison between the two models, one may define a fracture probability of the approach ${G}_{p}$ by the formula:

$$
{P}_{R}(t)=1-\exp\left[-a{\left(\frac{{G}_{p}}{{G}_{p0}}\right)}^{m/2}\right]\text{}
$$

where $a$ and $m$ are constants identified from the experimental results, and ${G}_{p0}$ a constant such that ${P}_{r}$ is equal to 5% when ${G}_{p}$ is equal to the value identified for a fracture probability of 5%.

The comparison between the two approaches brings out a certain number of links. The quantities involved in the initiation criterion (elastic energy or stress) are similar. These quantities are averaged over a zone situated in the neighbourhood of the notch root. Finally, one may be led to take the hydrostatic stress into account [WAD 07b].

The Beremin model allows the tension/compression distinction to be taken into account naturally thanks to the introduction of the maximum principal stress rather than that of the elastic energy. Conversely, the approach ${G}_{p}$ offers a simpler transition towards the earlier approaches of the industrial domain. It makes it possible to link up with the classical global approach because it is founded, as that approach is, on energy principles: ${G}_{p}=G$ notch in elasticity [LOR 14].

## 6 Implementation in Code_Aster

The mechanical analysis is carried out under the assumption of a thermo-elasto-plastic constitutive behaviour associated with a von Mises criterion with isotropic or linear kinematic hardening (VMIS_ISOT_TRAC, VMIS_ISOT_LINE, VMIS_CINE_LINE). One will specify what the calculation of ${G}_{p}$ consists in and how the identification of the material parameters $R=D/2$ (radius of the notch) and ${G}_{\mathit{pc}}$ (fracture limit) is carried out.

The methodology of calculation and identification with Code_Aster is presented in [U2.05.08](doc:u2.05.08). The plain user's documentation is in [U4.82.31].

### 6.1 Calculation of Gp

The calculation of ${G}_{p}$ , carried out with the aid of the macro-command CALC_GP, is based on the use of POST_ELEM, which allows the calculation of the elastic energy over a mesh-cell group. The modellings (finite elements, small strains, etc.) and the loadings which may be used are those of the command POST_ELEM, keyword ENER_ELTR. More precisely, for each time instant provided for in the list of analysis time instants, the following two stages are to be carried out:

1/ First calculate the quantity $\stackrel{~}{{G}_{p}}(\Delta a)$ for increasing values of $\Delta a$ by:

$\stackrel{~}{{G}_{p}}(\Delta a)=\frac{{\int}_{\Omega}{\Phi}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}d\Omega }{\Delta a}$

In 2D one must therefore identify the elements of the zone $C\left(\Delta a\right)$ by a mesh-cell group defined at mesh level as presented in [Figure 2-a], or by a geometric zone of Gauss points, then calculate the elastic energy over this zone and then divide it by $\Delta a$ .

In order to identify the elements of the zone $C\left(\Delta a\right)$ one will proceed as follows: the elements of the first chip will constitute a first mesh-cell group, the elements of chips 1 and 2 will constitute a second group, the elements of the chips $1,2,3,\mathrm{...},i$ will constitute a $i$th group, etc. A sufficiently large number of chips must be provided in order to be able to find the maximum of $\stackrel{~}{{G}_{p}}(\Delta a)$ , which is located most often at a distance of about $3R$ from the notch root.

Figure 6.1-1- Definition of the chips in the mesh

2/ Next it is a matter of identifying the maximum of this function:

${G}_{p}=\underset{\Delta a}{max}\stackrel{~}{{G}_{p}}(\Delta a)$

Examples and advice on use will be found in the document [:ref:` **U2.05.01**  < **U2.05.01** >`], in the tests ssnp131 (see [V6.03.131](doc:v6.03.131) and ssnv218 (see [V6.04.218](doc:v6.04.218)).

### 6.2 Identification of the parameters

The energy model is based on the pair of material parameters $({G}_{\mathit{pc}},R)$ which must therefore be determined at each temperature. One notes that ${G}_{\mathit{pc}}$ depends in reality on the notch radius [WAD 13]. One will see that the prediction of fracture does not depend on it.

One assumes known, on the one hand, Young's modulus $E$ and the critical stress ${\sigma}_{c}$ of a material. On the other hand, one assumes that the toughness ${K}_{\mathit{Jc}}$ is known, evaluated experimentally from a tensile test on a CT specimen, for example. Application of the "minimum with respect to the damage" at the scale of a material point in a state of simple tensile stress gives the relation between the volumetric energy dissipated by a material point which becomes damaged ${w}_{c}$ and the critical stress [WAD 13] *(assumption H11)* :

$$
{w}_{c}=\frac{{\sigma}_{c}^{2}}{2E}
$$

One then obtains the following equation, which must be solved in order to identify the two parameters:

$$
{G}_{\mathit{pc}}(R)=\frac{{\sigma}_{c}^{2}}{E}R
$$

The left-hand side ${G}_{\mathit{pc}}\left(R\right)$ is a non-linear function of $R$ . The right-hand side is a linear function of $R$ . Thus, in order to solve (25) one must calculate ${G}_{\mathit{pc}}$ for various values of $R$ as the figure [Figure 6.2-1] illustrates.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.16/100002000000054C000003C4874D43DF0BCEBDFA.png)

Figure 6.2-1: identification of the pair $({G}_{\mathit{pc}},R)$  [WAD 13]

For each notch of given radius $R$ , the parameter ${G}_{\mathit{pc}}$ is determined by simulation of a test on a specimen $\mathit{CT}$ in the following manner: for each value of the loading, increasing from 0 up to a critical value, one calculates on the one hand the parameter ${K}_{J}$ and on the other hand the parameter ${G}_{p}$ . For the critical value of the loading corresponding to ${K}_{J}={K}_{\mathit{Jc}}$ , one obtains ${G}_{p}={G}_{\mathit{pc}}$ *(Assumption H12)* .

Solving equation (25) for various values of $R$ finally makes it possible to determine both the value of $R$ and the value of ${G}_{\mathit{pc}}$ at a given temperature. In the case of a steel, one finds values lying between 10 and 100 microns, which is representative of a real crack.
