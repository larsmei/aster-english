# u2.04.05 User's guide for the THM model

> **Summary.**

THM modellings deal exclusively with the evolution of the thermo-hydro-mechanical constitutive behaviours of porous media, whether saturated or not by one or two fluids. The numerical and physical description of these modellings in Code_Aster is detailed in [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10) and [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11).

This documentation sets out the procedure to be followed in order to carry out simulations in the context of THM modellings. The first part describes the various analysis steps within the general framework of so-called «generalised» continuous media (cf. [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10)). In this part, what concerns the mechanical models described elsewhere will not be detailed. The second part restricts the application of these modellings to the treatment of porous media which undergo a degradation of their mechanical properties by damage of the soil or of the rocks, for example. To this end, the THM modellings are extended to media with microstructure by taking second-gradient effects into account (cf. [R5.04.03](doc:r5.04.03)). The aim is to correct the dependence of the solutions on the spatial discretisation when the mechanical constitutive law considered is of softening type — which is the case for any brittle material, and therefore for soils in particular.

## 1 Outline

### 1.1 Context of THM studies

First of all, the very precise framework of thermo-hydro-mechanical analyses must be defined. Their exclusive application is the study of porous media. With this in mind, the THM modelling covers the mechanical evolution of these media and the flows within them. The latter concern one or two fluids and are governed by Darcy's laws (Darcian fluids). The complete THM problem therefore deals with the flow of the fluid or fluids, the mechanics of the skeleton, and the thermal problem: the solution is very often fully coupled.

**Note:**

In the expression of Darcy's laws retained here, the differential acceleration of the water is neglected. In the case of very permeable and very porous media subjected to a seismic loading, this may constitute a limitation.

### 1.2 General points

The analyses rely on families of THM constitutive laws for saturated and unsaturated porous media. The mechanics of porous media brings together a very exhaustive collection of physical phenomena affecting solids and fluids. It assumes a coupling between the mechanical evolutions of the solids and of the fluids, seen as continuous media, and the hydraulic evolutions, which govern the problems of diffusion of fluids within walls or volumes, and the thermal evolutions. The formulation of the thermo-hydro-mechanical (THM) modelling in a porous medium as it is made in *Code_Aster* is detailed in [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11) and [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10). All the notations used here therefore refer to them. A few notations which are indispensable in what follows are nevertheless recalled:

As regards the fluids, one considers (the most complete case) two phases (liquid and gas) and two constituents, called for convenience water and air. The following indices are then used:

$w$ for the liquid water

$text{ad}$ for the dissolved air

$text{as}$ for the dry air

$text{vp}$ for the water vapour

The thermodynamic variables are:

- the pressures of the constituents: ${p}_{w}(x,t)$ , ${p}_{text{ad}}(x,t)$ , ${p}_{text{vp}}(x,t)$ , ${p}_{text{as}}(x,t)$ ,
- the temperature of the medium $T(x,t)$ .

These various variables are not entirely independent. Indeed, if a single constituent is considered, the thermodynamic equilibrium between its phases imposes a relation between the vapour pressure and the liquid pressure of that constituent. Ultimately, there is only one independent pressure per constituent, just as there is only one mass-conservation equation. The number of independent pressures is therefore equal to the number of independent constituents. The choice of these pressures varies according to the constitutive laws.

For the so-called saturated case (a single constituent, air or water), the pressure of that unique constituent has been chosen.

For the so-called unsaturated case (presence of air and of water), the following have been chosen as independent variables:

- the total gas pressure ${p}_{text{gz}}(x,t)={p}_{text{vp}}+{p}_{text{as}}$ ,
- the capillary pressure ${p}_{c}(x,t)={p}_{text{gz}}-{p}_{text{lq}}={p}_{text{gz}}-{p}_{w}-{p}_{text{ad}}$ .

The Aster terminology for these variables will be seen later.

## 2 The various steps of a THM analysis

### 2.1 Choice of the model

The numerical treatment in THM requires a quadratic mesh, since the elements are of
type $text{P2}$ in displacement and $text{P1}$ in pressure and temperature in order
to avoid oscillation problems.

The choice is made by using the command AFFE_MODELE, as in the example below:

```python
MODELE=AFFE_MODELE(MAILLAGE=MAIL,
                   AFFE=_F(TOUT='OUI',
                           PHENOMENE='MECANIQUE',
                           MODELISATION='AXIS_THH2MD',),
                   )
```

In every case, the phenomenon is ``MECANIQUE`` (even if the modelling does not contain the mechanics).

The user must then supply, as a mandatory item, the keyword MODELISATION. This keyword
makes it possible to define the type of element assigned to a type of mesh cell. The modellings available in
THM are indicated in the following table.

**Note**

Modellings ending with the letter D indicate that a treatment is applied
which makes it possible to diagonalise (« lump ») the matrix in order to avoid oscillations for
hydraulic problems. For this purpose the integration points are taken at the vertices of the elements.
As this treatment is poorly suited to mechanics, a modelling
known as «selective» is also available. In this case, the capacitive terms are integrated at the vertices whereas the
diffusive terms are integrated at the Gauss points. These modellings end with an S.
The other modellings integrate everything at the Gauss points.

The user is strongly advised to use the D or S modellings in the cases without
mechanics and to use the S modelling for the modellings with mechanics.

The «classical» modellings (with neither D nor S) are due to be removed and are not recommended.

### 2.2 Definition of the material

The material is defined by the command DEFI_MATERIAU, as in the example below:

```python
MATERBO=DEFI_MATERIAU(  ELAS        = _F(E=5.15000000E8, NU=0.20, ALPHA=0.,),
                        COMP_THM    = 'LIQU_AD_GAZ_VAPE',
                        THM_LIQU    = _F(
                                        RHO=1000.0,
                                        UN_SUR_K=0.,
                                        ALPHA=0.,
                                        CP=0.0,
                                        VISC=VISCOLIQ,
                                        D_VISC_TEMP=DVISCOL,
                                        ),
                        THM_GAZ     = _F(
                                        MASS_MOL=0.01,
                                        CP=0.0,
                                        VISC=VISCOGAZ,
                                        D_VISC_TEMP=ZERO,
                                        ),
                        THM_VAPE_GAZ= _F(
                                        MASS_MOL=0.01,
                                        CP=0.0,

                        THM_AIR_DISS= _F(
                                        CP=0.0,

                        THM_INIT    = _F(
                                        TEMP=300.0,

                                        PRE2=1.E5,

                        THM_DIFFU   = _F(
```

#### 2.2.1 Simple keyword `COMP_THM`

Makes it possible to select, as early as the definition of the material, the THM coupling law.

GAZ: constitutive law of a perfect gas, that is to say satisfying the relation
$P/rho =mathit{RT}/mathit{Mv}$ where $P$ is the pressure,
$rho$ the density, $text{Mv}$ the molar mass, $R$ the perfect-gas
constant and $T$ the temperature (cf. [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11) for further details).
For a saturated medium only. The necessary data of the material field are supplied in
the operator DEFI_MATERIAU, under the keyword THM_GAZ.

LIQU_SATU: constitutive law for a porous medium saturated by a single liquid
(cf. [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11) for further details). The necessary data of the material field
are supplied in the operator DEFI_MATERIAU, under the keyword THM_LIQU.

LIQU_GAZ_ATM: constitutive law for an unsaturated porous medium with a liquid and gas at
atmospheric pressure (cf. [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11) for further details). The necessary
data of the material field are supplied in the operator DEFI_MATERIAU, under the keywords
THM_LIQU and THM_GAZ.

LIQU_VAPE_GAZ: constitutive law for an unsaturated water/vapour/dry-air porous medium with
phase change (cf. [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11) for further details). The necessary
data of the material field are supplied in the operator DEFI_MATERIAU, under the keywords:
THM_LIQU, THM_VAPE and THM_GAZ.

LIQU_AD_GAZ_VAPE: constitutive law for an unsaturated water/vapour/dry-air/dissolved-air porous medium
with phase change (cf. [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11) for further details). The
necessary data of the material field are supplied in the operator DEFI_MATERIAU, under the
keywords THM_LIQU, THM_VAPE, THM_GAZ and THM_AIR_DISS.

LIQU_AD_GAZ: constitutive law for an unsaturated water/dry-air/dissolved-air porous medium with
phase change (cf. [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11) for further details). The necessary
data of the material field are supplied in the operator DEFI_MATERIAU, under the keywords
THM_LIQU, THM_GAZ and THM_AIR_DISS.

LIQU_VAPE: constitutive law for a porous medium saturated by a component present in
liquid or vapour form with phase change (cf. [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11) for further details).
The necessary data of the material field are supplied in the operator DEFI_MATERIAU, under the
keywords THM_LIQU and THM_VAPE. **This law is valid only for modellings of type THVD** .

LIQU_GAZ: constitutive law for an unsaturated liquid/gas porous medium without phase
change (cf. [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11) for further details). The necessary data of the material
field are supplied in the operator DEFI_MATERIAU, under the keywords THM_LIQU and THM_GAZ.

#### 2.2.2 Factor keyword THM_INIT

For all the thermo-hydro-mechanical constitutive behaviours, it makes it possible to describe a reference state of
the structure (cf. [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10) and [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11)).

Under this keyword, there are three quantities corresponding to degrees of freedom. In order to understand
these data properly, one must then distinguish the unknowns at the nodes, which we call
${leftlbrace urightrbrace }^{text{ddl}}$ and the values defined under the keyword THM_INIT which we
call ${p}^{text{ref}}$ and ${T}^{text{ref}}$

${leftlbrace urightrbrace }^{text{ddl}}=leftlbrace begin{array}{}{u}_{x}\ {u}_{y}\ {u}_{z}\ {text{PRE1}}^{text{ddl}}\ {text{PRE2}}^{text{ddl}}\ {T}^{text{ddl}}end{array}rightrbrace$

The meaning of the unknowns PRE1 and PRE2 varies according to the models. Denoting by ${p}_{w}$
the water pressure, ${p}_{text{ad}}$ the dissolved-air pressure, ${p}_{text{lq}}$
the liquid pressure ${p}_{text{lq}}={p}_{w}+{p}_{text{ad}}$ , ${p}_{text{as}}$ ,
${p}_{text{vp}}$ the vapour pressure, ${p}_{text{as}}$ the dry-air pressure and
${p}_{g}={p}_{text{as}}+{p}_{text{vp}}$  the total gas pressure and
${p}_{c}={p}_{g}-{p}_{text{lq}}$ the capillary pressure (also called suction), one has the
following meanings of the unknowns PRE1 and PRE2

| KIT behaviour | LIQU_SATU | LIQU_GAZ_ATM | GAZ | LIQU_VAPE_GAZ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| mathit{PRE1} | {p}_{text{lq}} | -{p}_{text{lq}} | {p}_{g} | {p}_{c}={p}_{g}-{p}_{text{lq}} |
| mathit{PRE2} |  |  |  | {p}_{g} |

| KIT behaviour | LIQU_GAZ | LIQU_VAPE | LIQU_AD_GAZ_VAPE or LIQU_AD_GAZ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| mathit{PRE1} | {p}_{c}={p}_{g}-{p}_{text{lq}} | {p}_{text{lq}} | {p}_{c}={p}_{g}-{p}_{text{lq}} |
| mathit{PRE2} | {p}_{g} |  | {p}_{g} |

The «total» pressures and temperature are then defined by:

$p={p}^{text{ddl}}+{p}^{text{ref}};T={T}^{text{ddl}}+{T}^{text{ref}}$

All the input or output values (boundary conditions or result of IMPR_RESU ) are
the nodal unknowns ${p}^{text{ddl}}text{et}{T}^{text{ddl}}$ .

On the other hand, it is the total pressures and temperature which are used in the constitutive
laws $frac{p}{rho}=frac{R}{M}T$ for perfect gases,
$frac{d{rho}_{l}}{{rho}_{l}}=frac{{mathrm{dp}}_{l}}{{K}_{l}}-3{alpha}_{l}mathrm{dT}$
for the liquid and in the saturation/capillary-pressure relation.

One may therefore choose to initialise the nodal values in THM_INIT (by default these values
are zero) or by the keyword ETAT_INIT of the command STAT_NON_LINE or by a combination of the
two (this may allow an easy conversion from kelvin to degrees Celsius, for example).

TEMP is the reference temperature ${T}_{text{ref}}$ . By default it is taken equal to
zero. Caution: the value of the **initial** temperature
$T={T}^{text{ddl}}+{T}^{text{ref}}$ must be strictly greater than zero.

PRE1 is taken equal to zero by default. As seen in the table:

- For the behaviours LIQU_SATU and LIQU_VAPE, it is the reference liquid pressure.
- For the behaviour GAZ, it is the reference gas pressure. In this case the initial gas
pressure $p={p}^{text{ddl}}+{p}^{text{ref}}$ must be non-zero.
- For the behaviour LIQU_GAZ_ATM, it is the reference liquid pressure with the sign changed.
- For the behaviours LIQU_VAPE_GAZ, LIQU_AD_GAZ, LIQU_AD_GAZ_VAPE and LIQU_GAZ, it is the
reference capillary pressure.

PRE2 is taken equal to zero by default. For the behaviours LIQU_VAPE_GAZ, LIQU_AD_GAZ,
LIQU_AD_GAZ_VAPE and LIQU_GAZ, it is the reference gas pressure. The **initial** gas
pressure $p={p}^{text{ddl}}+{p}^{text{ref}}$ must be non-zero.

PORO is the initial porosity.

PRES_VAPE is the reference vapour pressure for the behaviours LIQU_VAPE_GAZ,
LIQU_AD_GAZ_VAPE and LIQU_VAPE.

**Notes**

The initial vapour pressure must be taken consistently with the other data. Very
often, one starts from the knowledge of an initial hygrometric state. The hygrometric degree
is the ratio between the vapour pressure and the saturating vapour pressure at the temperature
considered. Kelvin's law is then used, which gives the liquid pressure as a function of
the vapour pressure, the temperature and the saturating vapour pressure:

This relation is valid only for isothermal evolutions. It is emphasised that
${p}_{w}^{text{ref}}$ corresponds to an equilibrium state to which
${p}_{mathit{pv}}^{mathit{sat}}$ corresponds; this equilibrium state in fact corresponds to
${p}_{w}^{0}={p}_{mathit{gz}}^{0}=1mathit{atm}$.
For evolutions with a variation of temperature, knowing a law giving the saturating vapour
pressure at the temperature ${T}_{0}$, for example

and a hygrometric degree $mathit{HR}$, the vapour pressure is deduced from it by means of
${p}_{mathit{pv}}({T}_{0})=mathit{HR}{p}_{mathit{pv}}^{mathit{sat}}({T}_{0})$.
Furthermore, a value of PRES_VAPE equal to zero must never be taken.

### 2.4 Loadings and boundary conditions

All the boundary conditions or loadings are assigned via the command `AFFE_CHAR_MECA` [U4.44.01](doc:u4.44.01). The loadings are then activated by the factor keyword `EXCIT` of the command `STAT_NON_LINE`.

In the classical manner, three types of boundary condition are possible:

- Dirichlet-type conditions, which consist in imposing, on a part of the boundary, fixed values for principal unknowns belonging to ${leftlbrace urightrbrace }^{text{ddl}}$ (and not $u={u}^{text{ddl}}+{u}^{text{init}}$ ); for this one uses the factor keyword DDL_IMPO or FACE_IMPO of AFFE_CHAR_MECA.
- Neumann-type conditions, which consist in imposing values on the «dual quantities», either by saying nothing (zero fluxes in hydraulics and thermal analysis), or by giving them a value via the keywords `FLUN`, `FLUN_HYDR1` and `FLUN_HYDR2` of the factor keyword `FLUX_THM_REP` of the command `AFFE_CHAR_MECA`. This flux is then multiplied by a function of time (equal to 1. by default) called by FONC_MULT in the sub-keyword `EXCIT` of the command `STAT_NON_LINE`. `FLUN`, `FLUN_HYDR1` and `FLUN_HYDR2` represent respectively the thermal fluxes, the water fluxes and the fluxes of the gaseous component (cf. the end of the paragraph).
- Mixed-type conditions (or «exchange» conditions), which consist — in the unsaturated case — in imposing an «external» condition for each principal hydraulic unknown (${p}_{c},{p}_{g}$ ) as well as the corresponding exchange coefficients. Ultimately, this consists in imposing a value on the «dual quantities» as a function of the external pressures and of the exchange coefficients (in a linear manner). The external pressures and the exchange coefficients are supplied respectively by the keywords `PRE1_EXT` and `PRE2_EXT` and `COEF_11`, `COEF_12`, `COEF_21`, COEF_22, using the factor keyword `ECHANGE_THM` of the command `AFFE_CHAR_MECA`.
- The mechanical conditions in total stresses $boldsymbol{sigma} , underline{n}$ are given via PRES_REP of the command AFFE_CHAR_MECA. One will refer to the documentation of this command in order to know its possibilities.

From the syntactic point of view, the Dirichlet conditions are therefore applied as in the following example

DIRI=AFFE_CHAR_MECA(MODELE=MODELE,

DDL_IMPO=(_F(GROUP_NO=’GAUCHE’,

TEMP=0.0,),

_F(TOUT='OUI',

PRE2=0.0,),

_F(GROUP_NO=’GAUCHE’,

PRE1=0.0,),

_F(TOUT='OUI',

DX=0.0,),

_F(TOUT='OUI',

DY=0.0,),

_F(TOUT='OUI',

DZ=0.0,),

),)

For the Neumann conditions, the syntax will then be as in the following example:

NEU1=AFFE_CHAR_MECA(MODELE=MODELE,

The various possibilities will now be summarised, distinguishing the case where values are imposed on PRE1 and/or PRE2 and the case where one works on combinations of the two. It is pointed out that one may of course have different types of boundary condition according to the pieces of boundary (node groups or mesh-cell groups) which one treats. For a more complete and more detailed overview of the way in which the boundary conditions are treated in the unsaturated case, one will refer to the note reproduced in Appendix 2.

- **Case of the boundary conditions involving the principal unknowns** **PRE1 and** **PRE2**

The usual case, in which values are imposed on PRE1 and/or PRE2, is summarised here.

- Dirichlet on PRE1 and Dirichlet on $mathit{PRE2}$

The user imposes a value on $mathit{PRE1}$ and $mathit{PRE2}$ ; the fluxes are results of the analysis.

- Dirichlet on PRE1 and Neumann on $mathit{PRE2}$

The user imposes a value on PRE1 and a value on the flux associated with PRE2, by saying nothing about PRE2 or by giving a value to FLUN_HYDR2.

- Dirichlet on PRE2 and Neumann on PRE1

The user imposes a value on PRE2 and a value on the flux associated with PRE1, by saying nothing about PRE1 or by giving a value to `FLUN_HYDR1`.

- Neumann on PRE2 and Neumann on PRE1

The two fluxes are imposed either by saying nothing about PRE1 and/or PRE2 (zero fluxes) or by giving a value to `FLUN_HYDR1` and/or `FLUN_HYDR2`.

- **Case of the boundary conditions involving a linear relation between the principal unknowns** **PRE1 and** **PRE2**

It is also possible to handle linear combinations of PRE1 and PRE2. This must however be handled with caution, so as to start from a correctly posed problem. The syntax of this operator is detailed in the documentation of `AFFE_CHAR_MECA`; the example below illustrates this type of condition:

P_DDL=AFFE_CHAR_MECA(MODELE=MODELE,

LIAISON_GROUP=(_F(

GROUP_NO_1= 'BORDS',

GROUP_NO_2= 'BORDS',

DDL_1='PRE1',

DDL_2='PRE2',

COEF_MULT_1 = x,

COEF_MULT_2 = y.,

COEF_IMPO =z,),),

);

This command means that, on the boundary defined by the node group $mathit{BORDS}$ , the pressures PRE1 and PRE2 are related by the linear relation

$xmathit{PRE1}+ymathit{PRE2}=z$

**Note:**

The imposed fluxes are scalar quantities which may be applied on a line or a surface internal to the solid modelled. In this case, these boundary conditions correspond to a source.

#### 2.5.2 General advice on use

`RELATION` is supplied by relations of type `KIT_XXXX`, which make it possible to solve simultaneously from two to four balance equations. The equations considered depend on the suffix `XXXX`, with the following rule:

M denotes the mechanical balance equation,

T denotes the thermal balance equation,

H denotes a hydraulic balance equation,

V denotes the presence of a phase in vapour form (in addition to the liquid).

A single letter $H$ means that the porous medium is saturated (a single pressure variable $p$ ), for example either by gas, or by liquid, or by a liquid/gas mixture (whose gas pressure is constant).

Two letters $H$ mean that the porous medium is unsaturated (two pressure variables $p$ ), for example a liquid/vapour/gas mixture.

The presence of the two letters $mathit{HV}$ means that the porous medium is saturated by a component (in practice water), but that this component may be in liquid or vapour form. There is then only one conservation equation for this component, hence a single pressure degree of freedom, but there is a liquid flux and a vapour flux. - Either the user chooses `HYDR_UTIL`: this keyword then makes it possible to supply the saturation curve and its derivative as a function of the capillary pressure, as well as the relative permeability and its derivative as a function of the saturation. The user enters these curves via a series of formulae subsequently called by DEFI_MATERIAU (cf. 2.2.7 ).
- Either the user chooses `HYDR_ENDO`: it is the same thing but allows the use of the laws with damage `MAZARS_UNIL` and `ENDO_ISOT_BETON` (the permeability is then coupled to the damage).
- Either the user chooses `HYDR_VGM` or `HYDR_VGC`, which means that the capillarity and permeability laws will obey the Mualem–Van Genuchten model predefined in the code; in other words, the capillarity is given by a Van Genuchten law:

${S}_{mathit{we}}=frac{S{-}{S}_{mathit{wr}}}{1{-}{S}_{mathit{wr}}}$ and $m=1{-}frac{1}{n}$

${S}_{text{we}}=frac{1}{{left[1+{left(frac{{P}_{c}-{P}_{e}}{{P}_{r}}right)}^{n}right]}^{m}}$

The relative permeability to water is then expressed by integrating the prediction model proposed by Mualem (1976) into the Van Genuchten capillarity model:

${k}_{r}^{w}=sqrt{{S}_{mathit{we}}}{(1{-}{(1{-}{{S}_{mathit{we}}}^{1/m})}^{m})}^{2}$

The permeability to the gas is formulated in a similar way by a Parker law for HYDR_VGM:

${k}_{r}^{mathit{gz}}=sqrt{(1{-}{S}_{mathit{we}})}{(1{-}{{S}_{mathit{we}}}^{1/m})}^{mathrm{2m}}$

or by a cubic law for `HYDR_VGC`:

${k}_{r}^{mathit{gz}}={(1{-}S)}^{3}$

with, in every case, the parameters $n$ , $mathit{Pr}$ , $mathit{Pe}$ , $mathit{Swr}$ , $mathit{Smax}$ defined in DEFI_MATERIAU:

> $n$  corresponds to the parameter `VG_N`
> $mathit{Pr}$  corresponds to the parameter `VG_PR`
> $mathit{Pe}$ corresponds to the parameter VG_PENTR (0 by default)
> $mathit{Swr}$  corresponds to the parameter `VG_SR`

To this must be added two additional parameters corresponding to a treatment which is carried out on these curves:

> $mathit{Smax}$  which corresponds to the parameter `VG_SMAX`
> $mathit{CSAT}$  which corresponds to the parameter `VG_SATUR`

What this treatment is, and what $mathit{CSAT}$ and $mathit{Smax}$ are, will now be set out. For $S>mathit{Smax}$ , these curves are interpolated by a polynomial of degree 2, $mathit{C1}$ at $mathit{Smax}$ , so as to avoid having to treat derivatives of infinite values. Indeed, for $S=1$ :

$frac{partial {k}_{r}^{w}(S)}{partial S}=infty$

and in the case of `HYDR_VGM`:

$frac{partial {k}_{r}^{mathit{gz}}(S)}{partial S}=infty$

In order to avoid having to treat this problem (which a priori has no physical meaning) these functions are replaced, from a saturation $mathit{Smax}$ , by a second-order polynomial $mathit{C1}$ at that point.

This gives, for the function ${k}_{r}^{w}(S)$ :

For $S=mathit{Smax}$ , one determines the polynomial $mathit{PL}(S)$ such that

$lbrace  begin{array}{c}mathit{PL}({S}_{max})={k}_{r}^{w}({S}_{max})\ mathit{PL}'({S}_{max})=frac{partial {k}_{r}^{w}}{partial S}({S}_{max})end{array}$ and $mathit{PL}(1)=0$

For $S>mathit{Smax}$ , ${k}_{r}^{w}(S)$ is replaced by $mathit{PL}(S)$ .

And, in the case of `HYDR_VGM`, for ${k}_{r}^{mathit{gz}}(S)$ :

For $S=mathit{Smax}$ , one determines the polynomial $mathit{PG}(S)$ such that

$lbrace  begin{array}{c}mathit{PG}({S}_{max})={k}_{r}^{mathit{gz}}({S}_{max})\ mathit{PG}'({S}_{max})=frac{partial {k}_{r}^{mathit{gz}}}{partial S}({S}_{max})end{array}$ and $mathit{PG}(0)=1$

For $S>mathit{Smax}$ , ${k}_{r}^{mathit{gz}}(S)$ is replaced by $mathit{PG}(S)$ as in the example. In the case of a cubic law, no special treatment is necessary.

For $S>mathit{Smax}$ :

$S(mathit{Pc})=1{-}frac{A}{B{-}{P}_{c}}$

With $A$ and $B$ such that the curve is $mathit{C1}$ at $mathit{Smax}$ .

#### 2.6.3 Isovalues

Caution: distinction between linear solution and quadratic mesh

It is recalled that, for any THM problem and in order to avoid any ambiguity for the user, the mesh used is always quadratic. On the other hand the solution of the hydraulic and thermal problem is linear: the unknowns $mathit{PRE1}$ , $mathit{PRE2}$ and $mathit{TEMP}$ therefore exist only on the vertex nodes of the mesh.

At the end of the analysis, one obtains a result in this form:

- The pressures $mathit{PRE1}$ , $mathit{PRE2}$ and the temperatures $mathit{TEMP}$ are on the vertex nodes of the elements.
- The internal variables and the stresses are on the Gauss points and/or the nodes of the quadratic mesh (according to whether one is in selective or lumped integration) and the mechanical displacements are on the vertices and the mid-side nodes.

There is therefore a conflict between the quadratic meshes and the linear analyses, which will pose a problem, for example, for plotting isovalues.

There then exists a simple way of overcoming this problem by projecting the whole of the results onto the linear mesh obtained from the quadratic mesh. Thus all the results will be defined at the same place (on the vertices).

For $mathit{TEMP}$ , $mathit{PRE1}$ and $mathit{PRE2}$ , it is sufficient to project as in the following example:

**## projection of the quadratic mesh onto the linear mesh:**

MAILQ4 = CREA_MAILLAGE(

MAILLAGE = MAIL,

QUAD_LINE = _F(TOUT = 'OUI'),

)

**## assignment of the corresponding model:**

MODELQ4=AFFE_MODELE(MAILLAGE=MAILQ4,

AFFE=_F(TOUT='OUI',

PHENOMENE='MECANIQUE',

MODELISATION='D_PLAN',),);

**## projection of the result onto this new model:**

RESULIN = PROJ_CHAMP(

METHODE='ELEM',

RESULTAT=resu,

MODELE_1=MODELQ8,

MODELE_2=MODELQ4,

NOM_CHAM ='DEPL',

TOUT_ORDRE='OUI')

**## usual post-processing, but on the linear mesh:**

IMPR_RESU(FORMAT='MED',

RESU=_F(

RESULTAT=RESULIN,

NUME_ORDRE=(1,10,20,),

> NOM_CMP = ('PRE1','PRE2',),

MAILLAGE=MAILQ4,),);

There exists an alternative method, which may prove faster for large industrial studies. This method uses `MACR_ADAP_MAIL` [U7.03.01](doc:u7.03.01). It is assumed that resu is composed of nbinst time instants stored in the list linst. The preceding post-processing can also be done in the following way:

PRE = [None]*nbinst;

## 3 THM modellings by a second-gradient approach

Second-gradient modellings and modellings of the second gradient of dilatation are described in the documentation [R5.04.03](doc:r5.04.03) within the framework of porous media. Their use is indispensable when the mechanical constitutive laws model a degradation of the material — one speaks of softening laws. Indeed, in this case, the solutions depend on the mesh, including when the medium is coupled with the hydraulic equations.

The numerical integration of these models followed an atypical protocol at the time of its implementation, in order to facilitate its use in Code_Aster. Thus, the regularising part (second gradient) is defined as a patch which it is sufficient to «stick» onto the structure to be modelled. The steps to be followed in order to produce such a simulation are described below.

1. Definition of the patch
2. Choice of the model
3. Definition of the material
4. Boundary conditions
5. Analysis

### 3.1 Definition of the patch

For the simulation of a THM study by a second-gradient type of approach, one must define a quadratic mesh for the discretisation of the structure, then a second mesh for the regularising patch. The particular feature of this second mesh is that it must necessarily be topologically identical to the first mesh (the same nodes and the same geometric elements). For reasons of numerical simplification and for reasons of consistency of the models, the following procedure is advised:

1. Definition of a spatial discretisation for the structure and the THM modelling;
2. Duplication of the preceding mesh over the whole of the structure by the command CREA_MAILLAGE in order to define the patch
3. Possible modification of the type of mesh cells for a study with Lagrange multipliers at the centres of the elements.

#### 3.1.1 Step 1. Definition of the mesh of the structure

This first step is to be produced by a mesher in order to discretise the structure. The mesh is then recovered by the *Code_Aster* command:

*reading of the mesh (quadratic)*

**MA = LIRE_MAILLAGE()**

#### 3.1.2 Step 2. Duplication of the mesh in order to define the patch

There exists in *Code_Aster* a procedure which makes it possible to duplicate a mesh while keeping the same nodes as the original mesh — it is indispensable that the nodes of the first mesh be included in the patch (cf. [R5.04.03](doc:r5.04.03)). It is necessary to define this second mesh in order to make it possible to define the second-gradient modelling on the regularising patch.

Duplication of the mesh (quadratic). Only the mesh cells are duplicated; the nodes remain common. The aim is to associate a different modelling with each of these meshes.

MAIL=CREA_MAILLAGE(MAILLAGE = MA,

CREA_GROUP_MA = ( _F( NOM = 'ROCHE_REG',

GROUP_MA = 'ROCHE')))

#### 3.1.3 Step 3. (Optional) modification of the mesh of the patch

There exist different finite-element discretisations for interpolating the second-gradient modellings (with or without Lagrange multipliers, Lagrange multipliers at the centre of the elements or at the vertex nodes). Consequently the mesh of the patch must be adapted to the discretisation space of the second-gradient modelling.

For example, in order to take into account an interpolation with Lagrange multipliers at the centres of the elements, the nodes at the centres of the elements must be defined, because these are not, in general, provided for in the spatial discretisations proposed by most meshers. Thus, in this case, one may use the following *Code_Aster* command:

Introduction of central nodes on the finite elements of the new mesh for a $mathit{P2}-mathit{P1}-mathit{P0}$ interpolation. Procedure necessary in order to take into account the interpolations of the Lagrange multipliers.

MAILLAGE=CREA_MAILLAGE(MAILLAGE = MAIL,

MODI_MAILLE =( _F( GROUP_MA = 'ROCHE_REG',

OPTION = 'QUAD8_9'),

_F( GROUP_MA = 'ROCHE_REG',

OPTION = 'TRIA6_7')))

This step is possible only in 2D when the finite-element interpolation considers Lagrange multipliers constant per element.

### 3.2 Choice of the model

The second-gradient or dilatation-second-gradient «regularising patches» are to be combined with any type of THM modelling. They are of interest, of course, only in the presence of a (softening) mechanical component. The modellings available are:

| Modelling | Geometric modelling | Phenomenon taken into account |
| --- | --- | --- |
| D_PLAN_2DG | Plane | Second gradient |
| D_PLAN_DIL | Plane | Second gradient of dilatation |
| 3D_DIL | 3D | Second gradient of dilatation |

**Note:**

*It should be noted that it is strongly advised to use the second-gradient-of-dilatation modellings in order to treat the constitutive behaviours of soils and rocks, or more particularly any type of material presenting a volume variation during its degradation.*

The nodal unknowns of the THM modellings are then enriched after addition of the second-gradient «patch». The number of unknowns varies according to the patch considered and the type of mesh cells characterising the spatial discretisation of the patch. For the second-gradient-of-dilatation modelling, it is therefore by the choice of the topology of the mesh cell that the interpolation of the Lagrange multipliers will be determined (see the table below). The various possibilities are summarised in the table below, where, in agreement with [R5.04.03](doc:r5.04.03), ${f}_{ij}$ denotes the components of the microscopic strain tensor, $chi$ the microscopic volumetric strain and $lambda$ the Lagrange multiplier:

| Modelling | Mesh-cell type | Degrees of freedom | Position of the degree of freedom |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| D_PLAN_2DG | TRIA7, QUAD9 | {f}_{ij} | At the vertices of each element |
|  |  | {lambda}_{ij} | At the centre of each element |
| D_PLAN_DIL | TRIA7, QUAD9 | chi | At the vertices of each element |
|  |  | lambda | At the centre of each element |
|  | TRIA6, QUAD8 | chi | At the vertices of each element |
| 3D_DIL | TETRA10, HEXA20, PENTA15 | chi , lambda | At the vertices of each element |

One then proceeds in the following way:

MODELE = AFFE_MODELE( MAILLAGE = MAILLAGE,

AFFE = ( _F( GROUP_MA = 'ROCHE',

PHENOMENE = 'MECANIQUE',

MODELISATION = 'D_PLAN_HMS'),

_F( GROUP_MA = 'ROCHE_REG',

PHENOMENE = 'MECANIQUE',

MODELISATION = 'D_PLAN_DIL')))

### 3.3 Definition of the material

In addition to the definition of the material parameters necessary for the THM analysis described in the preceding chapter, a parameter must be added which characterises the characteristic length of the second-gradient modelling (cf. [R5.04.03](doc:r5.04.03)). This datum in fact characterises the dimension of the neighbourhood which contributes to the description of a material point.

This length is defined from the material components $mathit{A1}$ and $mathit{A3}$ of the constitutive law of second-gradient linear elasticity inspired by the work of Mindlin, in the case of the second-gradient-of-dilatation modelling. It is in the determination of these parameters that the greatest difficulty lies. There does not exist today an analytical method for identifying them. The practice is to carry out a few preliminary tests. In the case of the second-gradient modellings this length is a function of the 5 components $mathit{A1}$ , $mathit{A2}$ , $mathit{A3},$ $mathit{A4}$ and $mathit{A5}$ , to be supplied under the keyword ELAS_2NDG of DEFI_MATERIAU (cf. [R5.04.03](doc:r5.04.03)).

One may, furthermore, add a numerical penalisation parameter. It has been shown that the penalisation term of the second-gradient-of-dilatation modellings improves the numerical convergence without disturbing the quality of the results.

Here is an example of the definition of the material parameters for the second-gradient part.

SOL2 = DEFI_MATERIAU( ELAS_2NDG = _F( A1 = 4.0E4,

A2 = 0.0,

A3 = 0.0,

A4 = 0.0,

A5 = 0.0,),

> NON_LOCAL = _F(PENA_LAGR=1.0E12,),);

Then assignment of the material parameters following the same procedure as for the definition of the modellings.

MATE = AFFE_MATERIAU( MAILLAGE = MAILLAGE,

AFFE = (_F( TOUT = ‘ROCHE’,

MATER = SOL_HM,),

> _F( GROUP_MA = 'ROCHE_REG',

MATER = SOL2)))

### 3.4 Impact on the boundary conditions

There is no impact to be made in the Code_Aster command file in order to take the boundary conditions into account with the second-gradient modellings, relative to the classical THM modellings. On the other hand, it is essential to note that the physical meaning of the boundary conditions of normal-stress pressure is modified with the second-gradient modellings (cf. [R5.04.03](doc:r5.04.03)).

### 3.5 Solution of the problem

The analysis is to be carried out by the command STAT_NON_LINE. It is sufficient to add to the classical THM analysis the second-gradient elasticity constitutive relation on the mesh corresponding to the patch, as in the example below (keyword ELAS). There is no incompatibility with the constitutive laws. It should be noted, all the same, that a THM simulation by a second-gradient approach is of interest only in the presence of a mechanical component in the coupled analysis.

It is important to note that, when the modelling takes into account an interpolation of the Lagrange multipliers which are constant per element, it is necessary to use the Mumps solver for the solution.

Definition of the non-linear static analysis with a constitutive law associated with each of the modellings: a Drucker–Prager type of constitutive behaviour for the first gradient, and linear elasticity for the second gradient

U1 = STAT_NON_LINE( MODELE = MODELE,

CHAM_MATER = MATE,

EXCIT = ( _F( CHARGE = CHCI ),…),

SOLVEUR = ( _F( METHODE='MUMPS',)),

COMPORTEMENT = ( _F(GROUP_MA='ROCHE',

RELATION = 'DRUCK_PRAGER',),

_F(GROUP_MA = 'ROCHE_REG',

RELATION = 'ELAS',),),

NEWTON = _F( MATRICE = 'TANGENTE',REAC_ITER = 1 ),

INCREMENT = _F( LIST_INST = TEMPS ))

**A2.1** **Variational formulation of the conservation equations**

Reference is made here to [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11). These equations are

$dot{{m}_{text{lq}}}+{dot{m}}_{text{vp}}+text{Div}({M}_{text{lq}}+{M}_{text{vp}})=0$                             eq A5.1-1

$dot{{m}_{text{as}}}+text{Div}({M}_{text{as}})=0$                                         eq A5.1-2

The variational formulation deduced is given by

$begin{array}{}-{int}_{Omega}(dot{{m}_{text{lq}}}+{dot{m}}_{text{vp}}){pi}_{1}dOmega +{int}_{Omega}({M}_{text{lq}}+{M}_{text{vp}}).nabla {pi}_{1}dOmega =\ {int}_{partial Omega }({M}_{{text{lq}}_{text{ext}}}+{M}_{{text{vp}}_{text{ext}}}).{pi}_{1}dGamma forall {pi}_{1}in {P}_{{1}_{text{ad}}}end{array}$             eq A5.1-3

$begin{array}{}-{int}_{Omega}dot{{m}_{text{as}}}{pi}_{2}dOmega +{int}_{Omega}{M}_{text{as}}.nabla {pi}_{2}dOmega =\ {int}_{partial Omega }{M}_{{text{as}}_{text{ext}}}.{pi}_{2}dGamma forall {pi}_{2}in {P}_{{2}_{text{ad}}}end{array}$                         eq A5.1-4

The capillary and gas pressures are related to the water, vapour and dry-air pressures by the relations:

${p}_{c}={p}_{text{gz}}-{p}_{text{lq}}$                                         eq A5.1-5

${p}_{text{gz}}={p}_{text{vp}}+{p}_{text{as}}$                                         eq A5.1-6

The vapour pressure is not an independent variable. It is related to the liquid pressure ${p}_{text{lq}}$ by the relations

$frac{{text{dp}}_{text{vp}}}{{rho}_{text{vp}}}=frac{{text{dp}}_{text{lq}}}{{rho}_{text{lq}}}+({h}_{text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{text{lq}}^{m})frac{text{dT}}{T}$                             eq A5.1-7

${text{dh}}_{text{lq}}^{m}={C}_{text{lq}}^{p}text{dT}+(1-3{alpha}_{text{lq}}T)frac{{text{dp}}_{text{lq}}}{{rho}_{text{lq}}}$                         eq A5.1-8

${text{dh}}_{text{vp}}^{m}={C}_{text{vp}}^{p}text{dT}$                                         eq A5.1-9

These relations show that the vapour pressure is completely determined by the knowledge of ${p}_{text{lq}}$ (and of its evolution). Often, these relations serve to establish Kelvin's law, $frac{{p}_{text{lq}}-{p}_{text{lq}}^{0}}{{rho}_{text{lq}}}=frac{R}{{M}_{text{vp}}^{text{ol}}}Tln(frac{{p}_{text{vp}}}{{p}_{text{vp}}^{text{sat}}(T)})$ , but this law is not used directly in Aster.

The Aster reference documents say nothing about what the variables ${pi}_{1}$ and ${pi}_{2}$ are. But two elements can put us on the track:

- On the one hand, ${pi}_{1}in {P}_{{1}_{text{ad}}}$ and ${pi}_{2}in {P}_{{2}_{text{ad}}}$ whereas ${P}_{{1}_{text{ad}}}$ and ${P}_{{2}_{text{ad}}}$ are the spaces to which $mathit{PRE1}$ and $mathit{PRE2}$ belong (thus including their boundary conditions).
- On the other hand, in chapter 7 of [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10), one sees that the virtual strain ${E}_{g}^{text{*}mathrm{el}}=(v,varepsilon (v),{pi}_{1},nabla {pi}_{1},{pi}_{2},nabla {pi}_{2},tau ,nabla tau )$ is related to the virtual nodal-displacement vector ${U}^{text{*}mathrm{el}}=(v,{pi}_{1},{pi}_{2},tau )$ by the same operator ${Q}_{g}^{text{el}}$ as the one which relates to each other the strain ${E}_{g}^{text{el}}=(u,varepsilon (u),{p}_{1},nabla {p}_{1},{p}_{2},nabla {p}_{2},T,nabla T)$ and the nodal displacement ${U}^{text{el}}=(u,{p}_{1},{p}_{2},T)$ :

- ${E}_{g}^{text{*}mathrm{el}}={Q}_{g}^{text{el}}{U}^{text{*}mathrm{el}}$
- ${E}_{g}^{text{el}}={Q}_{g}^{text{el}}{U}^{text{el}}$

It is then clear that ${pi}_{1}$ and ${pi}_{2}$ are virtual variations of ${p}_{1}$ and ${p}_{2}$

Hence the table:

${p}_{1}={p}_{c}Rightarrow {pi}_{1}=delta {p}_{c}={p}_{c}^{text{*}}$

${p}_{1}={p}_{text{lq}}Rightarrow {pi}_{1}=delta {p}_{text{lq}}={p}_{mathrm{lq}}^{text{*}}$

${p}_{2}={p}_{text{gz}}Rightarrow {pi}_{2}=delta {p}_{text{gz}}={p}_{mathrm{gz}}^{text{*}}$

**A2.2** **Case of boundary conditions involving the principal unknowns**

${p}_{text{gz}}={p}_{text{as}}+{p}_{text{vp}}={p}_{{}_{text{gz}}}^{text{imp}}$

The fluxes are then results of analyses by [eq A5.1-3] and [eq A5.1-4]

- **Dirichlet** $mathit{PRE1}$ **, Neumann** $mathit{PRE2}$

$begin{array}{}{p}_{c}={p}_{text{gz}}-{p}_{text{lq}}={p}_{{}_{c}}^{text{imp}}\ {p}_{{}_{1}}^{text{imp}}={p}_{{}_{c}}^{text{imp}}end{array}$

In order to give the proof in the non-homogeneous framework, one would have to introduce a lifting of the condition ${p}_{1}={p}_{{}_{1}}^{text{imp}}$ (that is to say a particular field satisfying this condition). That weighs down the expressions and brings nothing, so one places oneself in the homogeneous framework ${p}_{{}_{1}}^{text{imp}}=0$

In [eq A5.1-3] and [eq A5.1-4], one may therefore take ${pi}_{2}$ arbitrary and ${pi}_{1}$ satisfying ${pi}_{1}=0$ on $partial {O}_{d}$ . One then begins by taking ${pi}_{1}=0$ and ${pi}_{2}=0$ on the whole boundary $partial Omega$ and one obtains [eq A5.1-1] and [eq A5.1-2] in the sense of distributions. One then multiplies [eq A5.1-1] by ${pi}_{1}$ such that ${pi}_{1}=0$ on $partial {Omega}_{d}$ , one multiplies [eq A5.1-2] by an arbitrary ${pi}_{2}$ , one integrates by parts, one takes [eq A5.1-3] and [eq A5.1-4] into account and one obtains, denoting by $n$ the normal to the boundary:

${int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}{M}_{text{as}}.n.{pi}_{2}dGamma ={int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}{M}_{2text{ext}}{pi}_{2}dGamma ,phantom{rule{4em}{0ex}}forall {pi}_{2}$

$begin{array}{c}{M}_{text{as}}.n={M}_{2text{ext}}text{sur}partial {Omega}_{d}end{array}$

This corresponds to:

$begin{array}{c}{p}_{text{gz}}={p}_{text{as}}+{p}_{text{vp}}={p}_{{}_{text{gz}}}^{text{imp}}\ {p}_{2}^{text{imp}}={p}_{{}_{text{gz}}}^{text{imp}}end{array}$

The proof is the same as in the preceding paragraph and leads to:

$begin{array}{c}({M}_{text{lq}}+{M}_{text{vp}}).n={M}_{1text{ext}}text{sur}partial {O}_{d}end{array}$

**A** 2 **.3** **Case of boundary conditions involving linear relations between principal unknowns**

*Code_Aster* makes it possible to introduce, as boundary conditions, relations between degrees of freedom, carried by the same node or by different nodes. This possibility is reached via the keyword LIAISON_DDL of the command AFFE_CHAR_MECA.

Let ${p}_{{}_{text{lq}}}^{text{imp}}$ be the value which one wants to impose on the liquid pressure on $partial {O}_{d}$ . Taking [eq A5.1-5] into account, and the choice of the principal unknowns for this constitutive behaviour, one writes:

${p}_{text{gz}}-{p}_{c}={p}_{2}-{p}_{1}={p}_{{}_{text{lq}}}^{text{imp}}$                             eq A5.3-1

The linear relations are treated in Aster by introduction of Lagrange multipliers. This corresponds in the present case to the following formulation:

Find ${p}_{1},{p}_{2,}mu$ such that:

$begin{array}{}-{int}_{Omega}(dot{{m}_{text{lq}}}+{dot{m}}_{text{vp}}){pi}_{1}dOmega +{int}_{Omega}({M}_{text{lq}}+{M}_{text{vp}}).nabla {pi}_{1}dOmega -{int}_{Omega}dot{{m}_{text{as}}}{pi}_{2}dOmega +{int}_{Omega}{M}_{text{as}}.nabla {pi}_{2}dOmega +\ +{int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}{mu}^{text{*}}({p}_{2}-{p}_{1}-{p}_{mathrm{lq}}^{text{imp}})dGamma +{int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}mu ({pi}_{2}-{pi}_{1}-{p}_{mathrm{lq}}^{text{imp}})dGamma forall {pi}_{1},{pi}_{2},{mu}^{text{*}}end{array}$     eq A5.3-2

In order to give the proof in the non-homogeneous framework, one would have to introduce a lifting of the condition ${p}_{2}-{p}_{1}-{p}_{{}_{text{lq}}}^{text{imp}}=0$ (that is to say particular fields satisfying this condition). That weighs down the expressions and brings nothing, so one places oneself in the homogeneous framework ${p}_{{}_{text{lq}}}^{text{imp}}=0$ .

One then begins by taking ${pi}_{1}=0$ and ${pi}_{2}=0$ on the whole boundary $partial Omega$ and one obtains [eq A5.1-1] and [eq A5.1-2] in the sense of distributions. One then multiplies [eq A5.1-1] by an arbitrary ${pi}_{1}$ , one multiplies [eq A5.1-2] by an arbitrary ${pi}_{2}$ , one integrates by parts, one carries the results found into [eq A5.3-2] and one obtains:

$begin{array}{}{int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}({M}_{text{lq}}+{M}_{text{vp}}).n.{pi}_{1}dGamma +{int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}{M}_{text{as}}.n.{pi}_{2}dGamma +\ {int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}{mu}^{text{*}}({p}_{2}-{p}_{1})dGamma +{int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}mu ({pi}_{2}-{pi}_{1})dGamma =0forall {pi}_{1},{pi}_{2},{mu}^{text{*}}end{array}$      eq A5.3-3

It is clear that [eq A5.3-3] indeed gives back ${p}_{2}-{p}_{1}={p}_{{}_{text{lq}}}^{text{imp}}=0$

By furthermore taking ${pi}_{2}-{pi}_{1}=0$ , one finds:

${int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}({M}_{text{lq}}+{M}_{text{vp}}+{M}_{text{as}}).n.{pi}_{1}dGamma =0forall {Gamma}_{1}$

From which one deduces:

$begin{array}{c}({M}_{text{lq}}+{M}_{text{vp}}+{M}_{text{as}}).n=0text{sur}partial {Omega}_{d}end{array}$                 eq A5.3-4

A2.4    Case of mixed boundary conditions

In this section the case of mixed boundary conditions (or exchange conditions) applied in the form of a Neumann condition is addressed. These conditions may bear on one or the other of the conservation equations, or indeed on both.

The writing of the fluxes is then done in the form:

$begin{array}{c}left({M}_{text{lq}}+{M}_{text{vp}}right).n=phantom{rule{0.5em}{0ex}}{M}_{1text{ext}}text{sur}partial {Omega}_{d}end{array}$ avec ${M}_{1text{ext}}={lambda}_{11}({p}_{1}-{p}_{{}_{1}}^{text{ext}})-{lambda}_{12}({p}_{2}-{p}_{{}_{2}}^{text{ext}})$

and/or

$begin{array}{c}{M}_{text{as}}.n={M}_{2text{ext}}text{sur}partial {Omega}_{d}end{array}$ avec ${M}_{2text{ext}}={lambda}_{21}({p}_{1}-{p}_{{}_{1}}^{text{ext}})-{lambda}_{22}({p}_{2}-{p}_{{}_{2}}^{text{ext}})$

This condition is implemented in an explicit manner (the value of $mathit{PRE1}$ and/or $mathit{PRE2}$ being taken at the previous time). These mixed conditions are therefore applied as Neumann conditions (cf. previously).

**A** 2 **.** 5 **The non-linear cases**

Only more difficult questions are touched upon here, consisting in imposing either the vapour pressure or the dry-air pressure. Taking the relations [eq A5.1-7], [eq A5.1-8] and [eq A5.1-9] into account, imposing a value on the vapour pressure amounts to imposing a non-linear relation on the liquid pressure; just as imposing a dry-air pressure on the gas pressure.

By way of example, the case of an imposed dry-air pressure for a LIQU_VAPE_GAZ constitutive behaviour is addressed, and it is assumed that one knows how to write the non-linear relation relating the vapour pressure and the liquid pressure.

The relation to be imposed is therefore:

${p}_{text{as}}={p}_{text{gz}}-{p}_{text{vp}}={p}_{2}-{p}_{text{vp}}={p}_{{}_{text{as}}}^{text{imp}}$                             eq A5.4-1

By differentiating this relation, one will find a condition on the virtual variations of the pressures:

${text{dp}}_{text{as}}={text{dp}}_{text{gz}}-frac{partial {p}_{text{vp}}}{partial {p}_{text{lq}}}{text{dp}}_{text{lq}}={text{dp}}_{text{gz}}-frac{partial {p}_{text{vp}}}{partial {p}_{text{lq}}}({text{dp}}_{text{gz}}-{text{dp}}_{c})$

Or again

${text{dp}}_{text{as}}={text{dp}}_{2}-frac{partial {p}_{text{vp}}}{partial {p}_{text{lq}}}({text{dp}}_{2}-{text{dp}}_{1})=frac{partial {p}_{text{vp}}}{partial {p}_{text{lq}}}{text{dp}}_{1}+(1-frac{partial {p}_{text{vp}}}{partial {p}_{text{lq}}}){text{dp}}_{2}$

The variational formulation would then be:

$begin{array}{c}-{int}_{Omega}left(dot{{m}_{text{lq}}}+{dot{m}}_{text{vp}}right){pi}_{1}dOmega +{int}_{Omega}left({M}_{text{lq}}+{M}_{text{vp}}right).nabla {pi}_{1}dOmega -{int}_{Omega}dot{{m}_{text{as}}}{pi}_{2}dOmega +{int}_{Omega}{M}_{text{as}}.nabla {pi}_{2}dOmega +\ +{int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}{mu}^{text{*}}left({p}_{2}-{p}_{text{vp}}-{p}_{mathit{as}}^{text{imp}}right)dGamma +{int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}mu left(frac{partial {p}_{text{vp}}}{partial {p}_{text{lq}}}{pi}_{1}+left(1-frac{partial {p}_{text{vp}}}{partial {p}_{text{lq}}}right){pi}_{2}right)dGamma phantom{rule{4em}{0ex}}forall {pi}_{1},{pi}_{2},{mu}^{text{*}}end{array}$ Et on trouverait:

${int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}({M}_{text{lq}}+{M}_{text{vp}}).n.{pi}_{1}dGamma +{int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}{M}_{text{as}}.n.{pi}_{2}dGamma +{int}_{partial {Omega}_{d}}mu (frac{partial {p}_{text{vp}}}{partial {p}_{text{lq}}}{pi}_{1}+(1-frac{partial {p}_{text{vp}}}{partial {p}_{text{lq}}}){pi}_{2})dGamma =0forall {pi}_{1},{pi}_{2}$

By taking $frac{partial {p}_{text{vp}}}{partial {p}_{text{lq}}}{pi}_{1}+(1-frac{partial {p}_{text{vp}}}{partial {p}_{text{lq}}}){pi}_{2}=0$ one would find:

Numerical approach for the calculation of the input parameters of the law HYDR_TABBAL

In this appendix, the numerical approach used to calculate the input parameters, indispensable to the use of the law ’HYDR_TABBAL’ (see section 2.5), is detailed. It is recalled that these parameters are: ${S}_{mathit{BJH}}(mathit{HR})$ , ${A}_{0}$ , ${omega}_{mathit{BJH}}(mathit{HR})$ , $t(mathit{HR})$ and ${left(frac{partial gamma }{partial {epsilon}_{s}}right)|}_{mu}$ . The user is advised to evaluate these parameters in the order presented below:

- ${A}_{0}$ is called the specific surface of the material per unit volume (in m²/m³), or also the total surface of the pores per unit volume. There exist various methods for estimating the specific surface. Among them, the BET [3] and BJH [4] methods, which are numerical methods based on experimental water-retention curves. As a first step, the user is advised to calculate the value of the specific surface ${S}_{mathit{BET}}$ by the BET method according to equations A2.1 and A2.2:

$frac{mathit{HR}}{wleft(mathit{HR}-1right)}=frac{1+left(C-1right)mathit{HR}}{C{w}_{m}}$                 eq A2.1

> ${S}_{mathit{BET}}=frac{{N}_{A}{A}_{m}{V}_{m}}{{V}_{M}}$             eq A2.2

In equation A2.1, $w(mathit{HR})$ is the water-retention curve of the material written in terms of water content. The BET method normally applies in the range of $mathit{HR}$ situated between 5% and 35%. In this range, by plotting the curve $frac{mathit{HR}}{wleft(mathit{HR}-1right)}$ as a function of $(mathit{HR})$ , one obtains a straight line. From the latter, one can calculate a slope $frac{1}{C{w}_{m}}$ and an intercept $frac{C-1}{C{w}_{m}}$ . This will make it possible to evaluate the value of the parameters $C$ and ${w}_{m}$ of equation A2.1. The volume ${V}_{m}$ (in m3/g) of equation A2.2 is the volume of water corresponding to ${w}_{m}$ . Finally, ${S}_{mathit{BET}}$ is calculated from equation A2.2 with ${N}_{A}$ (=6.022 1023 mol-1) Avogadro's number, ${A}_{m}$ (=10.6 Å2) the surface occupied by a water molecule and ${V}_{M}$ (=18.10-6 m3/mol) the molar volume of water.

${A}_{0}$ is then calculated from the product of the dry density (g/m3) of the material by the specific surface ${S}_{mathit{BET}}$ (m²/g).

- $t(mathit{HR})$ is the thickness of the adsorbed water layer. This thickness can be evaluated by various empirical relations which can be found in the literature. Among them, that of Badmann [2] is cited, which is written as: $tleft(mathit{HR}right)={K}_{1}+{K}_{2}bullet ln(-lnleft(mathit{RH}right))$ with ${K}_{1}$ and ${K}_{2}$ coefficients to be recalibrated on a water-retention curve of the material ( $t(mathit{HR})={V}_{w}(mathit{HR})/{S}_{mathit{BET}}$  and ${V}_{w}(mathit{HR})$ the volume of water calculated from the retention curve). For cementitious materials and an ambient temperature, Badmann calculates ${K}_{1}$ = 0.385 nm and ${K}_{2}$ = -0.189 nm.
- For the tables ${S}_{mathit{BJH}}(mathit{HR})$ and ${omega}_{mathit{BJH}}(mathit{HR})$ , a BJH calculation according to the iterative method introduced by Barrett, Joyner and Halenda [4] is required. This method is described exhaustively in [5].

As input data necessary in order to carry out the BJH calculation, one cites: the water-retention curve of the material written in water content or in saturation (as a function of HR), the thickness of the adsorbed layer $t(mathit{HR})$ and a value of ${A}_{0}$ according to the BET method. It is advised, before proceeding with the BJH calculation, that the water-retention curve be fitted by a numerical model, for example the Van Genuchten model or others, or even a simple linear interpolation between the experimental points.

The user begins the BJH calculation by estimating the mean radius of the pores $stackrel{´}{{r}_{p,n}}(mathit{HR})$ which will be iteratively emptied of their water according to the level of HR. This radius is calculated according to equations A2.3, A2.4 and A2.5, with ${r}_{k}(mathit{HR})$ calculated according to the Kelvin–Laplace law (equation A2.5, where ${sigma}_{L/G}$ is the surface tension of the liquid/gas surface, equal to 0.072 N/m).

$stackrel{´}{{r}_{p,n}}(mathit{HR})=frac{({r}_{p,n}(mathit{HR})+{r}_{p,n-1}(mathit{HR}))}{2}$ eq A2.3

${r}_{p,n}(mathit{HR})={r}_{k,n}(mathit{HR})+{t}_{n}(mathit{HR})$              eq A2.4

${r}_{k,n}(mathit{HR})=frac{-2{sigma}_{L/G}{M}_{w}^{mathit{mol}}}{RT{rho}_{w}ln(mathit{HR})}$                  eq A2.5

Next, the quantity of water desorbed between two consecutive steps *n and n-1* is calculated: $delta {V}_{w,n}={V}_{w,n}-{V}_{w,n-1}$ with ${V}_{w,n}$ calculated from the water-retention curve for a level of HR.

The user moves on to the calculation of the volume $(delta {V}_{p,n})$ and of the surface $(delta {a}_{p,n})$ of the pores which will be iteratively emptied with the level of HR. This is done respectively by equations A2.6 and A2.7.

$delta {V}_{p,n}={left(frac{stackrel{´}{{r}_{p,n}}}{stackrel{´}{{r}_{p,n}}-{t}_{n}}right)}^{2}bullet [delta {V}_{w,n}]-delta {t}_{n}bullet sum_{i=1}^{n-1}[delta {a}_{p,n}bullet (1-frac{{t}_{n}}{stackrel{´}{{r}_{p,n}}})]$ eq A2.6

$delta {a}_{p,n}=2delta {V}_{p,n}/stackrel{´}{{r}_{p,n}}$ eq A2.7

Finally, the cumulative volume $sum_{i}delta {V}_{p,i}$ and the cumulative surface $sum_{i}delta {a}_{p,i}$ of the emptied pores are calculated for each level of HR. These two quantities will be used in the calculation (equations A2.8 and A2.9) of ${S}_{mathit{BJH}}(mathit{HR})$ and ${omega}_{mathit{BJH}}(mathit{HR})$ . These two quantities represent respectively the volume fraction of the pores filled with liquid water (saturated) and the surface fraction of the unsaturated pores (filled by gas and a layer of adsorbed water):

${S}_{mathit{BJH}}(mathit{HR})=1-sum_{i}delta {V}_{p,i}/({varphi}_{0}bullet {V}_{0})$ eq A2.8

${omega}_{mathit{BJH}}(mathit{HR})=sum_{i}delta {a}_{p,i}/{S}_{mathit{BET}}$ eq A2.9

In equation A2.8, $({varphi}_{0}bullet {V}_{0})$ represents the total volume of the pores, with $({varphi}_{0})$ the porosity of the material and $({V}_{0})$ the total volume of the specimen. ${S}_{mathit{BET}}$ is the specific surface of the material previously calculated by BET.

The validity of the BJH calculation can be checked, according to [5], by comparing the value of ${S}_{mathit{BET}}$ with the specific surface obtained by BJH ${S}_{mathit{tot},mathit{BJH}}$ (total cumulative surface of the emptied pores). A difference of less than 10% is advised between these two quantities.
