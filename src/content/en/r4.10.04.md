# r4.10.04 Detection of singularities and computation of an element-size map

**Summary:**

A method is proposed here which aims to improve the treatment of singularities in the mesh-adaptation strategies with the software HOMARD (in the case of refinement) or with the software GMSH (case of remeshing). This mechanism makes it possible, on the one hand, to detect the finite elements connected to singular zones and, on the other hand, to obtain, for a given global error, the size of the finite elements of the new mesh in the case of remeshing.

This functionality is accessible in the command CALC_ERREUR through the computation options SING_ELEM (field constant per element) or SING_ELNO (field at the nodes per element). This option is valid only in mechanics. An error estimator in mechanics and the strain energy on each element must have been computed beforehand. Strictly speaking, this method is valid only in elasticity.

> **Contents**

## 1 Introduction

The objective of the option proposed here is to improve the treatment of singularities in the mesh-adaptation strategies proposed in Code_Aster. Indeed, the presence of singularities (present in practice in any analysis of real structures by finite elements) implies two sorts of difficulties which will be qualified here as “theoretical” and “practical”.

The “theoretical” difficulties come from the fact that the contribution to the error in energy of the elements touching a singularity is of the form $C{h}^{\alpha}$ ($C$ a constant, $h$ the size of the element and $\alpha$ the order of the singularity) whereas the contribution of the elements outside a singularity is of the form $C{h}^{q}$ ($q$ depending only on the degree of interpolation of the shape functions of the element). The adaptation of the mesh must take this difference into account in order to be as efficient as possible. For example, in order to divide the contribution of the error by 4, one will have to take elements 16 times smaller in the case of a crack ($\alpha =1/2$ ) and elements 2 times smaller in the case outside a crack with quadratic elements ($p=2$ ).

The “practical” difficulties come from the fact that, in singularity zones, the contributions to the error in energy are large. If one aims to obtain a small error in energy, these zones must therefore be refined very strongly. Now, one may wonder about the influence of these errors in energy on physical quantities of interest to the engineer (displacement at such a point, maximum stress in such a sensitive zone, etc.). In other words, it is not because the singularity zones cause large errors on the energy that they have a great influence on the analysis outside these zones. **In practice, the error estimators quickly designate the singularity zones alone as being those to be refined: the singularity zones mask the other errors, for example a zone with a strong gradient which one would wish to refine.**

The Laboratoire de Mécanique et CAO of Saint-Quentin has developed a method making it possible, on the one hand, to detect the singularities, and on the other hand, to determine, for a given global error, the size of the finite elements of the new mesh in the case of remeshing.

The use of these two pieces of information can be envisaged from two angles:

- The finite elements considered as “singular” by the method can be excluded from the cutting process,
- the new size of the finite elements is given to a remesher so that the latter builds the new mesh while respecting as well as possible this new size map. Currently, the software HOMARD cuts an element once (for example in 2D, a triangle is divided into 4 but no more). In order to continue the cutting, one must call HOMARD again. An evolution is therefore to be expected so that one can divide an element several times and thus respect as well as possible the size map of the new mesh. It is however possible to use the free mesher GMSH which takes a size map directly as input.

## 2 Detection of the singularities

### 2.1 Principle of the method

When the exact solution of the problem studied presents singularities, the order of convergence of the finite-element solution is modified and therefore also that of the error estimator. Consider, for example, a plane elasticity problem discretised with triangular elements of degree $p$ .

If the exact solution ${U}_{\mathrm{ex}}$ is regular, one knows that ([bib2], [bib3]):

${\parallel u-{u}_{h}\parallel }_{\Omega}={\parallel e\parallel }_{\Omega}\le C{h}^{p}$                                     eq 2.1-1

Where ${\parallel e\parallel }_{\Omega}\le C{h}^{p}$ is the contribution to the error in energy, that is:

${\parallel e\parallel }_{\Omega}\le \frac{1}{2}\underset{\Omega}{\int}\varepsilon ({e}_{h})K\varepsilon ({e}_{h})d\Omega$                                 eq 2.1-2

On the other hand, if the exact solution presents a singularity, for example if, locally in the neighbourhood of a point ${M}_{0}$ , the displacement field is of the form (with $r$ and $\theta$ polar coordinates in the neighbourhood of the point ${M}_{0}$ ):

${U}_{\mathrm{ex}}={r}^{\alpha}V(\theta )+W\text{avec}0<\alpha <1$                         eq 2.1-3

Then, one shows that [Strang & Fix, 1976]:

${\parallel {e}_{h}\parallel }_{\Omega}\le C{h}^{\alpha}$                                     eq 2.1-4

It follows that the rate of convergence of the global error in energy becomes independent of the degree $p$ of the finite elements used and the same is true of that of the error measure (for example, if $p=1\text{ou}2$ then $\alpha =1/2$ for a crack).

In order to obtain a good prediction of the optimised meshes, the previous observations lead us to use a convergence rate ${q}_{E}$ per element such that the error estimator ${\varepsilon}_{E}$ satisfies:

${\varepsilon}_{E}=O({h}^{{q}_{E}})$                                         eq 2.1-5

A simple way of defining these local coefficients consists in taking:

- ${q}_{E}=\alpha$ if the element $E$ is connected to a singularity of order $\alpha$ ;
- ${q}_{E}=q$ for all the other finite elements where $q$ depends only on the type of finite elements used.

The method presented in what follows therefore comprises three phases:

- detection of the singular zones, in this case the singular nodes of the mesh;
- numerical evaluation of the coefficient ${q}_{E}$ for the elements connected to the nodes considered as singular (for the other elements, one then sets ${q}_{E}=q$ );
- computation of the size-modification coefficient ${r}_{E}$ .

### 2.2 Detection of the singular nodes

The idea is to use the local errors. Indeed, experiments show that these local errors present a peak in the neighbourhood of a singularity. For each node $i$ of the mesh, one therefore compares the mean error ${\bar{m}}^{i}$ of the elements connected to the node $i$ with the mean error $\bar{M}$ over the whole structure. The node $i$ is considered as singular if:

${\bar{m}}^{i}\ge \beta \bar{M}$                                     eq 2.2.1-1

with

${\bar{m}}^{i}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{\text{E connecté à i}}{{\varepsilon}_{E}}^{2}}{\sum_{\text{E connecté à i}}\mathrm{mes}(E)}}$ and $\bar{M}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{\text{E}\in \text{structure}}{{\varepsilon}_{E}}^{2}}{\sum_{\text{E}\in \text{structure}}\mathrm{mes}(E)}}$                     eq 2.2.1-2

where $\beta$ is a coefficient greater than 1 and $\mathrm{mes}(E)$ the area in 2D or the volume in 3D of the element $E$ . The numerical experiments have shown that the singular nodes are well detected by setting $\beta =2$ in dimension 2, $\beta =3$ in dimension 3 for linear finite elements and $\beta =2$ in dimension 3 for quadratic finite elements.

$$
\begin{array}{l}
    {\bar{m}}_{1}^{i}\ge \beta \bar{M} \\
    {\bar{m}}_{1}^{i}\ge {\bar{m}}_{2}^{i} \\
    {\bar{m}}_{1}^{i}\ge 3\text{Min}({\bar{m}}_{2}^{i},{\bar{m}}_{3}^{i})
\end{array}
$$

- Layer 1: elements which possess the node i to be tested,
- Layer 2: elements in contact (face, edge or node) with an element of layer 1,
- Layer 3: elements in contact (face, edge or node) with an element of layer 2.

**Note 1:**

From a numerical point of view, the detection of the singular nodes differs between the 2D and 3D cases. The conditions given in what follows for this detection are not based on a particular theory but rather on the experience acquired in this field by the Laboratoire de Mécanique et CAO of Saint-Quentin.
In 2D: a node i is considered as singular if it fulfils the following 3 conditions:

$$
\begin{split}\begin{array}{l}
    {\bar{m}}_{1}^{i}\ge \beta \bar{M} \\
    {\bar{m}}_{1}^{i}\ge {\bar{m}}_{2}^{i} \\
    {\bar{m}}_{1}^{i}\ge 3\text{Min}({\bar{m}}_{2}^{i},{\bar{m}}_{3}^{i})
\end{array}\end{split}
$$

Where ${\bar{m}}_{1}^{i}$, ${\bar{m}}_{2}^{i}$ and ${\bar{m}}_{3}^{i}$ are the means of the error for the elements belonging to layers 1, 2 and 3, respectively, with respect to the node i considered.

The layers are defined as follows:

In 3D: the node $i$ is considered as singular if it fulfils the condition ${\bar{m}}_{1}^{i}\ge \beta \bar{M}$ and if one of the nodes connected to the node $i$ considered fulfils the condition ${\bar{m}}_{i}^{\text{Noeud connecté à i}}\ge \beta \bar{M}$.
Unlike the 2D case, the node $i$ is singular only if one of its neighbours is also (isolated singular nodes are forgotten in order to keep only the singular edges).

**Note 2:**

In 2D, only the vertex nodes are examined. In 3D, only the vertex nodes located on a boundary of the structure are examined (only for reasons of analysis time; this condition could therefore be modified).

### 2.3 Evaluation of the order of the singularity

For each singular node $i$ detected, the order of the singularity, that is to say the value of ${q}_{E}$ which will be used for the elements connected to the node $i$ , is determined by identifying the value of the energy density of the finite-element solution in the neighbourhood of the node $i$ with the theoretical value in the neighbourhood of a singular point.

#### 2.3.1 Case of dimension 2

In this case, one computes the mean finite-element energy, in discs $A$ of centre $i$ and of radius $r$ :

${\bar{e}}_{h}(r)=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{mes}(A)}\underset{A}{\int}\varepsilon ({u}_{h})K\varepsilon ({u}_{h})\mathrm{dA}$                         eq 2.3.1-1

By identifying, by a least-squares method, this mean energy with the theoretical value in the neighbourhood of a singularity of order $\alpha$ :

$e(r)=k{r}^{2(\alpha -1)}+c$                                 eq 2.3.1-2

one obtains numerically a value $\bar{\alpha}$ close to $\alpha$ .

![Figure](/figures/r4.10.04/10000DAC00002A2B0000129BAE28B131A3F3BF28.svg)

**Figure 2.3.1-a: Numerical evaluation of** $\alpha$

In practice the numerical experiments show that it is sufficient to carry out the identification in a zone corresponding to 3 layers of elements around the singular point and to evaluate ${\bar{e}}^{h}(r)$ for 5 to 8 values of $r$ regularly distributed in this zone (10 values have been taken).

#### 2.3.2 Case of dimension 3

In 3D the situation is more complex. The singular points are not, most often, isolated and it is therefore frequent to be in the presence of singular edges. Consider, for example, the case of a cube clamped on one face and subjected to tensile forces: all the points of the edges of the clamped face are singular [Figure 2.3.2-b].

![Figure](/figures/r4.10.04/1000134000000D0600000FB6DA0D585F12186E3E.svg)

**Figure 2.3.2-b: Cube clamped in tension**

In this situation, the evaluation of the mean energy in balls $A$ of increasing radius and centred on a singular node does not make it possible to identify ${q}_{E}$ . Indeed, as the radius increases the extent of the singular zone contained in the ball $A$ increases and one does not obtain a rapid decrease of ${\bar{e}}_{h}$ [Figure 2.3.2-c].

![Figure](/figures/r4.10.04/10000C7A00001F8600000C4DA92240A409B0F84A.svg)

**Figure 2.3.2-c: Energy in concentric balls**

When the singular points are not isolated, the coefficient ${q}_{E}$ must be identified by computing the energy density in coaxial cylinders built on the edges whose ends have been considered as singular [Figure 2.3.2-d] and cf. the note of [§ 2.2].

![Figure](/figures/r4.10.04/1000049600001639000012807AD13ABDA6E4D44E.svg)

### 2.4 Extension to stress-concentration zones

In practice, it has been observed that the previous method, developed on cases presenting singularities, also makes it possible to take correctly into account the zones with strong stress gradients even if mathematically these zones do not correspond to singularities.

## 3 Construction of an optimal mesh

### 3.1 Generalities

The objective of an adaptation procedure is to guarantee the user a level of precision on the global error while minimising the analysis costs. In order to evaluate the discretisation errors, a relative global measure of the error $\varepsilon$ and the associated local contributions ${\varepsilon}_{E}$ are used, with:

${\varepsilon}^{2}=\sum_{E}{{\varepsilon}_{E}}^{2}$                                         eq 3.1-1

The idea is to use the results of this first finite-element analysis and the error estimators in order to determine an optimal mesh ${T}^{\ast }$ , that is to say a mesh which makes it possible to respect the desired precision while minimising the analysis costs. The mesh ${T}^{\ast }$ is then built with the aid of an automatic mesher and a second finite-element analysis is carried out.

### 3.2 Definition of optimality

For a given global error ${\varepsilon}_{0}$ , a mesh ${T}^{\ast }$ is optimal with respect to an error measure $\varepsilon$ if:

$\begin{array}{cc}{\varepsilon}^{\ast }={\varepsilon}_{0}& \text{précision demandée}\\ {N}^{\ast }& \text{nombre d'éléments de}{T}^{\ast }\text{est minimum}\end{array}$                 eq 3.2-1

This optimisation criterion naturally leads to minimising the analysis costs.

### 3.3 Determination of an optimal mesh

In order to determine the characteristics of the optimal mesh ${T}^{\ast }$ , the method consists in computing on each element $E$ of the mesh $T$ a size-modification coefficient:

${r}_{E}=\frac{{h}_{E}^{\ast }}{{h}_{E}}$                                         eq 3.3-1

Where ${h}_{E}$ is the current size of the element $E$ and ${h}_{E}^{\ast }$ the (unknown) size which must be imposed on the elements of ${T}^{\ast }$ in the zone of $E$ in order to ensure optimality [Figure 3.3-a]. A possible choice for defining the size of an element ${h}_{E}$ is to take the size of the largest side of this element. The determination of the optimal mesh is thus reduced to the determination, on the initial mesh $T$ , of a map of size-modification coefficients.

${E}^{\text{*}}\subset {T}^{\text{*}}$

$E\subset T$

![Figure](/figures/r4.10.04/10000B3400001987000018E8342BCBC933D31FCE.svg)

**Figure 3.3-a: Definition of the sizes**

The computation of the coefficients ${r}_{E}$ is based on the rate of convergence of the error:

$\varepsilon =O({h}^{q})$                                         eq 3.3-2

where $q$ depends on the type of finite element used but also on the regularity of the exact solution of the problem treated. For the “classical” error estimators, it is assumed that the rate of convergence of the error estimator is equal to the order of convergence of the finite-element solution. For the estimators in a quantity of interest, this rate of convergence is equal to twice the order of convergence of the finite-element solution ([bib4]).

In what follows, in order to compute the size-modification coefficient ${r}_{E}$ , the case of the regular solution ($q$ depends only on $p$ , the degree of interpolation of the shape functions of the element) is distinguished from the case of the singular solution ($q$ depends only on $\alpha$ , the order of the singularity of the displacement field).

### 3.4 “Classical” error estimators

“Classical” error estimators denote the estimators which supply a norm (*L2* norm, *H1* norm, energy norm) of the error in the solution.

#### 3.4.1 Case of the regular solution

First, it is assumed that the exact solution is sufficiently regular for the value of $q$ to depend only on the type of finite elements used and to be equal to the degree of interpolation used $p$ ($p$ is equal to 1 for linear finite elements and 2 for quadratic finite elements). In this case, in order to predict the optimal sizes, one writes that the ratio of the sizes is linked to the ratio of the contributions to the error by:

$\frac{{\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }}{{\varepsilon}_{E}}=\left[\frac{{h}_{E}^{\ast }}{{h}_{E}}\right]={r}_{E}^{p}$                                     eq 3.4.1-1

where ${\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }$ represents the contribution of the elements of ${T}^{\ast }$ located in the zone $E$ , that is to say:

${\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }={\left[\sum_{{E}^{\ast }\subset E}{\epsilon}_{{E}^{\ast }}^{2}\right]}^{1/2}$                                     eq 3.4.1-2

${\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }$ is the error of the element ${E}^{\ast }$ computed on the mesh $T$ .

The square of the error on the mesh ${T}^{\ast }$ can therefore be evaluated by:

$\sum_{E}{({\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast })}^{2}=\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2p}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2}$                                     eq 3.4.1-3

and the number of elements of ${T}^{\ast }$ by:

${N}^{\ast }=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}$                                         eq 3.4.1-4

Where $d$ is the dimension of the space (in practice, $d=2$ or $3$ ).

Indeed, ${r}_{E}=\frac{{h}_{E}^{\ast }}{{h}_{E}}={(\frac{V}{{{N}_{E}}^{\ast }})}^{1/d}{(\frac{N}{V})}^{1/d}$ with $N$ the number of elements of $T$ in $E$ (hence 1), ${N}_{E}^{\ast }$ the number of elements of ${T}^{\ast }$ in the zone of $E$ . One therefore has ${N}_{E}^{\ast }=\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}$ , that is ${N}^{\ast }=\sum_{E}{N}_{E}^{\ast }=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}$ the total number of elements of ${T}^{\ast }$ .

The problem to be solved is therefore:

$\text{Minimiser}{N}^{\ast }=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}\text{avec}\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2p}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2}={\varepsilon}_{0}^{2}$                     eq 3.4.1-5

It is an optimisation problem with a constraint on the optimisation variables.

Introducing a Lagrange multiplier, denoted $A$ , the problem [eq 3.4.1-5] amounts to making extremal the Lagrangian:

$L({\left\lbrace {r}_{E}\right\rbrace }_{E\in T};A)=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}+A(\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2p}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2}-{\varepsilon}_{0}^{2})$                         eq 3.4.1-6

The extremality conditions give:

$\frac{\partial L}{\partial {r}_{E}}=\frac{-d}{{r}_{E}^{d+1}}+2Ap{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2}{r}_{E}^{2p-1}=0\forall E\in T$                         eq 3.4.1-7

Whence:

${r}_{E}={\left[\frac{d}{2Ap{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2}}\right]}^{1/(2p+d)}$                                 eq 3.4.1-8

Substituting into the second equation of [eq 3.4.1-5], one deduces $A$ :

$A=\frac{d}{2p}{\left[\frac{\sum_{E}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2d/(2p+d)}}{{\varepsilon}_{0}^{2}}\right]}^{(2p+d)/2p}$                             eq 3.4.1-9

The expression for $A$ thus obtained is substituted into equation [eq 3.4.1-8] in order to obtain ${r}_{E}$ :

${r}_{E}=\frac{{\varepsilon}_{0}^{1/p}}{{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2/(2p+d)}{\left[\sum_{E}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2d/(2p+d)}\right]}^{1/2p}}$                             eq 3.4.1-10

#### 3.4.2 Case of the singular zones

In order to predict the optimal sizes, a convergence rate ${q}_{E}$ defined per element is used:

$\frac{{\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }}{{\varepsilon}_{E}}={\left[\frac{{h}_{E}^{\ast }}{{h}_{E}}\right]}^{{q}_{E}}={r}_{E}^{{q}_{E}}$                                 eq 3.4.2-1

where ${\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }$ represents the contribution of the elements of ${T}^{\ast }$ located in the zone $E$ , that is to say:

${\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }={\left[\sum_{{E}^{\ast }\subset E}{\varepsilon}_{{E}^{\ast }}^{2}\right]}^{1/2}$                                 eq 3.4.2-2

The square of the error on the mesh ${T}^{\ast }$ can therefore be evaluated by:

$\sum_{E}{({\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast })}^{2}=\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2{q}_{E}}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2}$                                     eq 3.4.2-3

and the number of elements of ${T}^{\ast }$ is still evaluated by:

${N}^{\ast }=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}$                                         eq 3.4.2-4

The new problem to be solved is therefore:

$\text{Minimiser}N\ast =\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}\text{avec}\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2{q}_{E}}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2}={\varepsilon}_{0}^{2}$                     eq 3.4.2-5

which is an optimisation problem with a constraint on the optimisation variables.

Introducing a Lagrange multiplier, denoted $A$ , the problem amounts to making extremal the Lagrangian:

$L({\left\lbrace {r}_{E}\right\rbrace }_{E\in T};A)=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}+A(\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2{q}_{E}}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2}-{\varepsilon}_{0}^{2})$                     eq 3.4.2-6

The extremality conditions give:

$\frac{\partial L}{\partial {r}_{E}}=-\frac{d}{{r}_{E}^{d+1}}+2A{q}_{E}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2}{r}_{E}^{2{q}_{E}-1}=0\forall E\in T$                     eq 3.4.2-7

Whence:

${r}_{E}={\left[\frac{d}{2A{q}_{E}}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2}\right]}^{1/(2{q}_{E}+d)}$                             eq 3.4.2-8

Substituting into the second equation of [eq 3.4.2-5], one obtains a non-linear equation in $A$ (because ${q}_{E}$ depends on the elements):

$\sum_{E}\left[{\left[\frac{d}{2A{q}_{E}}\right]}^{2{q}_{E}/(2{q}_{E}+d)}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{2d/(2{q}_{E}+d)}\right]-{\varepsilon}_{0}^{2}=0$                     eq 3.4.2-9

It is solved by Newton's method (the Lagrange multiplier is initialised by taking the Lagrange multiplier of the regular solution, that is to say the expression [eq 3.4.2-9] with ${q}_{E}=p$ ). Once $A$ has been computed, one deduces ${r}_{E}$ from equation [eq 3.4.2-8].

### 3.5 Error estimators in quantities of interest

Error estimators in quantities of interest denote the estimators which supply an error on a precise physical quantity (quantity of interest) on a chosen zone.

#### 3.5.1 Case of the regular solution

In the case of the estimators in a quantity of interest, the value of $q$ is equal to $2p$ [bib4] ($p$ is equal to 1 for linear finite elements and 2 for quadratic finite elements). In order to predict the optimal sizes, one writes that the ratio of the sizes is linked to the ratio of the contributions to the error by:

$\frac{{\epsilon}_{E}^{\ast }}{{\epsilon}_{E}}={\left[\frac{{h}_{E}^{\ast }}{{h}_{E}}\right]}^{2p}={r}_{E}^{2p}$                                     eq 3.5.1-1

where ${\epsilon}_{E}^{\ast }$ represents the contribution of the elements of ${T}^{\ast }$ located in the zone $E$ , that is to say:

${\epsilon}_{E}^{\ast }=\sum_{{E}^{\ast }\subset E}{\epsilon}_{{E}^{\ast }}$                                     eq 3.5.1-2

${\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }$ is the error of the element ${E}^{\ast }$ computed on the mesh $T$ .

The error on the mesh ${T}^{\ast }$ can therefore be evaluated by:

$\sum_{E}{\varepsilon}_{E}\ast =\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2p}{\varepsilon}_{E}$                                     eq 3.5.1-3

and the number of elements of ${T}^{\ast }$ by:

${N}^{\ast }=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}$                                         eq 3.5.1-4

Where $d$ is the dimension of the space (in practice, $d=2$ or $3$ ).

The problem to be solved is therefore:

$\text{Minimiser}{N}^{\ast }=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}\text{avec}\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2p}{\varepsilon}_{E}={\varepsilon}_{0}$                     eq 3.5.1-5

Here again, it is an optimisation problem with a constraint on the optimisation variables.

Introducing a Lagrange multiplier, denoted $A$ , the problem [eq 3.5.1-5] amounts to making extremal the Lagrangian:

$L({\left\lbrace {r}_{E}\right\rbrace }_{E\in T}\text{;A})=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}+A(\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2p}{\varepsilon}_{E}-{\varepsilon}_{0})$                         eq 3.5.1-6

The extremality conditions give:

$\frac{\partial L}{\partial {r}_{E}}=-\frac{d}{{r}_{E}^{d+1}}+2Ap{\varepsilon}_{E}{r}_{E}^{2p-1}=0\forall E\in T$                         eq 3.5.1-7

Whence:

${r}_{E}={\left[\frac{d}{2Ap{\varepsilon}_{E}}\right]}^{1/(2p+d)}$                                 eq 3.5.1-8

Substituting into the second equation of [eq 3.5.1-5], one deduces $A$ :

$A=\frac{d}{2p}{\left[\frac{\sum_{E}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{d/(2p+d)}}{{\varepsilon}_{0}}\right]}^{(2p+d)/2p}$                             eq 3.5.1-9

The expression for $A$ thus obtained is substituted into equation [eq 3.5.1-8] in order to obtain ${r}_{E}$ :

${r}_{E}=\frac{{\varepsilon}_{0}^{1/2p}}{{\varepsilon}_{E}^{1/(2p+d)}{\left[\sum_{E}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{d/(2p+d)}\right]}^{1/2p}}$                             eq 3.5.1-10

#### 3.5.2 Case of the singular zones

In order to predict the optimal sizes, one now imposes:

$\frac{{\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }}{{\varepsilon}_{E}}={\left[\frac{{h}_{E}^{\ast }}{{h}_{E}}\right]}^{2{q}_{E}}={r}_{E}^{2{q}_{E}}$                                 eq 3.5.2-1

where ${\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }$ represents the contribution of the elements of ${T}^{\ast }$ located in the zone $E$ , that is to say:

${\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }=\sum_{{E}^{\ast }\subset E}{\varepsilon}_{{E}^{\ast }}$                                     eq 3.5.2-2

The square of the error on the mesh ${T}^{\ast }$ can therefore be evaluated by:

$\sum_{E}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{\ast }=\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2{q}_{E}}{\varepsilon}_{E}$                                     eq 3.5.2-3

and the number of elements of ${T}^{\ast }$ is still evaluated by:

${N}^{\ast }=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}$                                         eq 3.5.2-4

The new problem to be solved is therefore:

$\text{Minimiser}{N}^{\ast }=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}\text{avec}\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2{q}_{E}}{\varepsilon}_{E}={\varepsilon}_{0}$                     eq 3.5.2-5

which is an optimisation problem with a constraint on the optimisation variables.

Introducing a Lagrange multiplier, denoted $A$ , the problem amounts to making extremal the Lagrangian:

$L({\left\lbrace {r}_{E}\right\rbrace }_{E\in T};A)=\sum_{E}\frac{1}{{r}_{E}^{d}}+A(\sum_{E}{r}_{E}^{2{q}_{E}}{\varepsilon}_{E}-{\varepsilon}_{0})$                     eq 3.5.2-6

The extremality conditions give:

$\frac{\partial L}{\partial {r}_{E}}=-\frac{d}{{r}_{E}^{d+1}}+2A{q}_{E}{\varepsilon}_{E}{r}_{E}^{2{q}_{E}}-1=0\forall E\in T$                     eq 3.5.2-7

Whence:

${r}_{E}={\left[\frac{d}{2A{q}_{E}}{\varepsilon}_{E}\right]}^{1/(2{q}_{E}+d)}$                             eq 3.5.2-8

Substituting into the second equation of [eq 3.5.2-5], one obtains a non-linear equation in $A$ :

$\sum_{E}\left[{\left[\frac{d}{2A{q}_{E}}\right]}^{2{q}_{E}/(2{q}_{E}+d)}{\varepsilon}_{E}^{d/(2{q}_{E}+d)}\right]-{\varepsilon}_{0}=0$                     eq 3.5.2-9

It is solved by Newton's method (the Lagrange multiplier is initialised by taking the Lagrange multiplier of the regular solution, that is to say the expression [eq 3.5.2-9] with ${q}_{E}=p$ ). Once $A$ has been computed, one deduces ${r}_{E}$ from equation [eq 3.5.2-8].

## 4 Use in Code_Aster

### 4.1 The commands

The order of the singularity and the size-modification map are computed by the command CALC_ERREUR by activating the options 'SING_ELEM' (field constant per element) or 'SING_ELNO' (field at the nodes per element).

The option 'SING_ELEM' computes, on each element, two components:

- 'DEGRE': the order of the singularity, that is to say the value of the coefficient ${q}_{E}$ (which is equal to *p* if the element is not connected to a singular node and which is equal to $\alpha$ otherwise);
- 'RAPPORT': the ratio between the current size ${h}_{E}$ and the new size ${h}_{E}^{\ast }$ of the finite element (${h}_{E}/{h}_{E}^{\ast }=1/{r}_{E}$ );
- 'TAILLE': the new size ${h}_{E}^{\ast }$ of the finite element (${h}_{E}^{\ast }={r}_{E}{h}_{E}$ ).

The computation of this option requires, beforehand, the computation of an error estimator (it is the absolute component which is used and this is hard-coded in *Code_Aster*) and of the total strain energy. In the case where one of these options is not computed, an alarm message is issued and the option 'SING_ELEM' is not computed.

The user can enter the optional keyword 'TYPE_ESTI' by indicating one of the following options:

- 'ERME_ELEM' for the estimator based on the residuals;
- 'ERZ(1 ou 2)_ELEM_SIGM' for the estimator based on the smoothed stresses (Zhu–Zienkiewicz version 1 or 2);
- 'QIRE_ELEM' for the estimator in a quantity of interest based on the residuals;
- 'QIZ(1 ou 2)_ELEM_SIGM' for the estimator in a quantity of interest based on the smoothed stresses (Zhu–Zienkiewicz version 1 or 2).

If this keyword is not entered then the estimator based on the residuals 'ERME_ELEM' is chosen by default (alarm message issued). If the two Zhu–Zienkiewicz estimators are present, 'ERZ1_ELEM' is chosen.

For the total strain energy, one uses:

- With STAT_NON_LINE: 'ETOT_ELEM' which is the total strain energy on a finite element (valid for an elastic behaviour and for an elastoplastic behaviour 'VMIS_ISOT_XXX').
- With MECA_STATIQUE: 'EPOT_ELEM' which is the potential energy of elastic strain on a finite element and integrated from the displacements and the temperature (valid only for an elastic behaviour).

The user must also enter the keyword 'PREC_ERR' which makes it possible to compute the precision ${\varepsilon}_{0}$ of equation [eq 2.2-1] in the following way: ${\varepsilon}_{0}=\text{PREC\_ERR}\ast {\text{Erreur}}_{\text{totale}}$ . The value of 'PREC_ERR' lies strictly between 0 and 1 (a fatal message is issued if this condition is not satisfied).

For the option 'SING_ELNO', it is a copy of the values of 'SING_ELEM' to the nodes of the element. The prior computation of 'SING_ELEM' is therefore necessary. If 'SING_ELEM' is absent, an alarm message is issued and the option 'SING_ELNO' is not computed.

### 4.2 Scope of use

The scope of use is the same (but more reduced) as that of the error estimator chosen, namely:

- For the residual estimator: finite elements of continuous media in 2D (triangles and quadrilaterals) or 3D (tetrahedra only) for an elastoplastic behaviour,
- For the Zhu–Zienkiewicz estimator: finite elements of continuous media in 2D (triangles and quadrilaterals) for an elastic behaviour.

Strictly speaking (cf. [§2]), the computation of the order of the singularity is obtained from the theoretical energy at the crack tip [eq 2.3.1-2], an equation valid only in elasticity. The use of this option in elastoplasticity must therefore be handled with caution.
