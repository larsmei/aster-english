# r7.01.33 Elasto-plasticity–damage coupling

**Summary:**

This document presents a method for coupling an elasto-plastic model with a damage model, which is implemented within the framework of KIT_DDI. The method uses an approach of partitioning the model into three modules: elasto-plasticity, damage and coupler. It is particularly this modularity which represents the principal interest of the method. It makes it possible to use the same “coupler” module for couplings between different elasto-plasticity and damage models without intervening in the implementation of the latter. Exactly the same idea is used for the mechanical/creep couplings (see [R7.01.19](doc:r7.01.19)).

For the moment, the implementation makes it possible to couple only the model GLRC_DM for the damage with the models VMIS_(ISOT/CINE)_LINE for the elasto-plasticity, and this only for DKTG modellings.

## 1 Introduction

The document is organised in two main parts: the first presents the general method of the elasto-plasticity–damage couplings and the second describes the specific features of the various couplings, which are at present limited to the coupling between GLRC_DM and VMIS_(ISOT/CINE)_LINE for the “global” models.

In the first part one considers in particular the following aspects: the issues of this type of coupling, the thermodynamic framework and the numerical implementation. In the second part one recalls what the global models () are for reinforced-concrete plates and shells and how the general method is applied in that framework.

## 2 Elasto-plasticity–damage models in general

### 2.1 Issues and difficulties

In the modelling of the behaviour of solids, one observes that the most important non-linear phenomena to be taken into account, during a structural analysis under extreme loading, concern the presence and the evolution of the anelastic strain (plasticity) and the degradation of the stiffness (damage). The relative importance of these two phenomena can vary drastically from one material to another. Thus, most often one considers only plasticity when modelling metals while neglecting damage, whereas for a material of concrete type, damage is judged more important and plasticity is not taken into account. Nevertheless, often this simplification is not justifiable and one is led to model both phenomena, plasticity and damage, at the same time.

For some time already the thermodynamic framework for the modelling of the two phenomena has been well established (e.g. see [bib1], [bib2]) and there exists no obstacle to extending it to elasto-plastic–damageable couplings. As regards the numerical solution of these models within the framework of the finite-element method (FEM), there exist very robust algorithms, notably for plasticity (e.g. see [bib13]) and also for damage (e.g. see [bib14]), provided that the localisation phenomenon does not become predominant. On the other hand, the numerical solution of a coupled model is much less obvious.

The numerical difficulties concerning the solution algorithm of a coupled model intervene on two aspects essentially:

- satisfaction of the (Kuhn–Tucker) conditions of the plasticity and damage thresholds,
- construction of the tangent matrix.

The first difficulty is due to the fact that the plastic and damageable internal variables act very differently on the stress level, which makes the system of equations difficult to solve. The second point is a simple consequence of the increased complexity of the coupled model with respect to the single-phenomenon models.

Recently, one has proposed in [bib3] and [bib4] a solution algorithm for an elasto-plastic–damageable model based on the strict partitioning into a plastic part and a damageable part. It consists in introducing an additional iterative process in order to satisfy the plastic admissibility and the damageable admissibility simultaneously. This iterative process is driven by what one calls the damage strain, a variable which, unlike the plastic strain, is not a state variable, but rather represents a means of coupling between different models. Thus, in the model introduced in [bib3] the choice of the elasto-plastic model being free, one nevertheless had to modify the damage formulation, which in that case provided only for the modelling of non-softening behaviours. In [bib4] one demonstrated the interest of integrating this type of law within the framework of mixed finite elements.

In this document one generalises the approach presented in [bib3] in order to be able to couple practically any elasto-plastic model with any damage model. The interest of such a platform in fact goes beyond the elasto-plastic–damageable coupling.

### 2.2 Thermodynamic framework

In this part one recalls the usual approach for constructing damage and elasto-plasticity models, based on thermodynamics. The combination of the two types of model can be done in a fairly natural manner, provided that the coupling is made at the level of the computation of the stresses. The whole of the approach presented here is carried out under the assumption that the internal variables of damage and of plasticity are not coupled directly to one another. Moreover, one places oneself within the framework of small strains and without thermal phenomenon.

#### 2.2.1 Plasticity

The free energy $\psi$ may be written as the sum of the elastic energy and of the so-called stored energy, due to plasticity:

$$
\psi (\varepsilon ,{\varepsilon}^{p},\alpha )={\psi}^{e}(\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{p})+{\psi}^{p}(\alpha )
$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the total strain, ${\varepsilon}^{p}$ the plastic strain and $\alpha$ a vector of internal variables. The essential assumption that one makes in () is that the elastic energy depends only on the elastic strain, ${\psi}^{e}={\psi}^{e}({\varepsilon}^{e})$ , where ${\varepsilon}^{e}=\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{p}$ .

In the second step one defines the plastic dissipation rate and one ensures that it always remains positive,

$$
{D}^{p}=\sigma :\dot{\varepsilon}-\rho \dot{\psi}=(\sigma -\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{e}}{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}):{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{e}+\sigma :{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{p}-A\mathrm{\ast }\dot{\alpha}\ge 0
$$

where

$$
A=\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{p}}{\partial \alpha }
$$

are the thermodynamic forces associated with internal variables and the symbol $\ast$ refers to the appropriate product operator between $A$ and $\dot{\alpha}$ .

One assumes that the dissipation does not evolve, i.e. $\dot{D}=0$ , when the plastic variables do not evolve, i.e. ${\dot{\epsilon}}^{p}=\dot{\alpha}=0$ , which allows us to define the stress as:

$$
\sigma =\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{e}}{\partial {\epsilon}^{e}}
$$

In this document, one contents oneself with materials which have a linear and isotropic behaviour. In this case, the elastic contribution of the free energy is given by:

$$
\rho \psi ({\epsilon}^{e})=\frac{1}{2}{\epsilon}^{e}:{D}^{e}:{\epsilon}^{e}
$$

where ${D}^{e}$ is the standard isotropic elasticity tensor. From the two preceding equations, one deduces the expression of the stress tensor:

$$
\sigma ={D}^{e}:{\epsilon}^{e}
$$

Another essential ingredient of an elasto-plastic model is the definition of the yield-threshold function, which depends on the stress tensor and on the thermodynamic forces associated with the internal variables. This function is negative when the strains are purely elastic and reaches zero when plastic flow is imminent:

$$
{\Phi}^{p}={\Phi}^{p}\left(\sigma ,A\right)\le 0
$$

The complete characterisation of the general plasticity model requires the definition of the evolution laws of the internal variables, i.e. the variables associated with the dissipation phenomena. In our case, the internal variables are the plastic strain tensor and the set of internal variables $\alpha$ . To establish the evolution laws of these internal variables one often uses the principle of maximum plastic dissipation. However this is not essential in our approach. One will make use of a more general writing:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{\dot{\epsilon}}^{p}={\dot{\gamma}}^{p}N={\dot{\gamma}}^{p}\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial \sigma }\\ \dot{\alpha}={\dot{\gamma}}^{p}H=-{\dot{\gamma}}^{p}\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial A}\end{array}
$$

$\dot{\gamma}$ is the plastic multiplier, $N$ is called the flow vector and $H$ is the hardening modulus. Moreover every solution must satisfy the *plastic admissibility* conditions, which are:

$$
{\Phi}^{p}\le 0,\dot{\gamma}\ge 0,{\Phi}^{p}\dot{\gamma}=0
$$

#### 2.2.2 Damage

For the damage models it is more difficult to have a construction as general as that of plasticity. Consequently one will treat a simple case but one that is fairly representative of the desired characteristics of the model. Thus, one defines the free energy as:

$$
\psi \left(\epsilon ,d\right)={\psi}^{e}\left(\epsilon \right)\zeta \left(d\right)
$$

where $\zeta$ is the damage function and $d$ the damage variable, which is a scalar but which may equally well be a tensor. Likewise, the function $\zeta$ may in general depend on $\epsilon$ , notably on the sign of its trace in order to distinguish behaviours that are typically very different in tension and in compression.

By using (), one defines the damage dissipation as,

$$
{D}^{d}=\sigma :\dot{\epsilon}-\rho \dot{\psi}=\left(\sigma -\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial \epsilon }\right):\dot{\epsilon}-\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial d}\dot{d}\ge 0
$$

The dissipation does not evolve, i.e. ${\dot{D}}^{d}=0$ , when the damage variable does not evolve, i.e. $\dot{d}=0$ , which allows us to define the stress as:

$$
\sigma =\rho \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial \epsilon }=\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{e}}{\partial \epsilon }\zeta \left(d\right)={D}^{e}\left(d\right):{\epsilon}^{e}
$$

where ${D}^{e}(d)$ represents the standard isotropic elasticity tensor evolving as a function of the damage. By making the assumption of an isotropic damage, taking $\zeta =1-d$ the stress becomes:

$$
\sigma =(1-d){D}^{e}:{\varepsilon}^{e}
$$

As for plasticity one introduces a damage threshold function:

$$
{\Phi}^{d}={\Phi}^{d}(\varepsilon ,Y)\le 0
$$

where $Y$ is the variable dual to $d$ . This time, the threshold is a direct function of the total strain, $\varepsilon$ , and one seeks to satisfy the damage admissibility conditions:

$$
{\Phi}^{d}\le 0,\dot{d}\ge 0,{\Phi}^{d}\dot{d}=0
$$

One must also formulate the evolution law of $d$ :

$$
\dot{d}=\dot{d}\left(Y\right)
$$

#### 2.2.3 Coupling

Finally, one combines the formulations of damage and of plasticity in order to arrive at a coupled model. The free energy of the coupled model is therefore written as:

$$
\psi (\varepsilon ,{\varepsilon}^{p},\alpha ,d)={\psi}^{e}(\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{p})\zeta (d)+{\psi}^{p}(\alpha )
$$

The dissipation is written:

$$
D=\sigma :\dot{\varepsilon}-\rho \dot{\psi}=(\sigma -\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{e}}{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}):{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{e}+\sigma :{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{p}-A\mathrm{\ast }\dot{\alpha}-\frac{\partial {\psi}^{d}}{\partial d}\dot{d}\ge 0
$$

The stress tensor is:

$$
\sigma =\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{d}}{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}=\rho \frac{\partial {\psi}^{e}}{\partial {\varepsilon}^{e}}\zeta (d)={D}^{e}(d):(\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{p})
$$

If the elasticity is linear isotropic and the damage is isotropic:

$$
\sigma =(1-d){D}^{e}:(\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{p})
$$

One then has two threshold functions: one for the plastic flow and the other for the damage:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{\Phi}^{p}={\Phi}^{p}(\sigma ,A)\le 0\\ {\Phi}^{d}={\Phi}^{d}({\varepsilon}^{e},Y)\le 0\end{array}
$$

The evolution laws of the damage variables are given by:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{p}={\dot{\gamma}}^{p}N={\dot{\gamma}}^{p}\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial \sigma }\\ \dot{\alpha}={\dot{\gamma}}^{p}H=-{\dot{\gamma}}^{p}\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial A}\\ \dot{d}=\dot{d}(Y)\end{array}
$$

The admissibility conditions are:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{\Phi}^{p}\le 0,\dot{\gamma}\ge 0,{\Phi}^{p}\dot{\gamma}=0\\ {\Phi}^{d}\le 0,\dot{d}\ge 0,{\Phi}^{d}\dot{d}=0\end{array}
$$

### 2.3 Numerical integration

#### 2.3.1 Reminder of the classical solution of non-linear problems by finite elements

In the method proposed one adds an additional iterative process with respect to a classical computation by the FEM, which one recalls in this section. In a global solution of a structural problem one seeks the equilibrium state, defined by:

$$
{f}^{int}(u(t))={f}^{\mathit{ext}}(t)
$$

where ${f}^{int}$ is the vector of the internal forces and ${f}^{\mathit{ext}}$ the vector of the external forces (the loading). The first is a function of the displacement vector, the principal variable of the system, and the second a function of the parameter *t* (pseudo-time), defining the notion of trajectory both for the loading and for the evolution of the displacement field. Within the framework of the FEM the internal force is determined by:

$$
{f}^{int}(u)={\int}_{\Omega}{B}^{T}\sigma (\varepsilon )d\Omega
$$

where $\Omega$ defines the domain considered, $\varepsilon$ the strain tensor, $\sigma$ the stress tensor and $B$ is the classical matrix corresponding to the discretised operator of the symmetric gradient which makes it possible to pass from the strains to the displacements.

As we have considered the small-strain assumption, the tensor $\varepsilon$ is defined as:

$$
{\epsilon}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial {u}_{i}}{\partial {x}_{j}}+\frac{\partial {u}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{i}})
$$

By using the definition of $B$ , one has:

$$
{\varepsilon}_{ij}={B}_{\mathit{ijk}}{u}_{k}
$$

after having discretised the displacement field by the FEM. When the behaviour of the material is non-linear, one most often has recourse to Newton's method to solve (). To do this, one is led to linearise (), which gives us:

$$
{f}^{int,(k+1)}={f}^{int,(k)}+\frac{\partial {f}^{int,(k)}}{\partial {u}^{(k)}}\Delta {u}^{(k)}
$$

where the index $k$ represents the number of the current iteration. At convergence one must have:

$$
{f}^{int,(k)}\stackrel{k\to \infty }{\to }{f}^{\mathit{ext}}(t)
$$

In order to be able to compute the displacement correction from one iteration to the next, $\Delta {u}^{(k)}$ , one must determine:

$$
K=\frac{\partial {f}^{int,(k)}}{\partial {u}^{(k)}}=\underset{\Omega}{\int}{B}^{T}{D}^{\mathit{epd}}Bd\Omega
$$

where ${D}^{\mathit{epd}}$ is the consistent tangent modulus for the constitutive law used:

$$
{D}^{\mathit{epd}}=\frac{\partial \sigma (\varepsilon )}{\partial \varepsilon }
$$

The method detailed below is positioned at the level of a material point (or even at the Gauss point) and intervenes at the level of the computation of $\sigma$ and ${D}^{\mathit{epd}}$ from the strain $\epsilon$ , given for each call of the global process of the Newton iterations.

#### 2.3.2 Approach to the coupled problem

In this document one does not detail the numerical integration in time for each of the models. One simply assumes that one knows how to do it, and this with an implicit (backward) Euler scheme. Let us say that, in an incremental solution by the finite-element method, for each Newton iteration of the global iterative process, one wishes to compute, for each Gauss point, the stresses ${\sigma}_{n+1}$ and the internal variables ${\epsilon}_{n+1}^{p}$ , ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ and ${d}_{n+1}$ from the values of the total strain, ${\epsilon}_{n+1}$ , while assuming that one has converged at the previous instant and that one knows ${\sigma}_{n}$ , ${\epsilon}_{n}^{p}$ , ${\alpha}_{n}$ and ${d}_{n}$ . The indices $n$ or $n+1$ indicate that the value of the variable corresponds to the instant ${t}_{n}$ or ${t}_{n+1}$ , respectively.

Thus one assumes that the two models can be integrated in uncoupled mode and that one is therefore capable of writing:

$$
{\sigma}_{n+1}^{p}={\sigma}^{p}\left({\tilde{\epsilon}}_{n+1}\right)
$$

and

$$
{\sigma}_{n+1}^{d}={\sigma}^{d}\left({\tilde{\epsilon}}_{n+1}\right)
$$

The exponents $d$ or $p$ in ${\sigma}^{d}$ and ${\sigma}^{p}$ mean that the stress is computed with the damage model and with the plasticity model, respectively. $\tilde{\epsilon}$ is a variable of strain type.

In the algorithm presented in what follows one introduces an additional iterative process so that the stresses ${\sigma}^{d}$ and ${\sigma}^{p}$ converge towards a single value. It is similar to that proposed in [bib3] except that here the construction of the damage model is more general, which requires considering a variable other than the damage strain (used in [bib3]) on which one iterates.

#### 2.3.3 Construction of the non-linear system

With respect to a classical solution of the constitutive law, one must here make two stress values converge by introducing another unknown of strain type. This partitioned system is constructed more easily by introducing the following decomposition of the total strain,

$$
\epsilon ={\epsilon}^{e}+{\epsilon}^{p}+{\epsilon}^{d}
$$

where one has introduced the damage strain, which allows us to write the elasticity relation as:

$$
\sigma ={D}^{e}:\left(\epsilon -{\epsilon}^{p}-{\epsilon}^{d}\right)
$$

or again:

$$
{\epsilon}^{d}\left(\sigma ,\epsilon ,{\epsilon}^{p}\right)=\epsilon -{\epsilon}^{p}-{({D}^{e})}^{-1}:\sigma
$$

One can summarise the equations to be solved in three groups:

**Plasticity module**

$$
{\sigma}_{n+1}^{p}={\sigma}^{p}{\left({\epsilon}_{n+1}-{\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d}\right)}_{e}
{\epsilon}_{n+1}^{p}={\epsilon}^{p}\left({\epsilon}_{n+1}-{\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d}\right)
$$

The index $e$ means that the elastoplastic law is computed with the elasticity operator ${D}^{e}$

**Damage module**

$$
{\sigma}_{n+1}^{d}={\sigma}^{d}{\left({\epsilon}_{n+1}-{\epsilon}_{n+1}^{p}\right)}_{e}
$$

**Coupling module**

$$
{\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d}={\epsilon}_{n+1}-{\epsilon}_{n+1}^{p}-{({D}^{e})}^{-1}:{\sigma}_{n+1}^{d}
{\sigma}_{n+1}^{p}={\sigma}_{n+1}^{d}
$$

In [bib3] one chose ${\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d}$ as the additional unknown, which is not possible in our case, since ${\sigma}^{d}$ is not a function of ${\epsilon}^{d}$ but of ${\bar{\epsilon}}^{\mathit{ed}}=\epsilon -{\epsilon}^{p}$ , the variable which is finally used to drive the iterative processes described below. Moreover it is also equal to ${\bar{\epsilon}}^{\mathit{ed}}={\epsilon}^{e}+{\epsilon}^{d}=\epsilon -{\epsilon}^{p}$ .

#### 2.3.4 Solution algorithm

To solve the non-linear system, defined in equations ( to ) by assigning them to the different modules (plasticity, damage and coupling), one applies the classical Newton method. In order to linearise the system, one must define the following derivatives:

$$
{D}^{\mathit{ep}}\left({\bar{\epsilon}}^{\mathit{ed}}\right)=\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{n+1}^{p}}{\partial {\bar{\epsilon}}^{\mathit{ed}}}
{D}^{d}\left({\bar{\epsilon}}^{\mathit{ed}}\right)=\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{n+1}^{d}}{\partial {\bar{\epsilon}}^{\mathit{ed}}}
\frac{\partial {\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d}}{\partial {\bar{\epsilon}}^{\mathit{ed}}}=1-{\left({D}^{e}\right)}^{-1}{D}^{d}\left({\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed}}\right)
$$

The non-linear system (to ) is first reduced to the last of the equations, that is to say to ${\sigma}_{n+1}^{p}={\sigma}_{n+1}^{d}$ , since one can express all the unknowns with respect to the value of ${\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed}}$ . One can therefore define the residual as:

$$
{R}_{\sigma}^{(k)}={\sigma}^{p}\left({\epsilon}_{n+1}-{\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d,(k)}\right)-{\sigma}^{d}\left({\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k)}\right)
$$

where the exponent $k$ indicates the current iteration and where:

$$
{\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d,(k)}={\epsilon}_{n+1}-{\epsilon}_{n+1}^{p,(k)}-{\left({D}^{e}\right)}^{-1}:{\sigma}^{d}\left({\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k)}\right)
$$

By applying Newton's method, one computes the new approximation of ${\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k+1)}$ as:

$$
{\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k+1)}={\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k)}+\Delta {\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k)}
$$

where

$$
\Delta {\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k)}=-{\left(\frac{\partial {R}_{\sigma}^{(k)}}{\partial {\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed}}}\right)}^{-1}{R}_{\sigma}^{(k)}
$$

The corresponding tangent modulus can be expressed entirely by the uncoupled moduli ():

$$
\left(\frac{\partial {R}_{\sigma}^{(k)}}{\partial {\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed}}}\right)=-{D}^{\mathit{ep},(k)}\frac{\partial {\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d,(k)}}{\partial {\bar{\epsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k)}}-{D}^{d,(k)}=-\left({D}^{d,(k)}+{D}^{\mathit{ep},(k)}\left(1-{\left({D}^{e}\right)}^{-1}{D}^{d,(k)}\right)\right)
$$

One recalls here that the total strain ${\epsilon}_{n+1}$ *,* coming from the global process of the Newton iterations, is constant for the local iterative process determining ${\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed}}$ . The algorithm for the computation of the variables at iteration $k+1$ is carried out in the following manner:

1. Initialisation: ${\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k=0)}={\varepsilon}_{n+1}-{\varepsilon}_{n}^{p}$

1. Loop over the $k=0,\mathrm{...},{N}_{max}$
2. Computation of the increment:

${\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k+1)}={\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k)}+\Delta {\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k)}$ where $\Delta {\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k)}={({D}^{d,(k)}+{D}^{\mathit{ep},(k)}(1-{({D}^{e})}^{-1}{D}^{d,(k)}))}^{-1}{R}_{\sigma}^{(k)}$

1. ${\sigma}_{n+1}^{d,(k+1)}={\sigma}^{d}{({\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k+1)})}_{e}$
2. ${\varepsilon}_{n+1}^{d,(k+1)}={\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k+1)}-{\varepsilon}_{n+1}^{\mathit{el},(k+1)}$ where ${\varepsilon}_{n+1}^{\mathit{el},(k+1)}={({D}^{e})}^{-1}:{\sigma}^{d}({\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k+1)})$
3. ${\sigma}_{n+1}^{p,(k+1)}={\sigma}^{p}{({\varepsilon}_{n+1}-{\varepsilon}_{n+1}^{d,(k+1)})}_{e}$
4. ${R}_{\sigma}^{(k+1)}={\sigma}_{n+1}^{p,(k+1)}-{\sigma}_{n+1}^{d,(k+1)}$
5. If $\mid \Delta {\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k)}{R}_{\sigma}^{(k+1)}\mid >\mathit{tol}$ resume the loop at 1), otherwise END

*Note 1: the only places where the physical specificities of the coupled models are taken into account are points 3) and 5), where one calls the plasticity and damage modules, respectively.*

*Note 2: the value of* ${\varepsilon}_{n+1}$ *is considered constant in the process of equilibrating the stresses and, when* ${\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed}}$ *varies, one in fact varies the value of* ${\varepsilon}_{n+1}^{p}$ *. This plastic strain is in principle determined by the elasto-plasticity module as a function of* ${\varepsilon}_{n+1}$ *(see ()). In reality,* ${\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed}}$ *is considered here as a variable and its link with* ${\varepsilon}_{n+1}^{p}$ *is restored only at the convergence of the iterative process. Thus:*

$$
{\bar{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ed},(k)}\mathrm{\ne }{\varepsilon}_{n+1}-{\varepsilon}^{p}({\varepsilon}_{n+1}-{\varepsilon}_{n+1}^{d,(k)})
$$

#### 2.3.5 Construction of the consistent tangent modulus

Finally, in order to obtain an optimal performance in the global iterative process one must compute the consistent tangent modulus, once the process described above has converged, i.e. ${\sigma}_{n+1}={\sigma}_{n+1}^{p}={\sigma}_{n+1}^{d}$ :

$$
{D}_{n+1}^{\mathit{epd}}=\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{n+1}}{\partial {\varepsilon}_{n+1}}
$$

First one observes that if one differentiates () with respect to ${\sigma}_{n+1}^{p}$ one can write:

$$
I={D}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ep}}\left({D}_{n+1}^{\mathit{epd}}-\frac{\partial {\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d}}{\partial {\sigma}_{n+1}^{p}}\right)
$$

and that in the same manner, by differentiating () with respect to ${\sigma}_{n+1}^{d}$ one obtains:

$$
I={D}_{n+1}^{d}\left({D}_{n+1}^{\mathit{epd}}-\frac{\partial {\epsilon}_{n+1}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{n+1}^{d}}\right)
$$

with, at the end of the iterative process ${\sigma}_{n+1}={\sigma}_{n+1}^{p}={\sigma}_{n+1}^{d}$ , one finally obtains:

$$
\frac{\partial {\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d}}{\partial {\sigma}_{n+1}}={({D}_{n+1}^{\mathit{epd}})}^{-1}-{({D}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ep}})}^{-1}
$$

By then using ${\epsilon}_{n+1}={\epsilon}_{n+1}^{e}+{\epsilon}_{n+1}^{p}+{\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d}$ and therefore that:

$$
\frac{\partial {\epsilon}_{n+1}}{\partial {\sigma}_{n+1}}=\frac{\partial {\epsilon}_{n+1}^{e}}{\partial {\sigma}_{n+1}}+\frac{\partial {\epsilon}_{n+1}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{n+1}}+\frac{\partial {\epsilon}_{n+1}^{d}}{\partial {\sigma}_{n+1}}
$$

one obtains the consistent tangent modulus directly as:

$$
{D}_{n+1}^{\mathit{epd}}={\left({\left({D}_{n+1}^{\mathit{epd}}\right)}^{-1}+{\left({D}_{n+1}^{\mathit{epd}}\right)}^{-1}+{\left({D}_{n+1}^{\mathit{epd}}\right)}^{-1}\right)}^{-1}
$$

### 2.4 Limitations of the coupler

There exist a few conditions to be satisfied for the use of this coupling method concerning aspects that are both physical and numerical:

- **Distinct internal variables:**

As specified in the introduction of the thermodynamic framework, the two coupled models must not share the same internal variables. The method is designed to be able to combine models representing uncoupled physical phenomena, which is judged to be the case in most of the target applications.

- **Existence of the consistent tangent moduli:**

For the approach to work, it is absolutely necessary that one have the consistent tangent moduli for the two modules, elasto-plastic and damageable. By “consistent tangent modulus” one means that it is the derivative, consistent with the time discretisation employed, of the stress tensor at the given instant with respect to the strain tensor at the same instant.

- **Non-singularity of the tangent moduli:**

One is obliged on several occasions to invert the tangent matrices of the individual modules. If the behaviour of a module has a flat non-linear slope (perfect elasto-plasticity for example) it is necessary to give it a non-zero value. This non-zero value must be chosen such that it is large enough for the algorithm to work and small enough for it to be consistent with the physical model studied.

## 3 Specific models

### 3.1 Reminder of the global models

In this context, a plate constitutive model said to be global, or of a structural element in general, means that the constitutive law is written directly in terms of a relation between the generalised stresses and the generalised strains. The global approach to the modelling of the behaviour of structures applies in particular to composite structures, for example reinforced concrete (see Figure 1), and represents an alternative to the so-called local or semi-global approaches, which are finer but more costly modellings (see [bib6] and [bib7]). In the local approach one uses a fine modelling for each of the phases (steel, concrete) and their interactions (bond) and in the semi-global approach one exploits the slenderness of the structure in order to simplify the description of the kinematics, which leads to the PMF (Multi-Fibre Beam) models or to multi-layer shells.

The interest of the global model lies in the fact that the corresponding finite element requires only one integration point through the thickness and above all in the obtaining of a homogenised behaviour. This advantage is even more important in the analysis of reinforced concrete, since one bypasses the localisation problems encountered when modelling concrete without reinforcement. Obviously, a global model represents the local phenomena in a coarse manner.

### 3.2 Implementation of the coupling GLRC_DM/

The most important assumption which is made in this particular coupling is that elasto-plasticity can be activated only by loading the plate in membrane. In other words, in pure bending the structure will never plastify. On the one hand, this simplification is justified by the target applications, in which the loadings are more important in membrane than in bending and in which one therefore does not expect dominant influences of bending. On the other hand, the assumption is imposed for technical reasons, because at present one does not have a “global” elasto-plastic model suitable for the coupling with a “global” damage model.

The global models are formulated in terms of generalised stresses and strains, which imposes on us, in the coupler (see §2), to replace the stresses, $\sigma$ , and the strains, $\epsilon$ , by:

$$
\sigma \to \Sigma =\left(\begin{array}{c}{N}_{xx}\\ {N}_{yy}\\ {N}_{xy}\\ {M}_{xx}\\ {M}_{yy}\\ {M}_{xy}\end{array}\right)
$$

where the generalised strains, ${\rm E}$ , are decomposed into membrane extensions $ϵ$ and the curvatures $\kappa$ while the generalised stresses are composed of the membrane forces $N$ and of the bending moments $M$ .

It is important to note that in this framework one makes the thin-plate assumption in which the transverse distortion and the shear force are negligible. Below one recalls the Hencky–Mindlin kinematics (see [bib9] for the details) for shells and plates as well as the definition of the generalised stresses:

$$
(\begin{array}{}{U}_{1}({x}_{1,}{x}_{2,}z)\\ {U}_{2}({x}_{1,}{x}_{2,}z)\\ {U}_{z}({x}_{1,}{x}_{2,}z)\end{array})=\underset{\begin{array}{}\mathrm{cinématique}\mathrm{de}\mathrm{plaque}\\ {u}^{s}\in {V}_{s}\end{array}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{(\begin{array}{}{u}_{1}({x}_{1,}{x}_{2})\\ {u}_{2}({x}_{1,}{x}_{2})\\ {u}_{z}({x}_{1,}{x}_{2})\end{array})+z(\begin{array}{}{\theta}_{2}({x}_{1,}{x}_{2})\\ -{\theta}_{1}({x}_{1,}{x}_{2})\\ 0\end{array})}}+\underset{\begin{array}{}\mathrm{déplacement}\mathrm{complémentaire}\\ {u}^{c}\in {V}_{c}\end{array}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{(\begin{array}{}{u}_{1}^{c}({x}_{1,}{x}_{2,}z)\\ {u}_{2}^{c}({x}_{1,}{x}_{2,}z)\\ {u}_{z}^{c}({x}_{1,}{x}_{2,}z)\end{array})}}
$$

where $U={({U}_{1}{U}_{2}{U}_{z})}^{T}$ is the displacement field in 3D, $u={({u}_{1}{u}_{2}{u}_{z})}^{T}$ the displacement of the middle surface and ${\theta}_{x}$ , ${\theta}_{y}$ its rotations. Thus, the strain tensor, defined as:

$$
{\varepsilon}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial {U}_{i}}{\partial {x}_{j}}+\frac{\partial {U}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{i}}),i,j=\mathrm{1..3}
$$

is also written as:

$$
\begin{array}{}{\varepsilon}_{11}=\underset{{\varepsilon}_{11}^{s}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\epsilon}_{11}+z{\kappa}_{11}}}+{u}_{1,1}^{c}\\ {\varepsilon}_{22}=\underset{{\varepsilon}_{22}^{s}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\epsilon}_{22}+z{\kappa}_{22}}}+{u}_{2,2}^{c}\\ {\varepsilon}_{12}=\underset{{\varepsilon}_{12}^{s}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\epsilon}_{12}+z{\kappa}_{12}}}+\frac{1}{2}({u}_{2,1}^{c}+{u}_{1,2}^{c})\\ {\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{1z}}={\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{1z}}^{c}=\frac{1}{2}{u}_{3,1}^{c}\\ {\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{2z}}={\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{2z}}^{c}=\frac{1}{2}{u}_{3,2}^{c}\\ {\varepsilon}_{zz}={\varepsilon}_{zz}^{c}={u}_{3,z}^{c}\end{array}
$$

where $\epsilon$ is the membrane-extension tensor, defined in the plane:

$$
{\epsilon}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial {u}_{i}}{\partial {x}_{j}}+\frac{\partial {u}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{i}}),i,j=\mathrm{1..2}
$$

and $\kappa$ the curvature-change tensor, defined in the plane:

$$
{\kappa}_{11}=\frac{\partial {\theta}_{2}}{\partial {x}_{1}}
$$

relations to which is added the plane-stress assumption ${\sigma}_{zz}=0$ , ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{1z}}=0$ , ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{2z}}=0$ which will determine the complementary displacement field ${\mathrm{u}}^{c}\in {V}_{c}$ . In the theory used here, one introduces only two rotation components ${\theta}_{x}$ and ${\theta}_{y}$ , which implies that the curvature-change tensor is 2D and has only 3 independent components.

Among the two coupled models, GLRC_DM is a global model and is therefore formulated directly in terms of $\Sigma =\Sigma (E)$ , but the models VMIS_ISOT_LINE and VMIS_CINE_LINE are the classical 3D constitutive laws. Since one wishes above all that the elasto-plastic part be represented in membrane, one applies the constitutive law only to the relation $N=N(\epsilon )$ , then one assumes that the bending behaviour remains linear elastic. One therefore has:

**Damage**:

$$
{\Sigma}^{d}(\epsilon ,\kappa )={\Sigma}_{\text{GLRC\_DM}}(\epsilon ,\kappa )
$$

**Elasto-plasticity**:

$$
{\Sigma}^{p}(\epsilon ,\kappa )=(\begin{array}{c}{N}_{\mathit{VMIS}}(\epsilon )\\ {H}_{\mathit{ELAS}}:\kappa \end{array})
$$

where ${\Sigma}_{\text{GLRC\_DM}}(\epsilon ,\kappa )$ represents the global constitutive law GLRC_DM, ${N}_{\mathit{VMIS}}(\epsilon )$ the von Mises law in plane stress and ${H}_{\mathit{ELAS}}$ the elastic tensor acting on the curvature (see [bib9] for its exact form). These two modules, of damage and of elasto-plasticity, are then used in the coupler (see §2) by replacing ${\sigma}^{p}\to {\Sigma}^{p}$ and ${\sigma}^{d}\to {\Sigma}^{d}$ .

### 3.3 Internal variables

The internal variables of the coupled model are stored in series: the first correspond to the damage model, which are followed by those of the elasto-plastic model. The coupler needs six (6) internal variables in order to store the tensor of strain type, ${\bar{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{ed}}$ , which drives the internal loop (see §2.3). In the case of the coupling GLRC_DM/ one adds four (4) of them because of the algorithm making it possible to satisfy the plane-stress condition. These four internal variables have the same meaning as those used for the DEBORST method (see [bib12]).

For GLRC_DM/ one therefore has 24 internal variables: V1–V7: GLRC_DM; V8–V14:  (one uses only V5 and V6 for VMIS_ISOT_LINE); V15–V20: strain tensor ${\bar{\epsilon}}^{\text{ed}}$ ; V21–V24: DEBORST method.

### 3.4 Validation

This model is validated by the tests SSNS106F,G (see [bib8]).
