# r7.01.40 Constitutive model LKR

**Summary:**

The constitutive model LKR is a thermomechanical extension of the elasto(visco)plastic model LETK [1] and [2]. Its development and its numerical integration are the outcome of the thesis work of S. Raude [3].

This document presents the main conceptual outlines of the model and the principal equations that govern its operation. The first paragraph introduces the main concepts of the model, essential to the understanding of the second paragraph, in which its principal equations taken from [3] are presented. The last paragraph returns briefly to the main outlines of the numerical integration of the model.

## 1 Physical framework and concepts of the LETK model

The aim of this paragraph is not to describe exhaustively the approach undertaken by [1] and [2] in order to arrive at the development of the LETK model. The purpose is to return to certain points essential to the understanding of paragraphs § 2 and § 3. The reader is invited to refer to [1] and [2] for more details. The concepts presented in this paragraph were retained by [3] in the development of the LKR model.

The model presented by [2] is an elasto(visco)plastic model with two mechanisms. The first mechanism, elastoplastic, is concerned with describing the instantaneous deviatoric behaviour of geomaterials. The second mechanism, viscoplastic, accounts for the effect of time on the deviatoric behaviour.

The instantaneous deviatoric behaviour is characterised by uniaxial/triaxial compression tests and extension tests (among others). The conceptual framework retained for the development of the elastoplastic mechanism is based mainly on the analysis of the behaviour in triaxial compression. The response of geomaterials to this type of loading is divided into two phases (see Figure ). For relatively small strains, the strength of a specimen increases with its axial strain. This phase, termed *pre-peak*, ends when the material reaches its maximum strength. Beyond this peak, the strength decreases until a residual value is reached. The evolution of a material between its peak and residual strengths is termed *post-peak*. The *pre-peak* and *post-peak* behaviours are detailed in paragraph § 1.1.

The effect of time on the deviatoric behaviour is characterised by creep tests, relaxation tests or triaxial compression tests at different loading rates. The conceptual framework retained for the development of the viscoplastic mechanism is based mainly on the analysis of the behaviour during a creep test. This point is detailed in paragraph § 1.2.

### 1.1 Elastoplastic mechanism

The elastoplastic mechanism is concerned with reproducing the following phenomena [1]:

- the elastic behaviour is non-linear for small strains;
- the maximum strength depends on the confinement level;
- the post-peak softening is governed by the progressive loss of cohesion and by the evolution of the dilatancy angle;
- the residual state, or critical state, is characterised by a zero cohesion, a zero dilatancy and a purely frictional behaviour.

#### 1.1.1 Pre-peak behaviour

The pre-peak behaviour can be decomposed into several phases. By way of example, [2] and [5] distinguish six of them (see Figure ):

1. This phase, termed compaction, concerns the low stress levels. It is accompanied by a contraction of the specimen and accounts for the initial closure of the pre-existing cracks within the material. This phase is considerably reduced, or even non-existent, when a confining pressure is applied to the specimen.

1. This phase corresponds to the linear/elastic part of the stress–strain curve and rarely exceeds 40% of the maximum strength. The volumetric behaviour remains contracting but evolves more slowly than during the first phase.

1. This phase marks the initiation of cracking and of dilatancy. The propagation of the cracks that have been created nevertheless remains stable. The stress level corresponding to this phase is generally between 30% and 60% of the maximum strength.
2. For deviators between 50% and 60% of the maximum strength, a marked intensification of cracking is observed. An increase in the confining pressure has a stabilising effect on the development of these cracks.
3. The coalescence of the cracks begins. This phase corresponds to deviators between 70% and 90% of the maximum strength.

Phases 3, 4 and 5 are not always the subject of such a distinction. Their characterisation and the deviator levels to which they correspond depend strongly on the confining pressure applied to the specimen. For low confinements, cracking will rapidly become unstable from the third phase onwards. At high confinement, this transition can be delayed until the end of the fifth phase.

1. This phase is associated with the unstable development of cracking and with a significant increase in dilatancy up to the maximum strength of the specimen.

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.40/100002010000113A00000C650E250C79FD1A87A7.png)

Figure 1.2: Phases characterising the mechanical and volumetric evolutions of a material during uniaxial or triaxial compression tests (from [5], taken up by [2])

In a simplified manner, three phases will be retained:

1. Phases 1 and 2 can constitute one and the same domain within which the behaviour is elastic. The upper limit of this phase corresponds to the elasticity limit of the material.
2. Phases 3, 4 and 5 can also constitute one and the same domain, which marks a transition in the volumetric behaviour (stable/unstable cracking). For low confinements, this transition takes place at low stress levels, close to the initial elasticity limit. For high confining pressures, this transition can take place at stress levels very close to the strength peak.
3. Phase 6 is associated with the unstable development of cracking. The upper limit of this phase coincides with the maximum strength of the material.

This decomposition into three simplified phases makes it possible to define three thresholds in stress space (Figure ). The upper limits of domains 1 and 3 characterise mechanical thresholds: the initial elasticity limit and the maximum strength, also called the peak strength. The upper limit of phase 2 marks a transition in the volumetric behaviour (contractancy/dilatancy). This transition between contracting and dilating behaviours takes place for stress states called characteristic states [6] and [7]. The set of characteristic states obtained for triaxial compression tests at different confining pressures defines the characteristic threshold.

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.40/1000020100000C3E0000071C5E3E3E96D5B1980B.png)

Figure 1.3: Evolution thresholds in the pre-peak phase.

Within the framework retained by [2], the pre-peak hardening is therefore expressed by a *dilation* of the elastic domain between the *damage* thresholds (or initial elasticity limit) and the *peak* threshold (or maximum strength) (Figure ).

When the stress state lies between the initial elasticity limit and the characteristic threshold, the plastic volumetric behaviour is termed contracting. When the stress state lies between the characteristic threshold and the peak threshold, the plastic volumetric behaviour is termed dilating. The characteristic threshold is defined as the union of the initial elasticity limit (for low confinements) and of a threshold called the *cleavage* limit, with reference to the work carried out by [8] and [9] (higher confinements) (Figure ).

#### 1.1.2 Post-peak behaviour

The post-peak behaviour characterises the evolution of the material in the vicinity of and beyond the strength peak (between its maximum and residual strengths). Most cohesive materials exhibit a softening behaviour after having reached their peak strength. This domain accounts for a deterioration of the mechanical properties of the material and, in particular, a loss of cohesion. The post-peak behaviour depends strongly on the confinement [2]. An increase in the confining pressure tends to stabilise the drop in strength. This stabilisation is nevertheless progressive and a function of external parameters such as the loading rate, the temperature, the stress path, the pore pressure, etc. Several failure patterns, or modes of collapse [1], are observed as a function of the confining pressure. It is commonly accepted to distinguish three of them:

- Low confinement: the specimen fails by *cleavage*; the failure is brittle and explosive. This failure mode is termed *diffuse bifurcation* by [10] (Figure );
- Intermediate confinement: the formation of one or more shear structure(s) within the specimen controls the loss of strength (Figure ). As soon as these so-called *induced* structures [1] are created, the loss of strength is entirely controlled by the behaviour along these discontinuities up to the residual threshold (residual strength criterion, critical state of the material, purely frictional behaviour, zero cohesion and dilatancy). This failure mode is termed *localised bifurcation* by [10];
- High confinement: the behaviour becomes ductile. The specimen can deform substantially without loss of strength or of homogeneity. The volumetric behaviour can remain purely contracting. There is *barrelling* of the specimen (Figure ).

The work carried out by [1] and [2] made it possible to bring out the existence of an intermediate state between the maximum and residual strengths of a material. This state coincides with a total loss of cohesion and a maximum of dilatancy, which correspond to the formation of one or more shear surface(s) (Figure ). The set of these intermediate states defines a threshold in stress space, called the intermediate threshold. [1] and [2] assume that the intermediate threshold coincides with the second part of the characteristic threshold introduced in paragraph § 1.1.1. These two thresholds must nevertheless not be confused. The characteristic threshold is a transition threshold in the pre-peak volumetric behaviour, whereas the intermediate threshold is a *mechanical* threshold through which the elastic domain hardens in the post-peak regime. The volumetric behaviour in the post-peak phase is always dilating, whatever the stress state. The evolution of the elasticity domain for the post-peak behaviour therefore takes place in two stages [2]:

- A first transition between the peak and intermediate thresholds, during which the cohesion of the material will progressively vanish (quantity $C$ in the Figure ) while the dilatancy will increase to reach its maximum on the intermediate threshold (quantity $\psi$ in the in the Figure ).
- A second transition between the intermediate and residual thresholds, during which the dilatancy decreases by progressive wear of the lips of the shear structures created at the intermediate threshold (Figure ). On the residual threshold, cohesion and dilatancy are zero; the behaviour is purely frictional (critical state) (quantity ${\mathrm{\phi }}_{\mathit{rés}}$ in the Figure ).

For low confinements, the transition between the peak threshold and the residual threshold takes place essentially by loss of cohesion. There is failure by cleavage or diffuse bifurcation (Figure ). The dilatancy only increases; no independent shear surface is created. For higher confinements, the tendency is reversed; the role of dilatancy becomes increasingly important (Figure ).

### 1.2 Figure 1.5: Evolution thresholds in the post-peak phase. Viscoplastic mechanism

The effect of time on the behaviour of a material is expressed by various phenomena. Let us mention, for example:

- Consolidation: decrease in volume with time following drainage of the material (hydromechanical phenomenon).
- Swelling: increase in volume generated by chemical modifications of the structure of the rock.
- Creep: delayed strains at constant loading.
- Relaxation: release of the stresses at constant strain.
- Ageing: degradation of the mechanical properties of the rock following a chemical alteration of its constituent minerals.
- Healing: physicochemical phenomenon of recrystallisation allowing the rock to recover its mechanical properties.

The delayed deviatoric behaviour of geomaterials can be characterised by various types of tests:

- Triaxial compression tests at different loading rates: it is commonly accepted that the maximum strength of a material decreases with a decrease in the loading rate [2].
- Creep tests (Figure ): a deviator is held constant after a phase of isotropic confinement. The evolution of the axial strains as a function of time makes it possible to characterise the rate and the amplitude of the delayed effects. Three distinct regimes are generally brought out: a primary creep during which the creep rate only decreases with time; a secondary creep during which the creep rate is constant; a tertiary creep during which the creep rate increases rapidly. This last regime generally leads to the failure of the specimen.
- Relaxation tests: the strain is held constant. The drop in stress with time also makes it possible to characterise the rate and the amplitude of the delayed effects.

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.40/10000201000008EE000002656A6A9C0EA8A0862E.png)

Figure 1.6: Results of creep tests carried out on Cox argillite [11], taken up by [12] (on the left), [13] taken up by [2] (on the right).

Depending on the deviator level applied to a specimen, the three creep regimes presented above may or may not exist. For low deviator levels, creep stabilises rapidly (primary creep). For higher deviators, the long-term behaviour evolves in an unstable manner. The transition time between secondary and tertiary creeps depends on the applied stress level and on external parameters such as the temperature. An increase in the confining pressure stabilises the long-term behaviour. The identification of the stress states corresponding to the transition between stable and unstable long-term behaviours for different confining pressures makes it possible to define a threshold in stress space, called the maximum viscoplastic threshold. This threshold is also called the long-term strength threshold by [2], because the transition stress level between stable and unstable creeps also corresponds to the maximum strength that can be reached during a triaxial compression test at an infinitely slow loading rate.

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.40/1000020100000CBB000006817B8AFA6DC200C76E.png)

Figure 1.7: Evolution thresholds for the viscoplastic mechanism.

The existence of this threshold is not unanimously accepted by the scientific community, because its determination remains difficult to define, in particular for materials with a slow creep kinetics. However, based on several studies listed by [2], the LETK model retains, as the long-term strength threshold, the threshold of initiation of extension cracks (unstable cracking), that is to say the envelope of the damage threshold and of the cleavage limit. The long-term strength threshold therefore coincides with the characteristic threshold defined in paragraph § 1.1.1. Here again, the role of these two thresholds must not be confused. The maximum viscoplastic threshold is a *mechanical* threshold through which the elasticity domain of the viscoplastic mechanism hardens, whereas the characteristic threshold is a transition threshold in the volumetric behaviour for both mechanisms. With these definitions, an unstable long-term behaviour necessarily corresponds to a dilating volumetric state. The threshold of initiation of the viscoplastic strains corresponds to the initial elasticity limit (damage threshold). The elastic domain of the viscoplastic mechanism therefore evolves between the initial elasticity limit (which is identical to that of the elastoplastic mechanism) and the long-term strength threshold. During a creep test, a deviator held constant between the initial elasticity limit and the maximum viscoplastic threshold corresponds to a stable creep (primary creep) and to a contracting volumetric behaviour. A deviator held constant between the maximum viscoplastic threshold and the peak threshold corresponds to an unstable long-term behaviour and to a dilating volumetric behaviour.

### 1.3 Anisotropy

Because of their geological history and their mode of deposition/formation, certain geomaterials present one or more preferential orientations, which can have an influence on their instantaneous and/or delayed mechanical behaviour. The properties generally affected by this initial anisotropy are: Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, the shear modulus, the uniaxial compressive strength, the amplitude of the delayed strains, …

Structural (or initial) anisotropy is among the physical phenomena taken into account by the LKR model (see § 2.2 and § 2.5).

### 1.4 Coupling between the two mechanisms

As indicated in paragraph 1.2, the maximum strength reached during a triaxial compression test depends on the loading rate and therefore on time. Now the maximum strength is a notion carried solely by the plastic mechanism (peak threshold), which is independent of time. In order to be able to reproduce this kind of phenomenon, it is therefore necessary to couple the two mechanisms so as to introduce, in an implicit manner, the notion of time within the plastic mechanism.

The coupling introduced by [2] operates at the level of the internal hardening variables of the two mechanisms. When the stress state lies above the maximum viscoplastic threshold, the hardening of the viscoplastic surface influences that of the plastic surface. The reader will refer to [2] for more details on this subject. The coupling between the two mechanisms will moreover be mentioned in paragraph 2.6, in which the expressions of the hardening variables of the two mechanisms are presented.

### 1.5 Remarks on the spherical behaviour

The LETK constitutive model was initially developed in order to reproduce the behaviour of structures during underground excavations. The stress path followed at the wall of a gallery during an excavation is not spherical. The LETK model is therefore concerned essentially with reproducing the deviatoric behaviour of geomaterials. Along a purely spherical stress path, the modelled behaviour will therefore remain linear elastic *.*

## 2 Thermomechanical extension of the LETK model (LKR model)

The aim of this paragraph is to present the developments made to the LETK constitutive model, whether on the mechanical part of the model or on the integration of the effects of temperature. The whole of the constitutive equations of the model is presented in this paragraph. A few comments will be made concerning the developments made to the LETK model and the integration of the effects of temperature and of anisotropy.

The LKR model can be coupled to the classical equations of thermo-poro-elasticity in the sense of [14] and [15]. The coupling equations are not detailed in this document. For more information, see [14].

### 2.1 Partition of the strain tensor

The strain tensor is divided into four parts. The elastic strain tensor is decomposed into a mechanical part, ${\epsilon}^{m,e}$ , and a thermal part, ${\epsilon}^{T,e}$ . The irreversible strains are decomposed into a plastic (instantaneous) part, ${\epsilon}^{p}$ , and a viscoplastic (delayed) part, ${\epsilon}^{\mathit{vp}}$ . Under the small-perturbation hypothesis, this decomposition is also valid for an incremental writing:

$$
\dot{\epsilon}={\dot{\epsilon}}^{m,e}+{\dot{\epsilon}}^{T,e}+{\dot{\epsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}+{\dot{\epsilon}}^{p}
$$

The notation $\dot{x}$ represents the derivative with respect to time of the quantity $x$ . The notion of physical time is valid only for the viscoplastic mechanism. The notion of time for the elastic and plastic mechanisms refers to a numerical integration time step.

### 2.2 Elastic mechanism

The increment of *mechanical* elastic strain is linked to the stress increment by a linear anisotropic elastic relation (transversely isotropic, ELAS_ISTR, or orthotropic, ELAS_ORTH):

$$
\dot{\sigma}={D}^{g}:{\dot{\epsilon}}^{m,e}
$$

Where ${D}^{g}$ is the fourth-order Hooke tensor in the *global* frame of the loading (X, Y, Z).

For an anisotropic material, the parameters defined in the command DEFI_MATERIAU [31] refer to the elastic constants in the local frame (L, T, N) [32]. The change of frame (local to global), transparent to the user, is carried out in the integration routines of the constitutive model. For the Hooke tensor, written in the form of a 6x6 matrix, one has:

$$
{D}^{g}={}^{T}\lbrace {P}_{66}^{\mathit{lg}}\rbrace .\lbrace {D}^{l}\rbrace .\lbrace {P}_{66}^{\mathit{lg}}\rbrace
$$

Where ${D}^{l}$ is the Hooke tensor in the *local* frame and ${P}_{66}^{\mathit{lg}}$ is the transformation matrix from the local frame to the global frame. The Hooke tensors for orthotropic and transversely isotropic elastic materials are defined in [32]. The matrix ${P}_{66}^{\mathit{lg}}$ involves the three angles of rotation about the axes of the global frame $(\alpha ,\beta ,\gamma )$ (respectively about Z, Y and X). These angles must be defined under the keyword ANGLE_REP of the factor keyword MASSIF (command AFFE_CARA_ELEM). In order to be able to use the LKR model, one (and only one) of the two keywords ELAS_ISTR and ELAS_ORTH must be present in the command DEFI_MATERIAU. If the LKR model is used together with a THM kit, one must necessarily use the keyword ELAS_ORTH for a 2D modelling (D_PLAN or AXIS) and the keyword ELAS_ISTR in 3D.

The elastic moduli of soft and hard rocks tend to decrease slightly with temperature. However, this evolution is less marked for clayey materials, which depend strongly on external parameters such as the relative humidity, and is negligible compared with the effects of the confining pressure in the temperature ranges commonly envisaged. The effects of temperature on the elastic moduli are therefore not taken into account in the LKR model.

The increment of *thermal* elastic strain, ${\dot{\epsilon}}^{T,e}$ , is linked to the temperature increment $\dot{T}$ by the relation:

$$
{\dot{\epsilon}}^{T,e}={\alpha}^{g}\dot{T}
$$

With ${\alpha}^{g}$ the second-order tensor of the thermal expansion coefficients in the *global* frame. In the command DEFI_MATERIAU, the user defines the thermal expansion coefficients in the *local* frame of the material (ALPHA_L, ALPHA_T, ALPHA_N). The passage from ${\alpha}^{l}$ to ${\alpha}^{g}$ is carried out in the integration routines of the model (same approach as in the relation but with “classical” 3x3 rotation matrices).

**Note:** the elastic parameters are, as a first approximation, assumed independent of temperature.

### 2.3 Plastic mechanism

The quantities ${a}^{p}$ , ${s}^{p}$ and ${m}^{p}$ are the hardening parameters of the plastic mechanism. The expressions of these parameters are detailed in paragraph § 2.3.2. #### 2.3.2 Hardening parameters

The hardening parameters ${a}^{p}$ , ${s}^{p}$ and ${m}^{p}$ are defined as functions of the hardening variable of the plastic mechanism, which will be denoted ${\xi}^{p}$ , and of the temperature $T$ . The expression of ${\xi}^{p}$ will be detailed in paragraph § 2.6 . The values taken by these functions account for the evolution of the elasticity domain between the various thresholds presented in the Figures and . However, compared with the mechanical case, these thresholds are no longer static; they evolve as a function of temperature.

Three phases will be distinguished. The first phase corresponds to a positive hardening of the elasticity domain between the initial elasticity limit and the peak threshold (Figure). The second and third phases correspond to a negative hardening between (1) the peak threshold and the intermediate threshold and (2) the intermediate threshold and the residual threshold (Figure).

The term *plastic surface* or *surface of the plastic mechanism* will be used to designate the boundary of the *current* elasticity domain of the plastic mechanism.

##### 2.3.2.1 Between the initial elasticity limit and the peak threshold

The elasticity domain evolves, in a first stage, between the initial elasticity limit, or damage threshold, and the peak threshold (number $2$ in Figure ).

$$

$$

The parameters ${r}^{m}$ and ${r}^{s}$ account for the influence of temperature on the maximum strength. These constants must be strictly negative (R_M and R_S must be strictly positive) and satisfy the condition ${r}^{s}\le {r}^{m}$ . $$

The parameter ${s}_{[0]}$ also varies with temperature via ${m}_{[0]}$ . ### 2.4 Viscoplastic mechanism

#### 2.4.1 Expression of the viscoplasticity criterion

It is recalled that the viscoplastic mechanism of the LETK model is based on a formalism of Perzyna type [23]. The expression of the viscoplasticity criterion, which will be denoted ${F}^{\mathit{vp}}$ , is identical, in its form, to that of the criterion of the plastic mechanism:

$$

The quantities ${a}^{\mathit{vp}}$ , ${s}^{\mathit{vp}}$ and ${m}^{\mathit{vp}}$ are the hardening parameters of the viscoplastic mechanism. For reasons similar to those mentioned in the introduction of paragraph § 2.3.2, these parameters are functions of the hardening variable of the viscoplastic mechanism, which will be denoted ${\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}$ , and of the temperature, $T$ . The expression of ${\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}$ is detailed in paragraph § 2.6. The other quantities appearing in equation () have the same expressions and meanings as in paragraph § 2.3. The term *viscoplastic surface*, or *surface of the viscoplastic mechanism*, will also be used to designate the boundary of the elasticity domain of the viscoplastic mechanism.

### 2.5 Plastic and viscoplastic anisotropies

The initial (visco)plastic anisotropy is taken into account by the so-called fabric-tensor method. This method consists in making the plastic and/or viscoplastic mechanisms dependent on the orientation of the loading, via the introduction of a microstructure tensor (fabric tensor) into the expressions of ${F}^{p}$ and/or ${F}^{\mathit{vp}}$ . In the LKR model, this dependence is introduced via the parameter ${\sigma}_{c}$ , which represents the uniaxial compressive strength of the material. ${\sigma}_{c}$ takes the following form:

$$
{\sigma}_{c}={\widehat{\sigma}}_{c}(1+{A}_{ij}{l}_{i}{l}_{j}+{b}_{1}{({A}_{ij}{l}_{i}{l}_{j})}^{2}+{b}_{2}{({A}_{ij}{l}_{i}{l}_{j})}^{3}+\mathrm{...})
$$

Where ${A}_{ij}$ is a second-order tensor that is a function of the fabric tensor of the material, ${l}_{i}$ is a vector describing the orientation of the loading in the local frame of the material, ${\widehat{\sigma}}_{c}$ and ${b}_{i}$ are material parameters. The expression is generally simplified to order 2 (sufficient to reproduce the behaviour observed in the laboratory) and for transversely isotropic materials (${A}_{ij}$ becomes a scalar ${A}_{22}={A}_{33}=-0.5{A}_{11}$ , which is a material parameter). The three material parameters introduced by the expression are therefore ${\widehat{\sigma}}_{c}$ (SIGMA_C), ${b}_{1}$ (B_SIGC) and ${A}_{11}$ (A_SIGC). A routine is specifically dedicated to the computation of ${\sigma}_{c}$ . The components ${l}_{i}$ have the expression:

$$
{l}_{i}=\frac{\sqrt{{\lbrace {\sigma}_{\mathit{iL}}^{l}\rbrace }^{2}+{\lbrace {\sigma}_{\mathit{iT}}^{l}\rbrace }^{2}+{\lbrace {\sigma}_{iN}^{l}\rbrace }^{2}}}{{\lbrace {\sigma}^{l}\rbrace }_{kk}^{2}}
$$

Where ${\sigma}^{l}$ is the stress tensor in the local frame:

$$
{\sigma}_{l}={}^{T}\lbrace {P}_{33}^{\mathit{gl}}\rbrace \lbrace {\sigma}^{g}\rbrace \lbrace {P}_{33}^{\mathit{gl}}\rbrace
$$

With ${P}_{33}^{\mathit{gl}}$ the transformation matrix from the global frame to the local frame and ${\sigma}^{g}$ the stress tensor expressed in the global frame.

### 2.6 Coupling between the two mechanisms

The plastic and viscoplastic mechanisms are coupled through their internal hardening variables. This coupling makes it possible, among other things, to reproduce the dependence of the maximum strength of geomaterials on the loading rate, and therefore on time. The coupling between the two mechanisms is triggered as soon as the stress state lies above the characteristic threshold [2]. The hardening of the viscoplastic surface influences that of the plastic surface. As long as the stress state is below the characteristic threshold, the increment of the hardening variable of the plastic mechanism, ${\dot{\xi}}^{p}$ , coincides with the increment of equivalent plastic strain, or plastic distortion, ${\dot{\gamma}}^{p}$ :

$$
{\dot{\xi}}^{p}={\dot{\gamma}}^{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}{\dot{e}}^{p}:{\dot{e}}^{p}}
$$

When the stress state lies above the characteristic threshold, ${\dot{\xi}}^{p}$ is the sum of the increment of plastic distortion ${\dot{\gamma}}^{p}$ and of the increment of viscoplastic distortion, ${\dot{\gamma}}^{\mathit{vp}}$ :

$$
{\dot{\xi}}^{p}={\dot{\gamma}}^{p}+{\dot{\gamma}}^{\mathit{vp}}
$$

The increment of the hardening variable of the viscoplastic mechanism is equal to the increment of viscoplastic distortion, whatever the stress state:

$$
{\dot{\xi}}^{\mathit{vp}}=\min\left\lbrace {\dot{\gamma}}^{\mathit{vp}};⟨{\xi}_{[5]}-{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}⟩\right\rbrace
$$

### 2.7 Flow rules

It is commonly accepted that the direction of the (visco)plastic flows is derived from a function ${g}^{(v)p}$ called the (visco)plastic potential. If this potential coincides with the function ${F}^{(v)p}$ , the flow rule is said to be *normal* or *associated*, in the sense that it is *associated* with the (visco)plasticity criterion ${F}^{(v)p}$ . A flow rule derived from a potential distinct from ${F}^{(v)p}$ (more precisely such that $\frac{\partial {g}^{(v)p}}{\partial \sigma }$ is not proportional to $\frac{\partial {F}^{(v)p}}{\partial \sigma }$ is said to be *non-associated*. Materials obeying the normality rule are generally termed *generalised* *standard* materials.

The notion of a standard material is “practical”, since it makes it possible to describe the plastic behaviour of a material from a single function [24]. Nevertheless, the hypotheses that underlie the theory of associated plasticity are not representative of the real behaviour of rocks, soils and concretes [25].

The notion of non-associated (visco)plasticity requires the definition of a quantity called the *dilatancy angle*, which will be denoted $\psi ` , qui permet de contrôler les variations de volume (visco)plastique [:ref:`24 <24>$].

The framework defined by [2], and taken up by [3], is inspired by the work carried out by [26] and [27]. This framework makes it possible to define explicitly the evolution of the dilatancy angle, and therefore the evolution of the (visco)plastic volume changes, without going through the determination of a (visco)plastic potential. The hypothesis supported by [27] states that (one denotes $\Sigma$ the stress space):

$$
\exists n\in \Sigma

### 2.8 Dilatancy laws

Inspired by the work carried out by [29], [2] proposed the following relation for the parameter $\beta$ :

$$
\beta =\frac{-2\sqrt{6}\sin\psi }{3-\sin\psi }
$$

Within the framework considered by [2], the dilatancy angle evolves as a function of the stress state and of the internal hardening variables. Two approaches are proposed. The first approach makes it possible to describe the evolution of the dilatancy angle for the pre-peak part of the plastic mechanism and for the viscoplastic mechanism. This approach is based on the notion of characteristic state. As long as the stress state is below the characteristic threshold, the dilatancy angle is negative and the (visco)plastic volumetric behaviour is contracting. When the stress state lies above the characteristic threshold, the dilatancy angle is positive and the (visco)plastic volumetric behaviour is dilating. The second approach makes it possible to describe the evolution of the dilatancy angle for the post-peak regime of the plastic mechanism.

In the first approach, one must be able to determine the position of the stress state with respect to the characteristic threshold. Because of the non-linearity of the functions ${F}^{p}$ (equation ) and ${F}^{\mathit{vp}}$ (equation ), the deviator level $q$ on the characteristic threshold cannot be evaluated directly from the effective mean stress $p'$ . It is necessary to carry out a change of frame. This change of frame makes it possible to express the quantities $q$ and $p'$ as functions of the major principal stress $\sigma {'}_{max}$ and of the minor principal stress $\sigma {'}_{min}$ . In order to obtain the change-of-frame relations, it suffices to write the (visco)plasticity criterion in these two planes:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{F}^{\text{*}}(p',q,{a}^{\text{*}},{s}^{\text{*}},{m}^{\text{*}})\equiv \frac{\mathit{qH}(\theta )}{{\sigma}_{c}{H}_{0}^{c}}-{\left(\frac{{m}^{\text{*}}}{{\sigma}_{c}}\left(p'-\frac{\mathit{qH}(\theta )}{3{H}_{0}^{c}}\right)+{s}^{\text{*}}\right)}^{{a}^{\text{*}}}=0\hfill \\ {F}^{\text{*}}(\sigma {'}_{min},\sigma {'}_{max},{a}^{\text{*}},{s}^{\text{*}},{m}^{\text{*}})\equiv \left(\frac{\sigma {'}_{max}-\sigma {'}_{min}}{{\sigma}_{c}}\right)-{\left(\frac{{m}^{\text{*}}}{{\sigma}_{c}}\sigma {'}_{min}+{s}^{\text{*}}\right)}^{{a}^{\text{*}}}=0\end{array}
$$

By analogy between the relations of equation (), one obtains:

$$
\sigma {'}_{min}=p'-\frac{\mathit{qH}(\theta )}{3{H}_{0}^{c}}
$$

\sin\psi ={\rho}_{1}\left(\frac{\sigma {'}_{max}-\sigma {'}_{\mathit{char}}}{{\rho}_{2}\sigma {'}_{max}+\sigma {'}_{\mathit{char}}}\right)

The parameters ${\rho}_{1}$ (RHO_1) and ${\rho}_{2}$ (RHO_2) are strictly positive material constants and $\sigma {'}_{\mathit{char}}$ is the value of $\sigma {'}_{max}$ on the characteristic threshold:

## 3 Numerical integration

In this paragraph, one returns briefly to the numerical integration of the thermomechanical model proposed. The model has been integrated according to two schemes: an explicit scheme and an implicit scheme. Only the main outlines of these two schemes are given, without necessarily detailing all the quantities involved. In what follows, the notation $\sigma$ will exceptionally be used to designate the effective stress tensor. This makes it possible not to burden the notations. The expression of the derivatives that appear in the set of equations will not be detailed.

### 3.1 Explicit integration scheme

The explicit integration scheme (ALGO_INTE='SPECIFIQUE') consists of a classical elastic prediction phase followed by a (visco)plastic correction phase if one of the two, or both, mechanism(s) is/are activated during this *elastic trial.* The input variables of the solution algorithm are:

- $t$ : time at the time instant “+”;
- ${t}^{-}$ : time at the time instant “−”;
- $T$ : temperature at the time instant “+”;
- ${T}^{-}$ : temperature at the time instant “−”;
- ${T}_{0}$ : reference temperature;
- ${\sigma}^{-}$ : effective stress tensor at the time instant “−”;
- ${\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-}$ : hardening variable of the viscoplastic mechanism at the time instant “−”;
- ${\xi}^{p-}$ : hardening variable of the plastic mechanism at the time instant “−”;
- $\Delta \epsilon$ : increment of total strain.

The output variables are:

- $\sigma$ : effective stress tensor at the time instant “+”;
- ${\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}$ : hardening variable of the viscoplastic mechanism at the time instant “+”;
- ${\xi}^{p}$ : hardening variable of the plastic mechanism at the time instant “+”;
- $\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}$ : tangent operator.

From the input data, one proceeds in the following manner:

- Elastic prediction: ${\sigma}^{e}={\sigma}^{-}+D:\left(\Delta \epsilon -\alpha \Delta T\right)$
- Viscoplastic mechanism active?

  - Computation of ${F}^{\mathit{vp}}\left({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-},T\right)$
  - If ${F}^{\mathit{vp}}\left({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-},T\right)<0$ , one is elastic:

    - $\Delta {\epsilon}^{\mathit{vp}}=0$
    - $\Delta {\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}=\Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}=0$
    - ${\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}={\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-}$
    - ${\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}={\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}-}$
  - If ${F}^{\mathit{vp}}\left({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-},T\right)\ge 0$ , one is viscoplastic:

    - $\Delta {\epsilon}^{\mathit{vp}}={A}_{v}^{T}{\left(\frac{⟨{F}^{\mathit{vp}}\left({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}},T\right)⟩}{{P}_{a}}\right)}^{{n}_{v}}{G}^{\mathit{vp}-}\Delta t$
    - $\Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\Delta {e}^{\mathit{vp}}:\Delta {e}^{\mathit{vp}}}$ with $\Delta {e}^{\mathit{vp}}=\Delta {\epsilon}^{\mathit{vp}}-\mathit{Tr}\frac{\left(\Delta {\epsilon}^{\mathit{vp}}\right)}{3}\delta$
    - $\Delta {\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}=min(\Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}},{\xi}_{[5]}-{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-})$
    - ${\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}={\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-}+\Delta {\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}$
    - ${\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}={\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}-}+\Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}$
    - ${\epsilon}^{\mathit{vp}}={\epsilon}^{\mathit{vp}-}+\Delta {\epsilon}^{\mathit{vp}}$
- Plastic mechanism active?

  - Position of the stress state with respect to the characteristic threshold:

    - If ${F}^{\mathit{vp}}\left({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}_{[5]},T\right)\ge 0$ , there is coupling between the two mechanisms
    - Otherwise, there is no coupling between the two mechanisms
  - Computation of ${F}^{p}\left({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{p-},T\right)$
  - If ${F}^{p}\left({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{p-},T\right)<0$ , one is elastic:

    - $\Delta {\epsilon}^{p}=0$
    - $\Delta {\gamma}^{p}=0$
    - ${\gamma}^{p}={\gamma}^{p-}$
    - ${\xi}^{p}={\xi}^{p-}+\Delta {\xi}^{p}$ with:

      - $\Delta {\xi}^{p}=0$ if there is no coupling between the two mechanisms
      - $\Delta {\xi}^{p}=\Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}$ if there is coupling between the two mechanisms
    - Update of the stresses:

$\sigma ={\sigma}^{-}+D\left(\Delta \epsilon -\Delta {\epsilon}^{\mathit{vp}}-\alpha \Delta T\right)$

  - If ${F}^{p}\left({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{p-},T\right)\ge 0$ , one is plastic:

    - Computation of $\Delta \lambda$ (equation )
    - $\Delta \epsilon =\Delta \lambda {G}^{p-}$
    - $\Delta {\gamma}^{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\Delta {e}^{p}:\Delta {e}^{p}}$ with $\Delta {e}^{p}=\Delta {\epsilon}^{p}-\mathit{Tr}\frac{\left(\Delta {\epsilon}^{p}\right)}{3}\delta$
    - $\Delta {\xi}^{p}=\Delta {\gamma}^{p}$ if there is no coupling between the two mechanisms
    - $\Delta {\xi}^{p}=\Delta {\gamma}^{p}+\Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}$ if there is coupling between the two mechanisms
    - ${\xi}^{p}={\xi}^{p-}+\Delta {\xi}^{p}$
    - Update of the stresses:

$\sigma ={\sigma}^{-}+D\left(\Delta \epsilon -\Delta {\epsilon}^{\mathit{vp}}-\Delta {\epsilon}^{p}-\alpha \Delta T\right)$

- Computation of the tangent operator $\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}$ (see § 3.1.2)

#### 3.1.1 Determination of the increment of the plastic multiplier

The increment of the plastic multiplier $\Delta \lambda$ is determined by applying the Kuhn–Tucker condition, that is to say by applying the following condition:

$$
{F}^{p}\left(\sigma ,{\xi}^{p},T\right)=0
$$

Or, in incremental form:

$$
{F}^{p}\left({\sigma}_{ij}^{-}+{D}_{ijkl}:\left(\Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}-\Delta \lambda {G}_{kl}^{p-}-⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩{G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}-}\Delta t-{\alpha}_{kl}\Delta T\right),{\xi}^{p-}+\Delta {\xi}^{p},{T}^{-}+\Delta T\right)=0
$$

One chooses to carry out an explicit solution by an Euler expansion:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{F}^{p}\left({\sigma}_{ij}^{-}+{D}_{ijkl}:\left(\Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}-\Delta \lambda {G}_{kl}^{p-}-⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩{G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}-}\Delta t-{\alpha}_{kl}\Delta T\right),{\xi}^{p-},{T}^{-}\right)-\\ \Delta \lambda {\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}\right)}_{{\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}:{G}_{kl}^{p-}\right)+\Delta {\xi}^{p}{\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p}}\right)}_{{\xi}^{p-}}+\Delta T{\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial T}\right)}_{{T}^{-}}=0\hfill \end{array}
$$

One denotes:

$$
{\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}\right)}_{{\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}=\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}\right)

\textrm{;}

{\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p}}\right)}_{{\xi}^{p-}}=\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p-}}\right)` ; :math:`{\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial T}\right)}_{{T}^{-}}=\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {T}^{-}}\right)
$$

Two cases are to be distinguished.

If there is coupling between the plastic and viscoplastic mechanisms (stress state above the characteristic threshold). One has $\Delta {\xi}^{p}=\Delta {\gamma}^{p}+\Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}$ , and therefore:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{F}^{p}\left({\sigma}_{ij}^{-}+{D}_{ijkl}:\left(\Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}-⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩{G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}-}\Delta t-{\alpha}_{kl}\Delta T\right),{\xi}^{p-},{T}^{-}\right)-\\ \Delta \lambda \left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}\right):\left({D}_{ijkl}:{G}_{kl}^{p-}\right)+\left(\Delta \lambda \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}{g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p-}+\Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}\right)\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p-}}+\Delta T\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {T}^{-}}\right)=0\hfill \end{array}
$$

And:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{F}^{p}\left({\sigma}_{ij}^{-}+{D}_{ijkl}:\left(\Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}-⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩{G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}-}\Delta t-{\alpha}_{kl}\Delta T\right),{\xi}^{p-},{T}^{-}\right)=\\ {F}^{p}\left({\sigma}_{ij}^{-},{\xi}^{p-},{T}^{-}\right)+\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}:\left(\Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}-⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩{G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}-}\Delta t-{\alpha}_{kl}\Delta T\right)\right)\hfill \end{array}
$$

Whence the expression of the plastic multiplier:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\Delta \lambda =\frac{{F}^{p}\left({\sigma}_{ij}^{-},{\xi}^{p-},{T}^{-}\right)+\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}:\left(\Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}-⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩{G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}-}\Delta t\right)\right)+\Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p-}}}{\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}\right):\left({D}_{ijkl}:{G}_{kl}^{p-}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}{g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p-}\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p-}}}+\\ \frac{\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {T}^{-}}-K\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:{\alpha}_{ij}\right)\Delta T}{\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}:{G}_{kl}^{p-}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}{g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p-}\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p-}}}\end{array}
$$

With:

$$
⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩={A}_{v}^{T}{\left(\frac{⟨{F}^{\mathit{vp}}({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-})⟩}{{P}_{a}}\right)}^{{n}_{v}}={A}_{v}^{T}{\left(\frac{⟨{F}^{\mathit{vp}}({\sigma}^{-}+{D}_{ijkl}:\left(\Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}-{\alpha}_{kl}\Delta T\right),{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-})⟩}{{P}_{a}}\right)}^{{n}_{v}}
$$

The quantity ${A}_{v}^{T}$ represents the quantity ${A}_{v}$ computed at the temperature $T$ (time instant “+”).

If there is no coupling between the two mechanisms (stress state below the characteristic threshold), one therefore has $\Delta {\xi}^{p}=\Delta {\gamma}^{p}$ , and one has the following expression of the plastic multiplier:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\Delta \lambda =\frac{{F}^{p}\left({\sigma}_{ij}^{-},{\xi}^{p-},{T}^{-}\right)+\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}:\left(\Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}-⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩{G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}-}\Delta t\right)\right)}{\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}\right):\left({D}_{ijkl}:{G}_{kl}^{p-}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}{g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p-}\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p-}}}+\\ \frac{\left(\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {T}^{-}}-K\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:{\alpha}_{ij}\right)\Delta T}{\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}:{G}_{kl}^{p-}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}{g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p-}\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p-}}}\end{array}
$$

#### 3.1.2 Tangent operator

The expression of the stress tensor at the time instant “+” is:

$$
{\sigma}_{ij}={\sigma}_{ij}^{-}+{D}_{ijkl}:\left(\Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}-\Delta \lambda {G}_{kl}^{p-}-⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩{G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}-}\Delta t-{\alpha}_{kl}\Delta T\right)
$$

The tangent operator is defined by:

$$
\frac{\partial \Delta {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}={D}_{ijkl}-\left({D}_{\mathit{ijmn}}:{G}_{\mathit{mn}}^{p-}\right)\otimes \frac{\partial \Delta \lambda }{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}-\left({D}_{\mathit{ijmn}}:{G}_{\mathit{mn}}^{\mathit{vp}-}\right)\otimes \frac{\partial ⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}\Delta t
$$

The same two cases are to be distinguished. When there is no coupling between the two mechanisms, one therefore has $\Delta {\xi}^{p}=\Delta {\gamma}^{p}$ . The first term is, from ():

$$
\frac{\partial \Delta \lambda }{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}=\frac{\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}-\left({D}_{\mathit{ijmn}}:{G}_{\mathit{mn}}^{\mathit{vp}-}\right)\otimes \frac{\partial ⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}\Delta t\right)}{\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}:{G}_{kl}^{p-}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}{g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p-}\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p-}}}
$$

And the second term:

$$
\frac{\partial ⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}=\frac{{A}_{v}^{T}{n}_{v}}{{P}_{a}}{\left(\frac{⟨{F}^{\mathit{vp}}({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-},T)⟩}{{P}_{a}}\right)}^{{n}_{v}-1}\frac{\partial {F}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{e}}:\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{e}}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}

\frac{\partial ⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}=\frac{{A}_{v}^{T}{n}_{v}}{{P}_{a}}{\left(\frac{⟨{F}^{\mathit{vp}}({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-},T)⟩}{{P}_{a}}\right)}^{{n}_{v}-1}\frac{\partial {F}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{e}}:{D}_{ijkl}
$$

The resulting tangent matrix is therefore:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial \Delta {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}={D}_{ijkl}-\left({D}_{\mathit{ijmn}}:{G}_{\mathit{mn}}^{p-}\right)\otimes \frac{\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}-\left({D}_{\mathit{ijmn}}:{G}_{\mathit{mn}}^{\mathit{vp}-}\right)\otimes \frac{\partial ⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}\Delta t\right)}{\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}:{G}_{kl}^{p-}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}{g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p-}\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p-}}}-\\ \frac{{A}_{v}^{T}{n}_{v}}{{P}_{a}}{\left(\frac{⟨{F}^{\mathit{vp}}({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-},T)⟩}{{P}_{a}}\right)}^{{n}_{v}-1}\left({D}_{\mathit{ijmn}}:{G}_{\mathit{mn}}^{\mathit{vp}-}\right)\otimes \left(\frac{\partial {F}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{e}}:{D}_{ijkl}\right)\Delta t\end{array}
$$

When there is coupling between the two mechanisms, one therefore has $\Delta {\xi}^{p}=\Delta {\gamma}^{p}+\Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}$ . The first term is, from ():

$$
\frac{\partial \Delta \lambda }{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}=\frac{\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}-\left({D}_{\mathit{ijmn}}:{G}_{\mathit{mn}}^{\mathit{vp}-}\right)\otimes \frac{\partial ⟨\mathrm{\phi }⟩}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}}\Delta t\right)+\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p-}}\frac{\partial \Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}}}:\frac{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{e}}:{D}_{ijkl}}{\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{-}}:\left({D}_{ijkl}:{G}_{kl}^{p-}\right)-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}{g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p-}\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p-}}}
$$

With the following two terms:

$$
\frac{\partial \Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}}}=\frac{2}{3}\frac{\Delta {e}_{ij}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\Delta {\gamma}^{\mathit{vp}}}:\left({\delta}_{\mathit{ik}}\otimes {\delta}_{\mathit{jl}}-\frac{1}{3}{\delta}_{ij}\otimes {\delta}_{kl}\right)
$$

And:

$$
\frac{\partial \Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{e}}=\frac{{A}_{v}^{T}{n}_{v}}{{P}_{a}}{\left(\frac{⟨{F}^{\mathit{vp}}({\sigma}^{e},{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-},T)⟩}{{P}_{a}}\right)}^{{n}_{v}-1}\frac{\partial {F}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}^{e}}\otimes {G}_{\mathit{mn}}^{\mathit{vp}-}\Delta t
$$

This explicit scheme has been removed from the current version of the model, because it is little used and not very robust compared with the implicit integration that follows.

#### 3.2.2 Correction phase: non-linear equations to be solved

This step consists in solving the system of non-linear equations established on the basis of the viscoplastic and/or plastic mechanisms. The unknowns of the system of non-linear equations are the effective stresses at the time instant “+”, $\sigma ` , le multiplicateur plastique :math:$Delta lambda ` , the hardening variable of the plastic mechanism at the time instant “+”, ${\xi}^{p}$ , and the hardening variable of the viscoplastic mechanism at the time instant “+”, ${\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}$ . The vector of the unknowns is therefore, at most, of size nine (3D). The non-linear equations to be solved are:

- The incremental state equation:

$$
{E}_{1}:\phantom{\rule{4em}{0ex}}\sigma -{\sigma}^{-}-D\left(\Delta \epsilon -\Delta \lambda {G}^{p}-⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩{G}^{\mathit{vp}}-\alpha \Delta T\right)=0
$$

- The Kuhn–Tucker condition:

$$
{E}_{2}:\phantom{\rule{4em}{0ex}}\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\text{Si}{F}^{p}<0\text{alors}\Delta \lambda =0\\ \text{Si}{F}^{p}=0\text{alors}\Delta \lambda >0\end{array}
$$

- The incremental evolution of the hardening variable of the plastic mechanism:

$$
{E}_{3}:\phantom{\rule{4em}{0ex}}{\xi}^{p}-{\xi}^{p-}-\Delta {\xi}^{p}=0
$$

- The incremental evolution of the hardening variable of the viscoplastic mechanism:

$$
{E}_{4}:\phantom{\rule{4em}{0ex}}{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}-{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-}-\Delta {\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}=0
$$

These unknowns form a square system, $R(\Delta Y)$ where $\Delta Y=(\Delta \sigma ,\Delta \lambda ,\Delta {\xi}^{p},\Delta {\xi}^{\mathit{vp}})$ . At iteration $k$ of the local Newton correction loop, the following matrix equation, of unknown $\delta (\Delta {Y}^{k+1})$ , is solved:

$$
\frac{\mathit{dR}(\Delta {Y}^{k})}{d(\Delta {Y}^{k})}\delta (\Delta {Y}^{k+1})=-R({\Delta Y}^{k})
$$

The Jacobian matrix $\frac{\mathit{dR}(\Delta {Y}^{k})}{d(\Delta {Y}^{k})}$ , non-symmetric, is constructed in the following manner:

$$
\frac{\mathit{dR}(\Delta {Y}^{k})}{d(\Delta {Y}^{k})}=\left(\begin{array}{cccc}\frac{\partial {E}_{1}}{\partial {({\sigma}_{ij})}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{1}}{\partial {(\Delta \lambda )}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{1}}{\partial {({\xi}^{p})}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{1}}{\partial {({\xi}^{\mathit{vp}})}^{k}}\\ \frac{\partial {E}_{2}}{\partial {({\sigma}_{ij})}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{2}}{\partial {(\Delta \lambda )}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{2}}{\partial {({\xi}^{p})}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{2}}{\partial {({\xi}^{\mathit{vp}})}^{k}}\\ \frac{\partial {E}_{3}}{\partial {({\sigma}_{ij})}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{3}}{\partial {(\Delta \lambda )}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{3}}{\partial {({\xi}^{p})}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{3}}{\partial {({\xi}^{\mathit{vp}})}^{k}}\\ \frac{\partial {E}_{4}}{\partial {({\sigma}_{ij})}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{4}}{\partial {(\Delta \lambda )}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{4}}{\partial {({\xi}^{p})}^{k}}& \frac{\partial {E}_{4}}{\partial {({\xi}^{\mathit{vp}})}^{k}}\end{array}\right)
$$

The derivative terms associated with ${R}_{1}$ , corresponding to the first row of $\frac{\mathit{dR}(\Delta {Y}^{k})}{d(\Delta {Y}^{k})}$ , are:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\frac{d{({R}_{1})}_{ij}}{d{(\Delta {Y}_{1})}_{\mathit{mn}}}={I}_{\mathit{ijmn}}-\frac{\partial {D}_{ijkl}}{\partial {\sigma}_{\mathit{mn}}}:\left(\Delta {\epsilon}_{kl}-\Delta \lambda {G}_{kl}^{p}-{⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩}^{+}{G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}}-{\alpha}_{kl}\Delta T\right)+\\ \Delta \lambda {D}_{ijkl}:\frac{\partial {G}_{kl}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{\mathit{mn}}}+{D}_{ijkl}:\left({G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}}\otimes \frac{\partial {⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{p})⟩}^{+}}{\partial {\sigma}_{\mathit{mn}}}\Delta t\right)+\\ {⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{p})⟩}^{+}\Delta t{D}_{ijkl}:\frac{\partial {G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{\mathit{mn}}}\end{array}
$$

$$
\frac{d{({R}_{1})}_{ij}}{d{(\Delta {Y}_{2})}_{\mathit{mn}}}={D}_{ijkl}:{G}_{kl}^{p}
$$

$$
\frac{d{({R}_{1})}_{ij}}{d{(\Delta {Y}_{3})}_{\mathit{mn}}}=\Delta \lambda {D}_{ijkl}:\frac{\partial {G}_{kl}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p}}
$$

$$
\frac{d{({R}_{1})}_{ij}}{d{(\Delta {Y}_{4})}_{\mathit{mn}}}=\Delta \lambda {D}_{ijkl}:\left(\frac{\partial {⟨\Phi ({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩}^{+}}{\partial {\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}}{G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}}+\frac{\partial {G}_{kl}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\xi}^{\mathit{vp}}}{⟨\Phi ({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩}^{+}\right)\Delta t
$$

The derivative terms associated with ${R}_{2}$ , corresponding to the second row of $\frac{\mathit{dR}(\Delta {Y}^{k})}{d(\Delta {Y}^{k})}$ , are, in elasticity ( ${R}_{2}\equiv \Delta \lambda$ ):

$$
\frac{d({R}_{2})}{d{(\Delta {Y}_{1})}_{ij}}={0}_{ij}

\textrm{;}

\frac{d({R}_{2})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{2})}=1` ; :math:`\frac{d({R}_{2})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{3})}=0` ; :math:`\frac{d({R}_{2})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{4})}=0
$$

And in plasticity ( ${R}_{2}\equiv {F}^{p}$ ):

$$
\frac{d({R}_{2})}{d{(\Delta {Y}_{1})}_{ij}}=\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}

\textrm{;}

\frac{d({R}_{2})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{2})}=0` ; :math:`\frac{d({R}_{2})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{3})}=\frac{\partial {F}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p}}` ; :math:`\frac{d({R}_{2})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{4})}=0
$$

The derivative terms associated with ${R}_{3}$ , corresponding to the third row of $\frac{\mathit{dR}(\Delta {Y}^{k})}{d(\Delta {Y}^{k})}$ , are:

$$
\frac{d({R}_{3})}{d{(\Delta {Y}_{1})}_{ij}}=\lbrace \begin{array}{c}-\Delta \lambda \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\frac{\partial {g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}\text{dans le cas contractant}\hfill \\ -\Delta \lambda \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\frac{\partial {g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\Delta t\left({g}_{\mathit{II}}^{\mathit{vp}}\frac{\partial {⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩}^{+}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}+\frac{\partial {g}_{\mathit{II}}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩}^{+}\right)\text{dans le cas dilatant}\end{array}
$$

$$
\frac{d({R}_{3})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{2})}=-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}{g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p}
$$

$$
\frac{d({R}_{3})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{3})}=1-\Delta \lambda \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\frac{\partial {g}_{\mathit{II}}^{p}}{\partial {\xi}^{p}}
$$

$$
\frac{d({R}_{3})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{4})}=\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\text{dans le cas contractant}\hfill \\ -\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\Delta t\left({g}_{\mathit{II}}^{\mathit{vp}}\frac{\partial {⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩}^{+}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}+\frac{\partial {g}_{\mathit{II}}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩}^{+}\right)\text{dans le cas dilatant}\end{array}
$$

The derivative terms associated with ${R}_{4}$ , corresponding to the fourth row of $\frac{\mathit{dR}(\Delta {Y}^{k})}{d(\Delta {Y}^{k})}$ , are:

$$
\frac{d({R}_{4})}{d{(\Delta {Y}_{1})}_{ij}}=-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\Delta t\left({g}_{\mathit{II}}^{\mathit{vp}}\frac{\partial {⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩}^{+}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}+\frac{\partial {g}_{\mathit{II}}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩}^{+}\right)\text{si}{\dot{\gamma}}^{\mathit{vp}}\le {\xi}_{[5]}-{\xi}^{\mathit{vp}-}
$$

$$
\frac{d({R}_{4})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{2})}=0
$$

$$
\frac{d({R}_{4})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{3})}=0
$$

$$
\frac{d({R}_{4})}{d(\Delta {Y}_{4})}=1-\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}\Delta t\left({g}_{\mathit{II}}^{\mathit{vp}}\frac{\partial {⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩}^{+}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}+\frac{\partial {g}_{\mathit{II}}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{⟨\mathrm{\phi }({F}^{\mathit{vp}})⟩}^{+}\right)
$$

The whole of the terms will not be detailed in this document.
