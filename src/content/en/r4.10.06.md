# r4.10.06 Error estimators in quantities of interest

**Summary:**

In this document, one presents the framework of error estimation in a quantity of interest. This framework is used with explicit residual estimators as well as with smoothing estimators. A number of quantities of interest are detailed, in particular their expression in terms of loading for the dual problem.

## 1 Model problem and notation

### 1.1 Equations

An elastic solid, tied to a Cartesian frame of ${ℝ}^{3}$ , occupies, in its natural state and before deformation, a bounded connected open domain $\Omega$ of ${ℝ}^{3}$ of regular boundary $\partial \Omega$ with outward normal $\overrightarrow{n}$ . This contour is the union of two disjoint parts ${\Gamma}_{U}$ and ${\Gamma}_{F}$ such that ${\Gamma}_{U}\cup {\Gamma}_{F}=\partial \Omega$ and ${\Gamma}_{U}\cap {\Gamma}_{F}=\varnothing$ .

Let $\overrightarrow{u}(x)={u}_{1}(x)\overrightarrow{{e}_{1}}+{u}_{2}(x)\overrightarrow{{e}_{2}}+{u}_{3}(x)\overrightarrow{{e}_{3}}$ be the displacement of a point $M$ of $\Omega$ with coordinates $x=({x}_{1},{x}_{2},{x}_{3})$ . On the contour ${\Gamma}_{U}$ , a displacement $\overrightarrow{u}(x)=\overrightarrow{0}$ is imposed and a surface force $\overrightarrow{F}(x)$  is imposed on ${\Gamma}_{F}$ . The solid is also subjected to a body force $\overrightarrow{f}(x)$ . The study is placed within the framework of linear elasticity and of the small-perturbation hypothesis, without viscous damping.

![Figure](/figures/r4.10.06/100000000000043E00000275D19FB9820C9E284F.jpg)

*Undeformed configuration*

In what follows, we adopt index notation for vectors and tensors, as well as Einstein's summation convention on repeated dummy indices. Thus, the following linear system:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{a}_{11}{x}_{1}+{a}_{12}{x}_{2}={b}_{1}\\ {a}_{21}{x}_{1}+{a}_{22}{x}_{2}={b}_{2}\end{array}
$$

may be written:

$$
{a}_{ij}{x}_{j}={b}_{i}
$$

The derivative of the ${i}^{\text{ème}}$ component of a vector $\overrightarrow{v}$ with respect to the ${j}^{\text{ème}}$ component of the space coordinates will be written ${v}_{i,j}$ .

The symmetric stress tensor ${\sigma}_{ij}$ is related to the symmetric linearised strain tensor ${\varepsilon}_{ij}$ by the relation:

$$
{\sigma}_{ij}={a}_{ijkl}{\varepsilon}_{kl}
$$

where ${a}_{ijkl}$ is the tensor of the elasticity coefficients. It satisfies the relations of:

- symmetry: ${a}_{\mathrm{ijkh}}={a}_{\mathrm{jikh}}={a}_{\mathrm{ijhk}}={a}_{\mathrm{khij}}$ ;
- positivity of the associated quadratic form: for any real symmetric second-order tensor ${{\rm X}}_{ij}$ , $\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\exists {\alpha}_{0}>0,& \text{tel que}{{\rm X}}_{ij}{a}_{\mathrm{ijkh}}(x){{\rm X}}_{\mathrm{kh}}\ge {\alpha}_{0}{{\rm X}}_{ij}{{\rm X}}_{ij}\\ \forall {{\rm X}}_{ij}={{\rm X}}_{ji},& \forall x\in \Omega \end{array}$ .

The strain tensor is related to the displacement by the relation:

$$
\varepsilon (u)=\frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{grad}(u)+{\mathrm{grad}}^{T}(u))
$$

For a homogeneous and isotropic material, the constitutive law is Hooke's law:

$$
\sigma =\lambda \text{tr}(\varepsilon )I+2\mu \varepsilon
$$

where $\lambda$ and $\mu$ are the Lamé coefficients.

The equilibrium equations, under the small-perturbation hypothesis, are written:

$$
div\sigma +\overrightarrow{f}=\overrightarrow{0}
$$

The boundary conditions defined previously are written:

$$
\overrightarrow{u}(x)=\overrightarrow{0}\mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{U}
$$

$$
\sigma (x)\overrightarrow{n}(x)=\overrightarrow{F}(x)\mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{F}
$$

The set of equations constitutes the problem $({\text{P}}_{1})$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cccc}{\sigma}_{ij,j}+{f}_{i}& =& 0& \text{dans}\Omega \\ {\sigma}_{ij}& =& {a}_{\mathrm{ijkh}}{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{kh}}& \text{dans}\Omega \\ {\varepsilon}_{ij}(u)& =& \frac{1}{2}({u}_{i,j}+{u}_{j,i})& \\ {u}_{i}(x)& =& 0& \text{sur}{\Gamma}_{U}\\ {\sigma}_{ij}{n}_{j}(x)& =& {F}_{i}(x)& \text{sur}{\Gamma}_{F}\end{array}
$$

### 1.2 Variational formulation

The space $V$ is defined as the space of the admissible functions $v$ , sufficiently regular, defined on $\Omega$ and with values in ${ℝ}^{3}$ :

$V=\left\lbrace v\in {H}^{1}(\Omega )\text{et}v=0\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{U}\right\rbrace$

The norms related to it are the following:

| ${L}^{2}$ norm | ${\parallel v\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(\Omega )}^{2}=\underset{\Omega}{\int}v\cdot vd\Omega$ |
| --- | --- |
| ${H}^{1}$ norm | ${\parallel v\parallel }_{{H}^{1}(O)}^{2}={\int}_{O}(v\cdot v+\nabla v:\nabla v)dO$ |
| ${H}^{1}$ semi-norm | ${\mid v\mid }_{{H}^{1}(O)}^{2}={\int}_{O}\nabla v:\nabla vdO$ |
| Energy norm | ${\parallel v\parallel }_{e}^{2}={\int}_{O}\sigma (v):\epsilon (v)dO$ |

The equilibrium equation is multiplied by a sufficiently regular function $v\in V$ then integrated over the domain $\Omega$ :

$$
0={\int}_{\Omega}{\sigma}_{ij,j}{v}_{i}d\Omega +{\int}_{\Omega}{f}_{i}{v}_{i}d\Omega
$$

$$
0={\int}_{\Omega}{({\sigma}_{ij}{v}_{i})}_{,j}d\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}{\sigma}_{ij}{v}_{i,j}d\Omega +{\int}_{\Omega}{f}_{i}{v}_{i}d\Omega
$$

Application of Green's formula gives:

$$
{\int}_{\Omega}{({\sigma}_{ij}{v}_{i})}_{,j}d\Omega ={\int}_{\partial \Omega }{\sigma}_{ij}{n}_{j}{v}_{i}d\Gamma
$$

The symmetry properties of the stress tensor imply:

$$
{\sigma}_{ij}{v}_{i,j}={\sigma}_{ij}{\varepsilon}_{ij}(v)
$$

Which makes it possible to write:

$$
{\int}_{\partial \Omega }{\sigma}_{ij}{n}_{j}{v}_{i}\mathrm{d\Gamma }-{\int}_{\Omega}{a}_{\mathrm{ijkh}}{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{kh}}(u){\varepsilon}_{ij}(v)d\Omega +{\int}_{\Omega}{f}_{i}{v}_{i}d\Omega =0
$$

The first term of the right-hand side is zero on ${\Gamma}_{U}$ and there remains:

$$
{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{F}}{F}_{i}{v}_{i}\mathrm{d\Gamma }-{\int}_{\Omega}{a}_{\mathrm{ijkh}}{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{kh}}(u){\varepsilon}_{ij}(v)d\Omega +{\int}_{\Omega}{f}_{i}{v}_{i}d\Omega =0
$$

The variational formulation of the problem $({\text{P}}_{1})$ is written:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\text{Trouver}u\in V\text{tel que}& \\ a(u,v)=l(v)& \forall v\in V\end{array}
$$

with:

$$
a(u,v)={\int}_{\Omega}{a}_{\mathrm{ijkh}}{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{kh}}(u){\varepsilon}_{ij}(v)d\Omega
$$

$$
l(v)={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{F}}{F}_{i}{v}_{i}d\Gamma +{\int}_{\Omega}{f}_{i}{v}_{i}d\Omega
$$

### 1.3 Discretisation by finite elements

Let ${\Omega}^{h}$ be a partition of $\Omega$ into $N$ elements. A finite-element space ${V}^{h}\subset V$ is constructed from continuous functions, piecewise polynomial, and of degree ${p}_{E}$ on each element $E$ . The discretisation of the problem $({\text{P}}_{1})$ by the Galerkin method yields the problem $({\text{P}}_{h})$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\text{Trouver}{u}^{h}\in {V}^{h}\text{tel que}& \\ a({u}^{h},{v}^{h})=l({v}^{h})& \forall {v}^{h}\in {V}^{h}\end{array}
$$

### 1.4 Discretisation error

The numerical error $e$ of the finite-element approximation ${u}^{h}$ is defined by:

$$
e=u-{u}^{h}
$$

Since $u$ and ${u}^{h}$ are elements of $V$ , then $e\in V$ . The error satisfies the residual equation obtained by replacing $u$ by ${u}^{h}+e$ in the variational formulation of the problem $({\text{P}}_{h})$ :

$$
\begin{array}{cc}a(e,v)=l(v)-a({u}^{h},v)={R}_{h}^{u}(v)& \forall v\in V\end{array}
$$

Since the bilinear form is positive definite, the residual ${R}_{h}^{u}$ is a bounded linear form on $V$ which belongs to its dual ${V}^{'}$ . The norm of the residual is such that:

$$
{\parallel {R}_{h}^{u}\parallel }_{{V}^{'}}=\underset{v\in V\setminus \left\lbrace 0\right\rbrace }{\sup}\frac{\mid {R}_{h}^{u}(v)\mid }{{\parallel v\parallel }_{e}}
$$

The residual measures the failure to satisfy certain properties of the equations of the problem and characterises the equilibrium defects and therefore the discretisation error. It is easy to deduce from the equation a new property. The function ${v}^{h}$ is used as a test function:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{R}_{h}^{u}({v}^{h})=l({v}^{h})-a({u}^{h},{v}^{h})& \forall {v}^{h}\in {V}^{h}\end{array}
$$

Then, by using the discretised form of the variational formulation, the orthogonality property or Galerkin property is obtained:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}a(e,{v}^{h})={R}_{h}^{u}({v}^{h})=0& \forall {v}^{h}\in {V}^{h}\end{array}
$$

The equation indicates that the error is the solution of an elasticity problem whose force loading is the equilibrium residual. This problem is as complicated to solve and as costly as the initial problem. Thus, instead of determining the error itself, it is estimates that will be sought.

### 1.5 A priori error estimation

As its name indicates, the *a priori*  estimation is carried out before the finite-element analysis because it does not involve the finite-element approximation ${u}^{h}$ . Thus functional analysis and numerical analysis make it possible, in many cases and under certain regularity hypotheses, to obtain *a priori*  estimation results; that is to say the prediction of the asymptotic rate of convergence of the finite-element error. For further details, the works of Ciarlet  or of Strang and Fix  may be consulted.

A function $\tilde{\eta}(h,d,u)$ is an *a priori*  error estimate if:

$$
\parallel u-{u}^{h}\parallel \le \tilde{\eta}(h,d,u)
$$

where $\parallel \cdot \parallel$ is a norm on the displacement fields, $h$ the size of the elements, $d$ a set of data of the problem and $u$ the exact solution.

Convergence of the finite-element method used may be obtained if:

$$
\underset{h\to 0}{\lim}\tilde{\eta}(h,d,u)=0
$$

and the rate of convergence, if there exists a real number $q>0$ such that:

$$
\parallel u-{u}^{h}\parallel \le {h}^{q}\stackrel{ˉ}{\eta}(d,u)
$$

The *a priori*  error estimators do not make it possible to quantify the errors on the finite-element solution because they involve the exact solution, which is generally not known. On the other hand, these estimates are valid only in the asymptotic regime, a regime which is difficult to reach for 3D analyses. For formulations using elements of isoparametric type , in the case of a regular solution and for the energy norm of the error, $q=p$ , where $p$ is the interpolation degree of the finite elements. On the other hand, in the case of a singular solution, $q=\min(p,\alpha )$ where $\alpha$ is the order of the singularity of the solution of the problem (for a crack, for example, $\alpha =\frac{1}{2}$ ).

## 2 A posteriori error estimators

### 2.1 Definition of a posteriori estimators

In *a posteriori*  estimation, the objective is not to find an estimate of the error function $e$ but to determine an estimate of a measure of the error. Thus the estimators that have been developed over the last thirty years provide a reliable and accurate estimate of the error in global norms, such as the ${H}^{1}$ norms, the ${L}^{2}$ norms or else energy norms, by post-processing of the finite-element solution. This choice of global norms is imposed by the bilinear form. When the form is positive definite and symmetric, as is the case here, this induces a scalar product for which the associated norm is the energy norm; it is thus natural to estimate the error in this norm.

The basic principle of this type of estimation is to use the approximate solution in order to estimate the discretisation error. In contrast to *a priori*  estimation, *a posteriori*  estimates can be made only once the approximate solution has been computed.

A function $\eta (h,{u}^{h},d)$ is an *a posteriori*  error estimate if:

$$
\parallel u-{u}^{h}\parallel \le \eta (h,{u}^{h},d)
$$

where $\parallel \cdot \parallel$ is a norm on the displacement fields, $h$ the size of the elements, $d$ a set of data of the problem and ${u}^{h}$ the approximate solution. Moreover, if $\eta (h,d,{u}^{h})$ can be localised in the form:

$$
\eta (h,{u}^{h},d)={(\sum_{E}{\eta}_{E}{({u}^{h},d)}^{2})}^{\frac{1}{2}}
$$

then the quantities ${\eta}_{E}({u}^{h},d)$ , elementary contributions of the estimate of the global error $\eta (h,{u}^{h},d)$ , are called local error indicators. They provide a basis for mesh adaptation.

Numerous estimators exist and may be classified into three categories:

- Error estimators based on equilibrium defects, stemming from the work of Babuška and Rheinboldt ;
- Error indicators constructed from smoothed stresses, stemming from the work of Zienkiewicz and Zhu ;
- Error measures based on the concept of error in constitutive relation, stemming from the work of Ladevèze .

These various estimators, today well mastered, will be presented in a more or less detailed manner. A more detailed review may be found in  to .

### 2.2 Quality of the estimators

When an estimator is defined, one must consider the criteria and the means that make it possible to judge the performance of this estimator. In general, one must seek to construct an estimator whose asymptotic behaviour (when the size $h$ of the elements tends to zero) follows that of the error. This behaviour is expressed by the existence of two constants ${C}_{1}$ and ${C}_{2}$ , depending on the data of the problem and on the discretisation but not on the size of the elements, satisfying the following relation (Ladevèze & Pelle, 2004):

$$
{C}_{1}\eta \le \parallel e\parallel \le {C}_{2}\eta
$$

where $\eta$ represents the estimate of the measure $\parallel e\parallel$ of the error $e$ on the domain under consideration. Thus, in order to judge the performance of the estimators, there exist various intrinsic criteria.

#### 2.2.1 Effectivity index

The effectivity index $\gamma$ is defined as the ratio between the error computed by an estimator ${e}_{\text{estimée}}$ and the true error ${e}_{\text{vraie}}$ :

$$
\gamma =\frac{{e}_{\text{estimée}}}{{e}_{\text{vraie}}}
$$

Unless an analytical solution is available, the true error is computed as the difference between a solution obtained on a very fine mesh (*overkill*  solution) and the solution obtained on a given mesh. An effectivity index close to unity characterises a good estimator. If this property is reached when the size of the elements tends to zero, the estimator is said to be asymptotically exact. Nevertheless, the estimate must be sufficiently accurate ($\gamma$  close to 1) for fairly coarse meshes in order to be usable for the discretisations current in engineering. Finally, it is desirable to overestimate the error ($\gamma >1$ ) in order to be usable as a stopping criterion in an adaptive process.

Systematic studies have been carried out by Strouboulis and Haque  and by Babuška  on various test cases having an analytical solution or for which a reference solution can be determined on a very fine mesh. Overall, it emerges that the quality of an estimator depends on the topology of the mesh, on the regularity of the solution and on the regularity (flatness) of the elements.

The single global index $\gamma$ does not make it possible to account for the way in which the local error behaves: it may be that, in a few cases, the estimator is satisfactory for this criterion without succeeding in locating the zones where the error in energy is significant .

#### 2.2.2 Robustness index

In order to free oneself from the local behaviour of the estimator, another criterion has been defined by Babuška and Rodriguez . For a given triangulation $T$ , the local effectivity index ${\gamma}_{\omega}$ relative to the subdomain $\omega$ is defined by:

$$
{\gamma}_{\omega}=\frac{{e}_{\text{estimée}}^{\omega}}{{e}_{\text{vraie}}^{\omega}}
$$

Babuška and Strouboulis have shown that one could access numerically the range of asymptotic variation of ${\gamma}_{\omega}$ :

$$
0<{C}_{\inf}^{\omega}\le {\gamma}_{\omega}\le {C}_{\sup}^{\omega}<\infty
$$

The deviation of ${\gamma}_{\omega}$ from 1 is measured by the index ${R}_{\omega}$ defined by:

$$
{R}_{\omega}=\max\left\lbrace ∣1-{C}_{\sup}^{\omega}∣+∣1-{C}_{\inf}^{\omega}∣;∣1-\frac{1}{{C}_{\sup}^{\omega}}∣+∣1-\frac{1}{{C}_{\inf}^{\omega}}∣\right\rbrace
$$

The robustness index $R$ is the largest ${R}_{\omega}$ obtained by varying the position of the cell $\omega$ of its neighbourhood on the meshes belonging to a class of triangulation. This approach makes it possible to highlight the discretisation error on a subdomain but also the distortion of the mesh elements and the regularity of the solution. Although it possesses mathematical bases, this technique seems complicated to implement for the complex meshes used in engineering.

## 3 Local error

### 3.1 Pollution error

#### 3.1.1 Definition of the pollution error

In order to define the pollution error, we shall provide a pragmatic definition by trying to understand what its behaviour may be and therefore its influence in the course of an adaptive process. To this end, we shall study the convergence of the error in the energy norm in various situations:

- Convergence of the global error for a global uniform refinement;
- Convergence of the error in the zone of interest for a global refinement;
- Convergence of the global error for a local refinement;
- Convergence of the error in the zone of interest for a local refinement.

The problem to be solved is the problem whose geometry and mesh are given by figure r4.10.06-fig-3-1. The imposed force is unitary, Young's modulus is equal to $E=1.0$ Pa and Poisson's ratio is $\nu =0.3$ . Finally the modelling employed is a plane-stress modelling. A series of finite-element approximations will be determined on a succession of linear meshes obtained by uniform refinement of the structure or by uniform refinement of the zone of interest $\omega$ . The error in the energy norm is then computed (therefore without estimation) from a solution obtained on a very fine mesh (*overkill*  solution) which will be our reference solution. Finally the absolute error is normalised by the energy norm of the solution on the structure or on the zone of interest, according to the case. The results are presented in figure r4.10.06-fig-3-2.

For a global refinement, the global error converges “a little less quickly” than $p=1$ ; by dividing the size of the mesh cells by 10, the error is not divided by 10 (even by dividing the size by 16 the error is not divided by 10) (square markers). However, this rate of convergence is reached only asymptotically. It should be noted that the curve shown seems to approach the theoretical convergence from the last segment onwards (for mesh-cell sizes between 1/8 and 1/16). It is therefore only the first part of a convergence curve that is plotted, when convergence in $p=1$ has not yet been reached (pre-asymptotic regime).

When only the zone of interest is refined, the solution is not improved in the rest of the structure. The global error remains practically unchanged (round markers). This indicates that the error in the zone of interest contributes very little to the global error.

![Figure](/figures/r4.10.06/1000000000000FA300000B5597038852CF01B620.png)

*Highlighting of the pollution error: convergence of the error*

On the other hand, by studying the curves relating to the error in the zone of interest, it is visible that in order to improve the solution in this zone in a significant manner it is not sufficient to refine only this zone. Indeed the two curves (diamond markers and triangular markers) show a decrease of the local error but that obtained by the local refinement shows a slower decrease: the two curves move apart. It therefore seems that the error in the rest of the structure also has an influence on the quality of the solution in the zone of interest. Which means that the sum of the local contributions of the global estimators on the subdomain $\omega$ does not represent exactly the local error. It therefore seems that the true local error is composed of two parts: a local component and a complementary component coming from the rest of the structure. The consequence of this is that by refining only the zone of interest, the error in the rest of the structure does not decrease (i.e. the complementary component remains constant). One may then imagine that the curve representing the local error with a local refinement (diamond markers) converges towards a non-zero value if the refinement is continued: this non-zero value will represent the complementary component (the local component tending towards zero). The value of the complementary component is then called the “pollution error”.

A more formal definition may be given. The pollution error was introduced by Babuška *et al.*  , , . They presume that the discretisation error in a subdomain $\omega$ of the structure is composed:

- of a local error on $\omega$ , disregarding the rest of the structure;
- of a pollution, or transported, error, coming from the discretisation in the rest of the structure.

The fundamental relation between the global error $e$ and the solution ${\Phi}_{E}$ of the local error problem (solution coming from the *Equilibrated Residual Method*  for example) is recalled:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}a(e,v)=\sum_{E\in \Omega }{a}_{E}({\Phi}_{E},v)& \forall v\in V\end{array}
$$

A local error ${e}_{\omega}^{\text{loc}}$ on a subdomain $\omega$ may be defined by:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}a({e}_{\omega}^{\text{loc}},v)=\sum_{E\subset \omega }{a}_{E}({\varphi}_{E},v)& \forall v\in V\end{array}
$$

Since the local error indicator function ${\varphi}_{E}$ depends only on the local residuals of the finite-element approximation evaluated on the patch of elements $E\text{*}$ , the local error ${e}^{\text{loc}}$  depends only on the local residuals on the patch $\tilde{\omega}$ , made up of the elements of $\omega$ and of the neighbouring elements. The complementary component of the true error is the pollution error ${e}_{\omega}^{\text{pol}}$ :

$$
\begin{array}{cc}a({e}_{\omega}^{\text{pol}},v)=\sum_{E\not\subset \omega }{a}_{E}({\varphi}_{E},v)& \forall v\in V\end{array}
$$

This part of the true error comes from the residuals located outside the subdomain and is transported as far as the subdomain, thus affecting the accuracy. The error ${e}_{\omega}$ in a subdomain $\omega$ is therefore the sum of the two components defined previously:

$$
{e}_{\omega}={e}_{\omega}^{\text{loc}}+{e}_{\omega}^{\text{pol}}
$$

In view of figure r4.10.06-fig-3-2 and of the previous definition, it becomes obvious that control of the pollution error is essential. Indeed, outside the zone of interest, when the mesh is coarse, it may be an important source of pollution error. Consequently, a local refinement on a subdomain must be counterbalanced by an adequate refinement outside the subdomain in order to control the pollution error. We shall see in what follows that the error estimators in a quantity of interest make it possible to ensure this balance between local refinement and refinement outside the subdomain.

#### 3.1.2 Estimation of the pollution error

In order to estimate the pollution error, various methods have been proposed. The method proposed by Babuška (Babuška *et al.* , 1995) is linked to the Green functions which describe the interaction between different points of the domain. The pollution error is estimated with the approximation error of the Green function associated with certain points. Another method has been used by Huerta and Díez (Huerta & Díez, 2000) and consists in finding an approximation of ${e}_{\omega}^{\text{pol}}$ by solution of the problem defining this error. Other work may also be cited (Ainsworth, 1999), (Oden & Feng, 1996). The industrial interest of the evaluation of this error being limited, we shall not develop these estimation methods any further. The numerous works on the computation of error bounds render the estimation of the pollution error unnecessary because it is included in the bounds. Moreover, for the estimation of a local error, as we have already indicated, the estimators in a quantity of interest make it possible to free oneself from the pollution error thanks to the dual problem.

### 3.2 Error in a quantity of interest

#### 3.2.1 Quantities of interest

Error estimates in the energy norm are too abstract to provide the users of analysis codes with sufficient information on the various aspects of the solution sought. It is then more useful to estimate an error in terms of quantities of interest; quantities having a physical meaning (an average of displacements in a subregion of the physical domain or an average of stresses on an interface or else, in the case of vibrations, one of the eigenfrequencies). Mathematically, they are characterised by linear or non-linear functionals in the space of functions to which the solutions belong.

Several examples are given: the average of a component of the displacement on a domain $\omega$ :

$$
Q(v)=\frac{1}{\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}{v}_{x}d\Omega
$$

the average of a component of the stress tensor on a domain $\omega$ :

$$
Q(v)=\frac{1}{\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}{\sigma}_{xx}d\Omega
$$

One then seeks to compute the quantity ${\varepsilon}^{Q}$ which may be expressed in the following form:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{Q}=Q(u)-Q({u}^{h})
$$

In the case where $Q$ is linear, the following equality is satisfied:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{Q}=Q(u-{u}^{h})=Q(e)
$$

In what follows, only the case of linear functionals will be considered because this corresponds to the framework of the simplified approach presented in what follows. In general, the treatment used for non-linear quantities (such as the Von Mises stress or the $J$ integral in fracture mechanics) is linearisation  and .

#### 3.2.2 Dual problem and fundamental relation

The error in a quantity of interest may be estimated by using duality properties of the operators involved in the weak formulation of the reference problem. From a mechanical point of view, this corresponds to the existence of a solution function of a dual problem, called the influence function, representing the influence of the forces of the reference problem on the total energy. The influence function is then used as a weighting of the equilibrium residuals of the reference problem.

A simplified approach is presented, inasmuch as the framework is limited to linear elasticity and to linear quantities of interest. This is expressed by the fact that $Q(\cdot )$ is a linear form and that $a(\cdot ,\cdot )$ is a bilinear form. This approach makes it possible to understand better the necessity of using the error on the influence function and not only the influence function.

The relation between the error in a quantity of interest and the residual may be expressed by the introduction of an unknown function $\omega$ , called the influence function:

$$
Q(e)=\omega ({R}_{h}^{u})
$$

Moreover, it is shown in  that the previous relation becomes:

$$
Q(e)={R}_{h}^{u}(\omega )
$$

By combining the previous relation with the residual equation:

$$
a(e,v)={R}_{h}^{u}(v)
$$

a relation between the error on the reference problem, the influence function and the error in a quantity of interest is obtained:

$$
a(e,\omega )=Q(e)
$$

This equality is necessarily satisfied when $\omega \in V$ is the solution of the following problem:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\text{Trouver}\omega \in V\text{tel}\text{que}& \\ a(v,\omega )=Q(v)& \forall v\in V\end{array}
$$

This problem is called the dual or adjoint problem. One notes that if $\omega$ could be computed exactly, then $Q(u)$ could be determined directly from the data of the problem because:

$$
Q(u)=a(u,\omega )=l(\omega )
$$

But unfortunately, it is as difficult to determine the influence function $\omega$ as to determine $u$ in the primal problem. This is why a finite-element approximation ${\omega}^{h}\in {V}^{h}$ of $\omega$ which satisfies

$$
\begin{array}{cc}a({v}^{h},{\omega}^{h})=Q({v}^{h})& \forall {v}^{h}\in {V}^{h}\end{array}
$$

is introduced. One also notes that by substituting $e$ for ${v}^{h}$ in the dual problem, one obtains:

$$
Q(e)=a(e,\omega )
$$

Replacing $\omega$ by ${\omega}^{h}$ in the previous relation is not sufficient to obtain an estimate of $Q(e)$ because $a(e,{\omega}^{h})=0$ (orthogonality property). This is why one must evaluate the error on the influence function denoted $\varepsilon =\omega -{\omega}^{h}$ . By introducing this error into the previous relation, a representation of the error is obtained:

$$
Q(e)=a(e,\varepsilon )
$$

In the case of non-linear problems and/or of non-linear quantities of interest, the foregoing does not apply. In this case, the primal problem is written:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\text{Trouver}u\in V\text{tel que}& \\ A(u;v)=L(v)& \forall v\in V\end{array}
$$

where $A(\cdot ;\cdot )$ is a semi-linear form such that the arguments which follow the semicolon are linear and $L(\cdot )$ a continuous linear form on $V$ .

The approach consists in considering the primal problem as a constrained minimisation problem , :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\mathrm{Trouver}u\in V\mathrm{tel}\mathrm{que}\\ Q(u)=\underset{z\in S}{\inf}Q(z)\\ \text{où}\\ S=\left\lbrace z\in V;A(z;v)=L(v),\forall v\in \in V\right\rbrace \end{array}
$$

In order to solve the minimisation problem, the pair $(u,\omega )\in V\times V$ is sought such that it satisfies the primal problem and the dual problem:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}A(u;\tilde{v})=L(\tilde{v})& \forall \tilde{v}\in V\\ {A}^{'}(u;v,\omega )={Q}^{'}(u;v)& \forall v\in V\end{array}
$$

Finally, the relation between the error in a quantity of interest ${\varepsilon}^{Q}$ and the residual is given by:

$$
{\epsilon}^{Q}=Q(u)-Q({u}^{h})=R({u}^{h};\omega )+\mathrm{\Delta A}+\mathrm{\Delta Q}
$$

where $\Delta A$ and $\Delta Q$ involve the second and third derivatives of $A$ and $Q$ .

#### 3.2.3 Estimation of the error in a quantity of interest

Starting from the fundamental relation , various techniques have been developed in order to estimate the error in a quantity of interest.

The simplest and most direct technique is to use the global estimators in the energy norm. By applying the Cauchy–Schwarz theorem, a relation between the error in a quantity of interest and the energy norms of the errors on the primal problem and the dual problem can be found:

$$
\mid Q(e)\mid \le {\parallel e\parallel }_{e}{\parallel \varepsilon \parallel }_{e}
$$

This expression thus allows us to estimate easily the error in a quantity of interest by using any error estimator in the energy norm, this without any additional development in an analysis code that has global estimators. Although this estimate is the simplest, it is also the coarsest: it does not take into account the local interactions between the primal error and the dual error (schematically this means that an error on the displacement does not result in an error in a quantity of interest). This can lead to heavy overestimates of the error in a quantity of interest.

A somewhat different approach, called *Dual Weighted Residual*  (DWR) , consists in considering the exact expression of the error in a quantity of interest:

$$
Q(e)=a(e,\omega -{\omega}^{h})={R}_{h}^{u}(\omega -{\omega}^{h})
$$

The dual problem is solved by using a high-order approximation method. Another method, less costly, consists in using high-order interpolation functions defined on elementary patches of the domain. This approach leads to a guaranteed upper bound of the error.

An “exact bounds” approach ( *exact bounds approach* )  uses the properties of the estimation based on the dual analysis. Thus, thanks to a displacement formulation of the finite-element method, a lower bound of the exact strain energy is obtained; thanks to a stress formulation, an upper bound of the exact complementary energy is obtained. From a practical point of view, this method is very costly since it implies solving the primal problem and the dual problem twice each.

Other methods exist for estimating the error in a quantity of interest but, from an industrial point of view, only the approach using global estimators in the energy norm seems to be convincing. It does not require any additional development in order to access an indication of the error and it is also much less costly.

#### 3.2.4 Bounds of the error

##### 3.2.4.1 A new expression for the error

Although very simple to obtain, the relation produces a considerable overestimate of the error. Various techniques have been proposed in order to remedy this problem. Prudhomme and Oden employ the parallelogram relation for problems with symmetric bilinear forms, giving access to an upper bound and a lower bound of the error , ].

If $e$ and $\varepsilon$ are the errors of the finite-element approximations, respectively of the solutions of the primal and dual problems, then:

$$
Q(e)=a(e,\varepsilon )=\frac{1}{4}{\parallel e+\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{\parallel e-\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}
$$

One introduces the scalar factor $s\in R$

$$
Q(e)=a(e,\varepsilon )=\frac{1}{4}{\parallel se+{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{\parallel se-{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}
$$

The scalar $s$ is chosen so that the quantities ${\parallel se\parallel }_{e}$ and ${\parallel {s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}$ have the same amplitude, which implies choosing $s=\sqrt{{\parallel \varepsilon \parallel }_{e}/{\parallel e\parallel }_{e}}$ .

Indeed $e$ and $\varepsilon$ may be of very different orders of magnitude. The scalar $s$ aims to “normalise” the two terms, in order to avoid taking the difference of two very close terms because this can induce a significant numerical error.

Formally, any existing error estimator may be used to evaluate the energy norms of the parallelogram relation; the “fairer” this estimate is, the better the quality of the error estimator in a quantity of interest will be.

##### 3.2.4.2 Construction of the bounds

The global estimators ${\eta}_{\inf}^{+}$ , ${\eta}_{\sup}^{+}$ , ${\eta}_{\inf}^{-}$ and ${\eta}_{\sup}^{-}$ are defined such that:

$$
{\eta}_{\inf}^{+}\le {\parallel se+{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}\le {\eta}_{\sup}^{+}
$$

$$
{\eta}_{\inf}^{-}\le {\parallel se-{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}\le {\eta}_{\sup}^{-}
$$

By rewriting the two previous inequalities thus:

$$
\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\inf}^{+})}^{2}\le \frac{1}{4}{\parallel se+{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}\le \frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\sup}^{+})}^{2}
$$

$$
\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\inf}^{-})}^{2}\le \frac{1}{4}{\parallel se-{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}\le \frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\sup}^{-})}^{2}
$$

and by adding them:

$$
\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\inf}^{+})}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\sup}^{-})}^{2}\le \frac{1}{4}{\parallel se+{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{\parallel se-{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}\le \frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\sup}^{+})}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\inf}^{-})}^{2}
$$

a bracketing of the error in a quantity of interest is obtained.

Then the bounds ${\eta}_{\inf}^{Q}$ , ${\eta}_{\sup}^{Q}$ are introduced such that:

$$
{\eta}_{\inf}^{Q}=\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\inf}^{+})}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\sup}^{-})}^{2}
$$

$$
{\eta}_{\sup}^{Q}=\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\sup}^{+})}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\inf}^{-})}^{2}
$$

The error in a quantity of interest $Q(e)$ is bounded by ${\eta}_{\inf}^{Q}$ and ${\eta}_{\sup}^{Q}$ :

$$
{\eta}_{\inf}^{Q}\le Q(e)\le {\eta}_{\sup}^{Q}
$$

It is also possible to use the estimates ${\eta}_{\text{eei}}^{Q}$  and ${\eta}_{\mathrm{ees}}^{Q}$ defined such that:

$$
{\eta}_{\text{eei}}^{Q}=\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\inf}^{+})}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\inf}^{-})}^{2}
$$

$$
{\eta}_{\text{ees}}^{Q}=\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\sup}^{+})}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{\sup}^{-})}^{2}
$$

as an estimate of the error in a quantity of interest $Q(e)$ .

Finally, a last estimate may be obtained, by averaging the bounds ${\eta}_{\inf}^{Q}$ and ${\eta}_{\sup}^{Q}$ or the estimates ${\eta}_{\mathrm{eei}}^{Q}$ and ${\eta}_{\mathrm{ees}}^{Q}$ , denoted ${\eta}_{\mathrm{moy}}^{Q}$ :

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}{\eta}_{\mathrm{moy}}^{Q}& =& \frac{1}{2}({\eta}_{\mathrm{eei}}^{Q}+{\eta}_{\mathrm{ees}}^{Q})\\ & =& \frac{1}{2}({\eta}_{\inf}^{Q}+{\eta}_{\sup}^{Q})\\ & =& \frac{1}{8}({({\eta}_{\inf}^{+})}^{2}+{({\eta}_{\sup}^{+})}^{2})-\frac{1}{8}({({\eta}_{\inf}^{-})}^{2}+{({\eta}_{\sup}^{-})}^{2})\end{array}
$$

The following figure summarises the various possible combinations of the error estimate in the energy norm in order to obtain an estimate or bounds of the error in a quantity of interest. This shows that if one has bounds for an estimator in the energy norm then one has bounds for the error in a quantity of interest.

##### 3.2.4.3 Estimates of the bounds of the error

In the previous chapter, it was shown that bounds of the global error were accessible by using implicit residual estimators. The main lines are given here in order to obtain bounds of the error in a quantity of interest by using the implicit estimators. It should be noted that this is only an example and that other estimation methods may provide bounds of the error in a quantity of interest.

In order to obtain the upper bounds defined previously, the functions ${\psi}_{E}^{u}$ and ${\psi}_{E}^{\omega}$ , corresponding respectively to the errors $e$ and $\varepsilon$ , are computed by introducing the local problems relative to the primal and dual problems. The following estimates are then obtained:

$$
{\eta}_{\sup}^{+}={\parallel s{\psi}_{E}^{u}+{s}^{-1}{\psi}_{E}^{\omega}\parallel }_{e}
$$

For the estimation of the lower bounds, one must proceed in a manner analogous to that for the estimation of the upper bounds by constructing functions continuous at the interfaces from ${\psi}_{E}^{u}$ and ${\psi}_{E}^{\omega}$ :

$$
{\eta}_{\inf}^{+}=\frac{∣(s{R}_{h}^{u}+{s}^{-1}{R}_{h}^{\omega})(s{\chi }^{u}+{s}^{-1}{\chi }^{\omega})∣}{{\parallel s{\chi }_{E}^{u}+{s}^{-1}{\chi }_{E}^{\omega}\parallel }_{e}}
$$

## 4 Estimation of the error in a quantity of interest

### 4.1 Error estimator in a quantity of interest based on explicit residuals

The error in a quantity of interest may be estimated from any estimator of the global error. Thus, an error estimator in a quantity of interest is constructed from the parallelogram relation and from the error estimator based on the explicit residuals $\eta$ whose expression on each element ${\eta}_{E}$ is [R4.10.02](doc:r4.10.02):

$$
{\eta}_{E}^{2}={h}_{E}^{2}{\parallel {r}_{E}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(E)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\Gamma \not\subset {\Gamma}_{F}}{l}_{\Gamma}{\parallel {t}_{\Gamma}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(\Gamma )}^{2}+\sum_{\Gamma \subset {\Gamma}_{F}}{l}_{\Gamma}{\parallel {t}_{\Gamma}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(\Gamma )}^{2}
$$

The use of the expression given by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

$$
\mid Q(e)\mid \le {\parallel e\parallel }_{e}{\parallel \varepsilon \parallel }_{e}
$$

with the explicit residual-type estimator easily gives an estimate of the error in a quantity of interest by estimating ${\parallel e\parallel }_{e}$ and ${\parallel \varepsilon \parallel }_{e}$ but this estimate is coarse.

The parallelogram relation given by:

$$
Q(e)=a(e,\varepsilon )=\frac{1}{4}{\parallel e+\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{\parallel e-\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}=\frac{1}{4}{\parallel se+{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{\parallel se-{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}
$$

with $s=\sqrt{{\parallel \varepsilon \parallel }_{e}/{\parallel e\parallel }_{e}}$ (cf. §3.2.4), provides a more accurate estimate because it allows a genuine coupling between the primal error and the dual error. The use of the error estimate of each of the two problems is not possible; the norm of a sum is not equal to the sum of the norms:

$$
{\parallel e+\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}=a(e+\varepsilon ,e+\varepsilon )={\parallel e\parallel }_{e}^{2}+{\parallel \varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}+2a(e,\varepsilon )
$$

$$
{\parallel e-\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}=a(e-\varepsilon ,e-\varepsilon )={\parallel e\parallel }_{e}^{2}+{\parallel \varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}-2a(e,\varepsilon )
$$

This is why one must go back to the definition of the residual error estimator in order to evaluate directly ${\parallel se+{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}$ and ${\parallel se-{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}$ . The solution error $e=u-{u}^{h}$ being the solution of an elasticity problem whose force data are the equilibrium residuals, it is therefore a matter of formulating the problems of which $(se+{s}^{-1}\epsilon )$ and $(se-{s}^{-1}\varepsilon )$ are solutions.

The primal problem and the dual problem are given by the following relations:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}a(u,v)=l(v)& \forall v\in V\\ a(v,\varepsilon )=Q(v)& \forall v\in V\end{array}
$$

The residuals associated with these problems are given respectively by:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{R}_{h}^{u}(v)=l(v)-a({u}^{h},v)\\ {R}_{h}^{\omega}(v)=Q(v)-a(v,{\omega}^{h})\end{array}
$$

By replacing $u$ by ${u}^{h}+e$ and $\omega$ by ${\omega}^{h}+\varepsilon$ and by using the symmetry of $a(\cdot ,\cdot )$ , one obtains the equations governing the errors $e$ and $\varepsilon$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}a(e,v)={R}_{h}^{u}(v)\\ a(\varepsilon ,v)={R}_{h}^{\omega}(v)\end{array}
$$

By linear combination, one has the following two problems:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}a(se+{s}^{-1}\varepsilon ,v)=(s{R}_{h}^{u}+{s}^{-1}{R}_{h}^{\omega})(v)\\ a(se-{s}^{-1}\varepsilon ,v)=(s{R}_{h}^{u}-{s}^{-1}{R}_{h}^{\omega})(v)\end{array}
$$

Thus the numerical error $(se+{s}^{-1}\epsilon )$ (and $(se-{s}^{-1}\varepsilon )$ ) is generated by the total residual $(s{R}_{h}^{u}+{s}^{-1}{R}_{h}^{\omega})$ (respectively $(s{R}_{h}^{u}-{s}^{-1}{R}_{h}^{\omega})$ ). In a manner analogous to the residual estimator, there exists a constant ${C}^{+}$ (and ${C}^{-}$ ), independent of the size of the elements, such that:

$$
{\parallel se+{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}\le {C}^{+}\left[\sum_{E}{h}_{E}^{2}{\parallel s{r}_{E}^{u}+{s}^{-1}{r}_{E}^{\omega}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(E)}^{2}+\sum_{\Gamma \not\subset {\Gamma}_{U}}{l}_{\Gamma}{\parallel s{t}_{\Gamma}^{u}+{s}^{-1}{t}_{\Gamma}^{\omega}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(\Gamma )}^{2}\right]
$$

$$
{\parallel se-{s}^{-1}\varepsilon \parallel }_{e}^{2}\le {C}^{-}\left[\sum_{E}{h}_{E}^{2}{\parallel s{r}_{E}^{u}-{s}^{-1}{r}_{E}^{\omega}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(E)}^{2}+\sum_{\Gamma \not\subset {\Gamma}_{U}}{l}_{\Gamma}{\parallel s{t}_{\Gamma}^{u}-{s}^{-1}{t}_{\Gamma}^{\omega}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(\Gamma )}^{2}\right]
$$

Finally the error estimator in a quantity of interest ${}^{\mathrm{REQI}}\eta _{E}$ is defined such that:

$$
{}^{\mathrm{REQI}}\eta _{E}=\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{E}^{+})}^{2}-\frac{1}{4}{({\eta}_{E}^{-})}^{2}
$$

with ${\eta}_{E}^{+}$ and ${\eta}_{E}^{-}$ defined by:

$$
({\eta}_{E}^{+}{)}^{2}={c}^{+}\left[{h}_{E}^{2}{\parallel s{r}_{E}^{u}+{s}^{-1}{r}_{E}^{\omega}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(E)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\Gamma \not\subset {\Gamma}_{F}}{l}_{\Gamma}{\parallel s{t}_{\Gamma}^{u}+{s}^{-1}{t}_{\Gamma}^{\omega}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(\Gamma )}^{2}+\sum_{\Gamma \subset {\Gamma}_{F}}{l}_{\Gamma}{\parallel s{t}_{\Gamma}^{u}+{s}^{-1}{t}_{\Gamma}^{\omega}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(\Gamma )}^{2}\right]
$$

$$
({\eta}_{E}^{-}{)}^{2}={c}^{-}\left[{h}_{E}^{2}{\parallel s{r}_{E}^{u}-{s}^{-1}{r}_{E}^{\omega}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(E)}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\Gamma \not\subset {\Gamma}_{F}}{l}_{\Gamma}{\parallel s{t}_{\Gamma}^{u}-{s}^{-1}{t}_{\Gamma}^{\omega}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(\Gamma )}^{2}+\sum_{\Gamma \subset {\Gamma}_{F}}{l}_{\Gamma}{\parallel s{t}_{\Gamma}^{u}-{s}^{-1}{t}_{\Gamma}^{\omega}\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(\Gamma )}^{2}\right]
$$

In view of the definitions of the bounds of the error in a quantity of interest given in part 1, the estimator that we have just defined does not bound the error but gives an approximate value of it:

$$
Q(e)\approx \sum_{E}{}^{\mathrm{REQI}}\eta _{E}
$$

The estimator thus defined is therefore more akin to an error indicator making it possible to guide a meshing strategy. This indicator is programmed in *Code_Aster* , it is available for all types of 2D and 3D elements (except pyramids) as well as for linear and quadratic elements.

### 4.2 Estimator in a quantity of interest based on field smoothing

An error estimator is constructed directly from the smoothing techniques [R4.10.01](doc:r4.10.01) and from the fundamental relation between the error in a quantity of interest, the error on the solution of the primal problem and that on the solution of the dual problem:

$$
Q(e)=a(e,\varepsilon )={\int}_{\Omega}\mathrm{Tr}\left[{}^{T}(\sigma (u)-\sigma ({u}^{h})){K}^{-1}(\sigma (\omega )-\sigma ({\omega}^{h}))\right]d\Omega
$$

The approach consists in constructing, from the finite-element solutions ${u}^{h}$ and ${\omega}^{h}$ , continuous stress fields $\tilde{\sigma}({u}^{h})$ and $\tilde{\sigma}({\omega}^{h})$ as close as possible to the exact solutions. This smoothing operation is carried out by the various techniques described in the first part. An upper bound is thus obtained:

$$
a(e,\varepsilon )\le C{\int}_{\Omega}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\tilde{\varepsilon}(u)-\varepsilon ({u}^{h}))K(\tilde{\varepsilon}(\omega )-\varepsilon ({\omega}^{h}))\right]d\Omega
$$

The local contributions of the error in a quantity of interest may be defined thus:

$$
{}^{\mathrm{ZZQI}}\eta _{E}={\int}_{E}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\tilde{\varepsilon}(u)-\varepsilon ({u}^{h}))K(\tilde{\varepsilon}(\omega )-\varepsilon ({\omega}^{h}))\right]dE
$$

The estimator is the sum over the elements of the contributions and is written:

$$
{}^{\mathrm{ZZQI}}\eta =\sum_{E}{}^{\mathrm{ZZQI}}\eta _{E}
$$

This estimator is very simple to implement and requires practically no development for a code provided with smoothing techniques. This indicator is programmed in *Code_Aster* , it is available for all types of 2D elements.

### 4.3 Estimator in a quantity of interest based on hierarchical bases

The starting point for constructing this estimator is again the fundamental relation of the error in a quantity of interest:

$$
Q(e)=a(e,\varepsilon )
$$

The solution of the primal problem and of the dual problem with “richer” meshes (in practice these are uniformly refined meshes) makes it possible to obtain the improved solution $u\text{*}$ of the primal problem and the improved solution of the dual problem $\omega \text{*}$ .

By considering that the approximations $u\text{*}$ and $\omega \text{*}$ are sufficiently accurate with respect to the original approximations ${u}^{h}$ and ${\omega}^{h}$ , the error in a quantity of interest is then given by:

$$
Q(e)=a(e,\varepsilon )=a(u-{u}^{h},\omega -{\omega}^{h})\approx a(u\text{*}-{u}^{h},\omega \text{*}-{\omega}^{h})=Q(e\text{*})
$$

A local error estimator in a quantity of interest may be defined thus:

$$
{}^{\mathrm{BHQI}}\eta _{E}={\int}_{E}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(\varepsilon (u\text{*})-\varepsilon ({u}^{h}))K(\varepsilon (\omega \text{*})-\varepsilon ({\omega}^{h}))\right]dE
$$

The global estimator is the sum over the elements and is written thus:

$$
{}^{\mathrm{BHQI}}\eta =\sum_{E}{}^{\mathrm{BHQI}}\eta _{E}
$$

This estimator may be of limited interest for an industrial use in view of the computational cost incurred. Indeed the primal problem and the dual problem will each have to be solved twice: once on the initial mesh and once on a finer mesh, therefore with an additional computational cost. However, this estimator can make the error in a quantity of interest available for classes of problems for which no estimator is available or implemented (eigenvalue problems or dynamics problems in *Code_Aster*  for example).

## 5 Formulation of the quantity of interest

As we have already seen in the first part, instead of evaluating a measure of the error such as the energy norm for example, it is more useful to estimate the error on a quantity of interest having a physical meaning. This quantity of interest ( *quantity of interest*  or *output* ) may be represented by a linear functional $Q(\cdot )$ defined on the space of test functions. The aim of estimation in a quantity of interest is therefore to ensure the quality of $Q({u}^{h})$ by estimating the quantity:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{Q}=Q(u-{u}^{h})=Q(e)
$$

In order to obtain the estimate, one must use the functional $Q(\cdot )$ as the loading of the dual problem defined by:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\text{Trouver}\omega \in V\text{tel que}& \\ a(v,\omega )=Q(v)& \forall v\in V\end{array}
$$

Numerous physical quantities are used in design and must be controlled. These quantities (grandeurs) may be, for example, the average of a component of the displacement or the average of a component of the stresses on a subdomain (zone of interest):

$$
Q(v)=\frac{1}{\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}{v}_{x}d\Omega
$$

$$
Q(v)=\frac{1}{\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}{\sigma}_{xx}d\Omega
$$

These quantities are not usable directly as the loading of the dual problem. They must be expressed in an adequate manner in order to recover the loading to be imposed on the local problem. The general form of these quantities is the following:

$$
Q(v)={\int}_{\Omega}{f}^{Q}\cdot vd\Omega +{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{F}}{F}^{Q}\cdot v\mathrm{d\Gamma }-a({u}^{Q},v)
$$

where ${f}^{Q}$ , ${F}^{Q}$ and ${u}^{Q}$ are given. In what follows, we shall see that simple quantities of interest are deduced directly from this general form in order to constitute particular cases of it in which one or more terms are zero.

### 5.1 Quantities (grandeurs) associated with the displacements

#### 5.1.1 Displacement component in a domain

The first quantity linked to the displacement is the mean value of a component of the displacement in a subdomain $\omega$ :

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{Q}^{\text{depl}}(v)=\frac{1}{\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}{v}_{i}d\Omega & i=1,2\text{ou}3\end{array}
$$

${Q}^{\text{depl}}(v)$ may be written:

$$
{\int}_{\Omega}{f}^{Q}\cdot vd\Omega
$$

which corresponds to the particular case where ${F}^{Q}=0$ and ${u}^{Q}=0$ and where ${f}^{Q}$ is a constant vector on $\omega$ and zero elsewhere.

The dual problem to be solved is written in the following manner:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{\int}_{\Omega}{\sigma}_{ij}(v){\varepsilon}_{ij}(\omega )d\Omega ={\int}_{\Omega}{f}_{i}^{Q}{v}_{i}d\Omega & \forall v\in V\end{array}
$$

By using the symmetry property of the stress tensor and by differentiating, one obtains:

$$
{\int}_{\Omega}{({\sigma}_{ij}(\omega ){v}_{i})}_{,j}d\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}{\sigma}_{ij,j}(\omega ){v}_{i}d\Omega ={\int}_{\Omega}{f}_{i}^{Q}{v}_{i}d\Omega
$$

Finally, by using Stokes' formula, one has:

$$
{\int}_{\Omega}({\sigma}_{ij,j}(\omega )+{f}^{Q}){v}_{i}d\Omega -{\int}_{\partial \Omega }{\sigma}_{ij}(\omega ){n}_{j}{v}_{i}d\Omega =0
$$

This allows us to write the local dual problem:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cccc}{\sigma}_{ij,j}(\omega )+{f}_{i}^{Q}& =& 0& \mathrm{dans}\Omega \\ {u}_{i}(x)& =& 0& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{U}\\ {\sigma}_{ij}(\omega ){n}_{j}(x)& =& 0& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{F}\end{array}
$$

One thus notes that imposing ${Q}^{\mathrm{depl}}(v)$ as the loading of the dual problem amounts to imposing a constant body loading on $\omega$ . It should be noted that if one seeks the error on the complete displacement vector, one will have to solve as many dual problems as there are components (2 in dimension 2 and 3 in dimension 3).

#### 5.1.2 Displacement component on a boundary

Another useful quantity linked to the displacement is the mean value of a component of the displacement on a boundary $\gamma \subset {\Gamma}_{F}$ :

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{Q}^{\mathrm{depl}}(v)=\frac{1}{\mid \gamma \mid }{\int}_{\gamma}{v}_{i}d\Gamma & i=1,2\mathrm{ou}3\end{array}
$$

${Q}^{\mathrm{depl}}(v)$ may be written:

$$
{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{F}}{F}^{Q}\cdot vd\Gamma
$$

which corresponds to the particular case where ${f}^{Q}=0$ and ${u}^{Q}=0$ and where ${F}^{Q}$ is a constant vector on $\gamma$ and zero elsewhere and with, for each ${v}_{i}$ , ${F}_{i}^{Q}=\frac{1}{\mid \gamma \mid }$ ; the other components of ${F}^{Q}$ being zero.

The local dual problem is written in the following manner:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cccc}{\sigma}_{ij,j}(\omega )& =& 0& \mathrm{dans}\Omega \\ {u}_{i}(x)& =& 0& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{U}\\ {\sigma}_{ij}(\omega ){n}_{j}(x)& =& {F}_{i}^{Q}& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{F}\end{array}
$$

Imposing ${Q}^{\mathrm{depl}}(v)$ as the loading of the dual problem amounts to imposing a line loading in dimension 2 and a surface loading in dimension 3 restricted to ${\Gamma}_{F}$ .

#### 5.1.3 Displacement normal to a boundary

Finally the last quantity linked to the displacement is the average of the displacement normal to a boundary $\gamma \subset {\Gamma}_{F}$ :

$$
{Q}^{\mathrm{depl}}(v)=\frac{1}{\mid \gamma \mid }{\int}_{\gamma}{v}_{n}d\Gamma
$$

${Q}^{\mathrm{depl}}(v)$ may be written:

$$
{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{F}}\mathrm{pn}\cdot vd\Gamma
$$

where $n$ is the normal vector to the boundary $\gamma$ and $p=\frac{1}{\mid \gamma \mid }$ on $\gamma$ and zero elsewhere.

The local dual problem is written in the following manner:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cccc}{\sigma}_{ij,j}(\omega )& =& 0& \mathrm{dans}\Omega \\ {u}_{i}(x)& =& 0& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{U}\\ {\sigma}_{ij}(\omega ){n}_{j}(x)& =& {\mathrm{pn}}_{i}& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{F}\end{array}
$$

Imposing ${Q}^{\mathrm{depl}}(v)$ as the loading of the dual problem amounts to imposing a line pressure loading in dimension 2 and a surface pressure loading in dimension 3.

### 5.2 Quantities (grandeurs) associated with the stresses

#### 5.2.1 Stress component in a domain

We now turn to the estimation of the error on the mean value of a component of the stresses in a subdomain $\omega$ :

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{Q}^{sigma}(v)=\frac{1}{\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}{\sigma}_{ij}d\Omega & i,j=1,2\mathrm{ou}3\end{array}
$$

In order to determine ${Q}^{sigma}(v)$ , it is sufficient to determine:

$$
-a({u}^{Q},v)={\int}_{\Omega}\sigma (v):\Sigma ({u}^{Q})d\Omega
$$

which corresponds to the particular case where ${f}^{Q}=0$ and ${F}^{Q}=0$ and where $\Sigma ({u}^{Q})$ is a constant symmetric operator on $\omega$ and zero elsewhere.

To this end, it is sufficient to write that the stress component is equal to the trace of the product between operators one of which is non-zero in $\omega$ :

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{Q}^{sigma}(v)=\frac{1}{\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}{\sigma}_{ij}d\Omega ={\int}_{\omega}\sigma :\Sigma d\Omega +\underset{=0}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\int}_{\Omega \setminus \omega }\sigma :\Sigma d\Omega }}={\int}_{\Omega}\sigma :\Sigma d\Omega & i,j=1,2\mathrm{ou}3\end{array}
$$

with, for each ${\sigma}_{ij}$ , ${\Sigma}_{ij}=\frac{1}{\mid \omega \mid }$ for $i=j$ ; ${\Sigma}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2\mid \omega \mid }$ for $i\ne j$ .

The dual problem to be solved is written in the following manner:

$$
{\int}_{\Omega}{\sigma}_{i}(v){\varepsilon}_{ij}(\omega )d\Omega ={\int}_{\Omega}{\sigma}_{ij}(v){\Sigma}_{ij}d\Omega
$$

By differentiating, one obtains:

$$
{\int}_{\Omega}{(({\sigma}_{ij}(\omega )-{a}_{ijkl}{\Sigma}_{kl}){v}_{i})}_{,j}d\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}{({\sigma}_{ij}(\omega )-{a}_{ijkl}{\Sigma}_{kl})}_{,j}{v}_{i}d\Omega =0
$$

Finally, by using Stokes' formula, one has:

$$
{\int}_{\Omega}{(({\sigma}_{ij}(\omega )-{a}_{ijkl}{\Sigma}_{kl}){v}_{i})}_{,j}d\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}({\sigma}_{ij}(\omega )-{a}_{ijkl}{\Sigma}_{kl}){n}_{j}{v}_{i}d\Omega =0
$$

This allows us to write the local dual problem:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cccc}{({\sigma}_{ij}(\omega )-{a}_{ijkl}{\Sigma}_{kl})}_{,j}& =& 0& \mathrm{dans}\Omega \\ {u}_{i}(x)& =& 0& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{U}\\ ({\sigma}_{ij}(\omega )-{a}_{ijkl}{\Sigma}_{kl}){n}_{j}(x)& =& 0& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{F}\end{array}
$$

One thus notes that imposing ${Q}^{sigma}(v)$ as the loading of the dual problem amounts to imposing an initial strain $\Sigma$ . It should be noted that if one seeks the error on the complete stress tensor, one will have to solve as many dual problems as there are components (3 in dimension 2 and 6 in dimension 3).

#### 5.2.2 Normal stress on a boundary

It may be of interest to consider the error on the normal stress on a boundary $\gamma$ :

$$
\begin{array}{c}{Q}^{sigma}(v)={\int}_{\gamma}n\cdot \sigma (v)nd\Omega \end{array}
$$

This quantity does not present itself in the general form. The introduction of an auxiliary function is necessary . This function $\chi$ is defined such that $\chi {\mid}_{\gamma}=1$ and equal to zero on the rest of $\partial \Omega$ .

If ${n}^{\gamma}=n{\mid}_{\gamma}$ , the function $\chi$ satisfies the following equation:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}a(u,\chi {n}^{\gamma})& ={\int}_{\Omega}{\sigma}_{ij}(u){\varepsilon}_{ij}(\chi {n}^{\gamma})d\Omega \\ & ={\int}_{\partial \Omega }{\sigma}_{ij}(u){n}_{j}{(\chi {n}^{\gamma})}_{i}d\Omega -\underset{=0}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\int}_{\Omega}{({\sigma}_{ij}(u))}_{,j}{(\chi {n}^{\gamma})}_{i}d\Omega }}\\ & ={\int}_{\gamma}{\sigma}_{ij}(u){n}_{j}{(\chi {n}^{\gamma})}_{i}d\Omega \end{array}
$$

Which shows that the quantity of interest may be written in an equivalent manner:

$$
{Q}^{sigma}(u)={\int}_{\gamma}n\cdot \sigma (u)nd\Omega =a(u,\chi {n}^{\gamma})=:\tilde{Q}(u)
$$

But since the previous equality is true only for a particular $u$ , it should be noted that:

$$
{Q}^{sigma}(v)={\int}_{\gamma}n\cdot \sigma (v)nd\Omega \ne a(v,\chi {n}^{\gamma})=:\tilde{Q}(v)
$$

For greater ease, the quantity $\tilde{Q}(v)$ will be used in place of the quantity ${Q}^{sigma}(v)$ . Indeed it corresponds to using ${u}^{Q}=-\chi {n}^{\gamma}$ .

The quantity of interest to be used is therefore written in the following manner:

$$
\tilde{Q}(v)=-a({u}^{Q},v)={\int}_{\Omega}\sigma (v):\Sigma (-\chi {n}^{\gamma})d\Omega
$$

which corresponds to the particular case where ${f}^{Q}=0$ and ${F}^{Q}=0$ .

In a manner analogous to the case of the average of the stresses, the local dual problem is written:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cccc}{({\sigma}_{ij}(\omega )-{a}_{ijkl}{\Sigma}_{kl})}_{,j}& =& 0& \mathrm{dans}\Omega \\ {u}_{i}(x)& =& 0& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{U}\\ ({\sigma}_{ij}(\omega )-{a}_{ijkl}{\Sigma}_{kl}){n}_{j}(x)& =& 0& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{F}\end{array}
$$

Imposing $\tilde{Q}(v)$ as the loading of the dual problem amounts to imposing an initial strain field computed from the displacement field ${u}^{Q}=-\chi {n}^{\gamma}$ . This quantity of interest has not been implemented in *Code_Aster* .

#### 5.2.3 Von Mises stress in a domain

The Von Mises stress is a mechanical quantity very useful in terms of sizing for the engineer. The difficulty in estimating the error on this quantity lies in the fact that its expression is not linear with respect to the displacement and is therefore unusable with the technique presented. The difficulty is removed thanks to a linearisation of the expression and under certain hypotheses .

The stress tensor $\sigma$ may be decomposed into the sum of a deviatoric tensor ${\sigma}^{d}$ (whose trace is zero) and of a spherical tensor ${\sigma}^{s}$ (whose off-diagonal terms are zero):

$$
\sigma ={\sigma}^{d}+{\sigma}^{s}
$$

where ${\sigma}^{d}$ and ${\sigma}^{s}$ are defined by:

$$
{\sigma}^{d}=\sigma -\frac{1}{3}\mathrm{tr}(\sigma )I
{\sigma}^{s}=\frac{1}{3}\mathrm{tr}(\sigma )I
$$

The Von Mises stress, denoted ${\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}$ , is defined by:

$$
{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}{\sigma}^{d}:{\sigma}^{d}}
$$

In linear elasticity, the stress depends linearly on the displacement, which allows us to write:

$$
{\sigma}^{d}(u)={\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h})+{\sigma}^{d}(e)
$$

By using this in the definition of the Von Mises stress, one obtains:

$$
{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}(u)=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}}(({\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h})+{\sigma}^{d}(e)):({\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h})+{\sigma}^{d}(e)))
$$

The previous expression is rewritten in order to bring out a function whose series expansion is known:

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}(u)& =& \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}({\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h}):{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h})+2{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h}):{\sigma}^{d}(e)+{\sigma}^{d}(e):{\sigma}^{d}(e))}\\ & =& {\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h})\sqrt{1+\frac{3}{2}\frac{2{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h}):{\sigma}^{d}(e)+{\sigma}^{d}(e):{\sigma}^{d}(e)}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}^{2}({u}^{h})}}\end{array}
$$

The series expansion to order 1 when $e$ tends to zero makes it possible to obtain the following approximation:

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}(u)& \approx & {\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h})(1+\frac{3}{2}\frac{{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h}):{\sigma}^{d}(e)}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}^{2}({u}^{h})})\\ & \approx & {\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h})+\frac{3}{2}\frac{{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h}):{\sigma}^{d}(e)}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h})}\end{array}
$$

Finally, an approximation of the error on the Von Mises stress is determined:

$$
{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}(u)-{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h})\approx \frac{3}{2}\frac{{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h}):{\sigma}^{d}(e)}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h})}
$$

Thus, in order to estimate the error on the average of the Von Mises stress in a domain:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{Q}=\frac{1}{\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}({\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}(u)-{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h}))d\Omega
$$

the following functional may be used:

$$
{Q}^{\mathrm{vm}}(v)=\frac{3}{2\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}\frac{{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h}):{\sigma}^{d}(v)}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h})}d\Omega
$$

As for the stress component on a domain, in order to determine ${Q}^{\mathrm{vm}}(v)$ , it is sufficient to determine:

$$
-a({u}^{Q},v)={\int}_{\Omega}\Sigma ({u}^{Q}):\sigma (v)d\Omega
$$

Which corresponds to the particular case where ${f}^{Q}=0$ and ${F}^{Q}=0$ and where $\Sigma ({u}^{Q})$ is a symmetric operator constant per element on $\omega$ and zero elsewhere. The fact that ${\sigma}^{d}(v)$ and not $\sigma (v)$ appears in the equation requires rewriting the functional ${Q}^{\mathrm{vm}}(v)$ in order to bring out $\sigma (v)$ :

$$
{Q}^{\mathrm{vm}}(v)=\frac{3}{2\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}\frac{{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h}):(\sigma (v)-{\sigma}^{s}(v))}{{\sigma}_{\text{vm}}({u}^{h})}d\Omega
$$

$$
{Q}^{\mathrm{vm}}(v)=\frac{3}{2\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}\frac{{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h}):\sigma (v)-{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h}):{\sigma}^{s}(v)}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h})}d\Omega
$$

Finally, the double contraction between a deviatoric tensor and a spherical tensor being zero, the functional is written:

$$
{Q}^{\mathrm{vm}}(v)=\frac{1}{\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}(\frac{3}{2{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h})}{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h})):\sigma (v)d\Omega
$$

with $\Sigma ({u}^{Q})=\frac{3}{2{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{Q})}{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{Q})$ and ${u}^{Q}={u}^{h}$ .

In a manner analogous to the case of the average of the stresses, the local dual problem is written:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cccc}{({\sigma}_{ij}(\omega )-{a}_{ijkl}{\Sigma}_{kl})}_{,j}& =& 0& \mathrm{dans}\Omega \\ {u}_{i}(x)& =& 0& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{U}\\ ({\sigma}_{ij}(\omega )-{a}_{ijkl}{\Sigma}_{kl}){n}_{j}(x)& =& 0& \mathrm{sur}{\Gamma}_{F}\end{array}
$$

Imposing ${Q}^{\mathrm{vm}}(v)$ as the loading of the dual problem amounts to imposing an initial strain field.

### 5.3 Quantities (grandeurs) linked to fracture mechanics

#### 5.3.1 The stress intensity factors

A method for computing ${K}_{I}$ , ${K}_{\mathrm{II}}$ and ${K}_{\mathrm{III}}$ in 2D (plane and axisymmetric) and 3D is recalled; it is based on the extrapolation of the displacement jumps on the crack faces. This method is applicable only to the case of plane cracks, in homogeneous and isotropic materials ; it is used in the command POST_K1_K2_K3.

The stress intensity factors are identified from the displacement jump $\left[U\right]$ by a least-squares method on a segment of length ${r}_{\max}$ . This is expressed by the following minimisation problem:

$$
K\text{minimise}F(k)=\frac{1}{2}{\int}_{0}^{{r}_{\max}}{(C\left[U(r)\right]-k\sqrt{r})}^{2}\mathrm{dr}
$$

In order to solve this problem, a value of $k$ is sought such that the derivative of $F$ vanishes:

$$
F'(K)={\int}_{0}^{{r}_{\max}}\sqrt{r}(K\sqrt{r}-C\left[U(r)\right])\mathrm{dr}=0
$$

Separation of the integral gives:

$$
{\int}_{0}^{{r}_{\max}}rK\mathrm{dr}={\int}_{0}^{{r}_{\max}}\sqrt{r}C\left[U(r)\right]\mathrm{dr}
$$

$$
K{\left[\frac{{r}^{2}}{2}\right]}_{0}^{{r}_{\max}}={\int}_{0}^{{r}_{\max}}\sqrt{r}C\left[U(r)\right]\mathrm{dr}
$$

The explicit formula for computing $K$ is deduced directly:

$$
K=\frac{\mathrm{2C}}{{r}_{\max}^{2}}{\int}_{0}^{{r}_{\max}}\sqrt{r}\left[U(r)\right]\mathrm{dr}
$$

By carrying out the integration by the trapezoidal method, the previous relation becomes:

$$
K=\frac{C}{{r}_{\max}^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\left[U\right]}_{i-1}\sqrt{{r}_{i-1}}+{\left[U\right]}_{i}\sqrt{{r}_{i}})({r}_{i}-{r}_{i-1})
$$

where $N$ is the number of nodes on the segment $\left[0,{r}_{\max}\right]$ . One notes that $K$ is a linear form of the displacement field for a fixed ${r}_{\max}$ , typically this corresponds to 4 or 5 elements.

In order to express the stress intensity factors in terms of loading for the dual problem, we shall use the equation .

By bringing out the displacement components on the upper face ${U}_{i}^{\sup}(r)$ and the displacement components on the lower face ${U}_{i}^{\inf}(r)$ , one obtains:

$$
{K}_{i}=\frac{2C}{{r}_{\max}^{2}}{\int}_{\Gamma}({U}_{i}^{\sup}(r)-{U}_{i}^{\inf}(r))\sqrt{r}\mathrm{dr}
$$

The contour $\Gamma$ is made up of the two crack faces; $\Gamma$ is the upper face and ${\Gamma}^{\inf}$ is the lower face. This makes it possible to separate the previous integral into two parts:

$$
{K}_{i}=\frac{\mathrm{2C}}{{r}_{\max}^{2}}\left[{\int}_{{\Gamma}^{\sup}}{U}_{i}(r)\sqrt{r}\mathrm{dr}-{\int}_{{\Gamma}^{\inf}}{U}_{i}(r)\sqrt{r}\text{dr}\right]
$$

In order to bring out the test function $v$ , the displacement components are written as the scalar product of two vectors:

$$
{K}_{i}=\frac{\mathrm{2C}}{{r}_{\max}^{2}}\left[{\int}_{{\Gamma}^{\sup}}v\cdot {g}_{i}(r)\sqrt{r}\mathrm{dr}-{\int}_{{\Gamma}^{\inf}}v\cdot {g}_{i}(r)\sqrt{r}\mathrm{dr}\right]
$$

With $i=I,\mathrm{II},\mathrm{III}$ ; ${g}_{I}$ the vector normal to the crack plane, ${g}_{\mathrm{II}}$ the vector normal to the crack front and ${g}_{\mathrm{III}}$ the vector tangent to the crack front. This therefore means that the expression of the quantity of interest is valid in dimension 3 and thus allows the error estimation of the stress intensity factors for problems in dimension 3.

Finally, by grouping all the terms, the following expression is obtained:

$$
Q(v)={K}_{i}(v)={\int}_{{\Gamma}^{\sup}}{f}^{\sup}\cdot v\mathrm{dr}+{\int}_{{\Gamma}^{\inf}}{f}^{\inf}\cdot v\mathrm{dr}
$$

with:

$$
{f}^{\sup}=\frac{2C}{{r}_{\max}^{2}}{g}_{i}(r)\sqrt{r}
$$

This expression corresponds to a line loading in dimension 2 (surface loading in dimension 3) to be imposed on a partition of the crack faces starting from the tip and of length ${r}_{\max}$ . The choice of the radius ${r}_{\max}$ must be made so that it lies in the singular zone and contains any Barsoum elements.

When the quantities of interest are not linear or when their expression does not make it possible to express them easily in terms of loading, a particular treatment is necessary. For non-linear quantities, the theory does not apply; this is why they must be linearised. The Von Mises stress is the typical example of this. Linearisation leads us to the following formula:

$$
{Q}^{\mathrm{vm}}(v)=\frac{1}{\mid \omega \mid }{\int}_{\omega}(\frac{3}{2{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h})}{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h})):\sigma (v)d\Omega
$$

Thus one approximates the Von Mises quantity of interest by another linear quantity. The loading to be imposed in *Code_Aster*  is a compound stress field:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{3K}}{2}\frac{{\sigma}^{d}({u}^{h})}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{vm}}({u}^{h})}
$$

When the definition of the quantity is not suited to the expression of a loading, one must, here too, use a treatment that will be specific to each quantity of interest. For our applications, the stress intensity factors (SIFs) are interesting quantities (grandeurs). By using a method based on the extrapolation of the displacement jumps on the crack faces, one can express the SIFs in the following manner, with $C=1$ in plane stress and $C=1-{\nu}^{2}$ in plane strain:

$$
K=\frac{\mathrm{2C}}{{r}_{\max}^{2}}{\int}_{0}^{{r}_{\max}}\sqrt{r}\left[U(r)\right]\mathrm{dr}
$$

Which makes it possible to obtain the following expression:

$$
Q(v)={K}_{i}(v)={\int}_{{\Gamma}^{\sup}}{f}^{\sup}\cdot v\mathrm{dr}+{\int}_{{\Gamma}^{\inf}}{f}^{\inf}\cdot v\mathrm{dr}
$$

with:

$$
{f}^{\sup}=\frac{\mathrm{2C}}{{r}_{\max}^{2}}g(r)\sqrt{r}
$$

with $g(r)$ a vector which orients the crack.

In *Code_Aster* , this vector is known under the name VECT_K1. One thus sees that a force must be imposed on each of the crack faces (:math:``  and ${f}^{\inf}$ ). The following table takes up these two results.
