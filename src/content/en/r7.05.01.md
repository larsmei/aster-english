# r7.05.01 Mechanical stability criteria

**Summary:**

This document presents the various mechanical stability criteria available in *Code_Aster*. They may be classified into two categories:

- The stability criterion associated with conservative systems, which appears as the generalisation of the Euler criterion based on the analysis of the updated global stiffness matrix.
- The stability criterion associated with dissipative systems, which must take into account the irreversibility constraints linked to the dissipation of energy.

These criteria are used to distinguish, in quasi-static problems, the unstable numerical solutions arising from the equilibrium analysis carried out in the finite-element method (first derivative of the energy zero but second derivative negative) from the physical, stable solutions, for which the second derivative of the energy is positive.

The criteria presented in this document are directly transposable to the framework of dynamics, but since they take into account neither the mass matrix nor the damping matrix, one cannot speak of a dynamic stability criterion in the classical sense (for example, of damping becoming negative or zero).

These criteria are called within the operators STAT_NON_LINE and DYNA_NON_LINE, so that they can be evaluated at each step of the incremental quasi-static or non-linear dynamic transient solution.

## 1 Stability of a conservative system

### 1.1 Definition of the stability of a conservative system

The equilibrium position of a conservative system is said to be stable if it is invariant under the effect of small perturbations. This amounts to verifying that the solution obtained does indeed lie at a local minimum of the potential energy or, put otherwise, to verifying that the Hill functional [bib19] is convex. Mathematically, this is expressed by the verification of the positivity of the second derivative of the potential energy **Φ** at the equilibrium point **u**. Consider a small perturbation of the equilibrium state ν, complying with the boundary conditions imposed on the structure. One must always recover the inequality:

$\phi (u)\le \phi (u+v)$                                     eq 1.1‑1

The principal cause of loss of stability for a conservative mechanical structure is buckling. One is therefore interested more particularly, in what follows, in this notion.

*Note:*  Other definitions of stability exist. One finds in particular stability in the sense of Rice, a criterion defined in 1975, which amounts to verifying the strict positivity of the eigenvalues of the acoustic tensor. However, one may have instability in the sense of Hill before instability in the sense of Rice. The criterion in the sense of Hill is therefore more conservative. That is why it is the one that is preferred.

### 1.2 General notion of buckling

Buckling is an instability phenomenon [bib6]. Its appearance may be observed in particular on slender members of low flexural stiffness. Beyond a certain loading level, the structure undergoes a significant change of configuration (which may manifest itself by the sudden appearance of undulations, for example). Two types of buckling are distinguished: buckling by bifurcation and buckling by limit point ([bib1], [bib7], [bib8]). In order to describe the behaviour of these two types of buckling, one considers a structure whose parameter $\mu$ is characteristic of the loading and whose parameter $\delta$ is characteristic of the displacement.

Between the point $O$ and the point $A$ , the structure admits a single family of curves $(\mu ,\delta )$ . It may, for example, be a matter of classical linear elasticity or of elastoplasticity, where, if the problem is well posed ( *cf.* [§ 1.3 ]), one has the classical result of existence and uniqueness of the solution. On the other hand, beyond the point $A$ , several families of curves are solutions of the equilibrium problem. This loss of uniqueness is accompanied by an instability of the initial branch (called the fundamental branch). The secondary branch may be stable (curve $\mathit{AB}$ ) or unstable (curve $\mathit{AB}’$ ). The load beyond which there is bifurcation is called the critical load ${\mu}_{\mathit{cr}}$ . Buckling by bifurcation is characterised by the fact that the mode (or buckling direction), which initiates the secondary branch, does not generate any additional work in the applied loading: the buckling mode being orthogonal to it.

An example of buckling by bifurcation with instability of the secondary branch is found in the case of a circular cylindrical shell under axial compression [bib10]. Examples of buckling by bifurcation with stability of the secondary branch are found in elastic beams in axial compression, circular rings in radial compression and rectangular plates in longitudinal compression.

On the figures [ Figure 1.2-b ] and [ Figure 1.2-c ], which illustrate buckling by limit point, the structure admits only a single family $(\mu ,\delta )$ of solution of the equilibrium equations. At the point $A$ , there is a loss of stability of the solution with a total loss of rigidity in the case of figure [ Figure 1.2-b ] and with a snap-through phenomenon in the case of figure [ Figure 1.2-c ] (the solution becomes stable again after a discontinuity of displacement; case of a spherical cap under external pressure). The point $A$ is then called the limit point. The problem therefore amounts in every case to seeking the load from which the fundamental equilibrium branch becomes unstable or of uncertain stability. This generally mobilises large displacements. One may finally have the case of collapse by plastic flow, which is akin to the limit point [ Figure 1.2-b ].

*Code_Aster* allows the search for linear buckling modes, described as the Euler method. It is sufficient to solve a generalised eigenvalue problem (by means of the operator CALC_MODES with the keyword TYPE_RESU=’MODE_FLAMB’). The two matrix arguments of the generalised problem are the stiffness matrix and the geometric stiffness matrix, arising from a prior linear elastic analysis (operator MECA_STATIQUE).

In all cases where the non-linearities, whether geometric or behavioural, cannot be neglected, the Euler approach is no longer valid.

We therefore propose an *ad hoc* criterion, which may be considered as a generalisation of the Euler criterion on an updated configuration. This criterion is constructed on the assembled tangent stiffness matrix, which is computed in the Newton-type algorithm in order to solve the non-linear quasi-static problems (operator STAT_NON_LINE) or the non-linear transient dynamic problems (operator DYNA_NON_LINE). This criterion, in the non-linear regime, makes it possible to treat rigorously the non-linear elastic constitutive relations. On the other hand, the laws that present a dissipative aspect are treated rigorously only if the loading, at every point of the structure, follows a monotonic evolution (this corresponds to the Hill assumption [bib4]).

The objective of this chapter is to introduce the general formalism of structural analysis adapted to the non-linear mechanical problem that we wish to address.

To begin with, we shall therefore briefly recall the setting in equations of a typical structural-analysis problem. For simplicity, we place ourselves, at least at the beginning, within the framework of small perturbations.

### 1.4 Stability study

The purpose of this chapter is to present the methods making it possible to determine the stability of the non-linear quasi-static equilibrium of a structure in a conservative system. To begin with, we are interested only in the detection of instability, or more exactly in the loss of uniqueness of the solution [bib6]. Among the recent survey works, one may cite [bib9] or [bib7] and [bib8], which present very complete papers on the non-linear stability analysis of structures.

The computation of the post-critical solution will not be addressed.

In order to carry out the stability analysis, we introduce an initial reference configuration ${\Omega}_{S0}$ , a current configuration ${\Omega}_{S}$ and a perturbed configuration ${\Omega}_{S1}$ :

| --- |

**Figure** 1.4-a **: Definition of the various configurations**

Let $u$ be the displacement field of the points of the structure. The behaviour is assumed, for the moment, to be linear isotropic elastic. The structure subjected to the imposed displacements and forces will deform and become the structure identified by the current configuration ${\Omega}_{S}$ . We seek to determine an equilibrium state characterised by the displacement field between the initial configuration ${\Omega}_{S0}$ and the current configuration ${\Omega}_{S}$ , as well as a Cauchy stress field, denoted $\sigma$ , or a second Piola–Kirchhoff stress field, denoted $\pi$ :

> $\pi =\detF.{F}^{-1}{\pi}_{I}$ with $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}F=\nabla u+I\text{: tenseur gradient de la transformation}\\ \detF=\frac{{\rho}_{0}}{\rho}\\ {\pi}_{I}\text{: tenseur de Piola - Kirchhoff I}\end{array}$ $\Rightarrow \pi =\frac{{\rho}_{0}}{\rho}.{F}^{-1}.\sigma .{F}^{-T}$ **eq 1.4‑1**

In this expression, the ratio between the initial density ${\rho}_{0}$ and the current density $\rho$ appears.

The following step is the prediction of the stability of this equilibrium.

To this end, we shall seek a criterion making it possible to determine whether there exists a single displacement field equilibrating the applied forces. We shall assume that the forces increase progressively and we shall seek to find from what moment there exist two configurations ${\Omega}_{S}$ and ${\Omega}_{S1}$ which satisfy the equations of the problem: we seek a bifurcation point, that is to say a loss of uniqueness of the solution. This time instant will be described as the buckling time instant.

#### 1.4.1 Writing of the geometrically non-linear elastic problem

The solution $\mathrm{u},\pi$ of the structural problem without thermal effects satisfies ([bib1], [bib7], [bib2]):

- Constraint equations:

> $u{\mid}_{\partial {\Omega}_{S0}}={U}_{d}$                                     eq 1.4.1-1

- Elastic constitutive relation:

$\pi ={\varphi}_{,\varepsilon }(\varepsilon )$                                 eq 1.4.1-2

with $\varepsilon$ which is the strain tensor. If one assumes a linear elastic behaviour:

> $\pi =\mathrm{C}\varepsilon$                                 eq 1.4.1-3

- Equilibrium equations:

> $\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\rho \underline{\gamma}=\nabla .\pi +{\mathrm{f}}_{d}\text{avec}\gamma =\frac{{\text{d}}^{2}\mathrm{u}}{\text{d}{t}^{2}}\\ \mathrm{F}.\pi .{\mathrm{n}}_{0}{\mid}_{\partial {\Omega}_{S0}}={\mathrm{F}}_{d}\end{array}$                 eq 1.4.1-4

The associated strain tensor is the Green–Lagrange tensor (referenced to the initial configuration):

> $\begin{array}{}\varepsilon (u)=\frac{1}{2}({F}^{T}F-I)\text{avec}F=\nabla u+I\\ \Rightarrow \varepsilon (u)={\varepsilon}^{L}(u)+\frac{1}{2}{\varepsilon}^{Q}(u,u)\end{array}$                 eq 1.4.1-5

with: $\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{\varepsilon}^{L}(\mathrm{u})=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla \mathrm{u}+{\nabla}^{T}\mathrm{u})\text{: partie linéaire}\\ {\varepsilon}^{Q}(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{u})={\nabla}^{T}\mathrm{u}.\nabla \mathrm{u}\text{: partie quadratique}\end{array}$ eq 1.4.1-6

We may now write the Principle of Virtual Powers in geometrically non-linear elasticity and in quasi-statics:

${p}^{\mathrm{intal}int}-{p}^{\mathrm{ext}}=0,\forall {u}^{\text{*}}\text{C A 0}$

$\text{Avec :}\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{p}^{int}=\underset{{\Omega}_{S0}}{\int}\mathrm{Tr}(\pi {\varepsilon}^{\text{*}})d\Omega =\underset{{\Omega}_{S0}}{\int}\mathrm{Tr}\left[({\varepsilon}^{L}(u)+\frac{1}{2}{\varepsilon}^{Q}(u,u))C({\varepsilon}^{L}({u}^{\text{*}})+{\varepsilon}^{Q}(u,{u}^{\text{*}}))\right]d\Omega \\ {p}^{\text{ext}}=\underset{\partial {\Omega}_{S0}}{\int}{F}_{d}.{u}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{dS}+\underset{{\Omega}_{S0}}{\int}{f}_{d}.{u}^{\text{*}}d\Omega \end{array}$     eq 1.4.1-7

With a view to obtaining a discretised formulation, one may rewrite the strain tensor:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\varepsilon (u)=\left[{B}^{L}+\frac{1}{2}{B}^{\mathrm{NL}}(u)\right].u\\ \pi =C\varepsilon (u)\text{avec}\pi \text{qui est le tenseur de Piola - Kirchhoff II}\end{array}$         eq 1.4.1-8

The power of the internal forces becomes:

${\mathrm{P}}^{int}=\underset{{\Omega}_{SO}}{\int}\mathit{Tr}\left[\pi .{[{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{L}}+{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{NL}}(\mathrm{u})]}^{T}{\mathrm{u}}^{\text{*}}\right]d\Omega$                     eq 1.4.1-9

Taking into account the constitutive relation [eq 1.4.1-3]:

> ${\mathrm{P}}^{int}=\underset{{\Omega}_{S0}}{\int}\mathit{Tr}\left[{[{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{L}}+\frac{1}{2}{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{NL}}(\mathrm{u})]}^{T}\mathrm{C}[{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{L}}+{\mathrm{B}}^{\mathrm{NL}}(\mathrm{u})]\mathrm{u}.{\mathrm{u}}^{\text{*}}\right]d\Omega$         eq 1.4.1-10

After discretisation by the finite elements, one may put this equation into matrix form:

> ${u}^{\text{*}}.[{K}_{0}+{K}^{L}(u)+{K}^{Q}(u)].u={P}^{\mathrm{ext}}$                     eq 1.4.1-11

The matrix ${\mathrm{K}}^{\mathrm{L}}$ is symmetric and one has the following expressions:

> $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{K}_{0}=\underset{{\Omega}_{S0}}{\int}{B}^{{L}^{T}}C{B}^{L}d\Omega \\ {K}^{L}=\underset{{\Omega}_{S0}}{\int}\left[\frac{1}{2}{B}^{\mathrm{NL}}{(u)}^{T}C{B}^{L}+{B}^{{L}^{T}}C{B}^{L}(u)\right]d\Omega \\ {K}^{Q}=\frac{1}{2}\underset{{\Omega}_{S0}}{\int}{B}^{\mathrm{NL}}{(u)}^{T}C{B}^{\mathrm{NL}}d\Omega \end{array}$                 eq 1.4.1-12

One obtains directly from what precedes the writing in matrix form of the equilibrium:

> $[{K}_{0}+{K}^{L}(u)+{K}^{Q}(u)].u={F}^{\mathrm{ext}}$                         eq 1.4.1-13

Or again, in an equivalent manner:

> ${F}^{int}={F}^{\mathrm{ext}}\text{avec}{F}^{int}=\underset{{\Omega}_{S0}}{\int}{[{B}^{L}+{B}^{\mathrm{NL}}(u)]}^{t}\pi d\mathrm{OMEGA}$     eq 1.4.1-14

We may just as well formulate the Principle of Virtual Powers from the Cauchy stress state and the Almansi strain tensor (therefore on the current configuration). One then obtains:

> $\underset{{\Omega}_{S}}{\int}\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma \varepsilon ({u}^{\text{*}}))d\Omega =\underset{\partial {\Omega}_{S}}{\int}{F}_{d}.{u}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{dS}+\underset{{\Omega}_{S}}{\int}{f}_{d}.{u}^{\text{*}}d\Omega$         eq 1.4.1-15

Which one may also put in the following form, after discretisation:

> $\underset{{\Omega}_{S}}{\int}{B}^{T}\sigma d\Omega ={F}^{int}={F}^{\mathrm{ext}}$                         eq 1.4.1-16

Or again, assuming the elastic constitutive relation:

> $Ku={F}^{\mathrm{ext}}\text{avec}K=\underset{{\Omega}_{S}}{\int}{B}^{T}CBd\Omega$                 eq 1.4.1-17

The integrals of these equations are computed over the current volume ${\Omega}_{S}$ which depends, of course, on the solution displacement field $u$ . Likewise, the operator $B$ must be computed on the current configuration ${\Omega}_{S}$ and not on the initial configuration ${\Omega}_{S0}$ , as was the case previously.

#### 1.4.2 Stability study in geometric non-linearity

One will seek whether there exists a second kinematically admissible displacement field which satisfies the equilibrium equations: one therefore seeks to know whether there will be bifurcation.

This second field will be written as the sum of a perturbation added to the first solution, that is: $u=\alpha {u}_{1}$ , with $\alpha$ which is a very small real number and which one will make tend towards 0. The field ${u}_{1}$ is chosen kinematically admissible to 0.

The Principle of Virtual Powers will then be written for this new field.

The strain field is put in the form:

$\varepsilon (u+\alpha {u}_{1})=\varepsilon (u)+\alpha \left[{\varepsilon}^{L}({u}_{1})+\frac{1}{2}({\varepsilon}^{Q}({u}_{1},u))\right]+\frac{{\alpha}^{2}}{2}{\varepsilon}^{q}({u}_{1},{u}_{1})$         eq 1.4.2-1

The virtual strains are given by:

> ${\varepsilon}_{1}^{\text{*}}={\varepsilon}^{L}({u}^{\text{*}})+{\varepsilon}^{q}(u,{u}^{\text{*}})+\alpha {\varepsilon}^{Q}({u}_{1},{u}^{\text{*}})=\varepsilon ({u}^{\text{*}})+\alpha {\varepsilon}^{Q}({u}_{1},u)$         eq 1.4.2-2

Likewise, if we choose ${\Omega}_{\mathrm{S0}}$ as the reference configuration, the stresses become:

> ${\pi}_{1}=\pi +\alpha C\left[{\varepsilon}^{L}({u}_{1})+\frac{1}{2}({\varepsilon}^{Q}(u,{u}_{1})+{\varepsilon}^{Q}({u}_{1},u))\right]+\frac{{\alpha}^{2}}{2}C{\varepsilon}^{Q}({u}_{1},{u}_{1})$     eq 1.4.2-3

We may now express the Principle of Virtual Powers for the perturbed displacement field. Let us take as assumptions that the imposed forces do not depend on the displacement and that the initial configuration is chosen as the reference.

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{P}_{1}^{int}={P}^{int}\\ +\alpha \left[\underset{{\Omega}_{\mathrm{S0}}}{\int}\mathrm{Tr}(\pi {\varepsilon}^{Q}({u}_{1},{u}^{\text{*}}))d\Omega +\underset{{\Omega}_{\mathrm{S0}}}{\int}\mathrm{Tr}\left[\varepsilon ({u}^{\text{*}})C({\varepsilon}^{L}({u}_{1})+\frac{1}{2}({\varepsilon}^{Q}(u,{u}_{1})+{\varepsilon}^{Q}({u}_{1},u)))\right]d\Omega \right]+o(\alpha )\\ {P}_{1}^{\text{ext}}={P}^{\text{ext}}\\ {P}_{1}^{int}-{P}_{1}^{\text{ext}}=0\end{array}$ eq 1.4.2-4

For $\alpha$ sufficiently small, it will suffice that the term proportional to $\alpha$ in the expression [eq 1.4.2-4] be zero for the Principle of Virtual Powers to be satisfied for the field $u=\alpha {u}_{1}$ . In this case, there will therefore no longer be uniqueness of the solution, which will express the loss of stability of the system.

When the imposed forces do not depend on the geometric configuration, the stability study is therefore stated as:

| Knowing the current state, i.e. the kinematically admissible displacement field $u$ and the stress field $\pi$ , if there exists a displacement field ${u}_{1}$ kinematically admissible to 0 and such that, for every displacement ${u}^{\text{*}}$ kinematically admissible to 0, one has: $\begin{array}{c}\underset{{\Omega}_{\mathit{S0}}}{\int}\mathit{Tr}(\pi {\varepsilon}^{Q}({\mathrm{u}}_{1},{\mathrm{u}}^{\text{*}}))d\Omega \\ +\underset{{\Omega}_{\mathit{S0}}}{\int}\mathit{Tr}\left[{\varepsilon}^{L}({\mathrm{u}}^{\text{*}})\mathrm{C}{\varepsilon}^{L}({\mathrm{u}}_{1})+{\varepsilon}^{Q}(\mathrm{u},{\mathrm{u}}^{\text{*}})\mathrm{C}{\varepsilon}^{L}({\mathrm{u}}_{1})+{\varepsilon}^{L}({\mathrm{u}}^{\text{*}})\mathrm{C}{\varepsilon}^{Q}(\mathrm{u},{\mathrm{u}}_{1})+{\varepsilon}^{Q}(\mathrm{u},{\mathrm{u}}^{\text{*}})\mathrm{C}{\varepsilon}^{Q}(\mathrm{u},{\mathrm{u}}_{1})\right]d\Omega \\ =0\end{array}$ eq 1.4.2-5 **Then the problem considered is unstable.** |
| --- |

One may express this bifurcation condition in matrix form by introducing, in addition, the geometric stiffness matrix $K(\pi )$ which discretises its first term:

> $\begin{array}{c}\forall {u}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{CA}0,{u}^{\text{*}T}{K}_{t}{u}_{1}=0\\ \text{Avec}{K}_{T}={K}_{0}+{K}^{L}(u)+{K}^{Q}(u)+K(\pi )\text{qui est la raideur tangente}\end{array}$     eq 1.4.2-6

If one writes the bifurcation condition on the current configuration ${\Omega}_{S}$ , then one has:

> $\forall {u}^{\text{*}}\text{CA}0,{u}^{\text{*}T}[K+K(\sigma )]{u}_{1}=0$                     eq 1.4.2-7

The stress to be considered is then the Cauchy stress and all the integrals are evaluated on the current domain ${\Omega}_{S}$ .

##### 1.4.2.1 Stability condition of a non-linear elastic equilibrium

It follows immediately that, if there exists a state such that the tangent matrix ${K}_{T}$ defined above is singular, we shall indeed have exhibited a non-zero displacement field ${u}_{1}$ which demonstrates the loss of uniqueness of the solution of the mechanical problem. This displacement field is the buckling mode. One may note that the bifurcation condition is indeed satisfied, whatever the norm and the sign of ${u}_{1}$ : in this sense, one therefore speaks of a buckling mode, as of a direction, because one has restricted oneself in [eq 1.4.2-4] to the first order in $\alpha$ .

##### 1.4.2.2 Case of small displacements: Euler load

When the displacements may be described as small before buckling, one may identify the initial configuration with the current geometry. The matrices ${K}^{L}$ and ${K}^{Q}$ may then be neglected. Moreover, the stress $\pi$ may be identified with the usual stress $\sigma$ ; the buckling equations are then written:

> $[{K}_{0}+K(\sigma )]{u}_{1}=0$                             eq 1.4.2.2-1

It should be noted that the matrix $K(\sigma )$ is proportional to $\sigma$ and therefore to the loading applied to the structure. If one multiplies the stress by $\lambda$ , one obtains:

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.01/Object_87.svg)

> eq 1.4.2.2-2

This equation immediately suggests a generalised eigenvalue problem, of the same type as in the case of the search for vibration modes, which is written:

> $[{K}_{0}-{\omega}^{2}M]{v}_{1}=0$                             eq 1.4.2.2-3

The matrix $K(\sigma )$ is replaced by the mass matrix

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.01/Object_90.svg)

, and one sees the natural circular frequency $\omega$ appear, whereas ${v}_{1}$ is the associated vibration mode.

If one wishes to study buckling under a loading of which only a part is controlled (variable part of the loading), by a principle of superposition, the constant contribution of the uncontrolled loading must be added to the term ${K}_{0}$ and only the stress generated by the controlled loading will be in the term in $\lambda$ . Formally, one therefore poses the following problem:

$[{K}_{0}+K({\sigma}_{\mathrm{cte}})+\lambda K({\sigma}_{\mathrm{var}})]{u}_{1}$

$\text{Avec :}\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{\mathrm{cte}}\text{: contrainte générée par le chargement non piloté}\\ {\sigma}_{\mathrm{var}}\text{: contrainte générée par le chargement piloté}\end{array}$         eq 1.4.2.2-4

The two stress fields are obtained by the solution of two linear problems, one for the uncontrolled loading, the other for the controlled part of the total loading ( *cf.*  [U2.08.04](doc:u2.08.04) and [bib17]).

##### 1.4.2.3 Particular case of imposed forces depending on the geometry

**Example of follower pressures:**

When the external forces depend on the configuration, this entails that the work of the external forces intervenes in the stability condition. Let us take the example of a pressure applied to the structure. This pressure will be assumed constant during buckling: in other words, the pressure value does not change during the displacement.

This assumption corresponds to two types of real problems. The first type is that in which the volume of the fluid imposing the pressure on the structure is very large compared with the volume variations generated by the displacement of the solid. The problems of reservoirs under internal pressure, in which the wall displacements are not negligible compared with the dimensions of the structure itself, therefore do not fall within this framework.

The second case corresponds to the existence of a fluid source which makes it possible to maintain the pressure at a constant value. It is then no longer necessary to concern oneself with the amplitude of the displacement of the solid.

The pressure value being taken as fixed, the variation of the normal over time is to be taken into account. This variation is due to the displacement field which modifies the surface of the structure. Likewise, if one reasons in terms of resultant and therefore of integral, the surface element may also change in area. Consequently, the resultant of the pressure forces will vary and it is appropriate to take this into account.

It is however difficult to exhibit the existence of a potential. That is why one reduces the problem to the conservative case.

We see rapidly that the power of the forces, expressed on the current configuration, associated with a pressure is given by the following equation (see for example [bib11]):

${P}_{\text{pression}}^{\text{ext}}=\underset{\partial {\Omega}_{\mathrm{SP}}}{\int}p\left[n+\alpha \frac{{\mathrm{dS}}_{1}}{\mathrm{dS}}{n}_{1}\right].{u}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{dS}$                     eq 1.4.2.3-1

In this equation, we note that the power of the external forces is modified in the displacement $\alpha {u}_{1}$ . We shall then have:

> ${P}_{1}^{\text{ext}}={P}^{\text{ext}}+\underset{\partial {\Omega}_{\mathrm{SP}}}{\int}p\alpha {n}_{1}.{u}^{\text{*}}{\mathrm{dS}}_{1}$                     eq 1.4.2.3-2

Finally, the matrix ${K}_{T}$ is enriched by an additional term, a function of the pressure:

> ${K}_{T}={K}_{0}+{K}^{L}(u)+{K}^{Q}(u)+K(\pi )+K(p)$                 eq 1.4.2.3-3

If one writes the operators on the current geometry, one arrives at:

> ${K}_{T}=K+K(\sigma )+K(p)$                         eq 1.4.2.3-4

When we are in the presence of follower pressure forces, the same methods as those presented previously will be able to be applied in order to compute the buckling loads: it will suffice to complete the matrix ${K}_{T}$ with the new term $K(p)$ . One can show that the matrix $K(p)$ is symmetric if the pressure forces do no work on the “edge” of the model.

##### 1.4.2.4 Vibrations under prestress

The same methodology may also be applied to the study of the vibrations of the structure in the current configuration ${\Omega}_{S}$ . This structure is prestressed and deformed. It suffices to write the geometrically non-linear Principle of Virtual Powers [eq 1.4.1-7] taking the inertia effects into account and injecting into it the assumption that the displacements are periodic functions of the type:

> ${\mathrm{u}}_{1}(t)={\mathrm{v}}_{1}\sin(\omega t)$                                 eq 1.4.2.4-1

It follows that:

> $[{\mathrm{K}}_{0}+{\mathrm{K}}^{L}(\mathrm{u})+{\mathrm{K}}^{Q}(\mathrm{u})+\mathrm{K}(\pi )+\mathrm{K}(p)-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{M}]{\mathrm{v}}_{1}=0$     eq 1.4.2.4-2

First of all, we note, in this equation, that when we have a critical state then the natural vibration frequency of the structure corresponding to the buckling mode is zero.

Moreover, we observe that the natural frequencies of the loaded structure are different from those of the initial structure for two reasons:

1. The natural circular frequency $\omega$ is modified by the prestress $p$ : this is the principal effect which is used, for example, to tune a violin. The tension of the string acts on the pitch of the corresponding note, therefore on its natural frequency.
2. A second effect is the variation of the frequency by modification of the geometry: the initial geometric stiffness matrix

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.01/Object_108.svg)

is replaced by the stiffness matrix on the current geometry: ${K}_{0}+{K}^{L}+{K}^{Q}$ . Which has the effect of modifying the vibratory equations.

The operator DYNA_NON_LINE makes it possible to carry out vibratory analyses on the current non-linear configuration (keyword MODE_VIBR), but without taking the prestress into account for the moment.

### 1.5 Implementation in the code

Strictly speaking, in order to ensure the stability analysis of a non-linear quasi-static analysis, the *ad hoc* stability criterion must be used at each step of the incremental analysis. Any non-linear stability criterion must therefore be intrinsically as inexpensive as possible in CPU time and in memory space.

Algorithmically speaking, it appears judicious to implant the call to the criterion inside the routine itself corresponding to the operator STAT_NON_LINE [bib15]. Indeed, the principle of a call at each step sits poorly with a call totally externalised from the incremental method of solution of the non-linear mechanical problem.

### 1.6 Euler criterion

This criterion (cf. [§1.4.2.2]) requires only the solution of a linear static problem, then the construction and the assembly of the geometric stiffness matrix. The latter and the assembled stiffness matrix are then to be passed as the argument of a solver [bib12] for the eigenvalue problem [eq 1.4.2.2-2].

As output one therefore recovers the buckling modes and the corresponding critical loads. For further details, the user may usefully consult the document [U2.08.04](doc:u2.08.04) [bib17].

### 1.7 Non-linear criterion

#### 1.7.1 Impact on the operator STAT_NON_LINE

Let us begin by briefly recalling the operation of the incremental method of solution of non-linear structural problems [bib15].

##### 1.7.1.1 Algorithm of STAT_NON_LINE

One will use the index *i*  (as in "time instant") to denote the number of a load increment and the exponent *n*  (as in "Newton") to denote the number of the current Newton iteration. The algorithm used in the operator STAT_NON_LINE may then be written schematically in the following way:

$({u}_{0},{\lambda}_{0})$ and ${\sigma}_{0}$  known

Loop over the time instants ${t}_{i}$  (or load increments): loading ${L}_{i}=L({t}_{i})$

- $({u}_{i-1},{\lambda}_{i-1})$ known
- Prediction: computation of $\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}$  and $\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}$
- Loop over the Newton iterations: computation of a sequence $(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n},\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n})$
- $({u}_{i}^{n},{\lambda}_{i}^{n})$ and $(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n},\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n})$ known
- Computation of the matrices and vectors associated with the follower loads
- Expression of the constitutive relation
- computation of the stresses ${\sigma}_{i}^{n}$  and of the internal variables ${\alpha}_{i}^{n}$ from the values ${\sigma}_{l-1}$  and ${\alpha}_{l-1}$ at the previous equilibrium (${t}_{i-1}$ ) and of the displacement increment $\Delta {u}_{i}^{n}={u}_{i}^{n}-{u}_{i-1}$ since this equilibrium
- computation of the "nodal forces": ${Q}^{T}{\sigma}_{i}^{n}+{B}^{T}{\lambda}_{i}^{n}$
- possible computation of the tangent stiffness matrix: ${K}_{i}^{n}=K({u}_{i}^{n})$
- Computation of the search direction $(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n+1},\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1})$ by solution of a linear system
- Line-search iterations: $\rho$
- Updating of the variables and of their increments:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{u}_{i}^{n+1}={u}_{i}^{n}+\rho \delta {u}_{i}^{n+1}\\ {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1}={\lambda}_{i}^{n}+\rho \delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1}\end{array}\text{et}\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\Delta u}_{i}^{n+1}=\Delta {u}_{i}^{n}+\rho \delta {u}_{i}^{n+1}\\ \Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1}=\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n}+\rho \delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1}\end{array}$

- Convergence test
- Archiving of the results at the time instant ${t}_{i}$

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{u}_{i}={u}_{i-1}+\Delta {u}_{i}\\ {\lambda}_{i}={\lambda}_{i-1}+\Delta {\lambda}_{i}\\ {\sigma}_{i}\\ {\alpha}_{i}\end{array}$

One notes that there are three levels of nested loops: an outer loop over the time steps, a loop of (so-called global) Newton iterations and possible sub-loops for the line search (if it is requested by the user) and certain constitutive relations requiring iterations (called internal), for example for elastoplasticity in plane stress.

If one chooses the criterion based on the assembled tangent matrix, one must have this matrix updated at each step at which one wishes to carry out the stability analysis.

This is the case when one uses a Newton-type method, and not a modified Newton-type method.

One then arrives at the following algorithm:

$({u}_{0},{\lambda}_{0})$ and ${\sigma}_{0}$ known

Loop over the time instants ${t}_{i}$  (or load increments): loading ${L}_{i}=L({t}_{i})$

- $({u}_{i-1},{\lambda}_{i-1})$ known
- Prediction: computation of $\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}$ and $\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}$
- Loop over the Newton iterations: computation of a sequence $(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n},\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n})$
- $({u}_{i}^{n},{\lambda}_{i}^{n})$ and $(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n},\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n})$ known
- Computation of the matrices and vectors associated with the follower loads
- Expression of the constitutive relation
- computation of the stresses ${\sigma}_{i}^{n}$ and of the internal variables ${\alpha}_{i}^{n}$  from the values ${\sigma}_{l-1}$  and ${\alpha}_{l-1}$ at the previous equilibrium (${t}_{i-1}$ ) and of the displacement increment $\Delta {u}_{i}^{n}={u}_{i}^{n}-{u}_{i-1}$ since this equilibrium
- computation of the "nodal forces": ${Q}^{T}{\sigma}_{i}^{n}+{B}^{T}{\lambda}_{i}^{n}$
- possible computation of the tangent stiffness matrix: ${K}_{i}^{n}=K({u}_{i}^{n})$
- Computation of the search direction $(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n+1},\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1})$ by solution of a linear system
- Line-search iterations: $\rho$
- Updating of the variables and of their increments:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{u}_{i}^{n+1}={u}_{i}^{n}+\rho \delta {u}_{i}^{n+1}\\ {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1}={\lambda}_{i}^{n}+\rho \delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1}\end{array}\text{et}\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\Delta u}_{i}^{n+1}=\Delta {u}_{i}^{n}+\rho \delta {u}_{i}^{n+1}\\ \Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1}=\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n}+\rho \delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1}\end{array}$

- Convergence test
- Archiving of the results at the time instant ${t}_{i}$

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{u}_{i}={u}_{i-1}+\Delta {u}_{i}\\ {\lambda}_{i}={\lambda}_{i-1}+\Delta {\lambda}_{i}\\ {\sigma}_{i}\\ {\alpha}_{i}\end{array}$

- Stability criterion, a function of the updated tangent stiffness: ${\mathrm{K}}_{i}^{n}=\mathrm{K}({\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n})$

The criterion is computed at the end of the step, just after archiving. It therefore has as arguments the quantities converged at the current step. Moreover, this choice of call position makes it possible to take the follower loadings correctly into account, since their computation is carried out during the Newton iterations. The criterion could therefore not be called before the end of these iterations.

##### 1.7.1.2 Impact on the result data structure of STAT_NON_LINE

The call of the non-linear stability criterion will induce the solution of an eigenvalue problem. The result of this analysis will therefore be a set of critical-load / buckling-mode pairs.

The critical loads are scalars and the associated modes are displacement fields, which will enrich the result data structure of STAT_NON_LINE.

#### 1.7.2 Particular features linked to the strain tensor

In the code, it is appropriate to distinguish two broad families of description of the strains.

On the one hand the linearised tensor corresponds to the case of small perturbations (argument PETIT of the keyword DEFORMATION), but also to the case of updated small perturbations (Lagrangian updated at each step of the incremental analysis: argument PETIT_REAC of the keyword DEFORMATION).

The strain tensor is then written (as [eq 1.3-3]):

> $\varepsilon =\frac{1}{2}(\nabla \mathrm{u}+{\nabla}^{T}\mathrm{u})$                                 eq 1.7.2-1

The use of PETIT_REAC implies a solution of the equilibrium of the structure on its current geometry with a linearised strain tensor. One therefore computes the strain increment with respect to the position $X$ , to the displacement $\mathrm{u}$ and to the displacement increment $\Delta u$  in the following way:

> $\Delta {\varepsilon}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\partial \Delta {u}_{i}}{\partial {(X+u)}_{j}}+\frac{\partial \Delta {u}_{j}}{\partial {(X+u)}_{i}})$                     eq 1.7.2-2

On the other hand, the code proposes Green–Lagrange type strain tensors (GROT_GDEP) for the treatment of large displacements (and of finite rotations for certain structural elements) but under the assumption of small strains. The tensor used is the following classical tensor [eq 1.4.1-5]:

> ${\varepsilon}_{ij}(u)=\frac{1}{2}({u}_{i,j}+{u}_{j,i}={u}_{k,i}{u}_{k,j})$                     eq 1.7.2-3

The keyword GROT_GDEP applies to beam, shell or 3D modellings.

Finally, the most complete large-transformation modelling framework accessible in *Code_Aster* is derived from the theory of Simo and corresponds to the keyword SIMO_MIEHE. It takes into account the large rotations and the large strains since the constitutive law is written in large strains. For further details on the fundamental differences between the various types of strains, the documentation [bib16] of *Code_Aster* presents the SIMO_MIEHE modelling in detail.

*Code_Aster* does not allow analyses in an Eulerian configuration: as with the Almansi tensor, for example. All the available strain tensors are of Lagrangian type.

The fundamental difference, as regards the writing of the criterion, lies between the linearised strains (PETIT and PETIT_REAC) and the GROT_GDEP and SIMO_MIEHE strains.

Indeed, *Code_Aster* needs, in order to carry out its search for equilibrium, the tangent matrix. The latter is written according to the equation ([§2.2.2.1] of the documentation on STAT_NON_LINE [bib15]):

> ${K}_{T}={Q}^{T}:\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial u}+\frac{\partial {Q}^{T}}{\partial u}:\sigma$                         eq 1.7.2-4

Now, ${Q}^{T}:\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial u}$ corresponds to the classical material-stiffness term and $\frac{\partial {Q}^{T}}{\partial u}:\sigma$ corresponds to the geometric-stiffness term which is present only in large displacements. Thus the buckling criterion (formally assimilable to [eq 1.4.2.2-2]): $(K+\lambda K(\sigma ))\nu =0$ is valid only in small strains, since the geometric-stiffness term is considered as negligible in the tangent matrix. One may then, with good reason, carry out a classical search for eigenvalues and eigenvectors of Euler-buckling type.

On the other hand, in large transformation, the evaluation of this criterion by the same method is problematic for two reasons: on the one hand, in the tangent matrix, the geometric-stiffness term is already computed and, on the other hand, the matrix $K(\sigma )$ that it would possibly be necessary to add is obtained in *Code_Aster* in small strains. For these reasons, it is necessary to evaluate the criterion differently according to the type of strain tensor requested by the user.

If one made the choice of an Eulerian description, the development of an updated Euler type criterion would be facilitated at the level of the computation of the term $\mathrm{K}(\sigma )$ , whatever the strain tensor.

##### 1.7.2.1 In linearised strains: PETIT and PETIT_REAC

As we have said previously, this case does not pose any major problem. It suffices to compute the geometric stiffness matrix and to carry out a classical search for modes and eigenvalues, of Euler type [eq 1.4.2.2-2]:

> $(K+\lambda K(\sigma ))\nu =0$                             eq 1.7.2.1-1

$K$ is the tangent matrix updated at the end of the time step.

In this case, one may therefore indeed speak of an updated Euler type criterion.

Since one is in small strains, the geometric stiffness matrix is proportional to the loading. Therefore, when one obtains the critical coefficient $\lambda$ , it suffices to multiply it by the real load at the current time step in order to obtain the critical buckling load. The case $\lambda =1$ therefore corresponds to the loss of stability.

Certain finite elements such as the DKT shells do not allow the computation of the geometric stiffness matrix, unlike elements of COQUE_3D type, for example.

##### 1.7.2.2 In large displacements: GROT_GDEP and SIMO_MIEHE

The classical method no longer applies in this case. Indeed, *Code_Aster* computes as the tangent matrix the material stiffness matrix plus the geometric stiffness matrix (and possibly the contribution due to follower pressures).

One of the ways of then verifying buckling is to carry out a search for the eigenvalues of the tangent matrix alone. If one of the eigenvalues is negative, it is because the matrix has become singular and an instability has occurred between the moment when all its eigenvalues were positive and the moment when one of them has become negative.

The problem to be treated is therefore slightly different since in the case of small strains (PETIT and PETIT_REAC), one has the following system to solve [eq 1.7.2.1-1]: , $(K+\lambda I)\nu =0$  whereas in the case GROT_GDEP and SIMO_MIEHE one must solve:

$(\mathrm{K}+\lambda \mathrm{I})\nu =0$                                     eq 1.7.2.2-1

With $\mathrm{I}$ which is the identity matrix and $\lambda$ is, this time, of physical dimension equivalent to $K$ , whereas in the case of small strains, the eigenvalue $\lambda$ is dimensionless (whence its direct interpretation as a load multiplier coefficient).

One of the defects inherent in this method compared with the more classical search explained above [§ 1.4.2 ] is that one can have buckling predictions only when one approaches “close” to the critical load, or even when one exceeds it. Far from this load, the first eigenvalue found has no real physical meaning since non-linearities may appear between the current step and the computed critical load. The ratio of critical coefficient to load at the time instant $i$  is therefore different from that at the time instant $i+1$ whereas in small strains this ratio remains constant.

Moreover, for all the time steps, all the eigenvalues and eigenvectors except the lowest have no physical meaning since, for an eigenvector–eigenvalue pair $({\mathrm{V}}_{i},{\lambda}_{i})$ , one has:

> $(\mathrm{K}(\mathrm{u})+\mathrm{K}(\sigma )){\mathrm{V}}_{i}={\lambda}_{i}{\mathrm{V}}_{i}$                             eq 1.7.2.2-2

This has a clear meaning only from the moment when ${\lambda}_{i}\to 0$ , in which case one recovers the critical load and the associated critical eigenvector.

Still compared with the Euler criterion (updated [eq 3.2.2.1-1] or not [eq 2.3.2.2-2]), one notes that the eigenvalue of the problem [eq 1.7.2.2-1]: $(\mathrm{K}+\lambda \mathrm{I})\nu =0$  is not made dimensionless. There follows from this a greater difficulty of interpretation as regards knowing whether the value is “small” or not. In other words, when can one say that one is close to a bifurcation?

In order to define a relevant interval of general use, so as to bound the neighbourhood of an instability, it would be of interest to make the eigenvalues dimensionless.

##### 1.7.2.3 Case of mixed modellings

Since *Code_Aster* makes it possible to assign several types of strains to the same structure, one must consider the case in which one uses several types of strain tensors in the same analysis.

The differentiation of the various elementary matrices being of no use, it is appropriate to resolve to decide at the global level between one method or the other. One has chosen to extract the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the tangent matrix without adding geometric stiffness matrices. Everything proceeds as if the structure were in a Green–Lagrange type strain from the point of view of the criterion. Indeed, let us consider an arbitrary solid composed of two parts I and II. On part I, the strain tensor that has been adopted is the linearised tensor PETIT and on part II that of Green‑Lagrange. The tangent matrix arising from the assembly of the two sub-matrices becomes:

> $\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{\mathrm{K}}_{I}& \text{*}& 0\\ \text{*}& \text{*}& \text{*}\\ 0& \text{*}& {\mathrm{K}}_{\mathit{II}}+{\mathrm{K}}_{\mathit{II}}(\sigma )\end{array}\right]$                         eq 1.7.2.3-1

The starred terms represent the nodes common to the two parts and are therefore a linear combination of the values of the two matrices. In this configuration, it appears that none of the solutions is satisfactory but that the least penalising is to carry out a search of “Green‑Lagrange type” [§ 1.7.2.2 ], that is to say to use $(\mathrm{K}+\lambda \mathrm{I})\nu =0$ [eq 1.7.2.2-1].

This solution not being exact but nevertheless the only one capable of being carried out simply, it is planned to add an alarm message informing the user that the results obtained are not guaranteed owing to the juxtaposition of several types of strain tensors.

#### 1.7.3 Improvement of the performance of the criterion

During the incremental solution of a non-linear quasi-static problem, ideally and if one accepts that the discretisation in time is sufficiently fine, a stability analysis should be carried out at each computation step. At each step, this induces the solution of an eigenvalue problem, admittedly limited to the search for a few modes. The stability analysis therefore brings a significant CPU overhead, to a non-linear analysis that may already be long.

The idea is to call the solution of an eigenvalue problem only when it is really necessary, therefore when the current configuration is “close” to an instability. If one can define this neighbourhood by a predefined interval, then one can call a Sturm test [bib12].

This test makes it possible to know whether there exists at least one eigenvalue on the search interval. If so, one will then be able to carry out the modal search. Otherwise, one continues the quasi-static incremental solution, without solving an eigenvalue problem.

The cost of a Sturm test is notably lower than the cost of the search for the critical loads.

The search interval for the Sturm test may either be given by the user, or have a default value in the code.

In the case of an updated Euler criterion (case of small strains [§ 1.7.2.1 ]), where the problem to be solved is written: $(\mathrm{K}+\lambda \mathrm{K}(\sigma ))\nu =0$ [eq 1.7.2.1-1], the search interval must be centred on the eigenvalue $\lambda =1$ (which corresponds to the value -1 for the algorithm of CALC_MODES, because it in fact solves a problem of the type: $\mathrm{K}\nu =\mu \mathrm{K}(\sigma )\nu$ ).

The bounds of the interval are the bounds of the load multiplier coefficient, therefore dimensionless quantities, which are a function of the safety coefficients and of the evaluation of the uncertainties for the given problem. The Sturm test is implemented in this framework.

In the specific case adapted to the Green–Lagrange tensor [§ 1.7.2.2 ], where one solves: $(\mathrm{K}+\lambda \mathrm{I})\nu =0$ [eq 1.7.2.2-1], the interval is centred on 0. Moreover, the bounds of the test interval are, unlike the previous case, not dimensionless [§ 1.7.2.2 ]. It is therefore more difficult to identify relevant and general values (for the case of the default values). The Sturm test is not currently implanted for this case.

### 1.8 Generalisation to dynamics

We shall not address here the framework of the dynamic instability criteria (negative damping…). The point is merely to indicate that the non-linear criterion presented here may perfectly well be applied directly in non-linear dynamics. It will then detect any potential buckling of the structure, in the sense of the singularity of the updated global tangent stiffness matrix.

In order to be exhaustive in terms of stability analysis on a non-linear dynamic study, the user should use two criteria:

- a buckling criterion (criterion on the stiffness),
- a dynamic criterion (criterion on the damping or on the global quadratic linearised problem [bib14], for example).

For the moment, the buckling criterion on the stiffness (identical to that of STAT_NON_LINE) is alone available in DYNA_NON_LINE.

The coupled fluid–structure modelling (u,p,f) [R4.02.02](doc:r4.02.02), which is available in DYNA_NON_LINE, requires a few adaptations in the use of the non-linear stability criterion. Indeed, this coupled formulation generates a global assembled stiffness matrix that is intrinsically singular on all the fluid degrees of freedom, which makes it incompatible with the eigenvalue search method used for the stability analysis. One may however circumvent the problem by correcting the assembled problem (stiffness matrix and geometric stiffness matrix if need be) by means of the use of two specific keywords. The stability analysis then concerns the structural degrees of freedom alone.

The validation test cases are: SSNL126 and SSLL105D.

More precisely, the test cases SSNL126 treat the case of a beam clamped at one end and subjected to a compression at the other end. The modelling is three-dimensional, with an elastoplastic constitutive relation with linear isotropic hardening. Two kinematic representations are presented:

### 1.10 Extension of the buckling criterion to the treatment of elastoplastic behaviour

Far from any exhaustiveness, we shall present here only the simplest approaches, with a view to their easy implantation in the code.

When the structure operates in an elastoplastic regime, buckling is affected by the loss of strength due to plasticity [bib2]. The modification comes from the constitutive relation during the additional displacement $\alpha {u}_{1}$ .

The stress becomes, in incremental form:

$\Delta {\pi}_{1}=\Delta \pi +\alpha {C}_{T}[\Delta {\varepsilon}^{L}(u)+\Delta {\varepsilon}^{Q}(u,u)]+\frac{{\alpha}^{2}}{2}{C}_{T}\Delta {\varepsilon}^{Q}({u}_{1},{u}_{1})$     1.11-1

In this expression, the behaviour matrix is the tangent matrix ${C}_{T}$ . The choice of this matrix is not immediate: indeed, the matrix depends on $\alpha {\mathrm{u}}_{1}$ and is therefore not known so long as the mode is unknown. One may, for example, unload during buckling if the mode develops in one direction and load if it develops in the opposite direction. It is therefore necessary to make an assumption for the behaviour during plastic buckling. To begin with, we shall apply the Hill assumption [bib4], which starts from the principle that the structure continues to load plastically during buckling.

Consider an elastoplastic law of Von Mises type. We define the three moduli: $E$ which is Young's modulus, ${E}_{T}$ the tangent modulus, and the secant modulus. These moduli are recalled on the following figure:

**Figure 1.11.5-a: Representation of the various moduli on a 1D tensile curve**

Then we propose three possible methodologies.

The tangent-modulus assumption consists simply in replacing Young's modulus by the tangent modulus in the constitutive relation. One then obtains:

${C}_{T}=\frac{E}{{E}_{T}}C$                             1.11-2

This method is very rudimentary, but it is always pessimistic, which may constitute an advantage, if one places oneself from the point of view of design.

The method usually used consists in employing the tangent matrix of the incremental analysis (operator STAT_NON_LINE [bib15]). We therefore have the following equation in the case of Von Mises plasticity [bib16]:

${C}_{T}=C\left[I-\frac{A[{\sigma}^{D}\otimes {\sigma}^{{D}^{T}}]AC}{h+\frac{{\sigma}^{{D}^{T}}AA{\sigma}^{D}}{{\parallel {\sigma}^{D}\parallel }_{\mathrm{VM}}}}\right]$

$\text{Avec}\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\sigma}^{D}\text{: vecteur déviateur des contraintes}\\ A\text{: matrice intervenant dans la norme de VonMises}({\parallel {\sigma}^{D}\parallel }_{\mathrm{VM}}=\sqrt{{\sigma}^{{D}^{T}}A{\sigma}^{D}})\\ h\text{:pente plastique définie par}h=\frac{E.{E}^{T}}{E-{E}^{T}}\end{array}$ 1.11-3

This method is perfectly rigorous only in non-linear elasticity or if one complies with the Hill assumption: it does not make it possible to predict the bifurcations in the loading paths. As soon as the constitutive relation is dissipative (see chapter **2** ), the computed critical loads will be exact only if one can verify that the loading is monotonic, at every point of the structure (Hill [bib4]).

The most realistic method consists in using the finite theory of strain solely in order to compute the plastic buckling load. The tangent behaviour matrix is given by the equation below:

> ${C}_{T}={\left[(\frac{1}{{E}_{T}}-\frac{1}{{E}_{S}})\frac{A[{\sigma}^{D}\otimes {\sigma}^{{D}^{T}}]A}{{\parallel {\sigma}^{D}\parallel }_{\mathrm{VM}}}+{C}^{-1}+(\frac{1}{{E}_{S}}-\frac{1}{E})A\right]}^{-1}$         1.11-4

Compared with the method based on the tangent stiffness matrix [eq 1.11-3], this criterion requires the construction and the assembly of a specific global matrix. This costly operation adds to the burden of the incremental solution.

For considerations of generality and of minimisation of the development cost and of the computation cost (CPU and memory), we therefore choose the criterion based on the tangent modulus [eq 1.11-3].

### 1.11 Conclusion

*Code_Aster* offers two stability criteria, in the sense of buckling, for structural analyses:

1. On the one hand, in the cases where a linearised approach is sufficient, one may apply an Euler type criterion ([bib13] and [bib18]), by a call to an operator for the solution of the generalised eigenvalue problem (CALC_MODES with the keyword TYPE_RESU=’MODE_FLAMB’).

1. On the other hand, for all the cases where it is indispensable to take the non-linearities into account, whether they are due to the constitutive relation or to the large transformations, the user may employ an adapted criterion, of generalised Euler type. The call of this criterion is made during the incremental solution of the quasi-static problem (operator STAT_NON_LINE

[bib15]).

At each time step, the criterion is based on the solution of an eigenvalue problem [bib13] on the updated global stiffness matrices. This criterion, which comes in two different forms, according to the strain tensor chosen, is based on a linearisation about the current computation step. It accepts any type of strain tensor, as well as any type of constitutive relation for which one is able to construct the global stiffness matrix, at each time instant. Moreover, the criterion chosen is perfectly rigorous in the case of non-linear elastic constitutive relations, and may be extended to the case of elastoplasticity associated with the Hill assumption [bib4].

## 2 Stability of a dissipative system

### 2.1 Definition of the stability of a dissipative system

When one is interested in dissipative phenomena (case of plastic or brittle materials...) one adds, in the expression of the total energy *Φ*, a term representing the dissipated part. The irreversible degradations are then associated with quantities, most often scalars, such as damage or plasticity. The buckling criterion presented previously does not take irreversibility into account. It is sufficient but not necessary in order to justify stability [bib19]. When the buckling criterion is reached, the uniqueness of the solution is no longer guaranteed, but one cannot conclude directly on the stability. One denotes by *u* the state variable deriving from the reversible part of the energy and by *α* the state variable deriving from the irreversible part of the mechanical phenomenon studied. The stability criterion then consists in verifying the positivity of the second derivative of the energy in the direction of the increment of the variable *α* [bib20]. Consider an admissible perturbation ( *ν,b≥0* ). The stability criterion then amounts to verifying that one always recovers the inequality:

$\phi (u,\alpha )\le \phi (u+v,\alpha +b)$                                 eq 2.1‑1

> From the mathematical point of view, this amounts to verifying that the function *Φ* realises a local minimum at         ( *u* , *α* ).
> One is interested more particularly in the case of damage models. One considers ( *u* , *α* ), a         state of the structure Ω satisfying the equilibrium:

> $\forall v\in {C}^{0},{\int}_{\Omega}(\frac{\partial \phi }{\partial u}(u,\alpha ).v)\mathit{dx}=0$                             eq 2.1‑2

> and the damage criterion, taking into account the irreversibility of the state variable *α:*

> $\frac{\partial \phi }{\partial \alpha }(u,\alpha )\ge 0\mathit{et}\forall \beta \in {C}^{0}\ge 0,{\int}_{\Omega}(\frac{\partial \phi }{\partial \alpha }(u,\alpha ).\beta )\mathit{dx}=0$                 eq 2.1‑3

> One rapidly obtains, with a Taylor expansion to order 2 [bib20], the equivalence between the         criterion 2.1-1     and the following criterion, written on the second derivative of *Φ:*

> ${D}^{2}\phi (u,\alpha )\ge 0$                             eq 2.1‑4

> where *D2* is the second-derivative operator.

### 2.2 Writing within the framework of the finite-element method

From the point of view of the finite elements and retaining the previous notations, this criterion is written as the positivity of the following Rayleigh quotient under inequality constraints:

$\forall (v,b\ge 0)\mathrm{\ne }0,{Q}_{\mathit{rc}}=\frac{{(v,b)}^{t}.{K}^{T}(v,b)}{{(v,b)}^{T}.(v,b)}\ge 0$                             eq 2.2‑1

The most classical way of verifying that a function is positive consists in computing its minimum and in ensuring its positivity. One presents in the part that follows the minimisation algorithm under inequality constraints programmed in *Code_Aster.*

### 2.3 Optimisation algorithm under inequality constraints

Among the algorithms available in the literature, the one that has proved the most robust and also the simplest to program is the power method, to which one adds, at each iteration, the projection of the degrees of freedom $b$ onto the set of positive values, so as to satisfy the imposed unilateral constraint [bib21]. The algorithm is constructed in two stages, in the following way:

1. The power method is very widely used in applied mathematics for the search for the maximum eigenvalues of a matrix **M**. A shift on the largest eigenvalue **λm** **:N=* **λmId-M** then makes it possible to carry out the search for the smallest eigenmodes. One presents below the algorithm in its initial form. ### 2.4 Implementation in the code

The stability study is triggered on the call of the command CRIT_STAB of the operator STAT_NON_LINE, under the condition TYPE = 'STABILITE'. The quantities, or degrees of freedom, satisfying the unilateral irreversibility condition are declared in a list DDL_STAB=('','',...).

#### 2.5.1 Analytical stability results

One demonstrates [bib23] that there exist two types of solutions to the problem studied:

- The homogeneous solution, uniformly damaged.
- The localised solutions which concentrate the damage of the bar on a precise zone.

The stability study of the homogeneous solution therefore amounts to verifying that the energy levels reached, considering small perturbations of the solution having the form of a localisation, are always higher than that obtained from the homogeneous solution.

Starting from the following gradient-damage formulation of the energy:

> $\varphi ={\int}_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{2}{\left(1-\alpha \right)}^{2}{E}_{0}\epsilon {(u)}^{2}+\frac{{{\sigma}_{M}}^{2}}{{E}_{0}}\alpha +\frac{{E}_{0}{l}^{2}}{2}\nabla \alpha .\nabla \alpha \right)\mathit{dx}$             eq 2.5.1‑1

the comparison of the energy levels then makes it possible to plot the stability diagram of the homogeneous solution as a function of the ratio between the length of the bar *L*, the internal length of the model *l* and of the applied loading *Ut* [bib23]:

#### 2.5.2 Stability results obtained with Code_Aster

By using the optimisation algorithm developed in the code (2.3), one recovers a stability diagram similar to within 5% on the loading from which the instability is detected [bib25]:

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.01/10000000000004CB000002E4860B7EB615E5BDD9.jpg)

Figure 2.5.2-a: Stability diagram of the bar in tension obtained with *Code_Aster*

Parametric studies on the influence of the number of natural frequencies computed and on which one relies for the stability study show that it is necessary to take about thirty of them for problems discretised at more or less 100 000 degrees of freedom but that it becomes important to consider a good hundred of them for problems of larger size. Which entails higher CPU computation costs, the algorithm being greedy in time. That is why it is really triggered only when the buckling study shows that the uniqueness of the solution is no longer ensured.

When the algorithm shows that the stability criterion is no longer satisfied (CHAR_STAB negative), the vector minimising the Rayleigh quotient (eq 2.2-1) is called the instability direction. One finds it as a result under the name MODE_STAB (by analogy with MODE_FLAMB for the buckling criterion). The perturbation of the current solution by this instability direction destabilises it and makes it possible to bifurcate towards a stable solution. In the example presented here, the instability direction is the localisation of the damage on one of the two ends of the bar.

### 2.6 Conclusion

*Code_Aster* makes it possible to carry out stability studies on dissipative problems such as plasticity or damage problems. The algorithm used is based on the power method to which is added the projection of the degrees of freedom, into the space complying with the unilateral irreversibility constraints. Its application is really implemented only when the uniqueness criterion is violated. Otherwise, uniqueness is sufficient to guarantee stability. If one detects, at a time step of the finite-element analysis, the loss of stability of the solution, the algorithm supplies the direction to be added as a perturbation in order to recover a stable bifurcated solution.
