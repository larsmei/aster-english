# r3.06.14 Introduction to the Hybrid High-Order (HHO) methods

**Summary:**

This document presents the HHO methods for problems in non-linear mechanics (small and large strains) and linear and non-linear thermal analysis.
The corresponding keywords in [AFFE_MODELE](doc:u4.41.01) for the mechanical modellings in *code_aster* are D_PLAN_HHO and 3D_HHO.
An extension to non-local formulations is possible (D_PLAN_GRAD_HHO and 3D_GRAD_HHO). For thermal analysis, there are AXIS_HHO, PLAN_HHO and 3D_HHO.
One must then choose between the formulations CONSTANTE, LINEAIRE, QUADRATIQUE, CUBIQUE or QUARTIQUE.

More complete theoretical details are given in [1] and [9].

## 1 Introduction

The HHO methods have been developed for some years by Di Pietro, Ern and their collaborators.
The first works concern the problems of linear diffusion [2] and of linear elasticity [3].
Since then, the HHO methods have been extended to different physics in order to treat both linear
and non-linear problems (see [1] for the references that follow). A complete introduction and a review of the applications is given in [9]
For linear problems, we may cite the works on the Stokes problem, on linear diffusion with variable coefficients,
on advection-diffusion-reaction, on the Cahn-Hilliard problem, and on flows in fractured porous media.
The use of curved meshes is considered for the linear-diffusion problem.
For non-linear problems, we may cite the works on the Leray-Lions problem, on the stationary Navier-Stokes equations,
on spectral problems, and on Bingham fluids.
As regards solid mechanics more particularly, there has been the extension to the problem of non-linear elasticity
in small strains, to the Biot problem, to the equations of Kirchhoff--Love plates,
and the works carried out in [1] on hyperelasticity in large strains [4],
plasticity in small [5] and large [6] strains and unilateral contact with Tresca friction.
From a numerical point of view, particular attention has been paid to the implementation of these methods in a generic manner
so as to include polyhedral meshes in any dimension.

The HHO methods are non-conforming methods, like the discontinuous Galerkin (dG) type methods, but unlike the latter,
they are formulated in terms of face unknowns (carried by the skeleton of the mesh).
Unknowns in the cells are also added for stability and approximation properties (whence the term Hybrid).
These cell unknowns can be eliminated locally by static condensation (or Schur complement).
The term High-Order comes from the fact that the HHO methods make it possible to do low order as well as high order
without modification of the method. The construction of the HHO methods rests on two ideas:

1. the local reconstruction of a gradient or of a potential from the cell unknowns and the face unknowns by "mimicking" an integration by parts;
2. a local stabilisation operator in order to impose weakly, on each face of the cell, the consistency between the face unknowns and the trace of the cell unknowns.

The HHO methods present numerous advantages:

1. arbitrary order of approximation ( $k \geq 0$ for linear diffusion and $k \geq 1$ for linear elasticity);
2. use of polyhedral meshes with possibly non-conforming interfaces (see for examples of polyhedral meshes);
3. robustness with respect to the physical parameters (elastic incompressibility, dominant advection...);
4. attractive numerical costs thanks to static condensation, which makes it possible to eliminate the unknowns carried by the cells while maintaining a compact stencil;
5. local conservation of the fluxes on each face of the mesh.

![Figure](/figures/r3.06.14/10000201000003DA000002CC07AA61538A499B58.png)

*Examples of polyhedral meshes supported by the HHO methods.*

Point *(3)* is particularly relevant in the context of this manuscript, in which we are interested in the problems
of solid mechanics, because the HHO methods will allow us to use a purely primal formulation
(without addition of Lagrange multipliers, unlike mixed formulations) in order to eliminate the numerical locking
arising from incompressibility, which is usually present for the $H^1$-conforming methods.
As for point *(2)*, one motivation is to be able to carry out a local refinement of the mesh without having to concern oneself
with the possible presence of hanging nodes as in the $H^1$-conforming methods.
Indeed, as illustrated in this figure, the presence of hanging nodes is handled simply
by treating the corresponding mesh cell as a polygon (or a polyhedron).

![Figure](/figures/r3.06.14/1000020000000618000002753DB6335AA4EC186F.png)

*Example of local refinement treated by the HHO methods (middle) and by the $H^1$-conforming methods (right) starting from the same initial mesh (left).*

The HHO methods present more or less close connections with other numerical methods developed in recent years.
For the low-order methods, there exist links with the Hybrid Finite Volumes and Mixed Finite Volumes methods,
with the Mimetic Finite Differences methods (see [8] for a unified framework of the three previous methods),
as well as with the Compatible Discrete Operator methods and the Gradient Schemes.
For the higher-order methods, a connection has been established between the HHO methods,
Hybridizable Discontinuous Galerkin (HDG) and nonconforming Virtual Element Methods (ncVEM).
The main similarities and differences between the HHO, HDG and ncVEM methods are:

- the reconstruction of the discrete operators for HHO replaces the flux equations for HDG, and these operators play the same role as the projection operators used by ncVEM;
- the stabilisation operators for HHO and HDG are defined from a functional point of view (and are different in the case where the cell unknowns have the same degree as the face unknowns), whereas for ncVEM these operators are defined from an algebraic point of view while leading to quadratic forms equivalent to HHO.

## 2 Description of the method

### 2.1 Case of linear elasticity

In this section we shall present the Hybrid High-Order (HHO) methods by means of the linear-elasticity problem.
The aim is to describe the main principles of construction of the HHO methods. For simplicity, we assume that
the domain $\Omega_{0}\subset \mathbb{R}^{d}$, with $2\le d\le 3$,
is a bounded, connected polyhedral domain with a Lipschitz boundary $\Gamma =\partial \Omega_{0}$.
The domain $\Omega_{0}$ deforms under the action of a body loading $f \in {L}^{2}(\Omega_0;\mathbb{R}^{d})$
and homogeneous Dirichlet-type conditions are imposed on the boundary $\Gamma$ (for simplicity).
The weak formulation of the problem is:

$$
& \text{Trouver } u  \in {H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega_0;\mathbb{R}^{d}) \text{ tel que:}

& 2\mu{(\nabla^{s}u,\nabla^{s}v)}_{{L}^{2}(\Omega_{0})}+\lambda{(\nabla \cdot u,\nabla \cdot v)}_{{L}^{2}(\Omega_{0})}={(f,v)}_{{L}^{2}(\Omega_{0})},
\: \forall v \in {H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{0};\mathbb{R}^{d})
$$

where ${H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega_0;\mathbb{R}^{d})=\lbrace v \in {H}^{1}(\Omega_0;\mathbb{R}^{d}) : v=0 \text{ sur } \Gamma \rbrace$
and $\mu>0$ and $\lambda\ge 0$ are the Lamé parameters of the material.
Moreover, $\nabla^{s}$ denotes the symmetric part of the gradient operator
and $\nabla \cdot$ the divergence operator.

### 2.2 Discrete framework

#### 2.2.1 Mesh

We consider a family of meshes ${(\mathcal{T}_{h})}_{h>0}$, where for each $h>0$,
the mesh $\mathcal{T}_{h}$ is composed of open, disjoint, non-empty polyhedra, which have plane faces,
and such that ${\overline\Omega}_{0}={\cup}_{T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}}\overline{T}$.
The global size of the mesh is described by the parameter $h=\max_{T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}}{h}_{T}$,
where ${h}_{T}$ is the diameter of the cell $T$.
A closed subset $F$ of ${\overline\Omega}_{0}$ is called a face if it is a subset
having a non-empty relative interior contained in an affine hyperplane ${H}_{F}$ and if:

- either there exist two distinct cells ${T}_{1},{T}_{2} \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$ such that $F=\partial {T}_{1}\cap \partial {T}_{2}\cap {H}_{F}$ (and $F$ is then called an interface)
- or there exists a cell $T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$ such that $F=\partial T\cap \Gamma \cap {H}_{F}$ (and $F$ is then called a boundary face).

The faces of the mesh are gathered in the set $\mathcal{F}_{h}$, which is thus partitioned
into two subsets, $\mathcal{F}_{h}^{i}$ the set of the interfaces and $\mathcal{F}_{h}^{b}$ the set of the boundary faces.
For every $T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$, ${F}_{T}$ denotes the set of the faces of the mesh that are included
in $\partial T$ and ${n}_{T}$ the unit outward normal to the cell $T$.
We assume in what follows that the family of meshes ${(\mathcal{T}_{h})}_{h>0}$ is regular in the sense specified in
[3], that is to say for every $h>0$, there exists a conforming submesh of $\mathcal{T}_{h}$ composed of simplices
which belongs to a regular family of simplicial meshes in the usual sense of Ciarlet
and such that each cell $T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$ of the mesh (respectively, each face $F \in \mathcal{F}_{h}$)
can be decomposed into a uniformly bounded number of sub-cells (respectively, sub-faces) which belong
to only one cell of the mesh (respectively, to only one face or to the interior of a cell)
with a uniformly comparable diameter.

#### 2.2.2 Approximation

Let $k\ge 1$ be a fixed polynomial degree. In each cell $T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$ of the mesh,
the local HHO unknowns are the pair $({v}_{T},{v}_{\partial T})$, where the cell unknown
${v}_{T} \in \mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d})$ is a vector-valued polynomial in $d$ variables of degree
at most $k$ in the cell $T$, and
${v}_{\partial T} \in \mathbb{P}_{d-1}^{k}({F}_{T};\mathbb{R}^{d}) := {\prod}_{F \in {F}_{T}}\mathbb{P}_{d-1}^{k}(F;\mathbb{R}^{d})$
is a vector-valued polynomial defined piecewise in $(d-1)$ variables of degree
at most $k$ on each face $F \in {F}_{T}$ of the cell $T$. We write more concisely in what follows that:

$$
{\widehat{v}}_{T} := ({v}_{T},{v}_{\partial T}) \in {U}_{T}^{k} := \mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times \mathbb{P}_{d-1}^{k}({F}_{T};\mathbb{R}^{d})
$$

The degrees of freedom are illustrated in this representation,
where a point represents a degree of freedom (but not necessarily a physical evaluation point)
and the geometric shape of the cell is only illustrative.
The degrees of freedom have no physical meaning a priori and, from an algebraic point of view,
they simply correspond to the coefficients in a polynomial basis.

![Figure](/figures/r3.06.14/10000201000005540000017FA10890A6A3434CC9.png)

*Pentagonal cell: degrees of freedom of the cell (blue) and of the faces (green) for different values
of the order of approximation $k=\lbrace 1,2,3\rbrace$ in the two-dimensional case.*

The space ${U}_{T}^{k}$ is equipped with the following local discrete seminorm:

$$
\vert{ \widehat{v}}_{T}\vert_{1,T}^{2} := {\Vert \nabla^{s}{v}_{T}\Vert }_{{L}^{2}(T)}^{2}+{\Vert {\gamma}_{\partial T}^{\frac{1}{2}}({v}_{T}-{v}_{\partial T})\Vert }_{{L}^{2}(\partial T)}^{2}
$$

with the piecewise-constant function ${\gamma}_{\partial T\mid F}$ such that:

$$
{\gamma}_{\partial T\mid F}={h}_{F}^{-1}, \: \forall F \in {F}_{T}
$$

where ${h}_{F}$ is the diameter of $F$. We also introduce the space $\mathrm{RM}(T)$
of the rigid-body motions on $T$ such that:

$$
\textrm{RM}(T) := \lbrace v : T\to \mathbb{R}^{d} \, \mid  \, v(x)=c+\omega x,\text{ où } c \in \mathbb{R}^{d} \text{ et } \omega \in {\mathrm{SO}}^{d}\rbrace
$$

where ${\mathrm{SO}}^{d}$ is the set of the rotation matrices in real Euclidean space.
Note that $\nabla^{s}=0$ is equivalent to $v \in \mathrm{RM}(T)$
and that $\mathbb{P}_{d}^{0}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d})\subset \mathrm{RM}(T)\subset \mathbb{P}_{d}^{1}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d})$.

**Lemma 1**

For every ${\widehat{v}}_{T} \in {U}_{T}^{k}$, one has $\vert{\widehat{v}}_{T}\vert{}_{1,T}=0$
if and only if ${v}_{T}$ is a rigid-body motion
and ${v}_{\partial T}$ is the trace of ${v}_{T}$ on $\partial T$.

**Proof:**

Note that $\vert {\widehat{v}}_{T} \vert {}_{1,T}=0$ is equivalent to:

$$
\nabla^{s}{v}_{T}=0 \text{ et } {v}_{T}-{v}_{\partial T}=0
$$

We deduce from this that ${v}_{T}$ is a rigid-body motion
because $\nabla^{s}{v}_{T}=0$ and that ${v}_{\partial T}$ is the trace of ${v}_{T}$
on $\partial T$ because ${v}_{T}-{v}_{\partial T}=0.$ ■

### 2.3 Local reconstruction and stabilisation operators

The HHO methods are based on the reconstruction of various discrete quantities which will come to play the role
of their continuous counterpart in the formulation of the discrete problem.
In the case of the discretisation of the linear-elasticity problem, the first key ingredient is
the reconstruction operator of the symmetric gradient ${E}_{T} : {U}_{T}^{k}\to \mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(T;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d})$
from the cell unknown $v_T \in \mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d})$
and the face unknowns ${v}_{\partial T} \in \mathbb{P}_{d-1}^{k}({F}_{T};\mathbb{R}^{d})$ composing
the pair ${\widehat{v}}_{T}=({v}_{T},{v}_{\partial T})$. For every ${\widehat{v}}_{T} \in {U}_{T}^{k}$,
the reconstructed symmetric gradient ${E}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T}) \in \mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(T;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d})$
is obtained by solving the following local problem: for every $\tau \in \mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(T;\mathbb{R}_{\mathrm{sym}}^{d\times d})$ :

$$
{({E}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T}),\tau)}_{{L}^{2}(T)}={(\nabla^{s}{v}_{T},\tau)}_{{L}^{2}(T)}+{({v}_{\partial T}-{v}_{T},\tau{n}_{T})}_{{L}^{2}(\partial T)}
$$

Solving this problem implies choosing a polynomial basis of $\mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(T;\mathbb{R})$
only and inverting the associated mass matrix for each component of the tensor ${E}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T})$.

The second key ingredient for constructing the HHO methods is the local stabilisation operator,
which makes it possible to impose weakly the equality between the face unknowns ${v}_{\partial T}$
and the trace of the cell unknown ${v}_{T}$ by means of a penalisation in the least-squares sense
of the difference ${v}_{\partial T}-{v}_{T} \in \mathbb{P}_{d-1}^{k}({F}_{T};\mathbb{R}^{d})$.
The stabilisation operator ${S}_{\partial T} \in \mathbb{P}_{d-1}^{k}({F}_{T};\mathbb{R}^{d})$ is defined such that, for every ${\widehat{v}}_{T}=({v}_{T},{v}_{\partial T})$ :

$$
{S}_{\partial T}({\widehat{v}}_{T})={\Pi}_{\partial T}^{k}({v}_{\partial T}-{R}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T}))+{\Pi}_{T}^{k}({v}_{T}-{R}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T}))
$$

where ${\Pi}_{\partial T}^{k}$ and ${\Pi}_{T}^{k}$ are the
$L^2$-orthogonal projectors onto $\mathbb{P}_{d-1}^{k}({F}_{T};\mathbb{R}^{d})$
and $\mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d})$ respectively.
Moreover, the local operator of reconstruction of a higher-order displacement field
${R}_{T} : {U}_{T}^{k}\to \mathbb{P}_{d}^{k+1}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d})$ is defined such that,
for every ${\widehat{v}}_{T} \in {U}_{T}^{k}$, ${R}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T}) \in \mathbb{P}_{d}^{k+1}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d})$
is obtained by solving the following Neumann problem: for every $w \in \mathbb{P}_{d}^{k+1}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d})$ :

$$
{(\nabla {R}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T}),\nabla w)}_{{L}^{2}(T)}={(\nabla {v}_{T},\nabla w)}_{{L}^{2}(T)}+{({v}_{\partial T}-{v}_{T},\nabla w \cdot {n}_{T})}_{{L}^{2}(\partial T)}
$$

This reconstruction is defined uniquely by imposing the mean value:

$$
{ \int}_{T}{R}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T})={ \int}_{T}{v}_{T}
$$

As ${E}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T})$ is not stable in the sense that ${E}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T})=0$
does not necessarily imply that ${v}_{\partial T}={v}_{T}=\mathrm{cst}$,
it is necessary to couple the operator ${E}_{T}$ to the stabilisation operator ${S}_{\partial T}$.
Thus, the addition of this stabilisation term makes it possible to recover a local stability property
which is necessary in order to demonstrate the coercivity of the discrete problem.

Let the reconstruction operator of the symmetric gradient defined by
and the stabilisation operator defined by . Let ${\gamma}_{\partial T}$ defined by .
Then, there exist $0<{\alpha}_{s}<{\alpha}_{b}<+\mathrm{ \infty }$, independent of $h$,
such that, for every $T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$ and every ${\widehat{v}}_{T} \in {U}_{T}^{k}$ :

$$
{\alpha}_{s}{|{\widehat{v}}_{T}|}_{1,T}\le \sqrt{{\Vert {E}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T})\Vert }^{2}+{\Vert {\gamma}_{\partial T}^{\frac{1}{2}}{S}_{\partial T}({\widehat{v}}_{T})\Vert }^{2}}\le {\alpha}_{b}{|{\widehat{v}}_{T}|}_{1,T}

Another important property of the reconstructed symmetric gradient is a commutativity property which
is essential in order to prove the robustness of the method in the incompressible limit.

**Lemma 3** (Commutativity)

For every $T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$ and every $v \in {H}^{1}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d})$, the following equality is true:

$$
{E}_{T}({\widehat{I}}_{T}^{k}(v))={\Pi}_{T}^{k}(\nabla^{s}v)
$$

where ${\widehat{I}}_{T}^{k} : {H}^{1}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d})\to {U}_{T}^{k}$ is the local reduction operator such that:

$$
{\widehat{I}}_{T}^{k}(v)=({\Pi}_{T}^{k}(v),{\Pi}_{\partial T}^{k}(v))
$$

**Proof:**

See Proposition 3 in [3]. ■

For what follows, we also define the discrete divergence operator
${D}_{T} : {U}_{T}^{k}\to \mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(T;\mathbb{R} )$ such that:

$$
{D}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T})=\mathrm{trace}({E}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T})), \:
\forall {\widehat{v}}_{T} \in {U}_{T}^{k}
$$

**Note** (Variants of the HHO methods):

*There exist several variants of the HHO method presented here. Once the polynomial degree* $k$
*has been fixed for the face unknowns, the cell unknown may be of polynomial degree*
$l \in \lbrace k-1,k,k+1\rbrace ,\:l\ge 1$ *, without change of the approximation and stability properties (cf.* [7] *).*

### 2.4 Global discrete HHO problem

Let us now define the global discrete problem. Set:

$$
\mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h};\mathbb{R}^{d}) := {\times}_{T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}}\mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(T;\mathbb{R}^{d}) \text{ et }
\mathbb{P}_{d-1}^{k}(\mathcal{F}_{h};\mathbb{R}^{d}) := {\times}_{F \in \mathcal{F}_{h}}\mathbb{P}_{d-1}^{k}(F;\mathbb{R}^{d})
$$

The global space of the HHO unknowns is defined such that:

$$
{U}_{h}^{k} := \mathbb{P}_{d}^{k}(\mathcal{T}_{h};\mathbb{R}^{d})\times \mathbb{P}_{d-1}^{k}(\mathcal{F}_{h};\mathbb{R}^{d})
$$

For a generic element ${\widehat{v}}_{h} \in {U}_{h}^{k}$, we use the notation
${\widehat{v}}_{h} := ({v}_{\mathcal{T}_{h}},{v}_{\mathcal{F}_{h}})$.
For every cell $T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$ of the mesh, we denote by ${\widehat{v}}_{T} \in {U}_{T}^{k}$
the local components of ${\widehat{v}}_{h}$ linked to the cell $T$
and to the faces composing its boundary $\partial T$, and for every face $F \in \mathcal{F}_{h}$,
we denote by ${v}_{F}$ the component of ${\widehat{v}}_{h}$ linked to the face $F$.
Homogeneous Dirichlet boundary conditions are applied in a strong manner on the unknowns linked to the boundary faces
$F \in \mathcal{F}_{h}^{b}$. We introduce for this purpose the subspace:

$$
{U}_{h,0}^{k} := \lbrace {\widehat{v}}_{h} \in {U}_{h}^{k} \, : \, {v}_{F}=0, \:\forall F \in \mathcal{F}_{h}^{b}\rbrace
$$

We define the function ${\Vert \cdot\Vert }_{{U}_{h,0}^{k}}$ such that:

$$
{\Vert {\widehat{v}}_{h}\Vert }_{{U}_{h,0}^{k}}^{2}={\sum}_{T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}}{|{\widehat{v}}_{T}|}_{1,T}^{2},\: \forall {\widehat{v}}_{h} \in {U}_{h,0}^{k}
$$

**Proposition 1**

The function ${\Vert \cdot\Vert }_{{U}_{h,0}^{k}}$ defines a norm on ${U}_{h,0}^{k}$.

**Proof:**

It suffices to show that for every ${\widehat{v}}_{h} \in {U}_{h,0}^{k}$,
${\Vert {\widehat{v}}_{h}\Vert }_{{U}_{h,0}^{k}}^{2}=0$ implies
${v}_{T}=0$ for every $T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$ and ${v}_{F}=0$
for every $F \in \mathcal{F}_{h}$. Note that ${\Vert {\widehat{v}}_{h}\Vert }_{{U}_{h,0}^{k}}^{2}=0$
implies that for every $T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$ :

$$
\nabla^{s}{v}_{T}=0, \:\text{ et }{v}_{T}{\mid}_{F}-{v}_{F}=0, \: \forall F \in {F}_{T}
$$

Take a cell $T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$ having at least one boundary face
$F \in {F}_{T}\cap \mathcal{F}_{h}^{b}$, so that ${v}_{F}=0$.
Then ${v}_{T}{\mid}_{F}=0$ because ${v}_{T}{\mid}_{F}-{v}_{F}=0$.
Combining this with $\nabla^{s}{v}_{T}=0$ and with a Korn inequality, we obtain ${v}_{T}=0$.
Moreover, since ${v}_{T}{\mid}_{F'}-{v}_{F'}=0, \:\: \forall F' \in {F}_{T}\setminus F$,
we deduce from this ${v}_{F'}=0$ for every $F' \in {F}_{T}$.
It suffices to extend this reasoning layer by layer of cells until all the cells
$T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}$ and all the faces $F \in \mathcal{F}_{h}$ have been covered. ■

The discrete problem is the following:

$$
& \text{Trouver } {\widehat{u}}_{h} \in {U}_{h,0}^{k} \text{ tel que :}

& {\sum}_{T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}}{a}_{T}({\widehat{u}}_{T},{\widehat{v}}_{T})
={\sum}_{T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}}{(f,{v}_{T})}_{{L}^{2}(T)}, \: \forall {\widehat{v}}_{T} \in {U}_{h,0}^{k}
$$

with

$$
{a}_{T}({\widehat{u}}_{T},{\widehat{v}}_{T})=2\mu \left\lbrace  {({E}_{T}({\widehat{u}}_{T}),{E}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T}))}_{{L}^{2}(T)}+{({S}_{\partial T}({\widehat{u}}_{T}),{S}_{\partial T}({\widehat{v}}_{T}))}_{{L}^{2}(\partial T)}\right\rbrace +\lambda{({D}_{T}({\widehat{u}}_{T}),{D}_{T}({\widehat{v}}_{T}))}_{{L}^{2}(T)}
$$

The right-hand side in is obtained by replacing the continuous operators by their discrete versions
and by adding the stabilisation term. We can show that there exists a unique solution
${\widehat{u}}_{h} \in {U}_{h,0}^{k}$ to the problem (this follows from the local stability property of Lemma 2).

Moreover, since the contributions in are computed locally to the cells,
it is very easy to parallelise these computations.

The HHO methods have optimal convergence rates on general meshes
both in the energy norm and in the $L^2$ norm (with an elliptic-regularity assumption).

**Theorem 1** (Error in the energy norm)

Let $k \geq 1$ and let $u \in {H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega_0;\mathbb{R}^{d})$,
the unique solution of the problem and ${\widehat{u}}_{h} \in {U}_{h,0}^{k}$
the unique solution of the problem . Also assuming the additional regularity
$u \in {H}^{k+2}(\Omega_0;\mathbb{R}^{d})$, there exists a constant $C>0$, independent of $h$,
$\mu$, and $\lambda$, such that:

$$
{\left({\sum}_{T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}}{|{\widehat{I}}_{T}^{k}u-{\widehat{u}}_{T}|}_{1,T}^{2}\right)}^{\frac{1}{2}} \le  C{h}^{k+1}(2\mu{|u|}_{{H}^{k+2}(\Omega_{0})}+\lambda{|\nabla \cdot u|}_{{H}^{k+1}(\Omega_{0})})
$$

**Proof:**

See Theorem 8 in [3]. ■

**Theorem 2** (Error in the $L^2$ norm)

Under the assumptions of Theorem 1 and also assuming elliptic regularity,
there exists a constant $C>0$, independent of $h$, $\mu$, and $\lambda$, such that:

$$
{\left({\sum}_{T \in \mathcal{T}_{h}}{\Vert {\Pi}_{T}^{k}u-{u}_{T}\Vert }_{{L}^{2}(T)}^{2}\right)}^{\frac{1}{2}}\le C{h}^{k+2}(2\mu{|u|}_{{H}^{k+2}(\Omega_{0})}+\lambda{|\nabla \cdot u|}_{{H}^{k+1}(\Omega_{0})})
$$

**Proof:**

See Theorem 11 in [3]. ■

The estimates of Theorem 1 and of Theorem 2 are robust in the incompressible limit
since they involve only the regularity of $\nabla \cdot u$ as a function of $\lambda$.
These convergence rates are confirmed numerically, as is the robustness.
Finally, it is important to recall that these convergence rates are one order higher than those obtained for
${H}^{1}$-conforming finite elements using polynomials of degree $k$ in the cells.

## 3 Implementation

The implementation in *code_aster* of new numerical methods that differ from the Lagrange elements can be difficult. We list the main reasons:

- Limited number of geometric supports possible in the mesh: triangle and quadrangle in 2D, and tetrahedron, hexahedron, prism and pyramid in 3D (no support of polyhedral meshes);
- Strong connection between the geometric support and the chosen element. The degree of approximation of the method is determined by the geometric support. For example, a linear approximation for triangles with 3 nodes (TRIA3) and a quadratic one for triangles with 6 nodes (TRIA6);
- The finite-element unknowns are supported only by physical nodes of the mesh (and not by faces or cells of the mesh, as is the case for the finite-volume, discontinuous Galerkin and HHO methods).

The main objective for the implementation of the HHO methods in *code_aster* is to reuse as much as possible the architecture of the code and not to modify the critical points of the code, which are:

- The data structures
- The finite-element catalogues
- The locality of the elementary computations
- The assembly operation

We limit ourselves, for the implementation of the HHO methods in *code_aster*, to the geometric supports that already exist.
Moreover, only the constant approximations $k=0$ (in thermal analysis only), linear $k=1$, quadratic $k=2$,
cubic $k=3$ and quartic $k=4$ are implemented. The constant approximation, $k=0$, is not available in mechanics because the kernel
of the strain tensor includes, in addition to the constant translations, the rigid rotations.
The various modifications are limited to two operators:

- [CREA_MAILLAGE](doc:u4.23.02): option MODI_HHO
- [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01): option HHO

### 3.1 HHO unknowns and numbering

We recall that the HHO unknowns, ${\widehat{v}}_{T}=({v}_{T},{v}_{\partial T})\in {U}_{T}^{k}$,
that we compute are the coefficients of the polynomials of the cells and of the faces (these coefficients have a priori no physical meaning).
Since the assembly must be carried out face by face (and cell by cell) and the HHO unknowns can be supported
only by physical nodes of the mesh in *code_aster*, we need each face of the mesh to possess
at least one node that is not shared with any other face of the mesh (typically a node located at the barycentre of the face) and that there
be a node at the barycentre of the cell.
This is the case in 2D for the triangle with 7 nodes (TRIA7) and the quadrangle with 9 nodes (QUAD9), and in 3D for the hexahedron with 27 nodes (HEXA27).
These geometric supports correspond to the quadratic elements for the isoparametric elements.
Unfortunately, there are not in *code_aster* tetrahedra, prisms and pyramids with this property.
This is why we have added a mesh converter in CREA_MAILLAGE named MODI_HHO
which makes it possible to transform a tetrahedron with 4 nodes into a tetrahedron with 15 nodes (likewise for the prisms (PENTA21) and the pyramids (PYRAM19)).
The new nodes are located at the barycentre of the faces and of the cell.

The face unknowns ${v}_{F}$ for the faces $F\in {F}_{h}$ are supported
by the node that is located at the barycentre of the face $F$.
These physical nodes of the mesh that support the face unknowns are named *unknown_nodes*.
Moreover, since the faces are assumed plane, we use the vertex nodes to describe the geometry of the cells and of the faces;
and to compute the various integrals. These physical nodes of the mesh that describe the geometry are named *geometric_nodes*.
Finally, the nodes located at the midpoints of the segments are not used and are named *empty_nodes*.
These different categories of nodes are illustrated here. These devices concerning the location of the HHO unknowns
make it possible to reuse the data structures and the assembly without modifications.

**Note** (Static condensation):

Static condensation (or Schur complement) makes it possible to eliminate locally the cell unknowns in the elementary contributions
(in the end, a smaller problem composed only of the face unknowns is solved).
One of the main difficulties is that the elementary contributions are computed locally to the element (cell or face)
and then assembled directly into the global problem using the numbering.
Whereas for the HHO methods, static condensation must be carried out before assembly in order to eliminate the cell unknowns.
For reasons of simplicity, static condensation is not implemented, so as not to change the architecture of the code.
However, it is possible to use it outside the operators with the HHO module in python. An example is given in [zzzz513f](doc:v1.01.513).

### 3.2 Dirichlet boundary conditions

The Dirichlet boundary conditions are imposed strongly by modifying the matrix and the loading.
The user must specify the imposed displacements on the Dirichlet boundary.
However, it is not obvious for the user to give the values of the face and cell unknowns
because these coefficients have a priori no physical meaning.
We have added an automatic translator which computes the $L^2$ projection, ${\Pi}_{F}^{k}({u}_{D})$,
and imposes the value ${v}_{F}={\Pi}_{F}^{k}({u}_{D})$ from the displacement ${u}_{D}$ given at the nodes of the face
(in the continuous sense). In practice, in AFFE_CHAR_CINE, it suffices to give the conditions on DX, DY and/or DZ
and the operator translates them automatically into conditions on the HHO polynomials.

### 3.3 Post-processing

The HHO unknowns are the coefficients of polynomials. These coefficients have a priori no physical meaning.
Thus, a post-processing must be applied in order to be able to visualise these results.
We create a new NOEU field which will interpolate, in the sense of the Lagrange elements, the HHO unknowns in the field HHO_DEPL
at the end of the analysis. The value of the displacement at a node of the mesh is the average of the values of the cell unknowns that contain this node.
Note that this field is continuous, unlike the HHO fields. It must be carefully noted that this field is not in equilibrium with that of the stresses.
It is there for purely visual purposes. The same post-processing is applied to the velocity and acceleration fields in the dynamic case.

### 3.4 Scope of use

The HHO method can be used only in static and transient non-linear mechanics ([STAT_NON_LINE](doc:u4.51.03)/[DYNA_NON_LINE](doc:u4.53.01))
and in linear and non-linear thermal analysis ([THER_LINEAIRE](doc:u4.54.01)/[THER_NON_LINE](doc:u4.54.02))

The following features are excluded:

- contact/friction
- model reduction
- macro-elements
- computation of vibration modes or of buckling criteria

The method can be used for the constitutive laws in small and large strains (model GDEF_LOG, PETIT, GREEN_LAGRANGE).

Moreover, the HHO method cannot be used simultaneously with a standard finite-element method.
The boundary conditions can be assigned only by elimination ([AFFE_CHAR_CINE](doc:u4.44.03) (_F)).

Most of the body and surface loadings are available.

The method works on all the 2D and 3D elements of the code. One must, where applicable, use [CREA_MAILLAGE](doc:u4.23.02)/MODI_HHO in order to
make the mesh compatible.

Certain parameters such as the stabilisation coefficient can be defined by zone in [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01)/HHO.
If no value is given, an automatic value is computed from the elastic parameters.
