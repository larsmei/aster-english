# r5.03.38 Isotropic multi-scale elasto-viscoplastic mechanical behaviour under irradiation: applications to irradiated reactor-pressure-vessel steels

**Summary:**

This document presents the formulation of the constitutive law VISC_ISOT_PLAS. It is an isotropic elasto-viscoplastic flow model representing the multi-scale effects of plasticity as well as the effects of irradiation. A large-strain modelling is available through the GDEF_LOG kinematics.

The parameters of the model comprise the elastic mechanical characteristics of the material (Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio) as well as the mechanical characteristics of the microstructure of the material considered: grain size and dislocation density for the unirradiated state, together with the concentration and size of the solute clusters for the irradiated state. The latter can be obtained directly from experiment by means of standard tensile tests.

The law supplies the von Mises stress as a function of the cumulative (visco)plastic strain. The hardening is entirely isotropic, which restricts the use of the law to monotonic loadings with small unloadings.

This modelling, developed mainly for needs specific to EDF, is above all designed to describe the monotonic mechanical behaviour of the steels of the pressure vessels of the French nuclear fleet of type 16MND5 (or of type A508 class 3) in the unirradiated and irradiated states.

## 1 Introduction

The constitutive laws usually available to describe reactor-pressure-vessel steel are empirical in nature, fitted to the experimental behaviour defined at a given temperature, strain rate and irradiation state. Their predictive capability is consequently limited and does not offer a convincing demonstration of the extrapolation of their domain of validity.

Multi-scale modelling represents a robust alternative by providing a physical constitutive description for each mechanism involved in plastic strain. In particular, taking into account the thermal activation of dislocation mobility makes it possible to predict, in a natural way, the effect of temperature and of strain rate.

The present document describes the integrated equations of the crystalline behaviour, leading to an isotropic polycrystalline law as a function of the local temperature and strain rate of a reactor-pressure-vessel steel characterised by the parameters of its microstructure: grain size and dislocation density for the unirradiated state, together with the concentration and the size of the solute clusters responsible for irradiation hardening for the irradiated state.

The law is designed to predict the monotonic behaviour of the steels of the pressure vessels of the French nuclear fleet of type 16MND5 (or of type A508 class 3) in the unirradiated and irradiated states. The parameters specific to each of the steels can be identified from tensile tests.

### 2.1 Notation and reminders on viscoplasticity

Recall that viscoplasticity denotes the theory of continuum mechanics which makes it possible to describe the anelastic behaviour of a material whose strain depends on the rate at which the loads are applied to the solid under consideration. It is therefore a particular case of plastic behaviour, the material undergoing permanent irreversible strains when a certain load level is reached. However, the plasticity criterion here also depends on the history of the loading (and on its rate), generally through a scalar internal state variable called the equivalent strain. It makes it possible to measure the capacity of the material to harden under the effect of its plastic (permanent) strain without fracturing, independently of the direction chosen. One speaks of isotropic hardening, and the material is described as ductile, as is the case for many metals and alloys.

The model involves the temperature explicitly, denoted $T \left( ^{\circ} C \right)$ when it is expressed in degrees Celsius or $T \left( K \right)$ in kelvin. In the final model, it is required that every temperature be expressed in degrees Celsius.

One places oneself here under the small-strain assumption. In particular, the total strains $\tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot}$ used in the constitutive relation VISC_ISOT_PLAS are the linearised strains. These are decomposed into an elastic part $\tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{elas}$ and a viscoplastic part $\tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{vp}$:

$$
\tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot} = \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{elas} + \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{vp}.
$$

The elastic part follows a standard isotropic linear Hooke’s law depending on Young’s modulus $E > 0$ and on Poisson’s ratio $\nu \in \left] -1; \frac{1}{2} \right[$ associated with the material used. Introducing the shear modulus $\mu:= \frac{E}{2 \left( 1+\nu \right)}$, the stress $\tensTwo{\sigma}$ is therefore expressed in the form:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma} = 2 \mu \, \left[ \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{elas} + \dfrac{\nu}{1 - 2 \nu} \text{Tr} \left( \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{elas} \right) \, \tensTwo{I} \right],
$$

where $\text{Tr}$ denotes the trace operator and $\tensTwo{I}$ the second-order identity tensor. One also introduces its deviatoric part $\tensTwo{\tilde{\sigma}} : = \tensTwo{\sigma} - \frac{1}{3} \text{Tr} \left( \tensTwo{\sigma}  \right) \tensTwo{I}$ as well as the equivalent stress (of von Mises type) $\sigma_{eq} :=  \sqrt{\frac{3}{2} \tensTwo{\tilde{\sigma}} : \tensTwo{\tilde{\sigma}} }$. Note that the normalisation factor $\frac{3}{2}$ makes it possible to ensure that $\sigma$ is indeed equal to $\sigma_{eq}$ in the case of a uniaxial (1D) loading. One then defines the following yield function $f$:

$$
f \left( \tensTwo{\sigma}, p, \dot{p} \right) := \sigma_{eq} - R \left( p, \dot{p} \right),
$$

where the isotropic hardening function $R$ (presented in the following section) depends both on the cumulative viscoplastic strain rate $\dot{p} : = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3} \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{vp} : \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{vp} }$ and on the cumulative viscoplastic strain $p(t) := \int_{0}^{t} \dot{p} \left( \tau \right) \, \text{d} \tau$, also called the equivalent strain. Note that the normalisation factor $\frac{2}{3}$ makes it possible to ensure that $\varepsilon^{vp}$ is indeed equal to $p$ in the uniaxial (1D) case. It is also mentioned that one of the specific features of this flow model is that the isotropic hardening law $R$ depends on $\dot{p}$ and not only on $p$, as is the case for the more standard isotropic hardening laws. No kinematic hardening is considered in this model, which restricts the use of the law to monotonic loadings with small unloadings.

It is now assumed that the flow rule comes from a viscoplastic potential and that it is associated. In other words, the evolution of the viscoplastic strain $\tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{vp}$ is governed by the flow equation and the flow direction is normal to the yield surface:

$$
\dot{\tensTwo{\varepsilon}}^{vp} = \lambda \tensTwo{n},
$$

where $\tensTwo{n} :=  \frac{\partial f}{\partial \tensTwo{\sigma}} = \frac{3 \tensTwo{\tilde{\sigma}}}{2\sigma_{eq}}$ denotes the normal to the yield surface and $\lambda$ corresponds to the viscoplastic multiplier. Its evolution is given by the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker conditions, also called consistency conditions:

$$
\begin{array}{l}
    \lambda \geq 0;                                              \\
    f \left( \tensTwo{\sigma}, p, \dot{p} \right) \leq 0;        \\
    \lambda \, f \left( \tensTwo{\sigma}, p, \dot{p} \right) = 0 .\\
\end{array}
$$

$$
\begin{split}\begin{array}{l}
    \lambda \geq 0;                                              \\
    f \left( \tensTwo{\sigma}, p, \dot{p} \right) \leq 0;        \\
    \lambda \, f \left( \tensTwo{\sigma}, p, \dot{p} \right) = 0 .\\
\end{array}\end{split}
$$

We do not enter here into energy considerations that go beyond the scope of this document. We simply mention that, under certain regularity assumptions, this formulation is equivalent to a problem of maximisation of the intrinsic dissipation, generalising Hill’s principle of maximum plastic work to the case of isotropic hardening. In particular, one deduces from  and from the fact that $\tensTwo{n}: \tensTwo{n} = \frac{3}{2}$ the following relation:

$$
\lambda = \dot{p}.
$$

One also checks that $\text{Tr} \left( \dot{\tensTwo{\varepsilon}}^{vp} \right) = 0$, that is to say that the viscoplastic strain is isochoric, *i.e.* with no change of volume. It only remains to describe the hardening function $R$ used in this model.

### 2.2 Components defining the isotropic hardening function

The isotropic hardening function $R$ introduced in the previous section is made up of several components, each associated with a component of the microstructure or with a mechanism of plasticity:

$$
R \left( p, \dot{p} \right) = \tensTwo{\sigma}_{HP} + \tensTwo{\sigma}_{VD} + \tensTwo{\sigma}_{VS} \left( p \right) + \tensTwo{\sigma}_{D} \left( \dot{p} \right) + \tensTwo{\sigma}_{E} \left( \dot{p} \right) + \sqrt{ \left(\tensTwo{\sigma}_{I} \right)^{2} + \left( \tensTwo{\sigma}_{auto} \left( p \right) \right)^{2} + \left( \tensTwo{\sigma}_{LT} \left( p, \dot{p} \right) \right)^{2} }
$$

The meaning of the various components is given briefly in the table below.

| $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{HP}$ | Component representing the effect of grain size (also called the Hall-Petch effect) |
| --- | --- |
| $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{VD}$ | Contribution of dynamic ageing |
| $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{VS}$ | Contribution of static ageing |
| $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{D}$ | Resistance to the drag of jogs on screw dislocations |
| $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{E}$ | Effective stress applied to the screw dislocations |
| $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{I}$ | Contribution of the irradiation defects |
| $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{auto}$ | Resistance induced by the interaction of dislocations of the same system |
| $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{LT}$ | Forest resistance (interactions with the other screw dislocations) |

In this balance, we omit the contribution of the intragranular carbides. On the one hand, their density is variable (lower bainite *vs* upper bainite) and, on the other hand, their contribution remains small compared with that of the dislocations. Thus, their possible contribution can be taken into account by slightly increasing the value of the initial dislocation density.

The components $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{D}$, $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{VS}$ and $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{VD}$ did not exist in the initial model described in . They bring second-order contributions compared with the initial contributions.

From now on we describe all the expressions retained for the various contributions. Their final versions are expressed in the International System of Units (SI), and an adaptation is carried out when this is not the case for the units of length and stress.

#### 2.2.1 The grain-size effect $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{HP}$ (also called the Hall-Petch effect)

The term $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{HP}$ does not follow from the multi-scale modelling, because grain-size effects remain very poorly understood. The Hall-Petch (HP) effect is therefore introduced with the help of experimental results. The effect has been confirmed and characterised in ferritic materials . It can be put in the form:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{HP} = \dfrac{\mu}{\mu \left( 25 ^{\circ}C \right)} \dfrac{K_{HP}}{\sqrt{d}},
$$

where $K_{HP}$ denotes the Hall-Petch constant (fixed at $0.45\, MPa\sqrt{m}$) and $d$ represents the mean size of the lath packets expressed in metres, also called TAILLE_GRAIN in the material parameters. Normalisation by the shear modulus $\mu$ is introduced in order to account for the dependence on temperature. In order to keep a certain consistency with , one uses the values of $E \left( T^{\circ}C \right) := \left( 2.6 MPa \right) \left( 78821 - 23.721 \, T \left( ^{\circ}C \right) \right)$ and $\nu = 0.3$ given by the RCC-M for the expression of $\mu \left( 25 ^{\circ}C \right): = \frac{E \left( 25^{\circ}C \right)}{2 \left(1 + \nu \right)}$. One then deduces the following expression for the contribution due to the Hall-Petch effect:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{HP} = \mu \, \sqrt{\dfrac{K_{HP0} }{TAILLE\_GRAIN}}
$$

where the value of the constant $K_{HP0}$ is given in the table below. An adaptation of the units is carried out when it is not the SI units that are chosen for $\mu$ and TAILLE_GRAIN.

#### 2.2.2 Dynamic ageing $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{VD}$ and static ageing $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{VS}$

These empirical terms were introduced in order to account for the yield-point drop as well as the slight increase of the yield stress beyond $150\,^{\circ}C$. Their contribution to the overall behaviour is small, but they influence the determination of the yield stress. In particular, static ageing $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{VS}$ generates a softening phenomenon during the elasto-viscoplastic transition. The two expressions that account for the experimental results are:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{VD} = A_{VD} \, \min \left( \left\langle B_{VD} \, T \left( ^{\circ}C \right) - C_{VD} \right\rangle_{+}, 1 \right)
$$

and

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{VS} \left( p \right) = \left\langle A_{VS} - B_{VS} \left( T \left( ^{\circ}C \right) + C_{VS} \right) \right\rangle_{+} \, e^{-D_{VS} \, p},
$$

where $\left\langle \bullet \right\rangle_{+}$ denotes the positive part of the quantity and the values of the various constants are given in the tables below. An adaptation of the units is carried out when it is not the SI units that are chosen for $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{VD}$ and $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{VS}$.

#### 2.2.3 The resistance of the jogs $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{D}$

This new component represents the low mobility of the jogs on the screw dislocations generated by intersection with the non-coplanar dislocations. Their motion is not conservative, that is to say that it requires the thermally activated emission of interstitials or of vacancies, which adds an additional resistance to the motion of the screw dislocations already subjected to lattice friction (*i.e.* the term $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{E}$). The initial expression of $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{D}$ as an Arrhenius exponential is often simplified to a power-type expression:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{D} \left( \dot{p} \right) = \sigma_{D0} \left( \dfrac{\dot{p}}{\gamma_{D0}} \right)^{\frac{1}{N_{D}}},
$$

where the values of the various constants are given in the table below. An adaptation of the units is carried out when it is not the SI units that are chosen for $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{D}$.

| $\sigma_{D0}$ | $5.0 \, 10^{6} \, Pa$ |
| --- | --- |
| $\gamma_{D0}$ | $10^{-6} \, s^{-1}$ |
| $N_{D}$ | $10$ |

#### 2.2.4 The effective stress $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{E}$

Following the discussion in , the effective stress in reactor-pressure-vessel steel (characterised by a high initial dislocation density) can be given by the equation:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{E} \left( \dot{p} \right) = \sigma_{E0}  \left( \left\langle 1 - \dfrac{T \left( K \right) }{ T_{a} \left( \dot{p} \right) } \right\rangle_{+} \right)^{2}
$$

with $\left\langle \bullet \right\rangle_{+}$ the positive part of the quantity, $T \left( K \right) = T \left( ^{\circ}C \right) + 273.15$ the temperature expressed in kelvin and the activation temperature

$$
T_{a} \left( \dot{p} \right) = \dfrac{\Delta G_{0}}{ k_{B} \ln \left( \frac{ \rho_{m} b F l_{c}}{\dot{p}} \right) },
$$

where the constants here are the activation energy $\Delta G_{0} = 0.84 \, eV$, the Boltzmann constant $k_{B} = 8.62 \, 10^{-5} eV \, K^{-1}$, the density of mobile dislocations $\rho_{m} = 10^{13} \, m^{-2}$, the norm of the Burgers vector $b = 2.48 \, 10^{-10} \, m$, the frequency factor $F = 10^{13} \, s^{-1}$ and the critical size $l_{c} = 10^{-8} \, m$. It is this component $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{E}$ that carries the principal sensitivity to strain rate and to temperature. It is expressed here in the following form:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{E} \left( \dot{p} \right) = \sigma_{E0}  \left( \left\langle 1 - \dfrac{T \left( ^{\circ}C \right) + T_{0} }{A_{E}} \left\langle B_{E} - \ln \left( C_{E} \, \dot{p} \right) \right\rangle_{+} \right\rangle_{+} \right)^{2}
$$

#### 2.2.5 Contribution of the irradiation defects $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{I}$

In addition to the components of the microstructure, neutron irradiation generates other defects causing irradiation hardening. The principal defects identified are the solute clusters, revealed by atom-probe tomography, and the dislocation loops (often confused with the matrix damage). Analyses of the probe results and of electron microscopy  show that the matrix damage increases rapidly and saturates at a fluence lower than $10^{23} \, n \, m^{-2}$, whereas the concentration of solute clusters C_AMAS, whose diameter TAILLE_AMAS is about $3 \, nm$, increases proportionally with the fluence.

For simplicity, we consider that only the solute clusters exist in the material and that the irradiation hardening is a function only of the material parameters C_AMAS and TAILLE_AMAS. The contribution of the clusters was determined by dislocation-dynamics simulations and by molecular dynamics, which made it possible to establish an analytical equation in . However, the equation is relatively complex and requires numerical precautions. Now, a simple empirical expression proved sufficiently robust to reproduce the values of the analytical equation. It is therefore this numerical expression that was finally retained for the law:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{I} = \mu \, \sqrt{ B_{I} \left( C_{I} \, TAILLE\_AMAS  \right)^{D_{I}} \, \left( C_{L} \, C\_AMAS \right)^{\frac{E_{I}}{F_{I}}} }
$$

when $C_{L} \, C\_AMAS > A_{I}$ and otherwise $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{I} = 0$. The values of the various constants are given in the table below. An adaptation of the units is carried out when it is not the SI units that are chosen for $\mu$, TAILLE_AMAS and C_AMAS.

| $C_{L}$ | $10^{-22} \, m^{3}$ |
| --- | --- |
| $A_{I}$ | $10^{-2}$ |
| $B_{I}$ | $3.35 \, 10^{-8}$ |
| $C_{I}$ | $10^{9} \, m^{-1}$ |
| $D_{I}$ | $2.3$ |
| $E_{I}$ | $8$ |

#### 2.2.6 The self-interaction $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{auto}$ associated with the evolution of the dislocation density

One of the original features of the model consists in dividing the dislocation population into four groups formed of screw dislocations, instead of the twelve usual systems in crystal plasticity. Indeed, since the only variability for a screw dislocation is its line direction (*i.e.* the Burgers vector $\vector{b}$), there exist only four different directions of $\vector{b}$: $\left[ 1 1 1 \right]$, $\left[ \bar{1} 1 1 \right]$, $\left[ 1 \bar{1} 1 \right]$ and $\left[ 1 1 \bar{1} \right]$.

The interaction between parallel screw dislocations is the self-interaction (from the English *self-interaction*). It therefore corresponds to the interaction with a quarter of the population, whence the formula:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{auto} \left( p \right) = \mu \, b \, \sqrt{\dfrac{\rho \left( p \right)}{4}},
$$

where $\mu$ still denotes the shear modulus, $b$ the norm of the Burgers vector $\vector{b}$ (here fixed at $2.48 \, 10^{-10} \, m$) and where $\rho$ represents the (line) density of dislocations in the material, whose evolution must now be described. This description differs from the initial model  and makes it possible to take better account of the phenomena associated with large strains (available only in the 'GDEF_LOG' formalism for this behaviour). That is why we set it out in detail.

Thus, as has just been seen, the interactions between dislocations depend on the dislocation density $\rho$. Because of storage and of the need to create new mobile dislocations, this density increases with the cumulative plastic strain $p$, leading to the hardening of the material.

An important development of the initial model  consists in introducing a new term representing the storage of non-screw dislocations, which is added to the initial storage of screw dislocations.

During plastic strain, in addition to classical storage, the mobile dislocations cut the other dislocations, forming immobile non-screw jogs (*jogs* in English). At small strain, the density of these jogs is very low, which is why it was neglected in the initial model . However, at large strain, their density becomes predominant because they cannot annihilate easily: one speaks of thermally activated annihilation. This density is denoted $\rho_{j}$ ($j$ for jog), to be distinguished from the density of the dislocations trapped and stored after their motion, denoted $\rho_{s}$. The evolution of the two densities gives that of the total density $\rho$:

$$
\dfrac{\text{d} \rho}{\text{d} p} = \dfrac{\text{d} \rho_{s}}{\text{d} p} + \dfrac{\text{d} \rho_{j}}{\text{d} p}.
$$

According to the Kocks-Mecking theory, the dislocation densities $\rho_{s}$ and $\rho_{j}$ become immobile after having travelled a distance denoted $\lambda_{s}$ and $\lambda_{j}$, respectively. Nevertheless, the two densities saturate when the mean distance between the two types of dislocations becomes smaller than $y_{s}$ and $y_{j}$, respectively. For simplicity, we normalise all the distances by the norm of the Burgers vector $b$. We thus carry out the following normalisations: $L_{s}:= \frac{\lambda_{s}}{b}$, $L_{j}:= \frac{\lambda_{j}}{b}$, $f_{s}:= \frac{y_{s}}{b}$ and $f_{j}:= \frac{y_{j}}{b}$. The evolutions of the two populations being independent, we can therefore write:

$$
\dfrac{1}{M} \, \dfrac{\text{d} \rho_{s}}{\text{d} p} = \frac{1}{b^{2} L_{s}} - f_{s} \rho_{s};
\dfrac{1}{M} \, \dfrac{\text{d} \rho_{j}}{\text{d} p } = \frac{1}{b^{2} L_{j}} - f_{j} \rho_{j},
$$

$$
\begin{align}\begin{aligned}\dfrac{1}{M} \, \dfrac{\text{d} \rho_{s}}{\text{d} p} = \frac{1}{b^{2} L_{s}} - f_{s} \rho_{s};\\\dfrac{1}{M} \, \dfrac{\text{d} \rho_{j}}{\text{d} p } = \frac{1}{b^{2} L_{j}} - f_{j} \rho_{j},\end{aligned}\end{align}
$$

with $M$ the Taylor factor (which we consider equal to $3$). Analytical integration of each evolution equation supplies the following two expressions for the densities:

$$
\rho_{s} \left( p \right) = \dfrac{1}{b^{2} L_{s} f_{s}} - A_{s} \, e^{-M \, f_{s} \, p};
\rho_{j} \left( p \right) = \dfrac{1}{b^{2} L_{j} f_{j}} - A_{j} \, e^{-M \, f_{j} \, p},
$$

$$
\begin{align}\begin{aligned}\rho_{s} \left( p \right) = \dfrac{1}{b^{2} L_{s} f_{s}} - A_{s} \, e^{-M \, f_{s} \, p};\\\rho_{j} \left( p \right) = \dfrac{1}{b^{2} L_{j} f_{j}} - A_{j} \, e^{-M \, f_{j} \, p},\end{aligned}\end{align}
$$

where the constants $A_{s}$ and $A_{j}$ must be determined from the initial conditions. On the one hand, one assumes that at the beginning of the strain the jog density $\rho_{j}$ is zero, which makes it possible to determine $A_{j}$:

$$
A_{j} = \frac{1}{b^{2} L_{j} f_{j}}.
$$

On the other hand, one assumes that the initial dislocation density $\rho$ is given by the material parameter D_DISLOC, which supplies the value of $A_{s}$:

$$
A_{s} = \frac{1}{b^{2} L_{s} f_{s}} - D\_DISLOC.
$$

Furthermore, since the motion of the jogs is restricted, we consider that they cannot move in order to annihilate. For simplicity, we therefore assume that $M \, f_{j} \approx 1$. One deduces that the total density is written:

$$
\rho \left( p \right) = D\_DISLOC \, e^{-M \, f_{s} \, p} + \frac{1}{b^{2} L_{s} f_{s}} \left( 1 - e^{-M \, f_{s} \, p} \right) + \frac{M}{b^{2} L_{j}} \left( 1 - e^{-p} \right)
$$

This expression is simplified still further by assuming that, at very large strain, the jog density on each system cannot exceed the saturation density of the screw dislocations. Since there are three types of jogs for each screw dislocation (intersections with the three other screw dislocations), we consider that the final jog density $\frac{M}{b^{2} L_{j}}$ is approximately equal to three times the density of the screw dislocations. One deduces that $L_{j} = L_{s} f_{s}$ and the previous expression is reduced to its final form:

$$
\rho \left( p \right) = D\_DISLOC \, e^{-M \, f_{s} \, p} + \frac{1}{b^{2} L_{s} f_{s}} \left( 4 - e^{- M \, f_{s} \, p} - 3 \, e^{-p} \right).
$$

The indices can now be omitted by renaming $L$ the mean free distance $L_{s}$ (normalised by $b$) as well as $f$ the annihilation distance $f_{s}$ (also normalised by $b$), whose expressions will soon be specified. Renaming $A_{auto}$ the Taylor factor $M$ and introducing $B_{auto} : = \frac{b^{2}}{4}$, one obtains the final expression of the self-interaction $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{auto} = \mu \, b \, \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{4}}$ used for the law VISC_ISOT_PLAS:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{auto} \left( p \right) = \mu \, \sqrt{B_{auto} \, D\_DISLOC \, e^{ - A_{auto} \, f \, p  } + \frac{1}{4 \, L \, f} \left( 4 - e^{- A_{auto} \, f \, p} - 3 e^{-p} \right) },
$$

with the following expressions retained for the mean free distance $L$ and the annihilation distance $f$ (both normalised)

$$
L = \frac{1}{A_{L} + B_{L} \sqrt{C_{L} \, C\_AMAS } };
$$

$$
f = \min \left( A_{f}, B_{f} + C_{f} \left( T \left( ^{\circ}C \right) + T_{0} \right) \right) .
$$

The values of the various constants are given in the table below. An adaptation of the units is carried out when it is not the SI units that are chosen for $\mu$, D_DISLOC and C_AMAS.

| $A_{auto}$ | $3$ |
| --- | --- |
| $B_{auto}$ | $1.5376 \, 10^{-20} \, m^{2}$ |

| $A_{L}$ | $2.222222222 \, 10^{-4}$ |
| --- | --- |
| $B_{L}$ | $6.7925e \, 10^{-6}$ |
| $C_{L}$ | $10^{-22} \, m^{3}$ |

| $A_{f}$ | $6.8$ |
| --- | --- |
| $B_{f}$ | $1.178$ |
| $C_{f}$ | $0.019 \, ^{\circ} C^{-1}$ |
| $T_{0}$ | $273.15 \, ^{\circ} C$ (*i.e.* absolute zero) |

**Note**

It will be observed from the foregoing that $A_{auto}$ is nothing other than the Taylor factor and that $B_{auto} = \frac{b^{2}}{4}$, that is to say one quarter of the square of the norm of the Burgers vector. Finally, it is also specified that $A_{L} = \frac{b}{\lambda_{0}}$ with $\lambda_{0} = 1.116 \, \mu m$ the mean free path in the unirradiated material.

#### 2.2.7 Contribution of the line tension $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{LT}$, also called the forest resistance

The interaction with the forest dislocations (*i.e.* different Burgers vectors $\vector{b}$) must account for the interaction with all the other dislocations. The maximum contribution is therefore $\mu b \sqrt{\frac{3 \rho}{4}} = \sqrt{3} \tensTwo{\sigma}_{auto}$. However, as explained in , the real contribution decreases with the effective stress $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{E}$ because of the curvature of the non-screw dislocations, which reduces the resistance of the junctions. This interaction $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{LT}$ (*long-term* in English) can then be expressed by the following formula:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{LT} \left( p, \dot{p} \right) = \left\langle \sqrt{3} \tensTwo{\sigma}_{auto} \left( p \right) - \tensTwo{\sigma}_{E} \left( \dot{p} \right) \right\rangle_{+},
$$

where one recalls that $\left\langle \bullet \right\rangle_{+}$ denotes the positive part of the quantity. The following section describes the numerical implementation of this continuum model.

## 3 Numerical implementation

The numerical implementation of the continuum model described in the previous section is carried out using the constitutive-law integration module MFront . One should refer to the user’s guide [U2.10.02](doc:u2.10.02) for further details concerning its coupling with code_aster, as well as to the file *VISC_ISOT_PLAS.mfront* (available in the sources) for the details of the writing of the constitutive law in the MFront formalism.

First of all, the formulation retained in order to discretise the problem is described. Within the isotropic hardening function $R$ defined in , one begins by isolating the term $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{D}$ associated with the resistance of the jogs, by introducing a pseudo-hardening $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{C} := R - \tensTwo{\sigma}_{D}$ and then associating with it a pseudo-yield surface:

$$
\tensTwo{\sigma}_{D} \left( \dot{p} \right) = \sigma_{eq} - \left( \underbrace{R \left( p, \dot{p} \right) - \tensTwo{\sigma}_{D} \left( \dot{p} \right)}_{:= \tensTwo{\sigma}_{C} \left( p, \dot{p} \right)}  \right)
$$

The power relation  defining $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{D}$ is then inverted in order to obtain an expression for the equivalent viscoplastic strain rate $\dot{p}$ in the form of a *Perzyna*-type formulation:

$$
\dot{p} = \gamma_{D0} \left( \dfrac{ \left\langle \sigma_{eq} - \tensTwo{\sigma}_{C} \left( p , \dot{p} \right) \right\rangle_{+} }{\sigma_{D0}} \right)^{N_{D}},
$$

where $\left\langle \bullet \right\rangle_{+}$ denotes the positive part of the quantity. Thus, in the final implementation of the law, it is the difference $\sigma_{eq} - \tensTwo{\sigma}_{C}$ that induces the plastic strain rate, although this rate also enters the stress $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{C}$.

### 3.1 Elements of the methodology used in MFront

The @DSL directive (for *Domain Specific Language* in English) used in MFront to describe the constitutive law VISC_ISOT_PLAS is the DSL *ImplicitII*. The latter is identical to the DSL *Implicit* except that it does not predefine the elastic strain tensor as the first internal state variable. Indeed, as will be shown, the only real unknown to be computed here will be the increment of equivalent viscoplastic strain $\Delta p$.

**Note**

Because of the dependence of our pseudo-hardening $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{C}$ on the equivalent viscoplastic strain rate $\dot{p}$, we were not able to choose directly: neither the DSL *IsotropicMisesCreep*, which treats the case $\dot{p} = \phi \left( \sigma_{eq} \right)$; nor the DSL *IsotropicStrainHardeningMisesCreep*, which treats the case $\dot{p} = \phi \left( \sigma_{eq}, p \right)$; nor the DSL *IsotropicPlasticMisesFlow*, which treats the case $\phi \left( \sigma_{eq}, p \right) \leq 0$ with $\dot{p} \geq 0$ and $\dot{p} \, \phi \left( \sigma_{eq}, p \right) = 0$; nor even the *StandardElastoViscoplasticity brick*.

A fully implicit formulation is used, *i.e.* the predefined MFront parameter $\theta$ is fixed at $1$. In addition, the solution algorithm chosen is the Newton-Raphson method and the Jacobian is computed analytically. A check of the types of physical units used to describe the constitutive law is carried out at compilation of the MFront file, thanks to the activation of the @Qt directive (for *Quantity type* in English).

The strategy for solving the constitutive law over a time interval $\left[ t, t + \Delta t \right]$ is that classically carried out in MFront and described in . It is merely adapted to our case. One therefore assumes that the increment of total strains $\Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot} : =  \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot} \left( t + \Delta t \right) - \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot} \left( t \right)$ is known.

One begins by initialising the local variables thanks to the @InitLocalVariables directive. Then, with the @Predictor directive, one makes an elastic prediction $\sigma_{eq}^{pred}$ and $\tensTwo{n}^{pred} := \frac{3 \tensTwo{\tilde{\sigma}}^{pred}}{2 \sigma_{eq}^{pred}}$, assuming that the increment of elastic strains $\Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{elas}$ is equal to $\Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot}$ (or, in other words, that the increment of viscoplastic strains $\Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{vp}$ is zero):

$$
\Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{elas} = \Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot}
$$

If one checks $\sigma_{eq}^{pred} - \tensTwo{\sigma}_C \left( p \left( t \right), 0 \right) < 0$, then one is indeed in the elastic regime and there is nothing more to be done: $\Delta p = 0$, $\Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{vp} = 0$ and Hooke’s law supplies the stress increment $\Delta \tensTwo{\sigma}$.

Otherwise, the increment of elastic strains $\Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{elas}$ must be corrected by an increment of viscoplastic strain:

$$
\Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{vp} = \int_{t}^{t + \Delta t} \dot{p} \left( \tau \right) \, \tensTwo{n} \left( \tau \right) \, \text{d} \tau \approx \Delta p \, n \left( t + \Delta t \right),
$$

the last equality being an approximation which is assumed from now on. Following the reasoning of , one can show that one then has the following relations: $\tensTwo{n} \left( t + \Delta t \right)  = \tensTwo{n}^{pred}$ and $\sigma_{eq} \left( t + \Delta t \right) = \sigma_{eq}^{pred} - 3 \mu \Delta p$. One deduces that the relation  taken at time $t + \Delta t$ is discretised in the following form:

$$
\dfrac{\Delta p}{ \Delta t} - \gamma_{D0} \left( \dfrac{ \left\langle \sigma_{eq}^{pred} - 3 \mu \Delta p - \tensTwo{\sigma}_{C} \left( p + \Delta p , \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} \right) \right\rangle_{+} }{\sigma_{D0}} \right)^{N_{D}} = 0.
$$

Denoting by $F$ the previous left-hand side, the previous relation is rewritten in the form $F \left( \Delta p \right) = 0$. One is therefore led to solve a scalar equation in a scalar unknown $\Delta p$ using the Newton-Raphson method via the @Integrator directive, which will be set out in the following section. Once the zero of the function $F$ has been found, the stress increment can then be computed thanks to Hooke’s law and via the @ComputeFinalStress directive:

$$
\Delta \sigma = 2 \mu \left( \vector{\tensTwo{I}} + \dfrac{\nu}{1 - 2 \nu} \tensTwo{I} \otimes \tensTwo{I} \right) : \left( \Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot} - \Delta p \, \tensTwo{n}^{pred} \right),
$$

where $\vector{\tensTwo{I}}$ denotes the fourth-order identity tensor. Finally, thanks to the @TangentOperator directive, one computes analytically the tangent operator $D_{t} : = \frac{ \partial \left( \Delta \tensTwo{\sigma} \right) }{ \partial \left( \Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}_{tot}  \right)}$ by differentiating the previous expression. Indeed, having a single scalar unknown directly supplies the inversion of the Jacobian

$$
\dfrac{ \partial \left( \Delta p \right) }{ \partial \left( \Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot} \right)} = - \,\dfrac{ \frac{\partial F}{\partial \sigma_{eq}^{pred} } \, \frac{ \partial \sigma_{eq}^{pred}}{\partial \left( \Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot} \right)}  }{ F' \left( \Delta p \right) }
$$

while the computation of the quantities $\frac{ \partial \sigma_{eq}^{pred}}{\partial \left( \Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot} \right)}$ and $\frac{ \partial \tensTwo{n}^{pred} }{ \partial \left( \Delta \tensTwo{\varepsilon}^{tot} \right)}$ is standard and set out in .

### 3.2 Details of the solution by the Newton-Raphson method

Recall that the Newton-Raphson method is an iterative method making it possible to find the zero of a function $F$ starting from a starting point $\Delta p^{0}$ and by setting successively:

$$
\Delta p^{n+1} = \Delta p^{n} - \dfrac{F \left( \Delta p^n \right) }{F' \left( \Delta p^n \right) },
$$

where one recalls that $F$ is here defined explicitly by the left-hand side of  and that its derivative can be computed analytically by differentiation. One checks in passing that it is a continuous function but only piecewise $C^{1}$, because of the positive parts appearing in many terms of $\tensTwo{\sigma}_{C}$.

Although here the zero seems correctly defined in a unique way, it is known that the Newton-Raphson method may fail to converge in this kind of situation. In particular, the choice of a good starting point is necessary. Moreover, it turns out that $\Delta p^{0} = 0$ is the worst, since it necessarily leads to non-convergence of the algorithm.

A secant method would have been better suited to our case (at the price of reducing the order of quadratic convergence to about $1.6$), but it was wished to remain within the MFront formalism in order to solve this equation and *to stretch the Newton-Raphson method a little*. This is in fact exactly what certain internal algorithms of *Code_Aster* do. Our starting point will therefore be chosen from an interval bracketing the zero that is sought.

One first sets $\Delta p^{try} = 10^{-11}$. Then, two cases are distinguished in the @Predictor directive. On the one hand, if $F \left( \Delta p^{try} \right) < 0$, then one sets $\Delta p^{left} = 10^{n_{0}-1} \Delta p^{try}$ and $\Delta p^{right} = 10^{n_{0}} \Delta p^{try}$ with $n_{0}$ the smallest strictly positive integer for which $F \left( \Delta p^{right} \right) > 0$. On the other hand, if $F \left( \Delta p^{try} \right) > 0$, then one sets $\Delta p^{left} = 10^{-n_{0}} \Delta p^{try}$ and $\Delta p^{right} = 10^{1-n_{0}} \Delta p^{try}$ with $n_{0}$ the smallest strictly positive integer for which $F \left( \Delta p^{left} \right) < 0$.

At the end of this process, which requires only evaluations of a scalar function, one has an interval $\left[  \Delta p^{left},  \Delta p^{right} \right]$ which brackets the zero that is sought. One then defines our starting point for the Newton-Raphson method $\Delta p^{0} : = \Delta p^{left}$.

However, the interval will also be required to be stable for the Newton-Raphson method, that is to say that the $\Delta p^{n+1}$ defined by  must remain in the interval $\left[  \Delta p^{left},  \Delta p^{right} \right]$. During the @Predictor directive, as long as this condition is not satisfied, a bisection $\Delta p^{n+1}=\frac{\Delta p^{left} + \Delta p^{right}}{2}$ is used instead, reducing the size of the interval until one can switch to a Newton-Raphson method. However, even when one has switched to the Newton-Raphson method, one continues to reduce the size of the interval at each iteration by distinguishing the cases $F \left( \Delta p^{n+1} \right)$ positive and negative. In other words, one sets $\Delta p^{left} = \Delta p^{n+1}$ if $F \left( \Delta p^{n+1} \right) < 0$ or else $\Delta p^{right} = \Delta p^{n+1}$ if $F \left( \Delta p^{n+1} \right) > 0$, so that the interval $\left[  \Delta p^{left},  \Delta p^{right} \right]$ always brackets the zero that is sought but its size decreases as the successive iterations of the algorithm proceed.

The stopping criterion of the DSL *ImplicitII* is the condition $vert F left( Delta p^{n} right) vert < epsilon$ where the predefined MFront parameter $epsilon$ is fixed at $5.0 \, 10^{-14}$ and corresponds to the keyword RESI_INTE. Likewise, the maximum number of iterations in the Newton-Raphson algorithm is fixed at $100$ via the predefined MFront parameter $iterMax$ corresponding to the keyword ITER_INTE_MAXI. In a few rare cases, the extreme sensitivity of the function $F$ with respect to its variable $\Delta p$ means that the stopping criterion is not satisfied but that machine epsilon has been reached for $\Delta p$ (and therefore that one has converged). Reducing the size of the interval at each iteration makes it possible to detect and to eliminate these cases without adding an additional stopping criterion. Likewise, it happens in very rare cases that the algorithm oscillates between two very close values, whose difference is of the order of machine epsilon. In a similar way, the size of the interval bracketing the zero makes it possible to handle this kind of situation. Finally, the choice of the stopping criterion at $5.0 \, 10^{-14}$ is established by a heuristic coming from the use of this law, which significantly excludes the two machine-epsilon phenomena described previously.
