# r4.03.05 Parametric and non-parametric probabilistic models in dynamics

**Summary:**

This document describes two probabilistic approaches, one parametric and the other non-parametric, in order to take into account model uncertainties and modelling uncertainties for dynamic systems in structural mechanics. The non-parametric approach is specific to the solution on a modal basis of linear systems (in transient or harmonic analysis) and to the solution on a modal basis of systems with localised non-linearities (in transient analysis). The parametric approach is a priori universal, but it is presented more particularly in the case where it is combined with the non-parametric approach.

## 1 General points

Uncertainties, even when they are small, can significantly change the prediction of the vibratory behaviour of the structure ([bib13], [bib16]). It is therefore necessary to take them into account in a quantifiable and explicit manner in order to increase the realism and the robustness of the predictions. In this context, a probabilistic model of the uncertainties contributes to the realism of the approach.

The classical probabilistic approach, called parametric, makes it possible to incorporate into the mechanical analysis the uncertainties on the data, for example the parametric uncertainties on the geometry, the boundary conditions or the material properties. In this approach, each parameter identified as a source of random uncertainties is modelled by a random variable. The input parameters of the model being thus characterised, the probabilistic numerical methods seek to characterise in a probabilistic way the result quantity or quantities of the model. For complex structures, for which the vibratory behaviour depends on a large number of parameters, this type of probabilistic analysis is limited by the large quantity of information necessary to characterise the input parameters and by the difficulties of implementing the propagation of the variability.

A new approach, called the non-parametric probabilistic approach to random uncertainties in structural dynamics, was recently proposed by C. Soize ([bib20] to [bib24]). This approach makes it possible to take into account model uncertainties (uncertainties on the geometry for example) and modelling uncertainties (uncertainties on the beam or plate kinematics for example). It is based on the construction of random matrices of linear dynamic systems, after projection onto a modal basis.

These two probabilistic approaches, one parametric and the other non-parametric, are complementary. Thus a mixed approach, parametric and non-parametric, can be developed (an original method that has given rise to publications ([bib6] and [bib11] ). In particular, this mixed method is well suited to taking uncertainties into account in the analysis of a non-linear dynamic system composed of a linear structure reduced on a modal basis and of localised non-linearities. Indeed, the uncertainties at the level of the linear structure can be treated naturally by the non-parametric approach and the uncertainties on the non-linearities can be treated naturally by the parametric approach.

The basic numerical model of the non-linear dynamic system is a finite-element model which will be called the « mean finite-element model ». Whether one is concerned with the parametric or the non-parametric approach, the probability laws must be defined adequately and as objectively as possible from this mean model. A Gaussian model of the random matrices is not suited to low-frequency dynamics (negative eigenfrequencies). In order to construct the corresponding probability laws, one uses Jayne's principle of maximum entropy ([bib14], [bib15], [bib17]) as well as the available information (mean finite-element model, algebraic properties of the matrices, etc.)

In this document, we present the non-parametric approach for transient or harmonic solutions of the dynamic system. The parametric approach is presented more particularly in the case where it is combined with the non-parametric method.

Readers seeking the fundamental results of stochastic dynamics may refer to [18] and readers seeking the theoretical details of the probabilistic approach presented in this document may refer to [bib19]. Examples of uses of the approach are given in [bib5], and [bib7]. In [bib10], experimental tests made it possible to show the predictive character of the approach.

## 2 Modellings of the dynamic system

### 2.1 Mean finite-element model

#### 2.1.1 Transient solution in absolute coordinates

In the absolute frame, the mechanical system is modelled by the finite-element method. This basic model (in general the one that would have been used in the deterministic study) is designated under the name «mean finite-element model». All quantities relating to the mean models are underlined.

Let $t\mapsto \underline{\mathrm{y}}(t)$ be the transient response in the absolute frame of the «mean finite-element model» defined on the time interval of the study $[0,T]$ and with values in ${ℝ}^{k}$ where $k$ is the number of degrees of freedom. The mass, damping and stiffness matrices are respectively denoted $\left[\underline{M}\right]$ , $\left[\underline{D}\right]$ and $\left[\underline{K}\right]$ .

The transient response $\underline{y}(t)$ of the «mean finite-element model» satisfies the following discretised non-linear differential equation:

$\left[\underline{M}\right]\underline{\ddot{y}}(t)+\left[\underline{D}\right]\underline{\dot{y}}+{f}_{c}(t,\underline{\dot{y}(t)},\underline{y(t)},\underline{w})=f(t)$ , *t*  ,    eq 2.1.1-1

with the initial conditions,

$\underline{y}(0)=\dot{\underline{y}}(0)=0$ ,                                    eq 2.1.1-2

- $f(t)\in {ℝ}^{m}$ represents the finite-element discretisation of the external forces.
- ${f}_{c}(t,\underline{y}(t),\underline{\dot{y}}(t),\underline{w})\in {ℝ}^{m}$ corresponds to the localised non-linearities (for example due to elastic impact stops). The elements ${\underline{w}}_{1,}\mathrm{...},{\underline{w}}_{v}$ of the vector $\underline{w}\in {ℝ}^{\nu}$ represent a set of parameters defining these non-linearities ( for example gap, impact stiffness, impact damping, etc.).

### 2.2 Mean reduced matrix model

One assumes that the vibration energy of the dynamic response is mainly localised in the low-frequency domain. One can therefore construct the mean reduced matrix model by projecting $\underline{y}(t)$ or $\underline{y}(\omega )$ onto the eigenspace generated by the first n modes of the associated homogeneous conservative linear dynamic system (infinite gaps) (blocked supports), which is written,

$\left[\underline{K}\right]\underline{\varphi}=\lambda \left[\underline{M}\right]\varphi$ .                                    eq 2.2-1

$0\le \underline{{\lambda}_{1}}\le \underline{{\lambda}_{2}}\le \mathrm{...}\le {\lambda}_{n}$                                 eq 2.2-2

$\text{<}\left[\underline{M}\right]{\varphi}_{\alpha},{\varphi}_{\beta}\text{>}=\underline{{\mu}_{\alpha}}{\delta}_{\alpha \beta }$                         eq 2.2-3

${\omega}_{\alpha}=\sqrt{({\lambda}_{\alpha})}$                                 eq 2.2-5

## 3 Probabilistic model

### 3.1 Introduction of the probabilistic model into the dynamic problem

In order to take into account the modelling uncertainties and the uncertainties on the data, a mixed non-parametric–parametric probabilistic formulation is used. For that, the vector of the $n$ generalised degrees of freedom ${\underline{q}}^{n}(t)$ (resp. ${\underline{q}}^{n}(\omega )$ ) is replaced by a random variable ${Q}^{n}(t)$ (resp. ${Q}^{n}(\omega )$ ).

In transient analysis, the stochastic process $t\to {Q}^{n}(t)$ indexed by $[0,T]$ and with values in ${R}^{n}$ is the solution of the non-linear dynamic system,

$\left[{M}_{n}\right]{\ddot{Q}}^{n}(t)+\left[{D}_{n}\right]{\dot{Q}}^{n}(t)+\left[{K}_{n}\right]{Q}^{n}(t)+{F}_{c}^{n}(t,{Q}^{n}(t),{\dot{Q}}^{n}(t);W)={F}^{n}(t),$             eq 3.1-1

$\dot{{Q}^{n}}(0)={Q}^{n}(0)=0$ ,                                        eq 3.1-2

and in harmonic analysis, the stochastic process $t\mapsto {Q}^{n}(\omega )$ indexed on $\left[{\omega}_{1},{\omega}_{2}\right]$ and with values in ${ℝ}^{n}$ is the solution of the system:

$(-{\omega}^{2}\left[{\underline{M}}_{n}\right]+i\omega \left[{\underline{D}}_{n}\right]+\left[{\underline{K}}_{n}\right]){Q}^{n}(\omega )={F}^{n}(\omega )$             eq 3.1-3

where, in both the transient and harmonic cases, $\left[{\underline{M}}_{n}\right]$ , $\left[{\underline{D}}_{n}\right]$ , and $\left[{\underline{K}}_{n}\right]$  are random real symmetric positive-definite full matrices and where $W$ is a random variable with values in ${ℝ}^{\nu}$ . The introduction of random matrices into the equations [eq 3.1-1] and [eq 3.1-3] makes it possible to model the random uncertainties associated with the linear part of the dynamic system. The vector-valued random variable $W$ introduced into the equation [eq 3.1-1] makes it possible to model the random uncertainties concerning the parameters of the impact non-linearities.

The parametric probabilistic approach and the non-parametric probabilistic approach introduce random matrices (, and ) and a random variable $W$ whose probability laws are a priori unknown. The choice of one probabilistic model rather than another must rest solely on the available information (algebraic properties of the generalised matrices, mean values of the parameters and of the generalised matrices, etc.). In order to construct objectively the probability laws of the probabilistic model of the uncertainties, ([bib20] to [bib24]), the principle of maximum entropy ([bib14], [bib15], [bib17]) is used with a system of constraints defined by this available information. The available information and the probabilistic model that follows from it are presented in the next paragraph.

### 3.3 Probabilistic model for the real variables (parametric uncertainties)

#### 3.3.1 Information available on the real variables

In the mixed probabilistic approach, the parametric probabilistic modelling consists in substituting the parameter $\underline{w}$ of the non-linearities in the non-linear dynamic systems given by [eq 2.1.1-1] or [eq 2.1.2-3] by a random variable denoted $W=({W}_{1},\dots ,{W}_{\nu})$ . In a purely parametric approach (i.e. without making the matrices of the dynamic system random), the parametric probabilistic modelling consists in substituting certain parameters $\underline{w}$ of the matrices $\left[{\underline{M}}_{n}(\underline{w})\right]$ , $\left[{\underline{K}}_{n}(\underline{w})\right]$ and $\left[{\underline{D}}_{n}(\underline{w})\right]$ of the mean reduced dynamic system by a random variable $W$ . These parameters may for example be material parameters.

One assumes that the components of $W$ are real random variables independent of one another and independent of the random matrices of the dynamic system. ### 3.4 Construction of the stochastic response and of the associated statistics

#### 3.4.1 Transient case

##### 3.4.1.1 Stochastic transient response

The excitations of the dynamic system are assumed deterministic, but in paragraph [§3.1], random matrices and random parameters have been introduced into the reduced matrix model. Therefore, the transient response $t\to {Q}_{n}(t)$ is a non-stationary stochastic process indexed by $[0,T]$ with values in ${ℝ}^{n}$ (using a few additional assumptions of existence, uniqueness and regularity of the deterministic solution, cf. [bib19]).

Consequently, to the vector of the $m$ free degrees of freedom $\underline{{y}^{n}}(t)$ there corresponds the stochastic process ${Y}^{n}(t)$ indexed by $[0,T]$ and with values in ${ℝ}^{m}$ such that

${Y}^{n}(t)=\left[{\underline{\Phi}}_{n}\right]{Q}^{n}(t),$                                 eq 3.4.1.1-1

In the case of the change to relative coordinates, to the stochastic process $t\to {Y}_{n}(t)$ indexed by $[0,T]$ and with values in ${ℝ}^{m}$ defined by the equation [eq 3.4.1.1-1] there corresponds the stochastic process $t\to {Z}_{n}(t)$ of the free d.o.f. of the structure in absolute coordinates indexed by and with values in ${ℝ}^{m}$ such that

${Z}_{n}(t)={Y}_{n}(t)+[R]{z}_{s}(t)$ .                            eq 3.4.1.1-2

##### 3.4.1.2 Elastic response spectrum

One denotes by ${Z}_{j}^{n}(t)$ the $j$ -th component of the vector ${Z}_{n}(t)$ corresponding to a random realisation of the stochastic response of the $j$ -th free degree of freedom of the structure. ${Z}_{j}^{n}(t)$ can be characterised by its elastic response spectrum (also called oscillator spectrum in the *Code_Aster*  documentation) which one denotes ${S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )$ where $\xi$ and $\omega$ are respectively the associated damping ratio and angular frequency.

With reasonable assumptions, in particular on the regularity of the non-linear mapping $(t,q,p;w)\mapsto {F}_{c}^{n}(t,q,p;w)$ , one can show that is a second-order process whose trajectories are almost surely continuous. Consequently, for every $\xi$ fixed in a given interval ${J}_{\xi}$ , $\omega \to {S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )$  is a stochastic process indexed on the analysis band ${J}_{\omega}$ with values in ${ℝ}^{+}$ . One accepts that this process is of second order, that is:

$E\lbrace {S}_{j}{(\xi ,\omega )}^{2}\rbrace <+\infty ,\forall \omega \in {J}_{\omega}$ .                        eq 3.4.1.2-1

#### 3.4.2 Harmonic case

In paragraph [§3.1], random matrices and random parameters have been introduced into the reduced matrix model. The harmonic response $t\mapsto {Q}^{n}(\omega )$ is therefore a stochastic process indexed on $\left[{\omega}_{1},{\omega}_{2}\right]$ with values in ${R}^{n}$ .

Consequently, to the vector of the $m$ free degrees of freedom ${\underline{y}}^{n}(\omega )$ there corresponds the stochastic process ${Y}^{n}(\omega )$ indexed on $\left[{\omega}_{1},{\omega}_{2}\right]$ and with values in ${R}^{m}$ such that

${Y}^{n}(\omega )=\left[{\underline{\Phi}}_{n}\right]{Q}^{n}(\omega )$                             eq 3.4.2-1

${Y}_{j}^{n}(\omega )$ the $j$ -th component of the vector ${Y}^{n}(\omega )$ is a random variable that one will accept to be of second order.

#### 3.4.3 Construction of the stochastic response by the Monte Carlo method

##### 3.4.3.1 Choice and implementation of the Monte Carlo method

The responses and the response spectra correspond to strongly non-linear transformations of the random matrices and of the random parameters that result from the probabilistic modelling of the uncertainties. Furthermore, one can of course construct only numerical approximations of these responses and of these response spectra. The statistics (first statistical moments, probability of exceeding a threshold, …) are formally written as multiple integrals of very large dimension because the number of random variables of the probabilistic model is high by construction. Finally, the number of observed quantities is very large (several d.o.f. for several frequencies). For all these reasons, the method best suited to computing the probabilistic solution (stochastic response and associated statistics) is the Monte-Carlo numerical simulation method.

The Monte-Carlo simulation method has the advantage of giving results whose accuracy one can control (verification of the convergence, cf. [§4.1]), unlike most methods based on approximations. It can be costly in computation time, but the use of variance-reduction techniques can make it possible to reduce the number of simulations required (cf. [bib8] or [bib9]).

The implementation of the Monte Carlo method consists, for the problem that concerns us, in generating ${n}_{s}$ realisations of the random matrices $\left[{M}_{n}\right]$ , $\left[{K}_{n}\right]$ and $\left[{D}_{n}\right]$ of the dynamic system and/or ${n}_{s}$ realisations of the vector random variable $W$ . The solutions of the deterministic dynamic system for each of the ${n}_{s}$ realisations of ( $\left[{M}_{n}\right]$ , $\left[{K}_{n}\right]$ , $\left[{D}_{n}\right]$ , $W$ ) produce ${n}_{s}$ realisations of the solution stochastic process $t\mapsto {Q}^{n}(t)$ (resp. $t\mapsto {Q}^{n}(\omega )$ ) and consequently of $t\to {Y}_{n}(t)$ , of $t\to {Z}_{n}(t)$ and of $\omega \to {S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )$ (resp. $\omega \mapsto {Y}_{j}^{n}(\omega )$ ). The generation of the random matrices is treated in the following paragraph; the generation of the random variable $W$ is more classical and is not recalled.

##### 3.4.3.2 Generation of the pseudo-random matrices

In order to generate the realisations of the random matrix $\left[{G}_{A}\right]$ , one uses the following algebraic representation of the random matrix $\left[{G}_{A}\right]$ whose probability law is defined by the equations [eq3.2.1‑2], [eq 3.2.2-1]:

$\left[{G}_{A}\right]={\left[L\right]}^{T}\left[L\right]$ ,                                    eq 3.4.3.2-1

the triangular random matrix $\left[L\right]$ being such that:

- The random variables ${\left[L\right]}_{ij},i\le j$ are independent.
- For $i<j$ , the real random variables ${\left[L\right]}_{ij}$ are written ${\left[L\right]}_{ij}={\sigma}_{n}{U}_{ij}$  where ${\sigma}_{n}={\delta}_{A}{(n+1)}^{-1/2}$ and where ${U}_{ij}$ is a real Gaussian random variable of mean 0 and of variance 1.
- For $i=j$ , the real random variables ${\left[L\right]}_{ij}$ are written ${\left[L\right]}_{ij}={\sigma}_{n}{(2{V}_{j})}^{1/2}$ where ${\sigma}_{n}$ is defined previously and where ${V}_{j}$ is a positive real random variable of gamma law whose probability density function ${p}_{{v}_{j}}(v)$ with respect to the measure $\mathit{dv}$ is written:

${p}_{{v}_{j}}(v)=\frac{{1}_{[0,+\infty [}(v)}{\Gamma ((n+1)/(2{\delta}_{A}^{2})+(1-j)/2)}{v}^{(n+1)/(2{\delta}_{A}^{2})+(1-j)/2}{e}^{-v}$ ,            eq 3.4.3.2-2

where ${1}_{[0,+\infty [}$ is the indicator function of $[0,+\infty [$ .

#### 3.4.4 Statistics on the spectra

In this chapter, one presents the definition of the statistics of the elastic response spectra ${S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )$ , in the case of a transient solution. In the harmonic case, the statistics on the random variables ${Y}_{j}^{n}(\omega )$ are defined in the same way and are therefore not presented.

##### 3.4.4.1 Estimation of the quantiles

For every $(\xi ,\omega )\in {J}_{\xi}\times {J}_{\omega}$ , ${S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )$ is a random variable with values in ${ℝ}^{+}$ . One seeks to estimate the quantile associated with the probability $\alpha$ denoted ${S}_{j,\alpha }(\xi ,\omega )$ and defined by:

${S}_{j,\alpha }(\xi ,\omega )={F}_{\xi ,\omega }^{-1}(1-\alpha )$                             eq 3.4.4.1-1

where ${F}_{\xi ,\omega }$ is the unknown cumulative distribution function of ${S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )$ .

Let $({S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{1})\text{, ... ,}{S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{r})\text{, ... ,}{S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{{n}_{s}}))$ be the sample composed of the ${n}_{s}$

realisations of ${S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )$ and $({S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{(1)})\text{, ... ,}{S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{(r)})\text{, ... ,}{S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{({n}_{s})}))$ the associated ordered sample.

A natural estimator of the quantile ${S}_{j,\alpha }(\xi ,\omega )$ for $\alpha =\frac{r}{{n}_{S}}$ , $1\le r\le {n}_{S}$ is:

${\hat{S}}_{j,\frac{r}{{n}_{S}}}(\xi ,\omega )={S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{(r)})$                         eq 3.4.4.1-2

To obtain a more robust estimator of the quantile, one can "average" the estimator over several series of ${n}_{s}$ realisations. If the desired probability is such that $\alpha <1/{n}_{S}$ , or if one wishes to reduce the number of simulations, it is possible to use more sophisticated estimators, for example by assuming that the cumulative distribution function ${F}_{\xi ,\omega }$ belongs to a given domain of attraction (extreme-value theory) or for example by using a Bayesian regularisation method (cf.[bib12]).

##### 3.4.4.2 Sample extreme values

For a sample of ${n}_{s}$ realisations of ${S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )$ denoted ${S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{1})\text{, ... ,}{S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{{n}_{s}})$ one defines the sample extreme values by:

$\omega \mapsto {\text{dB}}_{j\text{,min}}(\xi ,\omega ;{n}_{S})={\log}_{10}(\underset{r=1,...,{n}_{S}}{\min}{S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{r}))$                 eq 3.4.4.2-1

$\omega \mapsto {\text{dB}}_{j\text{,max}}(\xi ,\omega ;{n}_{S})={\log}_{10}(\underset{r=1,...,{n}_{S}}{\max}{S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{r}))$                 eq 3.4.4.2-2

##### 3.4.4.3 "Confidence domain" established from Chebyshev's inequality

For a sample of ${n}_{s}$ realisations of the process $\omega \mapsto {S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )$ denoted $\omega \mapsto {S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{1})$ , …, $\omega \mapsto {S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;{\theta}_{{n}_{S}})$ , one can construct the "confidence domain" of the random variable for every , using Chebyshev's inequality associated with a probability level ${P}_{C}$ :

$\text{Proba}\left\lbrace {\text{dB}}_{j}^{-}(\xi ,\omega )<{\text{dB}}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )<{\text{dB}}_{j}^{+}(\xi ,\omega )\right\rbrace \ge {P}_{C}$ ,                eq 3.4.4.3-1

where the lower envelope and the upper envelope are defined by:

${\text{dB}}_{j}^{+}(\xi ,\omega )={\log}_{10}({m}_{\mathrm{1j}}(\xi ,\omega )+\frac{{\sigma}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )}{\sqrt{1-{P}_{C}}})$ ,                    eq 3.4.4.3-2

${\text{dB}}_{j}^{-}(\xi ,\omega )=2{\log}_{10}({m}_{\mathrm{1j}}(\xi ,\omega ))-{\text{dB}}_{j}^{+}(\xi ,\omega )$ .                eq 3.4.4.3-3

with the mean and the standard deviation of ${\text{dB}}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )$ :

${m}_{\mathrm{1j}}(\xi ,\omega )=E\lbrace {S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )\rbrace$                             eq 3.4.4.3-4

${\sigma}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )=E{\left\lbrace {({S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega )-{m}_{\mathrm{1j}}(\xi ,\omega ))}^{2}\right\rbrace }^{1/2}$ .                    eq 3.4.4.3-5

The "confidence domain" thus constructed has proved to be a good approximation of the sample extreme values for the case treated in [bib21]. Now, this "confidence domain" involves only the first two moments, whose estimators converge more rapidly with respect to the number ${n}_{s}$ of simulations than the sample extreme values. It may therefore be interesting to use this construction of the "confidence domain" rather than a construction based on the estimation of the quantiles, which is more costly in number of simulations.

**Note:**

The term "confidence domain" may be considered by some as a misuse of language. One should rather use the less intuitive terminology "inter-quantile domain". Indeed, in the statistical literature, a confidence interval is theoretically the interval in which the true value of a parameter of a random variable lies (for example its mean) with a given probability. This terminology is used in the very precise framework of the theory of set estimation. The confidence interval is not a characterisation of the variability of a random variable, unlike a standard deviation or quantiles. One nevertheless uses "confidence domain" sparingly in what follows, because it is certainly a little more meaningful for non-specialists in statistics.

### 4.2 Choice of the dispersion parameters

To use the method, the dispersion parameters $\delta$ must be fixed. Two approaches can be used *a priori* to fix the value of these parameters.

The first approach consists in identifying the value of the parameters $\delta$ for a given structure or for a class of structure by means of appropriate methods. For that, one can use experimental results of the dynamic responses of the structure. One can also use numerical simulations constructed using a parametric approach to the uncertainties. In this latter case, it should be noted that only the errors on the data of the model are taken into account, since the modelling errors cannot be taken into account by the parametric approach.

The second approach consists in not fixing *a priori*  a fixed value of the parameters $\delta$ but in making them vary within a given range (only 3 scalars to be varied for the mass, stiffness and damping matrices on the non-parametric part, in comparison with the very large number of parameters to be varied simultaneously in a classical parametric study). This approach makes it possible to carry out a global analysis of sensitivity to the uncertainties. In the case of an absence of objective information on the dispersion parameters to be chosen, it is preferable to use such an approach. The non-parametric method proposed then appears as a robust and simple approach to sensitivity analysis with respect to uncertainties.

### 4.3 Main steps

The implementation in *Code_Aster*  is composed of three main steps: the construction of the mean reduced matrix model, the generation of the realisations of the response viewed as a stochastic process, and finally the statistical post-processing of these realisations. The last two steps in fact constitute the direct Monte Carlo numerical simulation method.

**Step 1: construction of the mean reduced matrix model**

The mean reduced matrix model is constructed by means of a classical sequence of operators depending on the precise analysis carried out, the main ones of which may be: ASSE_MATRICE, CALC_MODES, MODE_STATIQUE, CALC_CHAR_SEISME, PROJ_BASE, …

**Step 2: generation of the realisations of the transient response**

The ${n}_{s}$ realisations of the stochastic transient response are computed in a loop in the Python language composed of:

- Generation of the $p$ *th*  realisations of the random generalised matrices of mass, of stiffness and of damping by GENE_MATR_ALEA (doc. [U4.36.06](doc:u4.36.06)). These matrices are not diagonal and therefore require full storage.
- Generation of the $p$ *th*  realisations of the random variables of the parameters of the non-linearities by GENE_VARI_ALEA (doc. [U4.36.07](doc:u4.36.07)).
- Computation of the $p$ *th*  realisation ${Q}^{n}(t;p)$ or ${Q}^{n}(\omega ;p)$ solution of the stochastic matrix system $s$ . This realisation is the solution of the classical matrix system whose matrices and right-hand sides are the realisations previously generated. The computation is therefore performed by DYNA_VIBRA (with matr_asse_GENE_R and vect_asse_GENE as input).
- 1-Extraction of the time observations of the predefined physical degrees of freedom (for example ${\ddot{Z}}_{{}_{i}}^{n}(t;p)$  or ${Y}_{j}^{n}(\omega ;p)$ , but also possibly the displacement, velocity, stress fields, etc.) *via* recu_fonction (after a REST_GENE_PHYS for ${Y}_{j}^{n}(\omega ;p)$ ).

2-Computation of the corresponding spectra (by calc_fonction(SPEC_OSCI) for $\omega \to {S}_{j}(\xi ,\omega ;p)$ and calc_fonction(MODULE) for ${Y}_{j}^{n}(\omega ;p)$ *)* .

- Evaluation, *via* calc_fonction keywords COMB or PUISSANCE or ENVELOPPE, of the contributions to the estimators of the means, of the second-order moments, of the max. and min. sample extreme values for the normalised spectra:

${\hat{m}}_{\mathrm{1j}}(x,w;p)={S}_{j}(x,w;p)+{\hat{m}}_{\mathrm{1j}}(x,w;p-1)$ , ${\hat{m}}_{\mathrm{2j}}(x,w;p)={S}_{j}(x,w;p{)}^{2}+{\hat{m}}_{\mathrm{2j}}(x,w;p-1)$ ,

${\hat{S}}_{j\text{,max}}(x,w;p)=\text{Max}\left\lbrace {S}_{j}(x,w;p),{\hat{S}}_{j\text{,max}}(x,w;p-1)\right\rbrace$ , ${\hat{S}}_{j\text{,min}}(x,w;p)=\text{Min}\left\lbrace {S}_{j}(x,w;p),{\hat{S}}_{j\text{,min}}(x,w;p-1)\right\rbrace$ .

**Step 3: statistical post-processing**

The means, the standard deviations, the max. and min. sample extreme values for the normalised spectra can be evaluated *via* calc_fonction(COMB):

${m}_{\mathrm{1j}}(x,w)=\frac{1}{{n}_{s}}{\hat{m}}_{\mathrm{1j}}(x,w;{n}_{s})$ , ${m}_{\mathrm{2j}}(x,w)=\frac{1}{{n}_{s}}{\hat{m}}_{\mathrm{2j}}(x,w;{n}_{s})$ .

The confidence intervals can then be plotted from the sample extreme values or from the bounds obtained by Chebyshev cf. [§3.4.4].

In the transient case, an example is given by a test case of a plate in bending with impact non-linearities, cf. doc. [V5.06.001](doc:v5.06.001) [bib1]. Other details are given in the doc. [U2.08.05](doc:u2.08.05) [bib2].

### 4.4 Numerical efficiency of the non-parametric approach

The non-parametric approach is more economical in computation time than a purely parametric approach in which the parameters of geometry, materials, etc. are random variables.

In the purely parametric approach, the finite-element model depends on the uncertain parameters. For each Monte-Carlo simulation, the finite-element model is different. One must therefore, for each simulation, compute the elementary matrices, perform the assemblies, change to relative coordinates, solve the eigenvalue problem, project onto the modal basis, solve the reduced system and return to the physical basis then to relative coordinates.

In the non-parametric approach, only the reduced system is different at each simulation. It is therefore simply necessary, at each simulation, to solve the reduced system and return to the physical basis then to relative coordinates. In particular, the solution of the eigenvalue problem of the mean finite-element model is performed once and for all, before the Monte‑Carlo simulations.

The resulting gain in computation time is variable, but it can be important. As a first approximation, this gain in computation time depends on the ratio between the CPU time required for the eigenvalue solution and the CPU time required for the solution of the reduced system. The larger this ratio, the more advantageous the non-parametric approach is with respect to the purely parametric approach. In particular, the gain in computation time can be very important for structures with a very large number of degrees of freedom and a modal basis of small dimension.
