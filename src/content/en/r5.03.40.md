# r5.03.40 Static and dynamic modelling of beams in large rotations

**Summary** :

This note gives a mechanical formulation of beams in large displacements and large rotations but with an elastic constitutive behaviour. The essential difficulty of the analysis of rotations lies in the fact that they are not commutative and do not constitute a vector space, but a manifold.

At any time instant, the configuration of a beam cross-section is defined by the displacement vector of its centre of gravity and the rotation vector of the system of principal axes of inertia with respect to a reference position. As in the classical theory of beams, the internal forces are reduced to their resultant and their moment on the line of centres of the sections. One defines the associated strains.

The linearisation of the internal forces with respect to the displacements leads to the usual stiffness matrix, which is symmetric, and, because of the large displacements and rotations, to the geometric stiffness matrix, which is arbitrary.

The linearisation of the inertia forces leads, for the translational motion, to the usual mass matrix which is symmetric and, for the rotational motion, to a much more complicated matrix and without any symmetry.

The time-integration scheme is that of Newmark.

This modelling has been tested on five reference problems: three in statics and two in dynamics.

This work was undertaken within the framework of a development of modelling tools for the components of lines and substations. The aim of the modelling is the dynamic study of conductors fitted with **spacers**  (for the lines) or with **downleads onto apparatus**  (for the substations) and subjected to the Laplace forces resulting from short-circuit currents.

## 2 Introduction

The essential difficulty of the mechanics of beams in large displacements lies in the formulation of the rotations. The rotation of a section with respect to a reference configuration is defined by the rotation vector ([bib3], [bib4] and [bib5]). The quaternions are used for the updating of this vector.

In [bib4] and [bib5], the rotation increment is expressed in the reference configuration (total Lagrangian scheme). The computation of the mass matrices is complicated and cannot moreover be carried quite through to its end. But finally, all the matrices used are symmetric.

In [bib1] to [bib3], the rotation increment is expressed in the last computed configuration (updated Lagrangian scheme). It is this scheme that we have chosen. The computation of the matrices is completed without excessive difficulty but they are not symmetric.

Unlike [bib3], we have expressed the angular velocities and accelerations in the general axes and not in local axes. The matrices are thus more complicated, but one avoids the ambiguity which appears at the junction of two non-collinear beams.

## 3 Kinematics of a beam in finite rotations

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.40/100017FE000069D5000030449313D61A4D5A9183.svg)

**Figure 3-a: Evolution of a beam segment**

Let us follow the evolution of a beam segment from its initial position — or **reference**  position — [fig3‑a](a) to its deformed position at the time instant $t$ [fig 3-a] (b).

The cross-section of the centre $P$ of the beam in the reference position is located by the curvilinear abscissa $s$ of $P$ on the line of centres (or neutral fibre). One attaches to this section the orthonormal triad ${E}_{1}\text{}{E}_{2}\text{}{E}_{3}\text{}:\text{}{E}_{1}$ is the unit tangent to the line of centres at $P;{E}_{2}\text{et}{E}_{3}$ are directed along the principal axes of inertia of the section.

As in [bib1] to [bib5], one makes the hypothesis that in the course of the motion the sections initially straight remain plane and do not change shape.

From time instant 0 to time instant $t$ :

- $P$ comes to $P'$ and the position of $P'$ is defined by the vector ${x}_{o}(s,t)$ ;
- the orthonormal triad ${E}_{1}{E}_{2}{E}_{3}$ becomes the orthonormal triad ${t}_{1}{t}_{2}{t}_{3}.{t}_{2}$ and ${t}_{3}$ are still directed along the principal axes of inertia of the section and ${t}_{1}$ is still the unit normal to this section. But ${t}_{1}$ is not necessarily tangent to the line of centres at $P'$ : in other words, there may be, in the deformed position, a slip due to shear, as in the Timoshenko model.

The state of the section at the time instant $t$ is therefore defined by:

- the vector ${x}_{o}(s,t)$ , which gives the position of the centre of gravity ;
- the rotation vector which takes one from the triad ${E}_{1}{E}_{2}{E}_{3}$ to the triad ${t}_{1}{t}_{2}{t}_{3}$ , and which is defined in [§4.1].

The set of these two vectors constitutes the vector $\phi (s,t)$ .

### 4.1 Rotation vector

Suppose that, in the system of general axes $P\text{}{e}_{1}\text{}{e}_{2}\text{}{e}_{3}$ [fig 4.1-a], the point $M'$ is deduced from $M$ by the rotation of angle $\alpha$ about the axis passing through $P$ and of unit vector $u$ . Let us set:

$\Psi =\alpha u$

$\Psi$ is called the **rotation vector**  taking one from $M$ to $M'$ .

According to the Euler–Rodrigues formula [bib6] p. 186 and [bib7]:

$\overrightarrow{\mathrm{PM}'}=\overrightarrow{\mathrm{PM}}+\sin\alpha (u\wedge \overrightarrow{\mathrm{PM}})+(1-\cos\alpha )\left[u\wedge \left[u\wedge \overrightarrow{\mathrm{PM}}\right]\right]$ .

In general, the rotation vector of the product of two rotations **is not**  the geometric sum of the component rotation vectors. The 2D case is a particularly simple exception: the rotation vectors, perpendicular to the plane, add algebraically.

## 5 Passage from the local axes to the general axes

The components of the vectors are expressed in the general axes ${e}_{1}\text{}{e}_{2}\text{}{e}_{3}$ [fig 3-a]. The matrices of the operators which relate them are therefore valid only in these axes. But the mechanics of beams is formulated much more simply in the local principal axes of inertia ${t}_{1}{t}_{2}{t}_{3}$ in the current configuration. One is therefore led to make the change of axes from the general triad ${e}_{1}\text{}{e}_{2}\text{}{e}_{3}$ to the local triad ${t}_{1}{t}_{2}{t}_{3}$ by the product ${R}_{\text{tot}}$ of two rotations:

- the rotation ${R}_{o}$ , invariable, which brings the general axes $({e}_{1}\text{}{e}_{2}\text{}{e}_{3})$ onto the local axes in the reference position $({E}_{1}\text{}{E}_{2}\text{}{E}_{3})$ ;
- the rotation $R$ , depending on time, which brings the triad $({E}_{1}\text{}{E}_{2}\text{}{E}_{3})$ onto the local triad in the current deformed position $({t}_{1}{t}_{2}{t}_{3})$ , that is:

$$
{R}_{\text{tot}}={\mathrm{RR}}_{o}
$$

Given a vector $v$ , of components known in the general triad, its components in the local triad are the components in the general triad of the vector:

$$
V={R}_{\text{tot}}^{T}v.
$$

One can therefore replace the computations bearing on vectors expressed in local axes in the current configuration, by the same computations bearing on the same vectors rotated by ${R}_{\text{tot}}^{T}$ and expressed in general axes. In other words, this rotation ${R}_{\text{tot}}^{T}$ makes it possible to replace the computations in local axes of the current configuration, by the same computations in general axes.

## 6 Internal forces, strains and constitutive law

One places oneself within the framework of beam theory. The forces and the strains are defined by their reduction elements on the line of centres of gravity of the sections. Thus, the virtual work in the beam is computed by a simple curvilinear integral along this line.

### 6.1 Internal forces

One calls internal forces on the section of centre $P'$ and of normal ${t}_{1}$ the forces exerted on this section by the part of the beam situated in the direction of ${t}_{1}$ [fig 6.1-a]. These forces form a torsor whose reduction elements at $P'$ are: the resultant $f$ , the resulting moment $m$

If $\bar{f}$ and $\bar{m}$  are respectively the external force and moment given per unit of undeformed length $\bar{f}$ and $\bar{m}$  are assumed independent of the configuration, that is to say “conservative” or “non-follower” —, the static equilibrium of the beam segment $P'\text{}P''$ of length $\mathrm{ds}$ is written:

### 6.2 Change of curvature at a point of the line of centres

The local axes of the section of abscissa $s$ are deduced from the general axes by the relation :

$$
{t}_{i}(s)=R(s).{R}_{o}(s){e}_{i}.
$$

By differentiating with respect to $s$ :

${t}_{i}'=(R'{R}_{o}+{\mathrm{RR}}_{o}'){e}_{i},$

that is, by inverting the relation :

${t}_{i}=(R'{R}^{T}+{\mathrm{RR}}_{o}'{R}_{o}^{T}{R}^{T}){t}_{i}$ .

Let us set:

$$
R'{R}^{T}=\hat{\chi }
$$

The matrix $\hat{\chi }$ is antisymmetric because differentiation with respect to $s$ of  gives:

$\hat{\chi }+{\hat{\chi }}^{T}=0$ .

One checks that the matrix ${\hat{\chi }}_{0}$ defined by:

${\hat{\chi }}_{o}={\mathrm{RR}}_{o}'{R}_{o}^{T}{R}^{T}$

is also antisymmetric.

Therefore, by bringing in the axial vectors $\chi$ and ${\chi }_{0}$ :

${t}_{i}'=(\chi +{\chi }_{o})\wedge {t}_{i}$

- $\mathrm{ds}{\chi }_{o}$ is the rotation vector which takes one from ${t}_{i}(s)$ to ${t}_{i}(s+\text{ds})$ when the beam undergoes a **uniform** rotation $(R'=0)$ from its reference position to its current position. ${\chi }_{o}(s)$ characterises the **curvature of the reference configuration**  at the abscissa $s$ .
- $\mathrm{ds}\chi$ is the increment of rotation from ${t}_{i}(s)$ to ${t}_{i}(s+\text{ds})$ due to the variation of $R$ along the beam. It is this vector $\chi$ which characterises the **change of curvature**  between the reference configuration and the current deformed configuration.

### 6.3 Virtual work in the beam and strain vector

This paragraph is the extension to the three-dimensional case of an approach made in [bib8] on plane beams.

The configuration of the beam at the time instant $t$ is defined [§3] by the field $\phi (s,t)$ :

$\Phi (s,t)=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{x}_{0}(s,t)\\ \Psi (s,t)\end{array}\right\rbrace$ .

Let us compute the work $W$ of the external line forces $\bar{f}$ and $\bar{m}$  in the following virtual displacement:

$\delta \phi (s)=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\delta {x}_{o}(s)\\ \delta \Psi (s)\end{array}\right\rbrace$ .

One obviously has:

$W={\int}_{{s}_{1}}^{{s}_{2}}(\overline{f}.\delta {x}_{o}+\overline{m}.\delta \phi )\text{ds}$ .

The equilibrium equations  make it possible to replace the external forces $\bar{f}$ and $\bar{m}$  by the internal forces $f$ and $m$ :

$W={\int}_{{s}_{1}}^{{s}_{2}}\left[-\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}.\delta {x}_{o}-\frac{\partial m}{\partial s}.\delta \Psi -(\frac{\partial {x}_{o}}{\partial s}\wedge f).\delta \Psi \right]\text{ds}$ ,

that is, by integration by parts of the first two terms:

$W=-{\left[f.\delta {x}_{o}+m.\delta \Psi \right]}_{1}^{2}+{\int}_{{s}_{1}}^{{s}_{2}}\left[f.\delta {x}_{o}'+m.\delta \Psi '-({x}_{o}'\wedge f).\delta \Psi \right]\text{ds}.$

If one denotes by ${W}_{\mathrm{ext}}$ the total work of the external forces on the beam — along it and at the ends — the previous equation is written:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{W}_{\text{ext}}={\int}_{{s}_{1}}^{{s}_{2}}(\overline{f}.\delta {x}_{o}+\overline{m}.\delta \Psi )\text{ds}+{\left[f.\delta {x}_{o}+m.\delta \Psi \right]}_{1}^{2}\\ ={\int}_{{s}_{1}}^{{s}_{2}}\left[f.(\delta {x}_{o}'-\delta \Psi \wedge {x}_{o}')+m.\delta \Psi '\right]\text{ds}.\end{array}
$$

According to the theorem of virtual work for continuous media, the right-hand side is the virtual work of the internal forces, which one denotes ${W}_{int}$ . Following the idea of [bib8], let us seek to put the coefficients of $f\text{et de}m$ in the form of **the virtual increment of two vectors.**

Let us make the hypothesis that the vector ${x}_{o}'$ tangent to the line of centres but not necessarily of unit length, differs from the unit vector ${t}_{1}$ , normal to the section, only by an infinitesimal of the 1st order (of the order of $\delta \Psi$ ). This implies that the beam lengthens little and undergoes a small slip. Then:

${W}_{int}={\int}_{{s}_{1}}^{{s}_{2}}\left[f.\delta ({x}_{o}'-{t}_{1})+m.\delta \chi \right]\text{ds}$ .

Indeed, in the virtual displacement:

- ${x}_{o}'$ increases by $\delta {x}_{o}';$
- the unit vector ${t}_{1}$ rotates by $\delta \Psi$ ;
- the vector $\chi$ , defining the change of curvature [§6.2], increases by $\delta \Psi '$ .

One therefore has:

$$
{W}_{int}={\int}_{{s}_{1}}^{{s}_{2}}\left[f.\delta \varepsilon +m.\delta \chi \right]\text{ds},
$$

with:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\varepsilon \\ \chi \end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{x}_{o}'-{t}_{1}\\ \chi \end{array}\right\rbrace .
$$

In the equation , $\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}f\\ m\end{array}\right\rbrace$ and $\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\varepsilon \\ \chi \end{array}\right\rbrace$ therefore appear respectively as a **force torsor**  or **of generalised stresses**  and the **torsor of the associated strains** .

****  defines the elongation and the slip; $\khi$ defines the change of curvature [§6.2].

One observes that, if $\varepsilon$ must be small, on the other hand there is no limitation for $\khi$ . Most beams fall within this framework.

The relation  is written:

$$
{W}_{int}(\phi ;\delta \phi )={\int}_{{s}_{1}}^{{s}_{2}}B\delta \phi .\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}f\\ m\end{array}\right\rbrace \text{ds}
$$

with:

$$
B=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\frac{d}{\text{ds}}1& {x}_{o}'\wedge \\ 0& \frac{d}{\text{ds}}1\end{array}\right]
$$

$B$ is called the **strain matrix** .

### 6.4 Constitutive law

According to [§5], the components in local axes of the generalised stress and of the strain are the components in general axes of the vectors:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}F\\ M\end{array}\right\rbrace ={\Pi}^{T}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}f\\ m\end{array}\right\rbrace ;\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}E\\ X\end{array}\right\rbrace ={\Pi}^{T}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\varepsilon \\ \chi \end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

One assumes that the constitutive law is elastic and that, in local axes, it has the same expression as for a Timoshenko beam:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}F\\ M\end{array}\right\rbrace =\text{DIAG}\left[\text{EA},{\text{GA}}_{2},{\text{GA}}_{3},{\text{GI}}_{1},{\text{EI}}_{2},{\text{EI}}_{3}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}E\\ X\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

${A}_{2}$ and ${A}_{3}$ being two areas depending on the size and the shape of the section.

One sets:

$C=\text{DIAG}\left[\text{EA},{\text{GA}}_{2},{\text{GA}}_{3},{\text{GI}}_{1},{\text{EI}}_{2},{\text{EI}}_{3}\right].$

$C$ is called the **constitutive matrix** .

## 7 Elementary inertia forces

The inertia forces applied to an element $\mathrm{ds}$ form a torsor which admits, at the centre of gravity:

- a general resultant, $-\rho A\mathrm{ds}\ddot{{x}_{0}}(s,t)$ ;
- a resulting moment equal to the opposite of the absolute rate of the elementary angular momentum $H$ .

In order to express the angular velocity, let us proceed as in [§6.2] and differentiate the relation:

${t}_{i}={RE}_{i},$

with respect to $t$ , taking into account that ${E}_{i}$ does not depend on time. One obtains:

$$
{\dot{t}}_{i}=\dot{R}{R}^{T}{t}_{i}.
$$

Let us set:

$$
\dot{R}{R}^{T}=\hat{\omega}.
$$

By differentiating the relation  with respect to $t$ , one sees that the matrix $\hat{\omega}$ is antisymmetric. If one denotes by $\omega$ the axial vector of this matrix, the relation  is written:

${\dot{t}}_{i}(s,t)=\omega \wedge {t}_{i}(s,t).$

$\omega$ is therefore the **angular velocity vector**  of the beam section of abscissa $s$ at the time instant $t$ .

The elementary angular momentum has for expression:

$$
H=\mathrm{ds}{I}_{\rho}\omega =\mathrm{ds}R{J}_{\rho}{R}^{T}\omega
$$

where ${J}_{\rho}$ is the inertia tensor in the reference configuration:

${J}_{\rho}={R}_{0}\text{DIAG}\left[\rho {I}_{1},\rho {I}_{2},\rho {I}_{3}\right]{R}_{0}^{T}$

By differentiating with respect to time:

$\dot{H}=\mathrm{ds}{I}_{\rho}\dot{\omega}+\mathrm{ds}(\dot{R}{J}_{\rho}{R}^{T}+R{J}_{\rho}{\dot{R}}^{T})\omega$

But:

$\dot{R}{J}_{\rho}{R}^{T}\omega =\dot{R}{R}^{T}R{J}_{\rho}{R}^{T}\omega =\hat{\omega}{I}_{\rho}\omega =\omega \wedge {I}_{\omega}\omega$

and:

$R{J}_{\rho}{\dot{R}}^{T}\omega =R{J}_{\rho}{R}^{T}R{\dot{R}}^{T}\omega =0$

because, according to the equation :

$R{\dot{R}}^{T}\omega =-\hat{\omega}\omega =0$ .

Whence the moment of the inertia forces of the element:

$-\dot{H}=-\mathrm{ds}{I}_{\rho}\dot{\omega}-\mathrm{ds}\omega \wedge {I}_{\rho}\omega$

The virtual work of the inertia forces therefore has for expression:

$$
{W}_{\mathrm{iner}}=-{\int}_{{S}_{1}}^{{S}_{2}}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}\rho A\ddot{{x}_{0}}\\ {I}_{\rho}\dot{\omega}+\omega \wedge {I}_{\rho}\omega \end{array}\right\rbrace .\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}\delta {\omega}_{0}\\ \delta \Psi \end{array}\right\rbrace \mathrm{ds}
$$

### 8.2 Course of an analysis

- In the dynamic case, one seeks the fields of displacements, velocities and accelerations at a discrete sequence of time instants: ${t}_{1},{t}_{2}\text{...}{t}_{i-1},{t}_{i}\text{...}{t}_{n}.$
- In the static case, one splits the total load into load **increments**  which one adds successively starting from zero in order to reconstitute the complete load. At each loading step, called by abuse “time instant”, one computes the displacement field.

Knowing the state of the structure at the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ , one deduces from it its state at the time instant ${t}_{i}$ by **prediction–correction:**

- In the static case (STAT_NON_LINE), the prediction consists in computing the response of the structure to the $i$ -th load increment, keeping its behaviour at the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ .
- In the dynamic case, one must first **initialise**  the fields at the time instant ${t}_{i}$ , by formulae following from the time-integration algorithm used: in the operator DYNA_NON_LINE, it is the Newmark algorithm [§A3]. Then one applies the load increment between ${t}_{i-1}$ and ${t}_{i}$ with the behaviour in the initialised situation.

In the predicted state, the equilibrium equation is generally not satisfied and one must **correct**  the displacements by **iterations**  resting on **linearised equations.**

## 9 Linearisation of the equations of motion

Suppose computed the state of the structure at iteration $n$ of the time instant $i$ ; $n=1$ corresponds to the prediction phase. The weak form of the equilibrium equations is, at this iteration:

$$
W({\phi }_{i}^{n},{\dot{\phi }}_{i}^{n},{\ddot{\phi }}_{i}^{n};\delta \phi )={W}_{int}({\phi }_{i}^{n};\delta \phi )-{W}_{\text{iner}}({\phi }_{i}^{n},{\dot{\phi }}_{i}^{n},{\ddot{\phi }}_{i}^{n};\delta \phi )-{W}_{\text{ext}}({\phi }_{i}^{n};\delta \phi )
$$

- If this quantity is small enough, in the sense of the stopping criterion [bib10], one considers that this $n$ -th iteration gives the state of the structure at the time instant $i$ .
- Otherwise, one computes displacement **corrections**  $\Delta {\phi }_{i}^{n+1}$ such that:

$$
\begin{array}{}L\lbrace W\left[({\phi }_{i}^{n}+\Delta {\phi }_{i}^{n+1}),{({\phi }_{i}^{n}+\Delta {\phi }_{i}^{n+1})}^{.},{({\phi }_{i}^{n}+\Delta {\phi }_{i}^{n+1})}^{..};\delta \phi \right]=\\ W({\phi }_{i}^{n},{\dot{\phi }}_{i}^{n},{\ddot{\phi }}_{i}^{n};\delta \phi )+\text{DW}({\phi }_{i}^{n},{\dot{\phi }}_{i}^{n},{\ddot{\phi }}_{i}^{n};\delta \phi ).\Delta {\phi }_{i}^{n+1}=0.\end{array}

$\text{DW}({\phi }_{i}^{n},{\dot{\phi }}_{i}^{n},{\ddot{\phi }}_{i}^{n};\delta \phi ).\Delta {\phi }_{i}^{n+1}$ is the Fréchet differential of $W({\phi }_{i}^{n},{\dot{\phi }}_{i}^{n},{\ddot{\phi }}_{i}^{n};\delta \phi )$ in the direction $\Delta {\phi }_{i}^{n+1}$ [§A4].

**A3.1** **Classical Newmark scheme in translation**

The state of the line of centres (displacement, velocity and acceleration) is assumed known at the time instant $i-1$ The Newmark algorithm [bib10] and [bib13], rests on the following expansions of the displacement and of the velocity, at iteration $n+1$ of the time instant $i$ :

$\beta$ and $\gamma$ are the Newmark parameters which, in the case of the “trapezoidal rule”, are equal to $\frac{1}{4}$ and $\frac{1}{2}$ : the brackets in the equations  and  are then the arithmetic means of ${\ddot{x}}_{o,i-1}$ and of ${\ddot{x}}_{o,i}^{n+1}$

$$
{x}_{o,i}^{n+1}={x}_{o,i-1}+\Delta t{\dot{x}}_{o,i-1}+\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{2}\left[(1-2\beta ){\ddot{x}}_{o,i-1}+2\beta {\ddot{x}}_{o,i}^{n+1}\right]
$$

$$
{\dot{x}}_{o,i}^{n+1}={\dot{x}}_{o,i-1}+\Delta t\left[(1-\gamma ){\ddot{x}}_{o,i-1}+\gamma {\ddot{x}}_{o,i}^{n+1}\right]
$$

Rewriting each of these relations at iteration $n$ of the time instant $i$ and subtracting term by term from the previous relations, one obtains:

${\ddot{x}}_{o,i}^{n+1}={\ddot{x}}_{o,i}^{n}+\frac{1}{\beta \Delta {t}^{2}}({x}_{o,i}^{n+1}-{x}_{o,i}^{n})$ and ${\dot{x}}_{o,i}^{n+1}={\dot{x}}_{o,i}^{n}+\frac{\gamma}{\beta \Delta t}({x}_{o,i}^{n+1}-{x}_{o,i}^{n})$

If one sets:

one therefore has:

$$
{x}_{o,i}^{n+1}={x}_{o,i}^{n}+\Delta {x}_{o,i}^{n+1}
$$

**A3.2** **Newmark scheme adapted to large rotations**

$$
{\ddot{x}}_{o,i}^{n+1}={\ddot{x}}_{o,i}^{n}+\frac{1}{\beta \Delta {t}^{2}}\Delta {x}_{o,i}^{n+1}
\quad \text{et} \quad
{\dot{x}}_{o,i}^{n+1}={\dot{x}}_{o,i}^{n}+\frac{\gamma}{\beta \Delta t}\Delta {x}_{o,i}^{n+1}
$$

${\omega}_{i}^{n+1}={\omega}_{i}^{n}+\frac{\gamma}{\beta \Delta t}({\Psi}_{i-1,i}^{n+1}-{\Psi}_{i-1,i}^{n})$ and ${\dot{\omega}}_{i}^{n+1}={\dot{\omega}}_{i}^{n}+\frac{1}{\beta \Delta {t}^{2}}({\Psi}_{i-1,i}^{n+1}-{\Psi}_{i-1,i}^{n})$

$$

$$

$$

: Computation of the Fréchet differentials
One will have to use in what follows the following identity, called the *Lie bracket* , whose proof is immediate. If $\hat{A}$ and $\hat{B}$ are two antisymmetric matrices of the 3rd order, of axial vectors $A$ and $B$ , then one has, for any vector $v$ :

$$
(\hat{A}\hat{B}-\hat{B}\hat{A})v=(A\wedge B)\wedge v
$$

In other words: is the axial vector of the antisymmetric matrix $\hat{A}\hat{B}-\hat{B}\hat{A}$ .

One calls Fréchet differential of the function $f$ in the direction $\Delta x$ , the quantity:

$Df(x).\Delta x=\text{grad}f.\Delta x=\underset{\varepsilon \to 0}{\lim}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon }f(x+\varepsilon \Delta x).$

It is the principal part of the increment of $f$ corresponding to the increment $\Delta x$ of the variable $x$ .

Here is the catalogue of the Fréchet differentials involved in this note.

$$
D{x}_{o}'.\Delta {x}_{o}=\underset{\varepsilon \to 0}{\lim}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon }({x}_{o}+\varepsilon \Delta {x}_{o})'=(\Delta {x}_{o})'=\Delta {x}_{o}'
$$

$$
D{\dot{x}}_{o}.\Delta {x}_{o}=\Delta {\dot{x}}_{o}
$$

$$
D{\ddot{x}}_{o}.\Delta {x}_{o}=\Delta {\ddot{x}}_{o}
$$

$$
DR.\Delta \Psi =\underset{\varepsilon \to 0}{\lim}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon }\left[\exp(\varepsilon \hat{\Delta \Psi })R\right]=\hat{\Delta \Psi }R
$$

$$
D{R}^{T}.\Delta \Psi =\underset{\varepsilon \to 0}{\lim}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon }\left[{R}^{T}\exp(-\varepsilon \hat{\Delta \Psi })\right]=-{R}^{T}\hat{\Delta \Psi }
$$

because, according to the equation :

${\left[\exp(\varepsilon \hat{\Delta \Psi })\right]}^{T}=\exp(-\varepsilon \hat{\Delta \Psi }).$

$\hat{\omega}=\dot{R}{R}^{T}.$

Now:

$D\dot{R}.\Delta \Psi =\underset{\varepsilon \to 0}{\lim}\frac{d}{d\varepsilon }{\left[\exp(\varepsilon \hat{\Delta \Psi })R\right]}^{·}=\hat{\Delta \dot{\Psi}}R+\hat{\Delta \Psi }\dot{R}.$

: Necessity of the transport into a reference space for the vector operations relative to the rotational motion
The kinematic quantities (grandeurs), velocities and accelerations, in a given configuration lie in the vector space tangent to $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ at the point defined by the rotation of the configuration with respect to the reference position. One can combine the quantities (grandeurs) relative to two distinct configurations only after having transported them into one and the same vector space taken as reference. The example which follows will make this necessity understood.

Let us examine the angular velocity of a gyroscope at three time instants ${t}_{1},{t}_{2}$ and ${t}_{3}$ [fig A7-a]. Suppose that one passes from configuration 1 to configuration 2 by the rotation of angle $-\pi$ about ${e}_{1}$ , and from configuration 1 to configuration 3 by the rotation of angle $-\pi /2$ about the same axis. At 1, the angular velocity is carried by the axis of the gyro and has components $(0,0,\omega )$ in the general axes ${e}_{1}\text{}{e}_{2}\text{}{e}_{3}$ . At 2, the angular velocity is likewise carried by the axis and has general components $(0,0,–3\omega )$ . One wishes to determine the general components of the velocity at 3, knowing that this velocity is the average of the velocities at 1 and 2.

That the angular velocity at 3 is the average of the angular velocities at 1 and 2 does not mean that it is the average in the general axes, but in the axes attached to the gyro. In the example, since the gyro rotates about its axis, at 1 with the velocity $\omega$ , at 2 with the velocity 3 $\omega$ , then at 3 it rotates about this axis with the velocity $2\omega$ . Therefore, at 3, the general components of the angular velocity are $(0,2\omega ,0)$ .

Taking [§5] into account, one obtains the previous result by “transporting” the angular-velocity vector of configuration 2 onto configuration 1, taken as reference, that is to say by rotating this vector by the angle $\pi$ about ${e}_{1}$ . Its general components are then $(0,0,3\omega )$ . One takes the average of this vector and of the angular-velocity vector at 1, in order to obtain the vector of components $(0,0,2\omega )$ . One finally “transports” this last vector onto configuration 3 by rotating it by $-\pi /2$ about ${e}_{1}$ , and one indeed arrives at the angular-velocity vector of general components $(\mathrm{0, 2}\omega ,0)$ .

**A8.1** **Definitions**

A **quaternion** , denoted $(q)$ or $({x}_{o},x)$ , is defined by the set of a scalar ${x}_{o}$ and of a three-dimensional vector $x({x}_{1}\text{}{x}_{2}\text{}{x}_{3})$ :

$({x}_{o},x)={x}_{o}+{x}_{1}\iota +{x}_{2}\kappa +{x}_{3}\lambda ,$ ,

$\iota ,\kappa$ and $\lambda$ being three numbers satisfying the relations:

$\begin{array}{}{\iota}^{2}={\kappa}^{2}={\lambda}^{2}=-1;\\ \iota \kappa =-\kappa \iota =\lambda ;\kappa \lambda =-\lambda \kappa =\iota ;\lambda \iota =-\iota \lambda =\kappa .\end{array}$

**Conjugate quaternion**

The conjugate, denoted ${}^{\ast }$ , of a quaternion is obtained by changing the sign of the vector part:

${({x}_{o},x)}^{\ast }=({x}_{o},-x).$

**Purely vectorial quaternion**

A quaternion is said to be purely vectorial when its scalar part is zero and it is therefore of the form: $(0,x)$ . A quaternion $(v)$ is purely vectorial if and only if:

$(v)+{(v)}^{\ast }=(0).$

**A8.2** **Elements of the algebra of quaternions**

**Multiplication**

By applying the definition, one shows immediately that the multiplication, denoted $°$ , of two quaternions is:

$({x}_{o},x)°({y}_{o},y)=({x}_{o}{y}_{o}-x.y,{x}_{o}y+{y}_{o}x+x\wedge y).$

One checks that this multiplication is:

- associative:

$\left[({x}_{o},x)+({y}_{o},y)\right]°({z}_{o},z)=({x}_{o},x)°({z}_{o},z)+({y}_{o},y)°({z}_{o},z);$

- non-commutative because:

$({x}_{o},x)°({y}_{o},y)-({y}_{o},y)°({x}_{o},x)=(0,2x\wedge y).$

**Norm of a quaternion** , denoted $\parallel .\parallel$ :

${\parallel ({x}_{o},x)\parallel }^{2}=({x}_{o},x)°{({x}_{o},x)}^{\ast }={x}_{0}^{2}+{x}_{1}^{2}+{x}_{2}^{2}+{x}_{3}^{2}.$

A **quaternion**  is **unitary**  if its norm is equal to unity. One checks that:

${\left[({q}_{1})°({q}_{2})\right]}^{\ast }={({q}_{2})}^{\ast }°{({q}_{1})}^{\ast }.$

**A8.3** **Representation of a rotation by a quaternion**

If $({v}_{1})$ is a purely vectorial quaternion and $(u)$ a unitary quaternion, then the quaternion $({v}_{2})$ such that:
