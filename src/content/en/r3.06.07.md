# r3.06.07 Diagonalisation of the thermal mass matrix

**Summary:**

In order to improve the regularity of the solution in transient thermal problems, one solution consists in “lumping” (that is: condensing onto the diagonal) the thermal mass matrix (capacity matrix).

This possibility is accessible through the modellings PLAN_DIAG, AXIS_DIAG and 3D_DIAG for the phenomenon THERMIQUE. It is activated when calling the thermal analysis commands THER_LINEAIRE and THER_NON_LINE.

When these modellings are used, only the linear finite elements (2D and 3D) have a lumped mass matrix. Indeed, direct diagonalisation does not give satisfactory results for quadratic finite elements, so it is not allowed

The theoretical results are illustrated by the thermo-mechanical analysis of a cylinder subjected to a thermal shock.

## 1 Introduction

Transient thermal analyses are considered in which abrupt variations of the loadings occur — for example, thermal shocks. In certain cases, it is noted that the temperature oscillates spatially and temporally. Moreover, if a temperature profile is observed at a given time instant of the transient, the temperature may at certain nodes exceed the min. and max. bounds imposed by the initial conditions and the boundary conditions. This physically unacceptable result violates what is called the “maximum principle”.

The diagonalisation of the mass matrix can solve these problems of overshoot of the maximum. This is detailed in the note . Only the main results are recalled here.

The maximum principle is recalled in the continuous case, then sufficient conditions are expressed that make it possible to verify it for the discrete equations. It is shown in particular that the diagonalisation of the thermal mass matrix is one of these sufficient conditions, and various methods for diagonalising $\mathrm{M}$ are presented.

Another sufficient condition depends on the thermal stiffness matrix (conduction). From this point of view, the thermal finite elements used in *Code_Aster* are studied more particularly.

It follows that, in the case of linear elements, all the sufficient conditions for verifying the maximum principle are gathered. In particular, the diagonalisation of the mass does indeed make it possible to obtain a regular solution. On the other hand, for quadratic elements, the oscillations cannot be prevented.

The solution proposed in *Code_Aster* is therefore described: the modellings developed in 2D (AXIS_DIAG, PLAN_DIAG) and 3D work with linear elements only.

A numerical study of a thermal shock on a cylinder makes it possible to illustrate these results.

## 2 Maximum principle

### 2.1 Statement of the principle for the continuous case

One of the possible statements of the maximum principle for the heat operator is given here (in the absence of source terms, and in homogeneous isotropic linear thermal analysis) .

Let $\Omega$ be a bounded open set of ${ℝ}^{n}$ with boundary $\Gamma$ , whose closure is denoted $\stackrel{ˉ}{\Omega}$ .

Let $u(\text{x,t})$ be such that:

$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}-\Delta u\le 0\text{sur}\Omega \times ]0,T[,(T>0)$

of class ${C}^{2}$ with respect to $x$ and $u$ of class ${C}^{1}$ with respect to $t$ on $\Omega '\times ]0,T[$

Then $\underset{\overline{\Omega}\times \left[0,T\right]}{\text{Max}}u=\underset{P}{\text{Max}}u$ , where $P=(\overline{\Omega}\times \left\lbrace 0\right\rbrace )\cup (\Gamma \times \left[0,T\right])$ is the boundary of the cylinder $\Omega \times ]0\text{,T}[$ .

This result therefore ensures that the maximum of $u$ is necessarily reached either at the initial conditions or on a boundary of the domain during the transient.

### 2.2 Respect of the maximum principle at the discrete level

The heat equation (heat conduction) is considered:

$div(\lambda .\nabla T)+s(x,t)={\mathrm{\rho C}}_{p}\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}$ **+ Boundary conditions** **+ initial condition** $T({t}_{0}\text{,x})={T}^{0}(x)$

| with | $T$ | temperature |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | $s$ | heat per unit volume (internal sources) |
|  | $t$ | time variable |
|  | $x$ | space variable |
|  | $\lambda$ | thermal conductivity coefficient |
|  | ${\mathrm{\rho C}}_{p}$ | volumetric heat at constant pressure |

**Types of boundary conditions (in the linear case):**

- Imposed temperature: Dirichlet boundary condition

$T(x,t)={T}_{\text{imp}}(x,t)\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{\text{imp}}$

- Imposed normal flux: Neumann condition defining the flux entering the domain

$-q(x,t).n=f(x,t)\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{\text{flux}}$

- Exchange: Fourier boundary condition modelling the convective exchanges on the boundaries of the domain

$-q(x,t).n=h(x,t)({T}_{\text{ext}}(x,t)-T(x,t))\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{\text{échange}}$

The variational formulation of the problem is the following:

$\underset{\Omega}{\int}{\mathrm{\rho C}}_{p}\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}.\mathrm{\nu d}\Omega +\underset{\Omega}{\int}\lambda \nabla T.\nabla \nu d\Omega +\underset{{\Gamma}_{\text{échange}}}{\int}\mathrm{hT}.\nu d\Gamma =\underset{\Omega}{\int}s.\nu d\Omega +\underset{{\Gamma}_{\text{flux}}}{\int}f.\nu d\Gamma +\underset{{\Gamma}_{\text{échange}}}{\int}{\mathrm{hT}}_{\text{ext}}.\nu d\Gamma$

$\forall v$ satisfying $v={T}_{\text{imp}}(x,t)\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{\text{imp}}$

After spatial discretisation of this equation, the system is obtained:

$M\left\lbrace \frac{\partial T}{\partial t}(t)\right\rbrace +\text{KT}(t)=F(t)$ .

| with | $T(t)$ : vector of the nodal temperatures |
| --- | --- |
| $M$ : thermal mass matrix | $M=\sum_{e}\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}\mathrm{\rho C}N{N}^{T}\text{dV}$ |
| $K$ : thermal stiffness matrix | $K=\sum_{e}(\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}\lambda \nabla N.\nabla {N}^{T}\text{dV}+\underset{{\Gamma}_{{\text{échange}}_{e}}}{\int}hN.{N}^{T}d\Gamma )$ |
| $F$ : right-hand-side vector | $F=\sum_{e}(\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}sNd\Omega +\underset{{\Gamma}_{{\text{flux}}_{e}}}{\int}fNd\Gamma +\underset{{\Gamma}_{{\text{échange}}_{e}}}{\int}{\mathrm{hT}}_{\text{ext}}Nd\Gamma )$ |
| $N$ : (shape functions) |  |

For the time discretisation, a $\theta$ -method is applied $(\theta \in [0,1])$ , which leads to:

$(M+\theta \Delta tK){T}^{n+1}=(M+(\theta -1)\Delta tK){T}^{n}+\theta \langle {F}^{n+1}\rangle +(1-\theta )\langle {F}^{n}\rangle$

where ${T}^{n},{T}^{n+1}$ are the vectors of the nodal temperatures at the time instants ${t}_{n}{\text{,t}}_{\text{n+}1}$ .

### 2.3 Sufficient conditions for the respect of the maximum principle at the discrete level

One of the characteristics of non-respect of the maximum principle is the appearance of oscillations (temporal or spatial): if the variation of the temperature at a node is observed over time, it is noted that the solution oscillates and exceeds the minimum and maximum values determined by the initial conditions and the boundary conditions. Or again, at a given time instant, spatial oscillations are observed.

Sufficient conditions on $\Delta t$ , $K$ and $M$ are therefore sought so that the solution does not oscillate over time ( , , ). Indeed, necessary and sufficient conditions cannot be obtained. Conditions of non-oscillation of the solution over time are therefore sought. If these are verified, it will be checked that the spatial oscillations have also disappeared, and then the respect of the maximum principle is ensured.

**Assumptions:**

*In order to be able to express these sufficient conditions of non-oscillation, two assumptions must be added:*

- *one places oneself at the elementary level. The respect of the properties at the elementary level is sufficient for the non-oscillation conditions to be verified for the assembled matrices.*
- *it is considered that* **the stiffness matrix** $K$ **is formed only of the volumetric term** ${K}_{V}=\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}\lambda \nabla N.\nabla {N}^{T}\text{dV}$

*This assumption is not valid for all the boundary conditions (see paragraph 3.1.3).*

The sufficient conditions of non-oscillation amount to expressing conditions on the time step and on the diagonal and off-diagonal terms of $M$ and $K$ so that certain properties of these matrices are verified (based on the monotonicity of the matrices) :

In the general case, the off-diagonal terms may be of any sign. A quick study makes it possible to determine the conditions on t as a function of their signs so that the previous equations are verified:

|  | ${K}_{ij}\ge 0$ | ${K}_{ij}\le 0$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ${M}_{ij}\ge 0$ | ${M}_{ij}+\theta .\Delta {\text{tK}}_{ij}\le 0\text{}i\ne j$ [] unconditionally false except ${M}_{ij}={K}_{ij}=0$ | $\underset{i\ne j}{\max}(\frac{{M}_{ij}}{-\theta {K}_{ij}})\le \Delta t\le \underset{i}{\min}(\frac{{M}_{ii}}{(1-\theta ){K}_{ii}})$ |
| ${M}_{ij}\le 0$ | ${M}_{ij}+(\theta -1)\Delta {\text{tK}}_{ij}\ge 0\text{}i\ne j$ [] unconditionally false except ${M}_{ij}={K}_{ij}=0$ | $\underset{i\ne j}{\max}(\frac{{M}_{ij}}{(1-\theta ){K}_{ij}})\le \Delta t\le \underset{i}{\min}(\frac{{M}_{ii}}{(1-\theta ){K}_{ii}})$ |
|  |  |  |
|  | Whatever $\Delta t$ and the form of $M$ , there is a **risk** of oscillations. | Interval to be respected on $\Delta t$ . The diagonalisation of $M$ makes it possible to remove the lower bound. |

The sufficient conditions for avoiding the oscillations are then:

${K}_{ij}\le 0\text{}i\ne j$

$\Delta {t}_{\min}\le \Delta t\le \Delta {t}_{\max}$

with:

- $\Delta {t}_{\max}=\underset{i}{\min}(\frac{{M}_{ii}}{(1-\theta ){K}_{ii}})$ and
- $\underset{}{\Delta {t}_{\min}=\underset{i\ne j}{\max}}(\frac{{M}_{ij}}{(1-\theta ){K}_{ij}},\frac{{M}_{ij}}{-\theta {K}_{ij}})$ ,

Consequently, the elementary matrices must first satisfy ${K}_{ij}\le 0$ (this is the case of the **linear finite elements** studied further on).

As regards the interval on the time step:

If the oscillations are due to a time step that is too large $(\Delta t>\Delta {t}_{\max})$ , one may advise:

- **either to choose a time-integration scheme of implicit type** $(\theta =1)$ , in order to eliminate the upper bound of the interval.
- **or to reduce** $\Delta t$ . (In practice, it is difficult to know an order of magnitude of $\Delta {t}_{\max}$ ).

Quite often, the problem of oscillations arises for small time steps $(\Delta t<\Delta {t}_{\min})$ ; indeed, in order to take into account the variations of the solution (for example, during a thermal shock), one is led to choose a fine discretisation in time. In this case, in order to avoid the oscillations, one may suggest:

- **either to increase the value of the time steps**. In practice, this is not always possible because $\mathrm{Dt}$ may be imposed by the nature of the problem (rapid variation of the loading). Moreover, it is difficult to have an order of magnitude of $\Delta {t}_{\min}$ .
- **or to reduce the size of the mesh cells** and thus increase the number of elements. Indeed, the value of $\Delta {t}_{\min}$ depends directly on the spatial discretisation:

The expressions of the elementary matrices are indeed:

${M}_{ij}=\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}\rho {\text{CN}}_{i}{N}_{j}\text{dV}$

${K}_{ij}=\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}\lambda \nabla {N}_{i}\nabla {N}_{j}\text{dV}$

For the 2D elements, the terms of $M$ are therefore of the form $\mathrm{\rho C}\times \mathrm{Surface}$ whereas those of $K$ are a function of $\lambda$ only. This solution remains the best if one is not limited by the analysis cost, because the thermal solution and above all the mechanical solution will be all the more accurate.

- **Or to diagonalise the matrix** $M$ , which removes the lower bound of the interval.

This is the solution proposed here.

In the remainder of the study, only the problem of the oscillations that appear for time steps that are too small is considered: $\Delta t<\Delta {t}_{\min}$ . The chosen method of diagonalisation of the matrix $M$ is presented more precisely, and the various types of elements to which it applies.

## 3 Retained diagonalisation method and types of elements

### 3.1 Elements and boundary conditions such that the off-diagonal terms of $K$ are negative

It has been seen that the diagonalisation of $M$ is effective only when the off-diagonal terms of the stiffness matrix $K$ are negative. Otherwise, one of the sufficient conditions of non-oscillation is unconditionally false, whatever the form of $M$ .

For each of the finite elements used in thermal analysis in *Code_Aster,* it is checked whether the elementary stiffness matrix of the element has negative off-diagonal terms, relying mainly on , which gives the analytical expressions of the elementary matrices for the classical finite elements. The observations made in are summarised here.

#### 3.1.1 Linear elements

##### 3.1.1.1 Elements TRIA3, TETRA4, PENTA6

The elementary matrix $K$ is a function of the cotangents of the angles. If one of the angles is obtuse ($\ge 90°$ ), certain off-diagonal terms of $K$ are positive. If all the angles are acute, the property is verified.

The same type of result is obtained in 3D for the tetrahedron with 4 nodes and the pentahedron with 6 nodes.

##### 3.1.1.2 Elements QUAD4 and HEXA8

Certain off-diagonal terms of $K$ may be positive if the element is too elongated in one direction. Otherwise, the property is verified.

The same type of result is obtained in 3D for the element HEXA8.

##### 3.1.1.3 3D pyramid element with 5 nodes

For this element, the shape functions are no longer polynomials but rational fractions in $x,y,z$ . For this type of element, the expression, even approximate, of $K$ is not available.

#### 3.1.2 Quadratic elements

##### 3.1.2.1 Element TRIA6

In $K$ , certain off-diagonal terms are necessarily positive.

##### 3.1.2.2 Element QUAD9

Likewise, on the analytical expression of the terms of $K$ , it is observed that certain of the off-diagonal terms are necessarily positive.

##### 3.1.2.3 Element QUAD8

For this element, the complete expression of $K$ for the real element is not available. But for the reference element, it is noted that certain off-diagonal terms are positive.

#### 3.1.3 Boundary conditions

The matrix $K$ is not always reduced to the term ${K}_{V}=\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}\lambda \nabla N.\nabla {N}^{T}\text{dV}$ . Depending on the boundary conditions used, a surface term may be added (case of exchange or of the non-linear flux). This term may be positive.

For an exchange condition, for example, the term ${K}_{\mathrm{échange}}=\underset{{\Gamma}_{{\text{échange}}_{e}}}{\int}hN.{N}^{T}d\Gamma$ is added to the matrix $K$ . It is always positive and therefore does not satisfy the properties 2.3 stated above (${K}_{ij}\ge 0$ ). The risk of oscillation is therefore always present even when diagonalising the mass matrix. In this case, refining the mesh where the boundary condition is applied makes it possible to reduce the positive contribution of the boundary condition and often to suppress the oscillations.

#### 3.1.4 Conclusion on the elements: properties of the matrices $K$

For the linear elements, if the real element is not too irregular, the off-diagonal terms of $K$ are indeed negative. For the quadratic elements (in 2D) and certain boundary conditions, certain off-diagonal terms of $K$ are positive. Even when diagonalising $M$ , it cannot be ensured that the solution will not oscillate.

In *Code_Aster*, in order to eliminate the problems of oscillation and of overshoot of the maximum, only the mass matrices are diagonalised for thermal analyses carried out on linear elements. For the quadratic elements, it has been seen that the mass matrix could not be diagonalised directly. These elements are therefore subdivided into linear elements which are themselves lumped. This is applied to the 2D quadratic elements in *Code_Aster*, but not to the 3D quadratic elements, not for reasons of method but because automatic subdivision is difficult to implement in 3D.

### 3.2 Diagonalisation method: Integration at the nodes of the elements

If the elementary mass matrix is computed by numerical integration, its terms are written in the form

${M}_{ij}=\underset{{\Omega}_{c}}{\int}{\mathit{\rho CN}}_{i}{N}_{j}\text{dV}\mathrm{\simeq }\sum_{q=1}^{N}{W}_{q}({\mathit{\rho CN}}_{i}{N}_{j}{)}_{q}$

where ${N}_{i}{\mathrm{\rho CN}}_{j}$ is evaluated at the ${q}^{\text{ième}}$ integration point

and ${W}_{q}$ is the integration weight associated with this point.

Classically, the integration points are the Gauss points; the position of the $N$ points and their weight are defined so that the scheme integrates exactly the polynomials of degree $\mathrm{2N}+1$ . If **the integration points are chosen at the nodes of the element**, one obtains: ${M}_{ij}=0$ for $i\ne j$ . This integration method is also called the Newton–Cotes method.

**Note 1: Axisymmetric problems:**

| *If the integration points are at the nodes, one will have, for every type of element, zero masses on the axis of symmetry.* *Indeed,* ${M}_{ij}=\int\mathrm{\rho C}{N}_{i}{N}_{j}2\pi r\text{dr}\text{dz}$ ${M}_{ij}\simeq {\delta}_{ij}2\pi {\mathrm{\rho CW}}_{i}\text{Jac}r({x}_{i})$ *If the integration point* $i$ *is a node of the axis,* $r({x}_{i})=0$ *and the corresponding mass is zero.* *For the axisymmetric elements, the* **nodal integration method is therefore not suitable near the axis** *. In this case, the elements that touch the axis of revolution must be integrated at the Gauss points, using the usual modelling (* *AXIS* *).* |
| --- |

**Note 2: other possible methods of diagonalisation:**

Other methods are studied in , in particular in an attempt to diagonalise the quadratic elements. In practice, they are not retained at present in Code_Aster.

- Scaling of the diagonal terms ( , ): Hinton suggests the scaling of the diagonal terms of the *consistent* matrix $M$ *, so that the total mass of the element is conserved. It is noted that the lumped masses are always positive, even for the quadrangles with 8 and 9 nodes.*
- Row summation ( ): *The values of* ${M}_{ij}$ *are summed by row and the result is concentrated on the diagonal. Unfortunately, this procedure can lead to negative masses, in particular for the quadrangle with 8 nodes.*

**Note 3:**

For the quadratic elements, it is observed in that, even when diagonalising with the method of scaling of the diagonal terms, oscillations are obtained. These elements therefore cannot be used within the framework of diagonalisation (that is, for a mesh that is relatively coarse with respect to the rapidity of the thermal transient).

*Quadratic elements can of course be used in thermal analysis, provided that the fineness of the mesh is adapted to the severity of the thermal shock.*

## 4 Implementation in Code_Aster

In order to eliminate the oscillations of the temperature in space and in time, the modellings AXIS_DIAG, PLAN_DIAG and 3D_DIAG carry out the diagonalisation of the mass matrices during linear (THER_LINEAIRE) and non-linear (THER_NON_LINE) thermal analysis. In order to guarantee its effectiveness, it has been seen that it must be carried out on linear elements.

In the case where the mesh is linear, a diagonalisation of the mass matrices by integration at the nodes is simply carried out.

The modellings available are therefore:

### 4.1 2D modellings

| **Modelling** | **PLAN_DIAG** | **AXIS_DIAG** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mesh cell | Element | Element |
| TRIA3 | THPLTL3 | THAXTL3 |
| QUAD4 | THPLQL4 | THAXQL4 |
| SEG2 | THPLSL2 | THAXSL2 |

**Comments on the 2D elementary computations:**

For the linear elements: the mass terms (matrix on the left-hand side and vector on the right-hand side) are lumped by integration at the nodes. The new elements have elementary computation options identical to the classical elements. The only elementary options modified are therefore MASS_THER and CHAR_THER_EVOL.

In axisymmetric conditions: if elements of the mesh touch the axis, one must not integrate at the nodes that lie on the axis. This layer of elements must therefore be isolated and the modelling AXIS assigned to it.

## 5 Thermal analysis of a cylinder subjected to a cold shock

What has been shown previously is illustrated on a numerical example; namely that diagonalisation is effective for verifying the maximum principle.

The industrial example of the cooling of a cast elbow is taken as inspiration: a cold thermal shock ($289°C$ to $20°C$ ) is applied to a cracked elbow. During the cooling transient, the temperature computed at certain nodes reaches $310°$ without diagonalisation of the mass matrices. For the example treated here, the study is restricted to a hollow cylinder of the same dimension as the elbow, on which a cold thermal shock is applied.

The following figures show the temperature profiles through the thickness of the cylinder at the time instant $t=\mathrm{15s}$ (the time instant at which the overshoots of the maximum are the largest) without diagonalisation of the mass matrices.

## 6 Conclusion

The modellings AXIS_DIAG, PLAN_DIAG and 3D_DIAG are proposed in order to solve the problems of overshoot of the maximum with oscillation of the solution in space and in time that appear during certain transient thermal analyses with an abrupt variation of the loading.

At the discrete level, the analysis leads to a sufficient condition of non-oscillation on the step of the time discretisation, which must belong to an interval:

$\Delta {t}_{\min}\le \Delta t\le \Delta {t}_{\max}$

where the values of $\Delta {t}_{\min}$ and $\Delta {t}_{\max}$ depend on the coefficients of the thermal mass and stiffness matrices as well as on the parameter $\theta$ of the time discretisation.

In practice, if the oscillations come from a time step that is too large $(\Delta t>\Delta {t}_{\max})$ , the choice of an implicit scheme in time $(\theta =1)$ is suggested. If the time steps are too small, the diagonalisation of the mass matrix may make it possible to suppress the oscillations.

For the linear elements, it is shown that diagonalisation does indeed make it possible to avoid the oscillations of the solution. For the quadratic elements, a direct diagonalisation is not sufficient to avoid the oscillations, so this possibility is forbidden.
