# r5.03.80 Methods of load piloting

**Summary:**

This document describes the load-piloting methods available in *Code_Aster* (by a degree of freedom, by arc length, by strain increment and by elastic prediction). They introduce an additional unknown, the intensity of the pilotable part of the loading, and an additional equation, the piloting constraint. These methods make it possible, in particular, to calculate the response of a structure which would exhibit instabilities, of geometric origin (buckling) as well as of material origin (softening).

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Non-linearity and failure of Newton

The Newton method presented in [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01) fails on certain problems which exhibit a structural response that is not strictly monotonic as a function of the imposed displacements or loadings. In the figure , one presents the case where, for a given load level, several solution displacements exist. This problem is therefore not solvable with Newton if one pilots the problem by the loading.

A second case (figure ) concerns geometrically non-linear problems (typically the buckling of thin shells and “gentle” snap-back problems). In this case, for a load level, one has several solutions in displacement and for a displacement level one has several possible loadings. This problem is not directly solvable by the Newton method, whether by piloting in loading or in displacement.

### 1.2 Problems with unknown loading intensity

Piloting can also be used in the case where the problem exhibits, through its natural data setup, an additional unknown which is the intensity of the applied loading. Piloting makes it possible to treat the case where only the direction and the point of application of the loading are known, the intensity remaining an unknown of the problem. For example, in the figure , one sees the case of a cable stretched between two pylons. One knows that a loading which pulls the cable must be applied (one therefore knows the point of application and the direction of the loading) but one does not know the intensity $\eta$ to be applied in order to obtain a given sag $f$.

## 2 Principle of the load-piloting methods

In general, the piloting functionalities available in *Code_Aster* make it possible to determine *the intensity of part of the loading* in order to satisfy a constraint bearing on the displacements. Their use is limited to simulations for which time does not play a physical role, which excludes *a priori* dynamic or viscous problems. It is also incompatible with problems expressing a unilaterality condition (contact and friction). One can distinguish several ranges of use, to which as many piloting methods correspond (factor keyword PILOTAGE):

- Physical control of the forces by the displacement of a point of the structure: piloting by an imposed degree of freedom (TYPE='DDL_IMPO');
- Following of geometric instabilities (buckling), the response of the structure being able to exhibit a “gentle” snap-back: piloting by arc length (TYPE='LONG_ARC');
- Following of material instabilities (in the presence of softening constitutive laws), the response of the structure being able to exhibit a “brutal” snap-back: piloting by elastic prediction (TYPE= 'PRED_ELAS') or, more generally, by the strain increment, (TYPE='DEFORMATION');
- Analysis of the limit loads of structures (TYPE='ANA_LIM'), cf. [ref:R7.07.01 <R7.07.01>].

More precisely, the piloting methods rest on the following two ideas.

On the one hand, one considers that the loading (external forces and given displacements) decomposes additively into two terms, one known and imposed by the user (${L}_{\text{impo}}^{\text{méca}}$ and ${u}_{\text{impo}}^{d}$) and the other (${L}_{\text{pilo}}^{\text{méca}}$ and ${u}_{\text{pilo}}^{d}$) of which only the direction is known, its intensity $\eta$ becoming a new unknown of the problem:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{L}^{\text{méca}}={L}_{\text{impo}}^{\text{méca}}+\eta .{L}_{\text{pilo}}^{\text{méca}}\\ {u}^{d}={u}_{\text{impo}}^{d}+\eta .{u}_{\text{pilo}}^{d}\end{array}
$$

On the other hand, in order to be able to solve the problem, one associates with it a new equation which bears on the displacements and which depends on the time increment: it is the piloting constraint, which is expressed by:

$$
P(\Delta U)=\Delta \tau \text{avec}P(0)=0
$$

where $\Delta \tau$ is indirectly a user datum which is expressed via the current time step $\Delta t$ and a piloting coefficient (COEF_MULT) such that:

$$
\Delta \tau =\frac{\Delta t}{\text{COEF\_MULT}}
$$

The condition $P(0)=0$ is required in order to obtain a displacement increment which is all the smaller as the time step is small. Finally, the unknowns of the problem become the displacements $u$, the Lagrange multipliers $\lambda$ associated with the boundary conditions and the intensity of the piloted loading $\eta$, named ETA_PILOTAGE. The non-linear system to be solved is henceforth written:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}{L}^{int}(u)+{B}^{T}.\lambda & ={L}_{\text{impo}}^{\text{méca}}+\eta .{L}_{\text{pilo}}^{\text{méca}}\\ B.u& ={u}_{\text{impo}}^{d}+\eta .{u}_{\text{pilo}}^{d}\\ P(\Delta u)& =\Delta \tau \end{array}
$$

- At present, follower loadings (i.e. which depend on the displacements) and Dirichlet conditions of type «* DIDI *» are not pilotable.*
- The loading no longer depends directly on time but results from the solution of the whole non-linear system. This implies that the piloted part of the loading must not depend on the physical time, but corresponds to a force which one adjusts in order to satisfy an additional kinematic constraint.*

## 3 Solution of the global system

The introduction of a new equation does not unduly disturb the method for solving the non-linear system. Indeed, one proceeds as in [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01), that is to say that the solution is incremental. One denotes the time step $i$ as a subscript and the Newton iteration $n$ as a superscript. The non-linear problem is then solved in two stages:

- A prediction phase which gives a first estimate of the displacements and of the Lagrange multipliers, denoted $(\Delta {u}_{i}^{0},\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0})$;
- A Newton correction phase $(\delta {u}_{i}^{n},\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n})$ which corrects this first estimate;

With a sufficient number ${n}_{\mathrm{CV}}$ of Newton iterations, one obtains a converged result 1
:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}{u}_{i}^{\text{convergé}}={u}_{i-1}+\Delta {u}_{i}^{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n={n}_{\mathrm{CV}}}\delta {u}_{i}^{j}\\ {\lambda}_{i}^{\text{convergé}}={\lambda}_{i-1}+\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{n={n}_{\mathrm{CV}}}\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{j}\end{array}
$$

The principle is therefore to linearise the system () which one writes at time step $i$:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}{L}_{i}^{int}+{B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}& ={L}_{\text{impo},i}^{\text{méca}}+{\eta}_{i}.{L}_{\text{pilo},i}^{\text{méca}}\\ B.{u}_{i}& ={u}_{\text{impo},i}^{d}+{\eta}_{i}.{u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{d}\\ P(\Delta {u}_{i})& =\Delta {\tau}_{i}\end{array}
$$

There will be no linearisation with respect to the piloting variable ${\eta}_{i}$. In this way, one preserves the whole methodology for updating the tangent operator already implemented for analyses without piloting. Moreover, the “banded” structure of the tangent matrix is retained. The solution scheme is therefore no longer strictly a Newton method (which is not troublesome, because we shall see that the piloting equation is, at most, of degree two). In prediction, if one linearises the system () without the piloting equation, with respect to time about $({u}_{i-1},{\lambda}_{i-1})$, one obtains (see the whole development in [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01)):

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}{\mathrm{K}}_{i-1}.\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0}& ={\mathrm{L}}_{\text{impo},i}^{\text{méca}}+{\eta}_{i}.{\mathrm{L}}_{\text{pilo},i}^{\text{méca}}-{\mathrm{Q}}_{i-1}^{T}.{\sigma}_{i-1}+\Delta {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{varc}}\\ \mathrm{B}.\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{0}& ={\mathrm{u}}_{\text{impo},i}^{d}+{\eta}_{i}.{\mathrm{u}}_{\text{pilo},i}^{d}-\mathrm{B}.{\mathrm{u}}_{i-1}\end{array}
$$

In correction, one linearises the system () still without the piloting equation, with respect to time, but about $({u}_{i}^{n},{\lambda}_{i}^{n})$, one has:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}{\mathrm{K}}_{i}^{n-1}.\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}+{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n}& ={\mathrm{L}}_{\text{impo},i}^{\text{méca}}+{\eta}_{i}.{\mathrm{L}}_{\text{pilo},i}^{\text{méca}}-{\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int,n-1}-{\mathrm{B}}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}^{n-1}\\ \mathrm{B}.\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}& ={\eta}_{i}.{\mathrm{u}}_{\text{pilo},i}^{d}\end{array}
$$

One will bring the two systems together into a common writing, in order to simplify the presentation. The system to be solved is finally written:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc}{K}_{i}^{n-1}& {B}^{T}\\ B& 0\end{array}\right].\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{\delta u}_{i}^{n}\\ {\delta \lambda }_{i}^{n}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{L}_{\text{impo},i}^{n-1}\\ {u}_{\text{impo},i}\end{array}\right\rbrace +{\eta}_{i}.\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{L}_{\text{pilo},i}\\ {u}_{\text{pilo},i}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

Several remarks can be made:

- The matrix ${K}_{i}^{n-1}$ depends both on the current time step and possibly on the previous Newton iteration. The various ways of constructing it (quasi-Newton, elastic, secant, consistent, rate tangent, etc.) are described in [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01);
- One has assumed that the external loadings were linear (they depend only on the time step). One therefore does not consider follower loadings such as pressure or the centrifugal force, although from the theoretical point of view this poses no difficulty. On the other hand, the material may be described with a non-linear constitutive behaviour, which implies that ${\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{int,n-1}$ depends on the Newton iteration (result of the linearisation of the internal forces).
- The Dirichlet boundary conditions are always linear, which allows the matrix $B$ to be constant over the whole transient.
- With the right choice of the tangent matrix and of the right-hand side, formally, there is equivalence between $(\delta {u}_{i}^{n=0},\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n=0})$ and the prediction increment $(\Delta {u}_{i}^{0},\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0})$.

One can now express the corrections of the displacements $\delta {u}_{i}^{n}$ and of the Lagrange multipliers $\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n}$ as a function of ${\eta}_{i}$, subject to the solution of the linear system () with respect to each of the two right-hand sides. That is to say that one separates the two solutions:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{\delta u}_{i}^{n}\\ {\delta \lambda }_{i}^{n}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{\delta u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n}\\ {\delta \lambda }_{\text{impo},i}^{n}\end{array}\right\rbrace +{\eta}_{i}.\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{\delta u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\\ {\delta \lambda }_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

These two solutions correspond to the decoupling of the two loadings:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{L}_{i}^{n-1}\\ {u}_{i}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{L}_{\text{impo},i}^{n-1}\\ {u}_{\text{impo},i}\end{array}\right\rbrace +{\eta}_{i}.\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{L}_{\text{pilo},i}\\ {u}_{\text{pilo},i}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

One solves independently 2
:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc}{K}_{i}^{n-1}& {B}^{T}\\ B& 0\end{array}\right].\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{\delta u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n}\\ {\delta \lambda }_{\text{impo},i}^{n}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{L}_{\text{cst},i}^{n-1}\\ {u}_{\text{cst},i}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

And:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc}{K}_{i}^{n-1}& {B}^{T}\\ B& 0\end{array}\right].\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{\delta u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\\ {\delta \lambda }_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}{L}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n-1}\\ {u}_{\text{pilo},i}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

One can now substitute the displacement correction $\Delta {u}_{i}^{n}$ into the piloting control equation of the system; there results a scalar equation in ${\eta}_{i}$:

$$
\tilde{P}({\eta}_{i})\underset{\text{déf.}}{=}P(\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{\text{impo},i}^{n}+{\eta}_{i}.\delta {\mathrm{u}}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n})=\Delta {\tau}_{i}
$$

The method for solving this equation depends on the nature of the piloting control adopted, cf. [§ 7 ]. Finally, all that remains is to update the displacement unknowns and the Lagrange multipliers:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}\Delta {u}_{i}^{n}=\Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n}+{\eta}_{i}.\delta {u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\\ {\lambda}_{i}^{n}={\lambda}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {\lambda}_{\text{impo},i}^{n}+{\eta}_{i}.\delta {\lambda}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\end{array}
$$

The notion of a “converged” result is described in more detail in [R5.03.01].

In practice, the factorisation of the matrix is performed only once and one solves the two systems simultaneously.

## 4 Piloting control equation

### 4.1 Piloting by control of a displacement degree of freedom: DDL_IMPO

For this first type of piloting, the function $P$ merely extracts a degree of freedom from the displacement increment. In particular, it is therefore a linear function:

$$
P(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n})=\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace =\Delta {\tau}_{i}
$$

Where the nodal vector $\langle S\rangle$ is a selection vector which is zero everywhere except for the degree of freedom to be extracted, where it equals one:

$$
\langle S\rangle =\langle 0\dots 0\dots \underset{\text{noeud n, ddl i}}{1}\dots 0\dots 0\rangle
$$

Equation () then likewise reduces to a linear equation:

$$
{\eta}_{i}=\frac{\Delta {\tau}_{i}-\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace -\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n}\right\rbrace }{\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\right\rbrace }
$$

One will note that there is no solution when the piloted displacement correction $\delta {u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}$ does not make it possible to adjust the required degree of freedom, which can happen if, by mistake, one blocks the degree of freedom in question. In this case, the code will stop with ECHEC DU PILOTAGE.

### 4.2 Piloting by arc length: LONG_ARC

Another very widely used form of piloting consists in controlling the norm of the displacement increment (with respect to certain nodes and certain components): one then speaks of cylindrical arc-length piloting, see Bonnet and Wood [bib1]. More precisely, the function $P$ is expressed by:

$$
P(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n})={\parallel \Delta {u}_{i}^{n}\parallel }_{\text{<S>}}=\sqrt{(\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace ).(\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace )}=\Delta {\tau}_{i}
$$

Where, again, the nodal vector $\langle S\rangle$ makes it possible to select the degrees of freedom used for the calculation of the norm (it equals one for the selected degrees of freedom and zero elsewhere). In this case one will note that $\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace$ is not a scalar, but a vector corresponding to the product of the components of $\langle S\rangle$ by those of $\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace$. In this case, the piloting equation reduces to a second-degree equation:

$$
\begin{array}{}{\eta}_{i}^{2}.\left[(\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\right\rbrace ).(\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\right\rbrace )\right]+\\ 2.{\eta}_{i}.\left[(\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n}\right\rbrace ).(\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\right\rbrace )\right]+\\ \left[(\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n}\right\rbrace ).(\langle S\rangle .\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n}\right\rbrace )-\Delta {\tau}_{i}^{2}\right]=0\end{array}
$$

This equation may admit no solution. In that case, one chooses the value ${\eta}_{i}$ which minimises the polynomial (). One then indeed verifies $\tilde{P}({\eta}_{i})>\Delta {\tau}_{i}$. In the contrary case, it admits two roots (or a double root). One chooses that of the two which minimises:

- either the norm of the displacement increment $∥\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}∥$;
- either the equilibrium residual $R({u}_{i},{t}_{i})={L}_{i}^{int}-{L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}$;
- either the angle formed by $\Delta {u}_{i-1}$ and $\Delta {u}_{i}^{n}$ (where $\Delta {u}_{i-1}$ is the solution displacement increment of the previous time step), that is to say the one which maximises the cosine of this angle, whose expression is:

$$
\cos(\Delta {u}_{i-1},\Delta {u}_{i}^{n})=\frac{\langle \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n}+{\eta}_{i}.\delta {u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\rangle .\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace }{∥\Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n}+{\eta}_{i}.\delta {u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}∥.∥\Delta {u}_{i-1}∥}
$$

To calculate the angle, one uses only the unknowns corresponding to the displacements, to the exclusion of all the others (in particular the rotations in the structural elements).

One notes that this last angular criterion selects the wrong solution when one passes from a loading phase to an unloading phase or vice versa, that is to say when the piloting coefficient ${C}_{i}$ changes sign. The value ${C}_{i-1}$ is therefore stored and updated at each converged time step, its initialisation for each call to STAT_NON_LINE being transparent to the user: ${C}_{i-1}$ is thus initialised when the initial state is read, ${C}_{i}$ having been included in the analysis parameters to be archived in the result concept. If ${C}_{i}{C}_{i-1}>0$, one maximises criterion (). In the contrary case, one minimises it in order to select the displacement increment in the opposite direction to the previous increment.

### 4.3 Piloting by the strain increment: DEFORMATION

Piloting by strain increment consists in requiring that the strain increment of the current step remain close in direction to the strain at the beginning of the time step, and this for at least one Gauss point of the structure. One thus requires, qualitatively, that **at a minimum** one point of the structure retain the strain mode which it had previously (for example, tension in a given direction).

#### 4.3.1 Case of small strains

For small strains, one can account for this requirement by means of the following choice of piloting function:

$$
P(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n})=\underset{g}{\text{Max}}\frac{{\varepsilon}_{g,i-1}}{\parallel {\varepsilon}_{g,i-1}\parallel }.\Delta {\varepsilon}_{g,i}=\Delta {\tau}_{i}
$$

Where the index $g$ runs over the Gauss points of the structure (or only the mesh cells specified by the keyword GROUP_MA in PILOTAGE) and where the strain at a Gauss point is deduced from the nodal displacement vector via the use of the matrices “symmetric part of the gradient of the shape functions” ${B}_{g}$ (not to be confused with the matrix of the Dirichlet conditions):

$$
{\varepsilon}_{g,i-1}={B}_{g}\cdot {u}_{i-1}
$$

The piloting control as a function of ${\eta}_{i}$ is then written:

$$
P(\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n})=\underset{g}{\text{Max}}({A}_{g}^{(0)}+{\eta}_{i}\cdot {A}_{g}^{(1)})=\underset{g}{\text{Max}}({L}_{g}({\eta}_{i}))=\Delta {\tau}_{i}
$$

With the two terms:

$$
{A}_{g}^{(0)}=\frac{{\varepsilon}_{g,i-1}}{\parallel {\varepsilon}_{g,i-1}\parallel }\cdot {B}_{g}(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n})
$$

And:

$$
{A}_{g}^{(1)}=\frac{{\varepsilon}_{g,i-1}}{\parallel {\varepsilon}_{g,i-1}\parallel }\cdot {B}_{g}(\delta {u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n})
$$

This piloting does not depend on the constitutive law, provided that it uses the small-strain tensor (option DEFORMATION='PETIT' or DEFORMATION='PETIT_REAC').

#### 4.3.2 Case of large strains

In the presence of large strains, one can generalise the piloting function () by employing Green–Lagrange strains (Lagrangian measure of the strains in the initial configuration):

$$
P(\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n})=\underset{g}{\text{Max}}\frac{{\mathrm{E}}_{g,i-1}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{\mathrm{E}}_{g,i-1}\mathrm{\parallel }}.\Delta {\mathrm{E}}_{g,i}
$$

The strain measure $E$ is written as a function of the transformation-gradient tensor $F$:

$$
\mathrm{E}=\frac{1}{2}\cdot ({\mathrm{F}}^{T}.\mathrm{F}-\mathrm{I})
$$

However, one would no longer arrive, as previously, at a piecewise affine function. To remedy this, one decides to carry out a linearisation of $\Delta {E}_{g,i}$ with respect to $\Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}$. $P$ then has an expression similar to () with:

$$
{A}_{g}^{(0)}=\frac{{E}_{g,i-1}}{\parallel {E}_{g,i-1}\parallel }\cdot \text{sym}\left[{F}_{g,i}^{T}.{\nabla}_{g}(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n})\right]
$$

And:

$$
{A}_{g}^{(1)}=\frac{{E}_{g,i-1}}{\parallel {E}_{g,i-1}\parallel }\cdot \text{sym}\left[{F}_{g,i}^{T}.\delta {u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n}\right]
$$

Where ${\nabla}_{g}(u)$ denotes the (non-symmetrised) gradient of the displacements $u$ evaluated at the Gauss point of index $g$. Just as in the previous case, this piloting does not depend on the constitutive law, provided that it uses the large-strain tensor.

#### 4.3.3 Solution of the non-linear piloting equation

The function $P(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n})$ is convex and piecewise linear. It generally admits none, one or two solutions, cf. figure (). When it admits no solution, one stops the algorithm of STAT_NON_LINE (piloting failure): if the user uses the automatic subdivision of the time step, the latter will then be activated.

In the case where it admits two solutions, three possibilities are offered: one chooses that of the two which minimises:

- either the norm of the displacement increment $∥\Delta {\mathrm{u}}_{i}^{n}∥$;
- either the equilibrium residual $R({u}_{i},{t}_{i})={L}_{i}^{int}-{L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}$;
- either the angle formed by $\Delta {u}_{i-1}$ and $\Delta {u}_{i}^{n}$;

### 4.4 Piloting by elastic prediction: PRED_ELAS

#### 4.4.1 Algorithm

If piloting by the strain increment proves sufficient to follow dissipative solutions in most material instabilities, the existence of solutions is nevertheless not proved. One then prefers a piloting method based on the elastic prediction, for which the existence of solutions is demonstrated but which, on the other hand, is specific to each constitutive law (implemented only for the laws ENDO_SCALAIRE, ENDO_FRAGILE, ENDO_ISOT_BETON, ENDO_ORTH_BETON, BETON_DOUBLE_DP, VMIS_ISOT_* and those relating to joint elements [R5.06.09](doc:r5.06.09)). More precisely, when the constitutive law is governed by a threshold, one defines $P$ as the maximum over all the Gauss points of the value of the threshold function in the case of an elastic trial (incremental elastic response of the material).

Thus, let us consider that the state of the material is described by the strain $\varepsilon$ and a set of internal variables $a$. Let us call respectively $\sigma (\varepsilon ,a)$ and $A(\varepsilon ,a)$ the stresses and the thermodynamic forces associated with $a$. Let us further suppose that the evolution laws of $a$ are governed by a threshold $f(A,\varepsilon ,a)$:

$$
f(A,\varepsilon ,a)\le 0\text{et}\lambda \cdot f(A,\varepsilon ,a)=0
$$

And a flow function $G(A,\varepsilon ,a)$:

$$
\dot{a}=\lambda G\cdot (A,\varepsilon ,a)
$$

Such a formulation encompasses most dissipative constitutive models which are independent of the loading rate. The threshold function then equals, for an elastic trial:

$$
{f}^{\text{el}}(\varepsilon )=f(A(\varepsilon ,{a}_{i-1}),\varepsilon ,{a}_{i-1})
$$

One simplifies the problem by linearising ${f}^{\text{el}}$ with respect to $\varepsilon$ in the neighbourhood of the point ${\varepsilon}^{\text{*}}$ such that ${f}^{\text{el}}=\Delta \tau$:

$$
{f}_{L}^{\text{el}}(\varepsilon )\underset{\text{def}}{=}{f}^{\text{el}}({\varepsilon}^{\text{*}})+{(\frac{\partial f}{\partial A}\cdot \frac{\partial A}{\partial \varepsilon }+\frac{\partial f}{\partial \varepsilon })\mid }_{{\varepsilon}^{\text{*}}}\cdot (\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{\text{*}})
$$

The choice of ${\varepsilon}^{\text{*}}$ makes it possible to solve the exact problem, as shown in [bib3]. It therefore requires solving a local non-linear problem for each Gauss point, then the global algorithm is based on straight lines, which ensures it a high efficiency. There exist zero, one or two solutions to the local problem ${f}^{\text{el}}=\Delta \tau$. In the case of absence of a solution to the local problem, the global problem cannot have a solution: one stops. Otherwise (case of one or two solutions), one linearises about each of the solutions.

Finally, the piloting control function is defined as the maximum of ${f}_{L}^{\text{el}}$ with respect to all the Gauss points $g$, a function which depends only on $\Deltau$:

$$
P(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n})=\underset{g}{\text{Max}}{f}_{L}^{\text{el}}({\varepsilon}_{g,i-1}+{B}_{g}\cdot \Delta {u}_{i}^{n})=\Delta {\tau}_{i}
$$

Finally, the piloting control equation is written:

$$
P(\Delta {u}_{i}^{n})=\underset{g}{\text{Max}}({A}_{g}^{(0)}+{\eta}_{i}\cdot {A}_{g}^{(1)})=\underset{g}{\text{Max}}({L}_{g}({\eta}_{i}))=\Delta {\tau}_{i}
$$

With:

$$
{A}^{(0)}={f}^{\text{el}}({\varepsilon}^{\text{*}})+{(\frac{\partial f}{\partial A}\cdot \frac{\partial A}{\partial \varepsilon }+\frac{\partial f}{\partial \varepsilon })\mid }_{{\varepsilon}^{\text{*}}}\cdot ({B}_{g}({u}_{i-1}+\Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}+\delta {u}_{\text{impo},i}^{n})-{\varepsilon}^{\text{*}})
$$

And:

$$
{A}^{(1)}={(\frac{\partial f}{\partial A}\cdot \frac{\partial A}{\partial \varepsilon }+\frac{\partial f}{\partial \varepsilon })\mid }_{{\varepsilon}^{\text{*}}}\cdot ({B}_{g}\cdot \delta {u}_{\text{pilo},i}^{n})
$$

#### 4.4.2 Expressions as a function of the constitutive laws

One is thus brought back to a problem identical to that of piloting by the strain increment. One of course employs the same solution algorithms as those presented in the previous paragraph (see § 11 ).

The characteristics of the piloting equation depend on the constitutive law because the expression of the elastic threshold function ${f}^{\text{el}}$ is itself a datum of the non-linear constitutive problem:

- For the elements with internal discontinuity PLAN_ELDI and AXIS_ELDI, the constitutive law is of type CZM_EXP and the expression of the piloting law will be found in the documentation [R7.02.14](doc:r7.02.14). The elastic threshold function is written ${f}_{\mathit{el}}({\sigma}_{n},{\sigma}_{t})=\sqrt{\text{<}{\sigma}_{n}{\text{>}}_{+}^{2}+{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}-R(\kappa )$;
- For the joint elements *_JOINT, the constitutive laws are regularised CZM laws CZM_EXP_REG and CZM_LIN_REG (see [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11)), and for elements of interface type *_INTERFACE, the constitutive laws are non-regularised CZM laws such as CZM_OUV_MIX and CZM_TAC_MIX. The expression of the piloting law will be found in the documentation [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11). The elastic threshold function is written ${f}_{\mathit{el}}\mathrm{\simeq }\underset{\mathit{pt}\mathit{gauss}}{\max}\left\lbrace \frac{\mathrm{\parallel }{\delta}^{0}+\eta {\delta}^{1}\mathrm{\parallel }-{\kappa}^{-}}{{G}_{c}/{\sigma}_{c}+{\kappa}^{-}}\right\rbrace =\frac{\Delta \tau }{C}$;
- For the law ENDO_ISOT_BETON ([R7.01.04](doc:r7.01.04)), there is a particularity. Instead of seeking a piloting parameter $\eta$ which makes the criterion depart by a value $\mathrm{\Delta \tau }$ with the damage coming from the previous time step, one seeks a parameter $\eta$ which brings us back onto the criterion with a damage increased by $\mathrm{\Delta \tau }$. The elastic threshold function is therefore written ${\tilde{f}}^{\text{el}}(\eta ,{d}^{-})=\mathrm{\Delta \tau }\Rightarrow {\tilde{f}}^{\text{el}}(\eta ,{d}^{-}+\mathrm{\Delta \tau })=0$;
- For the law ENDO_ORTH_BETON, see [R7.01.09](doc:r7.01.09);
- For the other damage laws of elastic type such as ENDO_SCALAIRE or ENDO_FRAGILE (with a local formulation or with gradients), just as for the law ENDO_ISOT_BETON, the elastic threshold function is chosen so as to seek a piloting parameter controlled by the damage value and not by the departure from the criterion. The elastic threshold function is written:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{f}}^{\text{el}}(\eta )=\frac{1}{2}({e}_{0}+\eta {e}_{1})\cdot E\cdot ({e}_{0}+\eta {e}_{1})-s\\ \lbrace  \begin{array}{cccc}\text{loi locale}& {e}_{0}={\epsilon}_{0}& {e}_{1}={\epsilon}_{1}& s=k({d}^{-})\\ \text{gradient d'endommagement}& {e}_{0}={\epsilon}_{0}& {e}_{1}={\epsilon}_{1}& s=k({d}^{-})-c\Delta {d}^{-}\\ \text{déformation régularisée}& {e}_{0}={\bar{\epsilon}}_{0}& {e}_{1}={\bar{\epsilon}}_{1}& s=k({d}^{-})\end{array}\end{array}
$$

#### 4.4.3 Illustration of the maximisation algorithm

In order to illustrate our argument and in particular to understand why such a choice of ${\varepsilon}^{\text{*}}$ makes it possible to solve the exact problem, one takes the example of a cohesive constitutive law CZM_EXP_REG or CZM_LIN_REG (cf [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11)) implemented on a joint element (cf [R3.06.09](doc:r3.06.09)). The displacement jump $\delta$ is the primal variable (denoted generically $\epsilon$ in what precedes), and $\kappa$ the internal variable (denoted generically $A$). The threshold function is then written:

$$
{f}^{\mathrm{èl}}(\delta )=\frac{{\parallel \delta \parallel }_{\mathrm{èq}}-{\kappa}_{i-1}}{{\kappa}_{\mathrm{REF}}}\text{avec}{\kappa}_{i-1}=\underset{v\in [0,{t}_{i-1}]}{\max}{\parallel \delta (v)\parallel }_{\mathrm{èq}};{\parallel \delta \parallel }_{\mathrm{èq}}=\sqrt{{\langle {\delta}_{n}\rangle }_{+}^{2}+{\delta}_{\tau}^{2}}
$$

Without detailing the proof, one can show that ${\parallel \delta \parallel }_{\mathrm{èq}}$ satisfies a triangle inequality. This allows us to prove the convexity of the function:

$$
{f}^{\mathrm{èl}}(\eta )={f}^{\mathrm{èl}}(\delta (\eta ))=\frac{{\parallel {\delta}_{i-1}+\Delta {\delta}_{i}^{n}+d{\delta}^{\mathrm{cte}}+\eta d{\delta}^{\mathrm{pilo}}\parallel }_{\mathrm{èq}}-{\kappa}_{i-1}}{{\kappa}_{\mathrm{REF}}}
$$

We have plotted in a figure a **qualitative** graphical representation of the functions ${f}^{\mathrm{èl}}$ for the various Gauss points of the joint elements, as well as the solutions of the exact global maximisation problem over the set of Gauss points:

$\underset{g}{\max}{f}_{g}^{\mathrm{èl}}(\eta )\stackrel{\mathrm{dèf}}{=}\underset{g}{\max}{f}^{\mathrm{èl}}({\delta}_{g}(\eta ))=\Delta {\tau}_{i}$

It then appears that the solutions $\eta$ of this exact problem are identical to the solutions obtained with the algorithm for solving the piecewise affine equation, that is to say the solutions of equation () $\underset{g}{\max}{A}_{1}^{(g)}\eta +{A}_{0}^{(g)}=\Delta {\tau}_{i}$, with in our example:

${A}_{1}^{(g)}=\frac{\partial {f}_{g}^{\mathrm{èl}}}{\partial \eta }({\eta}_{g}^{\text{*}});{A}_{0}^{(g)}=\Delta \tau -{A}_{1}^{(g)}{\eta}_{g}^{\text{*}}$ where ${\eta}_{g}^{\text{*}}$ is a solution of the local equation ${f}_{g}^{\mathrm{èl}}(\eta )=\Delta {\tau}_{i}$

We once again refer the reader to [3] for a rigorous proof.

#### 4.4.4 Choice of solution

In the case where the global problem has two solutions, the choice algorithms described therein are reused. In general, selection by the residual is retained because of its robustness. However, choosing the solution which minimises the residual does not guarantee us the convergence of the Newton method. Certain cases have been exhibited for which the solution of maximum initial residual converges whereas this is not the case for the solution of minimum initial residual.

As a general rule, for piloted analyses the time step no longer fulfils the role of loading, but merely a parameter for following the solution. Although the mechanical solutions are identical, one can thus obtain a significant difference at the same piloting step for problems with large instabilities, by changing for example the execution machine.
