# r5.01.01 Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem (GEP)

**Summary:**

Whether it is to study the vibrations of a structure or to look for its buckling modes, the engineer often has to solve a modal problem: either generalised (GEP) or quadratic (QEP) [Modal solvers and solution of the generalised problem (GEP)](doc:r5.01.02). To do this, several methods are proposed via the operator [CALC_MODES](doc:u4.52.02): inverse powers and Rayleigh coefficient, Lanczos, IRA, Bathe & Wilson and QZ. Each has its field of use, its advantages, its disadvantages and its development history.

In order to treat large modal problems efficiently (in mesh size and/or in number of modes sought), one advises the use of the macro-command [CALC_MODES](doc:u4.52.02) with the option `BANDE` split into several sub-bands. It decomposes the modal analysis of a standard GEP (symmetric and real) into a succession of independent sub-analyses, less costly, more robust and more accurate. Even in sequential mode, the gains can be appreciable: factors of 2 to 5 in time, 2 or 3 in peak RAM and 10 to 104 on the mean error of the modes.

Moreover, its multi-level parallelism can provide additional gains of the order of 20 in time and 2 in peak RAM, by reserving about sixty processors.

In the first part of the document, we summarise the general problem of solving a modal problem, the various classes of methods and their variants in the public-domain libraries. All things that one must bear in mind before tackling, in the second part, the general architecture of a modal analysis. We then detail the numerical, software and functional aspects of each of the approaches available in the code.

A specific chapter details the implementation of parallelism and of high-performance computing in the framework of modal analyses of standard GEP type.

## 1 Introduction

A majority of studies concerning the dynamic behaviour of structures is carried out by performing a transient analysis on a modal basis. In order to compute these vibration modes, numerous algorithms have been developed over some sixty years. In order to cope with the continual increase in the size of the problems and with the degradation of the conditioning of the discretised operators, only the most efficient and the most robust in practice have been incorporated into the modal analysis operator [CALC_MODES](doc:u4.52.02).

The optimal fields of use of the search criterion of this operator can be separated. When it is a matter of determining a few eigenvalues (typically half a dozen) or of refining a few estimates, the criterion `PROCHE`, `SEPARE` or `AJUSTE` is entirely appropriate. It groups heuristic algorithms and those of power type (cf. §4).

On the other hand, in order to capture a significant part of the spectrum, one resorts to the criterion `BANDE`, `CENTRE`, `PLUS_PETITE` or `PLUS_GRANDE`. The latter brings together the so-called “subspace” methods (Lanczos cf. §5/§6, IRAM §7, Bathe & Wilson §8) which project the working operator in order to obtain an approximate modal problem of reduced size (then treated by a global method of QR or Jacobi type).

This operator also makes it possible to compute, in a robust manner, the whole spectrum of the problem thanks to the criterion `TOUT`. To do this, one uses a reference global method (QZ method cf. §9) which computes all the modes exhaustively. In view of its cost, it is however to be reserved for certain uses: a problem of small size (fewer than ${10}^{4}$ degrees of freedom) or an algorithm benchmark.

The various search criteria can moreover complement one another, because the methods implemented with `PROCHE`, `SEPARE` or `AJUSTE` are very efficient for optimising eigenmodes that have already almost converged. In one or two iterations, they can thus improve the eigenvectors computed a first time *via* `BANDE`, `CENTRE`, `PLUS_PETITE`, `PLUS_GRANDE` or `TOUT`. The projection onto the modal basis will only be the better for it!

## 2 Generalities on modal solvers in structural mechanics

### 2.1 Modal problems

Whether it is to study the **vibrations of a structure, possibly damped and/or rotating**, or to look for its **buckling modes**, the engineer often has to solve a modal problem. To do this, *Code_Aster* proposes an operator 1
which treats two types of modal problems: the **generalised** ones (GEP for ‘Generalized Eigenvalue Problem’) and the **quadratic** ones (QEP for ‘Quadratic Eigenvalue Problem’):

$\begin{array}{c}\text{Trouver}(\lambda ,\mathrm{u})\text{tel}\text{que}\\ (\mathrm{A}-\lambda \mathrm{B})\mathrm{u}=0(\text{GEP})\\ (\mathrm{A}+\lambda \mathrm{B}+{\lambda}^{2}\mathrm{C})\mathrm{u}=0(\text{QEP})\end{array}$                             (2.1-1)

In order to solve these two classes of problems, one often transforms them into a **standard modal problem** (SEP for ‘Standard Eigenvalue Problem’). This type of approach has the merit of being generic and of then relying on classical modal solvers:

$\begin{array}{c}\text{Trouver}(\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{\mathrm{u}})\text{tel}\text{que}\\ ({\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}-\tilde{\lambda}\mathrm{Id})\tilde{\mathrm{u}}=0(\text{SEP})\end{array}$                             (2.1-2)

A **spectral transformation**, for example of ‘shift and invert’ type, makes it possible to transform a GEP into an SEP

$\underset{{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}\mathrm{B}}}\underset{\tilde{\mathrm{u}}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\mathrm{u}}}-\underset{\tilde{\lambda}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{1}{\lambda -\sigma }}}\underset{\tilde{\mathrm{u}}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\mathrm{u}}}=0$                         (2.1-3)

The complex number $\sigma$ is a spectral shift (often called ‘**shift**’) which directs the search zone in the spectrum. This value can be parameterised in *Code_Aster*, for example, by the keyword CENTRE/FREQ. In the same vein, one can transform a QEP into a GEP *via* a **linearisation technique** of the type

$\left[\underset{\tilde{\mathrm{A}}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\begin{array}{cc}-\mathrm{A}& 0\\ 0& \mathrm{N}\end{array}\right]}}-\underset{\tilde{\lambda}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\lambda}}\underset{\tilde{\mathrm{B}}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{B}& \mathrm{C}\\ \mathrm{N}& 0\end{array}\right]}}\right]\underset{\tilde{\mathrm{u}}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{u}\\ \lambda \mathrm{u}\end{array}\right]}}=0$ with $\mathrm{N}=\alpha \mathrm{Id}(\text{par}\text{ex}.\alpha :=\frac{(\mathrm{\parallel }\mathrm{A}\mathrm{\parallel }+\mathrm{\parallel }\mathrm{B}\mathrm{\parallel }+\mathrm{\parallel }\mathrm{C}\mathrm{\parallel })}{3})$ (2.1-4)

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Shape1.gif)

*Figure 2.1-1. Modal deformed shapes of structures loaded dynamically (cooling tower, nuclear reactor vessel) and modal stresses on an alternator.*

In structural mechanics, depending on the problem, the matrices $A$, $B$ and $C$ mentioned above are linear combinations of the various mechanical matrices: mass, stiffness, geometric stiffness, viscous damping or damping induced by the structure, gyroscopic effects. They are often real (except in the presence of hysteretic damping), but not always symmetric (for example, because of gyroscopic effects) and rarely positive definite (because of the Lagranges in particular). This departure from the norm of the usual modal problems (symmetric positive definite SEPs) and this variability of the problems of course complicate their algorithmic treatment.

A **major numerical difficulty between the QEPs** and the two other classes of problems (GEP and SEP) concerns the sorting of the solutions computed. For a problem of size $n$, a QEP admits $\mathrm{2n}$ eigenmodes (with finite or infinite eigenvalue) which may be unpaired (real, pure imaginary, arbitrary complex) or in pairs (, $(\lambda ,\stackrel{ˉ}{\lambda})$  $(\lambda ,-\stackrel{ˉ}{\lambda})$ …). One must then define robust heuristics in order to filter the eigenvalues desired by the user 2
.

Another **complication inherent in the QEP is of an algorithmic nature**. There is no Schur decomposition (resp. generalised Schur) as for the SEPs (resp. GEPs) on which the solution algorithm can rely. For example, for the SEP (2.1-2), this decomposition assures us of the existence of a unitary matrix (hence well conditioned and easily invertible) $U$, making it possible to rewrite the working matrix ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}$ in a form that is easier to handle 3
: the upper triangular matrix $T$.

$\mathrm{U}{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}{\mathrm{U}}^{\text{*}}=\mathrm{T}$                                         (2.1-5)

### 2.2 Solution methods

Modal solvers can be grouped into (at least) **four families**. They make it possible to solve SEPs, sometimes GEPs and rarely QEPs directly. In order to treat these last two types of problem, one has already mentioned that one often had to pre-process the initial mechanical problem (spectral transformation, linearisation technique) in order to build a working problem “SEP-compatible” with the modal solvers.

- **Algorithms of** **QR** type (cf. §9 and appendix 1) which were presented by H.Rutishauser (1958) and formalised concurrently by J.C.Francis and V.N.Kublanovskaya (1961). QR is a fundamental algorithm often involved in the other methods. One finds it in CALC_MODES with OPTION='TOUT' (directly with the QZ method cf. §9 and indirectly in Lanczos and IRAM cf. §6,7).

Field of use: **Computation of the whole spectrum**.

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Shape2.gif)

*Figure 2.2-1. Functional diagram of methods of* QR *type: appearance of the eigenvalues sought on the diagonal.*

*Advantages:* Good convergence, robustness, direct computation of the Schur form, also adapted to the GEP.

*Disadvantages:* Prohibitive memory and computational complexities (to be reserved for problems of small size, fewer than ${10}^{3}$ degrees of freedom), sensitivities to differences in magnitude of the terms of the matrices.

*Variants:* With implicit or explicit shift, single or double ...

- The **subspace methods** which consist in projecting the working operator onto a space $\mathrm{H}$ such that the spectrum of the projected operator is a good approximation of the part of the initial spectrum that one is looking for. These algorithms are the hard core of the operator CALC_MODES with OPTION among ('BANDE', 'CENTRE', 'PLUS_PETITE', 'PLUS_GRANDE') (cf. §5/6/7/8).

Field of use: **Computation of a part of the spectrum**.

*Advantages:* Reduction of the size of the problem and of the memory and computational complexities, requires only the computation of a matrix-vector product and not the knowledge of the whole matrix.

*Disadvantages:* Uses numerous pre- and post-processings *,* convergence can become problematic, captures multiplicities and clusters more or less easily depending on the variants.

*Variants:* Subspace iterations, Bathe and Wilson(1971), Lanczos(1950), Arnoldi 1951), Davidson (1975), Sorensen(1992), Jacobi-Davidson(1996)...

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Shape3.gif)

**Figure 2.2-2. Functional diagram of subspace-type methods. Computation of** $p$ **modes by projecting the working matrix of size** $n$ **onto a space of size** $m$ **(** $p<m<n$ **).**

- **Algorithms of power type** which were historically developed first in order to solve generic modal problems. They are basic algorithms of which the others are an improvement. They are involved in the operator CALC_MODES with OPTION among ('PROCHE', 'SEPARE', 'AJUSTE') (cf. §4).

*Field of use:* **Computation of the extreme values of the spectrum.**

Advantages: Simplicity, very good estimate of the eigenvector in a few iterations.

*Disadvantages:* Convergence can become problematic, poor capture of multiplicities, of clusters...

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/1000000000000249000000AA34852D0EAD1A2005.png)

*Variants:* Inverse powers, (bi) iteration of the Rayleigh quotient...

**Figure 2.2-3. Functional diagram of power-type methods from the decomposition** $A=UD{U}^{\text{*}}$ **of the diagonalisable operator** $A$ **(** $U$ **unitary matrix and** $D$ **diagonal matrix of the eigenvalues).**

- The **other approaches** are more or less empirical and specialised. They are often linked to other problems: search for roots of polynomials, of arbitrary functions... One may thus cite the bisection method used in pre-processings in CALC_MODES with OPTION among ('SEPARE', 'AJUSTE') (cf. §4), but also that of Müller-Traub (in QEP), of Jacobi, of Laguerre etc. They sometimes make it possible to treat GEPs and QEPs directly.

Note:

- Numerous parallels can be drawn between these families (the QR method is thus only a subspace iteration method applied to the whole space), but they also lead to processes analogous to those developed for other problems. Thus in optimisation: the Rayleigh quotient method is to the inverse power method what Newton's method is to a classical descent method. For the solution of linear systems: the conjugate gradient method is a subspace method for symmetric positive definite systems. For the search for roots of polynomials: the power method is a Bernoulli method applied to the “companion” matrix of the associated polynomial.

### 2.3 Linear algebra libraries

In order to carry out efficiently the solution of a modal problem, **the question of resorting to a library or to an external product is now unavoidable**. Why? Because this strategy allows:

- Developments that are less technical, less invasive and much faster in the host code.
- Acquiring, at lower cost, a wide field of use while externalising a good number of the associated contingencies (typology of the problem, representation of the data, architecture of the target machine…).
- Benefiting from the feedback of a varied community of users and from the (very) sharp skills of international teams.

**These libraries often combine efficiency, reliability, performance and portability**:

- Efficiency because they exploit the spatial and temporal locality of the data and play on the memory hierarchy (example of the various categories of BLAS).
- Reliability because they sometimes propose tools for estimating the error committed on the solution (estimation of the conditioning and of the ‘backward/forward errors’) or even for improving it (e.g. matrix balancing).

Since **the emergence in the 1970s/1980s of the first public libraries**  4
**and vendor libraries**  5
and of their communities of users, the offer has multiplied. The trend being of course to propose high-performance solutions (vector, parallelism with centralised then distributed memory, multi-level parallelism *via* threads) as well as “toolkits” for handling linear algebra algorithms and the associated data structures. Let us cite in a non-exhaustive manner: ScaLAPACK(Dongarra & Demmel 1997), SparseKIT(Saad 1988), PETSc(Argonne 1991), HyPre(LL 2000), TRILINOS(Sandia 2000)…

## 3 Context

### 3.1 Problem statement

We consider the generalised modal problem (GEP)

Find $(\lambda ,U)$ such that $Au=\lambda Bu,u\ne 0$                         (3.1-1)

where $A$ and $B$ are matrices with real or complex coefficients, symmetric or not (in structure and/or in values). This type of problem corresponds, in mechanics, in particular to:

- **The study of the free vibrations of a structure** which is undamped and non-rotating. For this structure, one looks for the smallest eigenvalues or else those which lie in a given interval in order to know whether an exciting force can create a resonance. In this standard case, the matrix $A$ is the stiffness matrix, denoted $\mathrm{K}$, real and symmetric (possibly augmented by the geometric stiffness matrix denoted ${K}_{g}$, if the structure is prestressed) and $B$ is the mass or inertia matrix denoted $M$ (real symmetric). The eigenvalues obtained are the squares of the circular frequencies associated with the frequencies sought.

The system to be solved can be written: $(K+{K}_{g})u={\omega}^{2}Mu$ where $\omega =2\pi f$ is the circular frequency, $f$ the eigenfrequency and $u$ the associated eigen displacement vector. The eigenmodes $(\omega ,u)$ are real.

**In the presence of hysteretic damping**, $\mathrm{K}$ becomes complex symmetric. Then the eigenmodes are potentially complex and unpaired.

On the other hand, if $K$ and/or $M$ remain real but possibly non-symmetric
, the eigenmodes are either real, or in pairs $(\lambda ,\stackrel{ˉ}{\lambda})$.

This type of problem is activated by the keyword TYPE_RESU=‘DYNAMIQUE’.

- **The search for a linear buckling mode**. In the framework of linearised theory, assuming *a priori* that the stability phenomena are adequately described by the system of equations obtained by assuming the linear dependence of the displacement with respect to the critical load level, the search for the buckling mode $u$ associated with this critical load level $\lambda$ reduces to a generalised eigenvalue problem of the form: $(\mathrm{K}+\lambda {\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{g}})\mathrm{u}=0$ with $K$ the stiffness matrix and ${K}_{g}$ the geometric stiffness matrix. In order to fit into the “mould” of a standard GEP computation, the code computes, in a first stage, the real eigenmodes $(-\lambda ,u)$. Then it converts them to the format of a buckling analysis: $(\lambda ,\mathrm{u})$.

This type of problem is activated by the keyword TYPE_RESU=‘MODE_FLAMB’. It is to be reserved for real symmetric GEPs (otherwise the code detects it and produces a fatal error).

Notes:

- CALC_MODES makes it possible to automate the choice of the algorithm as a function of the search criterion, and to reduce the costs (CPU and memory) of a search for a significant part of the spectrum (only for a GEP with real modes) when one looks for the modes on a global band split into sub-bands.
- The user can specify the class of membership of the analysis by initialising the keyword TYPE_RESU to 'DYNAMIQUE' (default value) or to 'MODE_FLAMB'. The display of the results will then be formatted taking this specificity into account. In the first case one will speak of frequency (FREQ) whereas in the second, one will speak of critical load (CHAR_CRIT).
- In the presence of damping and of gyroscopic effects, **the study of the dynamic stability** of a structure leads to the solution of a modal problem of higher order, called quadratic (QEP): $(K+i\omega C-{\omega}^{2}M)u=0$. It is solved by the two modal operators and is the subject of a specific note[Boi09].

Now that the links between structural mechanics and the solution of generalised modal problems have been recalled, we shall take an interest in the treatment of the boundary conditions in the code and in their effects on the mass and stiffness matrices.

### 3.2 Taking boundary conditions into account

There are two ways, when building the stiffness and mass matrices, of taking the boundary conditions into account (this description in terms of a dynamic problem extrapolates easily to buckling):

- **Double dualisation,** using Lagrange degrees of freedom[Pel01], makes it possible to enforce

$Cu=0$ (CLL for Linear Boundary Condition),

with $C$ a real matrix of size $p\times n$ ($K$ and $M$ are of order $n$). It entails the handling of larger matrices (called “dualised”) because they incorporate these new unknowns. The dualised stiffness and mass matrices then have the form

$\tilde{\mathrm{K}}=(\begin{array}{ccc}\mathrm{K}& \beta {\mathrm{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}& \beta {\mathrm{C}}^{\mathrm{T}}\\ \beta \mathrm{C}& -\alpha \mathrm{Id}& \alpha \mathrm{Id}\\ \beta \mathrm{C}& \alpha \mathrm{Id}& -\alpha \mathrm{Id}\end{array})$ $\tilde{M}=(\begin{array}{ccc}M& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array})$

with $\alpha$ and $\beta$ strictly positive reals (which serve to balance the terms of the matrix). The dimension of the problem has been increased by $\mathrm{2p}$, because to the $n$ so-called "physical" degrees of freedom, one has added Lagranges. There are two Lagranges per linear relation assigned to the $p$ boundary conditions.

- **The setting to zero of** $p$ **rows and columns of the stiffness and mass matrices**. This is valid only for blockings of degrees of freedom (simple Dirichlet, no proportionality relation between dofs). One cannot take a linear relation into account and one will speak of kinematic blocking (CLB for Blocking Boundary Condition). The stiffness and mass matrices become:

$\tilde{K}=(\begin{array}{cc}\stackrel{ˉ}{K}& 0\\ 0& \mathrm{Id}\end{array})$ $\tilde{M}=(\begin{array}{cc}\stackrel{ˉ}{M}& 0\\ 0& 0\end{array})$

The dimension of the problem remains unchanged but one must however remove the participations of the blocked dofs from the components of the initial matrices ($\stackrel{ˉ}{K}$ is obtained from $K$ by eliminating the rows and the columns of the dofs which are blocked; likewise for $\stackrel{ˉ}{M}$).

When one imposes boundary conditions, the number of eigenvalues (with all their multiplicities) actually involved in the physics of the phenomenon is therefore lower than the size $n$ of the transformed problem:

- ${n}_{\mathit{ddl}-\mathit{actifs}}=n-\frac{3p'}{2}\text{avec}p'=2p$ (double dualisation),
- ${n}_{\mathrm{ddl}-\mathrm{actifs}}=n-p$ (kinematic blocking).

On the other hand, in the modal analysis algorithms, one must **make sure that the solutions belong to the admissible space**. One brings oneself back to it *via* auxiliary treatments. Thus when one uses kinematic blockings (CLB), one must, in the various algorithms and at each iteration, use a “positioning vector” ${\mathrm{u}}_{\mathit{bloq}}$, defined by

- if the $i$ th degree of freedom is not blocked ${\mathrm{u}}_{\mathit{bloq}}(i)=1$,
- otherwise ${\mathrm{u}}_{\mathit{bloq}}(i)=0$,

and pre-multiply each vector handled

${\mathrm{u}}^{1}(i)={\mathrm{u}}^{0}(i)\cdot {\mathrm{u}}_{\mathit{bloq}}(i)(i=\mathrm{1...n})\mathrm{\Rightarrow }{\mathrm{u}}^{1}$

This trick introduces the constraint of the blockings into the whole algorithmics and implicitly directs the search for the solution into the admissible space.

Likewise, if one uses the double dualisation method, one needs a positioning vector of the Lagrange degrees of freedom ${\mathrm{u}}_{\mathit{lagr}}$ defined like ${\mathrm{u}}_{\mathit{bloq}}$. It is used only when choosing the random initial vector. In order that this vector ${u}^{0}$ satisfy the boundary conditions (CLL) one proceeds as follows:

$\mid \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{u}}^{1}(i)={\mathrm{u}}^{0}(i)\cdot {\mathrm{u}}_{\mathit{lagr}}(i)(i=\mathrm{1...n})\\ \tilde{\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{u}}^{2}={\mathrm{u}}^{1}\end{array}\mathrm{\Rightarrow }{\mathrm{u}}^{1}$

On the other hand, one often includes the additional constraint that this **initial vector belong to the range of the working operator**. This makes it possible to enrich the modal analysis more rapidly by not limiting oneself to the kernel. Thus, in the case of Lanczos and of IRAM, one will take as initial vector, not the previous ${u}^{2}$, but ${u}^{3}$ such that

${\mathrm{u}}^{3}={(\mathrm{K}-\sigma \mathrm{M})}^{\text{-1}}\mathrm{M}{\mathrm{u}}^{2}$

In what follows, in order to simplify the notations, we shall not make the *distinction* between the initial matrices and their dualised counterparts (denoted with a tilde) except when necessary. Very often, they will be denoted by $A$ and $B$ in order to stay close to the usual modal notation without attaching oneself to such or such a class of problems.

Note:

- All the computation options work whatever the type of taking into account of the boundary conditions: AFFE_CHAR_MECA or AFFE_CHAR_CINE. However, these must not be redundant: for example, an AFFE_CHAR_MECA + AFFE_CHAR_CINE on the same unknown.

### 3.3 Properties of the matrices

In the (most common) case where the matrices considered are **symmetric and with real coefficients**, one lists the cases described in the table below. The matrices may be positive definite (denoted $>0$), positive semi-definite ($\ge 0$), indefinite ($\le 0\text{ou}\ge 0$) or even singular ($S$).

|  | **Free structure** | **Lagranges** | **Buckling** | **Fluid-structure** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $A(K)$ | $\ge 0$ and $S$ | $<0$ or $>0$ | $>0$ | $\ge 0$ and S |
| $B$ (resp. $M$ or ${K}_{g}$) | $>0$ | $\ge 0$ and $S$ | $\le 0\text{ou}\ge 0$ | $>0$ |

**Table 3.3-1. Properties of the matrices of the GEP.**

The columns of this table being mutually exclusive, in practice, a buckling problem using double Lagranges to model some of its boundary conditions sees its dualised matrices ($\tilde{K}\text{et}\tilde{{K}_{g}}$) potentially become indefinite.

**This range of properties must be taken into account when choosing the pair “(working operator, inner product)”.** This framework can thus reinforce, with efficiency and transparency, the robustness and the field of use of the modal analysis algorithm in all the cases encountered by *Code_Aster*.

In the case where the **matrices are complex symmetric or real non-symmetric**, one can no longer define a matrix inner product. Only the QZ (§9) and IRAM(§7) methods are then available. The first does not need this type of mechanism and one “bluffs” the second by supplying it with a “fake” matrix inner product, in fact the usual Euclidean inner product (cf. §7.5). This last trick is licit with IRAM, because as a variant of Arnoldi, it can work with a non-symmetric pair (working operator, inner product).

The following paragraphs will allow us to measure the effect of these properties on the spectrum of the generalised problem.

### 3.4 Properties of the eigenmodes

Let us first recall that if the matrix of the SEP $\mathrm{A}\mathrm{u}=\lambda \mathrm{u}$ is real symmetric, then its eigen elements are real; The eigen elements of a matrix are its eigenvalues and its eigenvectors. On the other hand, $A$ being normal, its eigenvectors are orthogonal.

In the case of the GEP $\mathrm{A}\mathrm{u}=\lambda \mathrm{B}\mathrm{u}$, this condition is not sufficient. Thus, let us consider the following generalised problem:

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}1& 1\\ 1& 0\end{array}\right](\begin{array}{}{u}_{1}\\ {u}_{2}\end{array})=\lambda \left[\begin{array}{cc}1& 0\\ 0& -1\end{array}\right](\begin{array}{}{u}_{1}\\ {u}_{2}\end{array})$

its eigenmodes are

. ${\lambda}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}(1\pm i\sqrt{(3)})\text{et}{u}_{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+{\lambda}_{\pm}^{2}}}(\begin{array}{}-{\lambda}_{\pm}\\ 1\end{array})$

If one adds the hypothesis "one of the matrices $A$ or $B$ is **positive definite**", then the generalised problem has **real solutions**. One even has the following more precise characterisation (sufficient condition).

**Theorem1**

Let $A$ and $B$ be two real symmetric matrices. If there exist $\alpha \in \mathrm{ℝ}$ and $\beta \in ℝ$ such that $\alpha A+\beta B$ is positive definite, then the generalised problem has real eigen elements.

*Proof:*

This result is obtained immediately by multiplying the generalised problem by $\alpha$ and by performing a spectral shift $\beta$. One then obtains the problem $(\alpha A+\beta B)u=(\lambda \alpha +\beta )Bu$. Since $\alpha A+\beta B$ is positive definite, it admits a unique Cholesky decomposition in the form $C{C}^{T}$ with $C$ a regular matrix.

The problem is then written ${C}^{\text{-1}}B{C}^{-T}z=\mu z$ with $z={C}^{T}u$ and $\mu =\frac{1}{\alpha \lambda +\beta }$, which allows one to conclude, because the matrix ${C}^{\text{-1}}B{C}^{-T}$ is symmetric.

Notes

- This characterisation is not necessary; thus the generalised problem associated with the matrices $A=\mathrm{diag}(1,-2,-1)$ and $B=\mathrm{diag}(-2,1,1)$ admits a real spectrum while not satisfying the positive-definiteness condition.
- In the case of complex Hermitian matrices, this theorem remains valid. On the other hand, in the non-Hermitian complex case (this is the case when taking hysteretic damping into account in Code_Aster), the eigenmodes may be complex: eigenvectors with complex components and eigenvalues $\lambda$ real or arbitrary complex.
- In the real non-symmetric case, the eigenmodes may be complex: eigenvectors with complex components and eigenvalues $\lambda$ real or complex in pairs $(\lambda ,\stackrel{ˉ}{\lambda})$.

**Proposition 2**

If the matrices $A$ and $B$ are real and symmetric, the eigenvectors of the generalised problem are $A$ and $B$-orthogonal, which means that they satisfy the relations

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}{u}_{i}^{T}B{u}_{j}={\delta}_{ij}{a}_{j}\\ {u}_{i}^{T}A{u}_{j}={\lambda}_{j}{\delta}_{ij}{a}_{j}\end{array}$

where ${a}_{j}$ is a scalar depending on the norm of the $j$ th eigenvector, ${\delta}_{ij}$ is the Kronecker symbol and ${u}_{j}$ is the eigenvector associated with the eigenvalue ${\lambda}_{j}$.

*Proof:*

Immediate for distinct eigenvalues, by writing the $\mathrm{A}$ and $B$-inner products between two pairs $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$, then by using the symmetry of the matrices (cf. [Imb91]).

Notes:

- One shows that the $A$ and $B$-orthogonalities of the eigenvectors are a consequence of the Hermitian character of the matrices. They are clearly a generalisation of the properties of the Hermitian standard problem (or even normal): in the case of a matrix with complex Hermitian coefficients, the inner product to be considered is a Hermitian product.
- Orthogonality with respect to the matrices does not at all mean that the eigenvectors are orthogonal for the classical Euclidean norm. The latter can only be the fruit of particular symmetries (cf. tutorial no. 1 [BQ00]).
- This property simplifies the computations of modal recombinations (operator DYNA_VIBRA [U4.53.03](doc:u4.53.03)), when one handles generalised stiffness and mass matrices which are diagonal. The quantities j ${k}_{j}={\lambda}_{j}{a}_{j}$ and ${m}_{j}={a}_{j}$ are called, respectively, modal stiffness and modal mass of the $j$ th mode.
- For non-Hermitian matrices, theorem 1 is no longer satisfied.

Knowing that the modes are often real, we shall now concern ourselves with their estimation.

### 3.5 Estimation of the real spectrum

Document R5.01.04 is dedicated to this problem, which is transverse to the modal operators: INFO_MODE and CALC_MODES.

Let us just recall that in the most common case of real modes (real symmetric GEP), the problem of counting eigenvalues is solved with the help of the famous Sturm test (cf. §2.2/3.2). The situation is much less favourable when the spectrum lies in the complex plane (complex or non-symmetric GEP and QEP). In this case, only the operator INFO_MODE has an adapted method: the APM method (cf. §2.3/3.3). But because of its enormous computational costs and of its novel character, it is advised to reserve it, for the moment, for simplified problems of small size (<104 degrees of freedom).

Now that we are in a position to count the spectrum of the GEP, it remains to build it! The generic algorithms being intended for SEPs, one must transform our initial problem.

### 3.6 Spectral transformation

These techniques make it possible to meet a triple objective:

- identify an SEP,
- Direct the search of the spectrum,
- Separate the eigenvalues.

The spectral computation algorithms converging all the better as the (working) spectrum that they treat is separated, these techniques can be considered as preconditionings of the initial problem. They make it possible to make the separation of certain modes much more important than that of other modes, and thus to improve their convergence.

The most widespread of these transformations is the so-called **"shift and invert"** technique which consists in working with the operator ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}$ such that:

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/1000000000000133000000D26C3F3E2CD4E2FACC.png)

> $\text{Au}=\lambda \text{Bu}\mathrm{\Rightarrow }\underset{{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{(\text{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}\mathrm{B}}}\mathrm{u}=\underset{\mu}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{1}{\lambda -\sigma }}}\mathrm{u}$

**Figure 3.6-1. Effect of the "shift and invert" on the separation of the eigenvalues.**

Figure 3.6-1 shows that this separation and this orientation of the working spectrum in $\mu$ are due to the particular properties of the hyperbolic function. On the other hand, one observes that only the eigenvalues are affected by the transformation. At the end of the modal process it is therefore sufficient to return to the $\lambda$ plane by a suitable change of variable.

Notes:

- The variable $\sigma$ is usually designated by the term “shift” or spectral shift.
- The working matrix ${A}_{\sigma}$ must of course be invertible; this can moreover become one of the motivations of this shift (cf. §6.5).

For the record, let us note that with a complex shift several scenarios are conceivable:

- Work entirely in complex arithmetic,
- In real arithmetic, by isolating the real and imaginary contributions of ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}$, for example via the working operators

${A}_{\sigma}^{+}=\text{Re}({A}_{\sigma})\mathrm{\Rightarrow }{\mu}^{+}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{1}{\lambda -\sigma }+\frac{1}{\lambda -\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}})$ ,

${A}_{\sigma}^{-}=\text{Im}({A}_{\sigma})\mathrm{\Rightarrow }{\mu}^{-}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{2i}}(\frac{1}{\lambda -\sigma }-\frac{1}{\lambda -\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}})$ .

Each of these approaches has its advantages and its disadvantages. For the QEPs of *Code_Aster* [Boi09], it is the first approach which has been retained for Sorensen (METHODE=’SORENSEN’+APPROCHE=’COMPLEXE’). The second is reserved for the other approaches: METHODE=’SORENSEN’ or ‘TRI_DIAG’ + APPROCHE=’REEL’/’IMAGINAIRE’).

Notes

- This choice of the working operator is inseparable from that of the (pseudo)-inner product. It makes it possible to orient oneself towards such or such an algorithm and can thus influence the robustness of the computation.
- Other classes of spectral transformations exist. For example that of Cayley, with a double shift (${\sigma}_{1,}{\sigma}_{2}$), makes it possible to select the eigenvalues situated to the right of a vertical axis

$\underset{{A}_{\sigma}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{(\mathrm{A}-{\sigma}_{1}\mathrm{B})}^{\text{-1}}(\mathrm{A}-{\sigma}_{2}\mathrm{B})}}\mathrm{u}=\underset{\mu}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{\lambda -{\sigma}_{2}}{\lambda -{\sigma}_{1}}}}\mathrm{u}$

The following paragraph will synthesise what precedes in the global flowchart for the solution of a generalised modal problem of *Code_Aster*.

### 3.7 Implementation

The course of a modal analysis in *Code_Aster* can be broken down into four phases. The following paragraphs detail them.

#### 3.7.1 Determination of the shift

**The first operation consists in determining the shift** as well as certain parameters of the problem. This is carried out in a more or less transparent manner depending on the computation option chosen by the user:

- OPTION='SEPARE' or 'AJUSTE' => the shift is determined by the first phase of the algorithm and the number of eigenmodes sought per frequency band (supplied by the Sturm criterion) is bounded by NMAX_FREQ.
- OPTION='PROCHE' => the shift is fixed by the user and the number of eigenmodes is equal to the number of shifts.
- OPTION='PLUS_PETITE' => the shift is zero and the number of modes is parameterised by NMAX_FREQ.
- OPTION='BANDE' => the shift is equal to the middle of the band fixed by the user and the number of modes is determined by the Sturm criterion.
- OPTION='CENTRE' => the shift is fixed by the user and the number of eigenmodes is parameterised by NMAX_FREQ.

Notes:

- This phase does not concern the QZ algorithm. Indeed the latter computes the whole spectrum of the GEP. It is just after the modal analysis that one takes the user's desiderata into account (OPTION=’BANDE’ or ’CENTRE’ or 'PLUS_PETITE' or 'PLUS_GRANDE' or ’TOUT’) in order to select the modes sought.
- For a GEP with complex modes ($\mathrm{K}$ complex symmetric or non-symmetric matrices), only the options ’CENTRE’, 'PLUS_PETITE', 'PLUS_GRANDE', ’TOUT’ are available (with Sorensen and QZ).

#### 3.7.2 Factorisation of dynamic matrices

**In the second phase of pre-processings, one factorises** [Boi08] **“dynamic” matrices of the type** $Q(\sigma ):=A-\sigma B$ **.** That is to say that one decomposes the dynamic matrix $Q(\sigma )$ into a product of particular matrices (triangular, diagonal) which are easier to handle in order to solve linear systems of the type $Q(\sigma )x=y$. In the symmetric case, the decomposition is of the form $Q(\sigma )=LD{L}^{T}$, in the non-symmetric case, one has $Q(\sigma )=LU$ **,** with $U$, $L$ and $D$, respectively, upper triangular, lower triangular and diagonal.

Once this (costly) numerical factorisation has been carried out, the solution of other linear systems comprising the same matrix but with a different right-hand side (problem of multiple right-hand sides type) is very fast.

**This situation is found**

```
*   à chaque fois que l’on met en œuvre le **test de Sturm** . C’est-à-dire pour certains scénarios de la phase 1 de sélection du shift (OPTION='SEPARE’/'AJUSTE' ou 'BANDE') et en prévision de la phase 4 de post-vérification lors du comptage des valeurs propres réelles (GEP à matrices réelles et symétriques uniquement).
```

- when one has to handle a working matrix comprising an inverse. For example in the case of Lanczos or of IRAM (OPTION among ['BANDE','CENTRE','PLUS_*','TOUT'] *+* SOLVEUR_MODAL=_F(METHODE=’TRI_DIAG’/‘SORENSEN’)), one is interested in ${A}_{\sigma}={(A-\sigma B)}^{-1}B$. With the inverse power method (OPTION='SEPARE’/'AJUSTE'/'PROCHE'), it is a matter of ${A}^{\sigma}={(A-\sigma B)}^{-1}$. The two other approaches, Bathe & Wilson or QZ (OPTION among ['BANDE','CENTRE','PLUS_*','TOUT'] *+* SOLVEUR_MODAL=_F(METHODE=’JACOBI’/’QZ’)) are not concerned by this preliminary factorisation of a working matrix.

Notes:

- This factorisation moreover undergoes the same hazards as the Sturm criterion when the shift is close to an eigenvalue. One then proceeds to the same shifts according to algorithm 1 of R5.01.04.
- When this pre-processing phase implements algorithm 5 of R5.01.04 and NMAX_ITER_SHIFT is reached, the computation produces an alarm if it is a factorisation for the Sturm test (risk of numerical instabilities), a fatal error if one seeks to factorise the working matrix (risk of wrong results).
- When one uses the QZ algorithm on non-symmetric or complex GEPs, one does not need to factorise a dynamic matrix. This distinction makes it possible to save a little computational complexity, the QZ algorithm already being quite costly in itself!
- By chaining the operators INFO_MODE [U4.52.01](doc:u4.52.01) and CALC_MODES, one can better orient / calibrate one's spectral computation or even limit the extra cost generated by the initial Sturm test of the option 'BANDE' (cf. keyword TABLE_FREQ/CHAR_CRIT[U4.52.02]).

#### 3.7.3 Modal analysis

**One carries out the modal analysis proper:** one solves the standard SEP problem, then one returns to the initial GEP. During this conversion, one filters and transcribes the results of the previous computation.

Concerning the eigenvalues, one retains all the solutions computed: real, complex alone or in pairs.

In CALC_MODES with OPTION='SEPARE’/'AJUSTE'/'PROCHE', two variants are available: the inverse iteration method ('DIRECT') and its acceleration by Rayleigh quotient ('RAYLEIGH'). They refine the eigenvalues previously detected by the heuristics (‘SEPARE’ or ’AJUSTE’) or the estimates supplied by the user (‘PROCHE‘).

As for CALC_MODES with OPTION='BANDE'/'CENTRE'/'PLUS_*'/'TOUT', it allows the use of four distinct methods: the Bathe & Wilson method ('JACOBI'), the subspace methods of Lanczos type ('TRI_DIAG *'* ) and Sorensen ('SORENSEN') and the global QZ method (‘QZ’).

Concerning the eigenvectors, one forces their $B$-orthonormalisation only for a real symmetric GEP (otherwise one cannot define a $B$-inner product). With the Sorensen method and with QZ, this orthonormalisation is carried out explicitly, at the end of the modal analysis, *via* the IGSM algorithm of Kahan-Parlett (cf. appendix 2). For the other modal solvers, this step is included in their internal computation procedures.

When one carries out an explicit reorthogonalisation, the latter concerns only the eigenmodes supposed to belong to the same eigenspace (*cf.* note below). The theory assures us of the orthogonality of the other cases. This “targeted” orthogonalisation often makes it possible to gain at least 50% of the global computation time of the operator (sometimes up to 90%).

**Notes:**

- *Each of these methods has internal stopping tests. Not to mention that the projection methods employ auxiliary modal methods: Jacobi (cf. Appendix 3) for ‘Bathe & Wilson’ and* $\text{QR}/\text{QL}$ *(cf. Appendix 1) for Lanczos and Arnoldi. They also require stopping tests. The user often has access to these parameters, although it is strongly recommended, at least at first, to keep their default values.*
- *The reorthogonalisation carried out in post-processing of Sorensen and of QZ is parameterisable: a negative value of the coefficient* PARA_ORTHO_SOREN *indicates that it must be total and not targeted (as a result the computation will be much more costly); the value of* SEUIL_FREQ/CHAR_CRIT *makes it possible to increase or reduce the number of modes to be reorthogonalised (two modes are considered as belonging to the same eigenspace if they are lower than this criterion in absolute value and if their gap is too). Normally these parameters (of developer level) must not be modified in a standard study.*

#### 3.7.4 Verification post-processings

This last part groups the **post-processings which verify the correct course of the analysis**. They are of two types:

- **Norm** of **the residual of the initial problem**

$\begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{c}u\mathrm{\Leftarrow }\frac{u}{{\mathrm{\parallel }u\mathrm{\parallel }}_{\infty}}\\ \text{Si}\mid \lambda \mid >\text{SEUIL\_FREQ}\text{alors}\end{array}\\ \frac{{\mathrm{\parallel }\text{Au}-\lambda \text{Bu}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}}{{\mathrm{\parallel }\text{Au}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}}?<\text{SEUIL},\\ \text{Sinon}\\ \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{\parallel }\text{Au}-\lambda \text{Bu}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}?<\text{SEUIL},\\ \text{Fin si}.\end{array}\end{array}$

**Algorithm 1. Test of the norm of the residual.**

This sequence is parameterised by the keywords SEUIL and SEUIL_FREQ, belonging respectively to the factor keywords VERI_MODE and CALC_FREQ. On the other hand, this post-processing is activated by the initialisation to 'OUI' (default value) of STOP_ERREUR in the factor keyword VERI_MODE. When this rule is activated and not respected, the computation stops; otherwise the error is merely reported by an alarm. One can of course only too strongly recommend not to deactivate this override parameter!

- *The Sturm test* can also be activated *with the options '* PROCHE *', '* SEPARE *' and '* AJUSTE *'.* It does not *then fulfil* quite *the same function.* Indeed, *these options serve to refine initial estimates of eigenvalues and to compute the associated eigenvectors: the presence of 'holes' in the spectrum computed is then possible and licit.* On the other hand, *the other options ('* BANDE *' etc...) must compute all the modes specified, without omitting any:* no hole in the spectrum is *then tolerated.*

The user did not ask for it to be refined! With the initial setting* FREQ=(100.0, 200.0, 300.0) *this alarm* would no longer appear *.*

*In this framework, the activation of the Sturm test is therefore to be handled with caution. Functionally, it serves rather to defer the verification tests when one activates the keyword* AMELIORATION *at the end of a subspace method (cf. previous note).*

## 4 Inverse power method (CALC_MODES with OPTION among ['PROCHE','AJUSTE','SEPARE'])

### 4.1 Introduction

This method is accessible in *Code_Aster* only for the case of matrices with real coefficients. One will therefore limit oneself to this case in the remainder of this chapter. In order to compute several eigenvalues of the generalised problem, one **does not use the general method of inverse iterations as it stands**.

One can, for example, combine it with a **deflation technique**  [11]_
so as to filter automatically the spectral information updated in order not to find it again at the next iteration. With the deflation by restriction [12]_
of Wielandt one builds iteratively the working operator (in the symmetric case), denoting by $({\mu}_{k},{\mathrm{u}}_{k})$ the mode to be filtered

${\mathrm{A}}_{k+1}={\mathrm{A}}_{k}-{\mu}_{k}{\mathrm{u}}_{k}{\mathrm{u}}_{k}^{\mathrm{t}}$ .

This strategy, which does not apprehend the multiplicities, must be completed by a Sturm criterion. On the other hand, the fact of working in finite arithmetic and of not actually building the operator ${A}_{k}$ at each iteration constrains one to blend this approach with high-performance orthogonalisation processes.

All these complications have led to choosing another path, which breaks down into two parts:

- The *localisation of the eigenvalues* (determination of an approximate value of each eigenvalue contained in an interval given by a **bisection technique**, refined or not by a **secant method**).
- *The improvement of these estimates and the computation of their associated eigenvectors* by an **inverse iteration method**.

The search for an approximate value for each eigenvalue considered is selected in the factor keyword CALC_FREQ *via* OPTION:

- If OPTION='SEPARE', in each interval of frequencies defined by the keyword FREQ, an approximate value of each eigenvalue contained in this interval is computed by using the dichotomy method (cf. §4.2.1).
- If OPTION='AJUSTE', one first carries out the same operations as previously and then, starting from these approximations, one refines the result by the secant method.

For these two options, one computes at the same time the modal position of each eigenvalue, which makes it possible to detect the multiple modes. Either one retains only the NMAX_FREQ lowest frequencies contained in the maximum interval specified by the user, or one computes all the values of this interval (if NMAX_FREQ=0).

- If OPTION='PROCHE', the frequencies given by the keyword FREQ are considered as the approximate values of the eigenvalues sought.

Notes:

- Of course, as one has already specified, these options are to be used only to determine or refine a few eigenvalues. For a more extended search, one must use the other search options.
- With the option 'PROCHE', one cannot compute multiple modes.
- It is a costly algorithm because it makes much use of the Sturm test and therefore of its associated factorisations.
- The bounds of the search intervals are supplied by FREQ or CHAR_CRIT depending on the initialisation of TYPE_RESU.

We shall now detail the various algorithms (and their parameters) which are put in place in the first part of the process.

### 4.2 Localisation and separation of the eigenvalues

#### 4.2.1 Bisection method

As one has already seen previously, corollary 5bis of Sylvester's law of inertia (cf. § 3.5) makes it possible to determine the number of eigenvalues contained in a given interval by carrying out two decompositions $LD{L}^{T}$. This criterion can therefore lead to refining the interval until it encompasses only one eigenvalue. This driving being put in place, one passes from one iteration to the other by using the **principle of dichotomy**.

On a starting interval $[{\lambda}_{a},{\lambda}_{b}]$, one therefore proceeds as follows, knowing $pm({\lambda}_{a})$ and $pm({\lambda}_{b})$:

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Object_189.svg)

**Algorithm 3. Bisection method.**

One stops the process if one has cut the starting interval more than NMAX_ITER_SEPARE times, or if for a given interval one has ($(\frac{\mid {\lambda}_{a}-{\lambda}_{b}\mid }{{\lambda}^{\text{*}}})\le \text{PREC\_SEPARE}$ in this case one no longer refines the search in this interval). One finally obtains a list of frequencies. In each interval defined by the arguments of this list, there lies an eigenvalue having a certain multiplicity. As an approximation of this eigenvalue, one takes the middle of the interval.

Notes:

- One could have used as a criterion the change of sign of the characteristic polynomial, but besides the fact that it is very costly to evaluate, it does not make it possible, as it stands, to detect the multiplicities,
- The initialisation of the process can be carried out empirically according to the user's needs. In order to encompass a part of the spectrum one can also use the regions of the complex plane of the Gerschgörin-Hadamard theorems (on $\mathrm{A},{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{T}}$ ...). In this respect, the bisection method can prove more efficient than a QR in the presence of a cluster. Its convergence, although linear, is indeed bounded by $1/2$ whereas that of QR can tend towards 1[LT86].

#### 4.2.2 Secant method

The secant method is a **simplification of the Newton-Raphson method**. At an arbitrary step, $k$ knowing a value ${\lambda}_{k}$ and by approximating the non-linear function $p(\lambda )$ by its tangent at this point, one determines the next value ${\lambda}_{k+1}$ as being the intersection of this straight line with the $\lambda$ axis, and so on, according to the iterative scheme

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/1000000000000147000000655E601FBD8342E793.png)

> ${\lambda}_{k+1}={\lambda}_{k}-p({\lambda}_{k})\frac{{\lambda}_{k}-{\lambda}_{k-1}}{p({\lambda}_{k})-p({\lambda}_{k-1})}$

**Figure 4.2-2. Secant method.**

The tangent being approximated by a finite difference so as not to have to compute a derivative of $p(\lambda )$, only the estimation of the polynomial is required. One considers that one has reached convergence when $\frac{\mid {\lambda}_{k+1}-{\lambda}_{k}\mid }{{\lambda}_{k}}<\text{PREC\_AJUSTE}$ and, moreover, one limits oneself to NMAX_ITER_AJUSTE iterations if this criterion is not reached.

Notes:

- This method has an almost quadratic convergence when it is close to the solution; otherwise, it can diverge. Hence the interest of combining the bisection method with this approach. This elegant strategy of combining bisection and secant was initiated by Van Wijngaardeen and Dekker(1960).
- If one does not update the computation of the last point in the secant formula (${\lambda}_{k+1}={\lambda}_{0}$), one recovers the classical method of the “false position” [13]_ . The latter converges less quickly (while requiring one function evaluation per iteration), but it is robust and stable (like the bisection method). One is sure of improving the initial estimate.
- One shows that the secant method achieves the best compromise “speed of convergence/number of function evaluations per iteration”.
- The secant method relies on a linear interpolation between the last two iterates ( ${\lambda}_{k+1},p({\lambda}_{k+1})$ ) and ( ${\lambda}_{k},p({\lambda}_{k})$ ). By generalising this process to a higher order of interpolation, that is to say the last three iterates, one recovers the Müller-Traub method used for the QEPs with CALC_MODES with OPTION among ['PROCHE','AJUSTE','SEPARE'][Boi09].

We shall now detail the inverse power algorithm (coupled with a Rayleigh acceleration) constituting the second part of the process.

### 4.3 Inverse power method

#### 4.3.1 Principle

In order to determine the eigenvalue of the generalised problem $\mathrm{A}\mathrm{u}=\lambda \mathrm{B}\mathrm{u}$ closest in modulus to $\sigma$, one applies the power method to the operator ${(A-\sigma B)}^{-1}B$. In fact, one builds only the **factorised matrix**  [14]_
${\mathrm{A}}^{\sigma}={(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}$ and this amounts to treating the generalised problem ${({\mathrm{A}}^{\sigma})}^{\text{-1}}\mathrm{B}\mathrm{u}=\underset{\mu}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{1}{\lambda -\sigma }}}\mathrm{u}$. The power method converging preferentially towards the eigenvalues of largest modulus (in $\mu$), one will thus capture the $\lambda$ closest to the shift.

The principle is the following: knowing an estimate $\sigma$ of the eigenvalue sought and starting from a normalised initial vector ${\mathrm{y}}_{0},$ one builds a sequence of approximate eigenvectors ${({\mathrm{y}}_{k})}_{k}$ by the recurrence formula

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Object_204.svg)

*Algorithm 4. Inverse power method.*

With ${\lambda}_{1,}{\lambda}_{2}$ ... the first eigenvalues (of eigenvector ${\mathrm{u}}_{i}$) closest in modulus to $\sigma$, one shows that one has a linear convergence of ${\mathrm{y}}_{k}\to {\mathrm{u}}_{1}$ and a quadratic convergence of ${\mu}_{k}\to \frac{1}{\mid {\lambda}_{1}-\sigma \mid }$ and $\frac{\tilde{{\mathrm{y}}_{k}}(i)}{\tilde{{\mathrm{y}}_{k-1}}(i)}\to \frac{1}{\mid {\lambda}_{1}-\sigma \mid }(i=\mathrm{1....n})$ (the convergence factor of these sequences is of the order of $\frac{\mid {\lambda}_{1}-\sigma \mid }{\underset{i\ne 1}{\min}\mid {\lambda}_{i}-\sigma \mid }$).

Notes:

- This result is acquired (up to the convergence factor) only when the dominant eigenvalue (here the one closest in modulus to the shift) is unique. Otherwise, results nevertheless remain possible, even in the complex framework, but the rigorous analysis of the convergence of this algorithm is still incomplete[GL89][Vau00].
- In theory, these results require that the initial vector not be orthogonal to the left eigenspace sought. In practice, rounding errors avoid this problem (cf. [LT86] pp500-509).
- Even if the estimate of the eigenvalue is crude, the algorithm quickly supplies a very good estimate of the eigenvector.
- The major disadvantage of this method is that one must carry out a factorisation for each eigenvalue to be computed.

In general one works in the Euclidean norm or in the infinity norm, but in order to facilitate the post-modal computations one seeks here to $B$-normalise the eigenvectors (when $B$ is indefinite, one works with the associated pseudo-norm). The basic algorithm can be rewritten by setting ${\mathrm{z}}_{0}=\mathrm{B}{\mathrm{y}}_{0}$

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Object_209.svg)

**Algorithm 5. Inverse power method with** $B$ **-pseudo-norm.**

Let us note that $\rho ({y}_{k})\to \frac{1}{\mid {\lambda}_{1}-\sigma \mid }$. This presentation avoids the matrix-vector products by the matrix $B$ when computing the inner products and already foreshadows the Rayleigh coefficient of the following paragraph. One observes that the convergence factor is all the smaller as the spectral shift $\sigma$ is close to the eigenvalue sought and therefore as $A-\sigma B$ is close to **singularity**. This is in fact **not prejudicial** to the process because the error made in solving the system is "mainly" in the direction generated by the eigenvector which is the direction sought. This means that when solving ${\mathrm{A}}^{\sigma}{\mathrm{y}}_{k}={\mathrm{z}}_{k+1}$, one does not find the exact solution ${\mathrm{y}}_{k}$ but that the rounding errors lead to a neighbouring solution of the form ${\tilde{\mathrm{y}}}_{k}={\mathrm{y}}_{k}+\mathrm{w}$. The latter is proportional to the exact solution, but as the normalisation is arbitrary, everything proceeds correctly[Par80].

Note:

- This poor conditioning, far from having an unfavourable effect, even improves the convergence of the algorithm.

This algorithm is therefore used to improve the eigenvector associated with the approximate value of phase 1. In order to refine this estimate of the eigenvalue, one introduces a Rayleigh quotient.

#### 4.3.2 Rayleigh quotient iteration method

Let us recall that the **Rayleigh quotient** applied to the generalised problem is defined by the number $R(x)$, with $x$ a non-zero vector of ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{n}$, such that:

$R(\mathrm{x})=\frac{{\mathrm{x}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{x}}{{\mathrm{x}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{B}\mathrm{x}}$

This quotient has the remarkable property of stationarity in the neighbourhood of any eigenvector and of reaching an **extremum** (local) which is **the corresponding eigenvalue**: for each fixed $x$, $R(x)$ minimises $\lambda \to {\parallel (A-\lambda B)x\parallel }_{2}$.

Which we can translate by "if $x$ is an approximation of an eigenvector of the system $Ax=\lambda Bx$, then $R(x)$ is an approximation of the eigenvalue associated with the vector $x$", and conversely, we have seen that if one had a good estimate of an eigenvalue, the inverse iteration method made it possible to obtain a good estimate of the corresponding eigenvector. Hence the idea of combining these two properties by considering the inverse iteration algorithm with spectral shift for which one re-evaluates, at each iteration, the eigenvalue via the Rayleigh quotient. One then obtains the so-called Rayleigh quotient iteration algorithm (in $B$-pseudo-norm)

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Object_219.svg)

*Algorithm 6. Rayleigh quotient iteration method with* $B$ *-pseudo-norm.*

For the standard modal problem, one can show[Par80] that the convergence of this algorithm is cubic in the case where the working operator is normal [15]_
(*a fortiori* in the Hermitian case) and at best quadratic in the other cases. If one used this method without modifying it, one would have, at each iteration of the improvement process of each eigenvalue, to carry out a factorisation $\mathrm{L}\mathrm{D}{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{T}}$, which would be very costly. Hence the idea of carrying out [16]_
this Rayleigh shift only if one is in a neighbourhood (arbitrary notion to be defined) of the solution.

Note:

- In a more general framework, some authors have introduced an algorithm called “of bi-iterations of the Rayleigh quotient”. Based on the stationarity of the bi-quotient ${R}_{b}(x,y)=\frac{{y}^{T}Ax}{{y}^{T}Bx}$ in the neighbourhood of the right and left eigenvectors, it supplies (in the non-Hermitian case) the two types of eigenvectors. Its cost is however prohibitive because it requires twice as many factorisations (cf. B.N.Parlett 1969 [Par80]).

## 5 Subspace method (CALC_MODES with OPTION among ['BANDE','CENTRE','PLUS_*'])

### 5.1 Introduction

The case of GEPs with complex modes (cf. §3.1) can be treated in *Code_Aster* only by the IRAM and QZ methods. It is therefore dealt with in the chapter relating to these methods. For the sake of readability, one will limit oneself to the case of matrices with real coefficients in order to set out the general principles of the subspace methods.

If one wishes **only to compute the** $p$ **eigen elements of a generalised problem of order** $n$ where $n\ll p$ (for example, the $p$ smallest eigenvalues or all the eigenvalues comprised in a given interval), one has seen that it was preferable to resort to subspace methods. They are based on the Rayleigh-Ritz analysis which consists in projecting efficiently the problem considered onto a subspace of dimension $m(p<m<n)$ and in looking for certain eigen elements of this projected problem (much easier to treat) with the help of robust algorithms (QR or QL for Lanczos and IRAM, Jacobi for the Bathe and Wilson method).

The efficiency criteria of the said projection are:

- The small size of the projection space (directly linked to the computational and memory complexities).
- The ease of its construction.
- The robustness of the orthogonal projection [18]_ .
- The putting into canonical form of the projected matrix.
- The good approximation of the part of the initial spectrum sought by that of the projected operator.
- The minimisation of the computational and memory complexities and that of the rounding effects (these are above all linked to the orthogonality problems raised by the 3rd point).

One will first present the Rayleigh-Ritz analysis before detailing three methods derived from this analysis: the Lanczos method, the one called Sorensen (IRA) and that of Bathe and Wilson.

### 5.2 Rayleigh-Ritz analysis

Let us consider the standard modal problem of order $n$, $Au=\lambda u$, and the subspace $H$ of ${ℝ}^{n}$ generated by an orthonormal basis $({\mathrm{q}}_{1,}{\mathrm{q}}_{2,}\mathrm{...},{\mathrm{q}}_{m})$. The latter constitutes the orthogonal matrix ${\mathrm{Q}}_{m}$ making it possible to define the projection operator ${\mathrm{P}}_{m}={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}{\mathrm{Q}}_{m}^{T}$.

The **Galerkin method** used by this analysis consists in solving the following problem

$\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\text{Trouver}(\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{\mathrm{u}})\in \mathrm{ℝ}\times H\text{tel que}\\ {\mathrm{P}}_{m}(\mathrm{A}\tilde{\mathrm{u}}-\tilde{\lambda}\tilde{\mathrm{u}})=0\end{array}\mathrm{\iff }\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\text{Avec}\tilde{\mathrm{u}}={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}\mathrm{x},\text{trouver}(\tilde{\lambda},\mathrm{x})\in \mathrm{ℝ}\times {\mathrm{ℝ}}^{m}\text{tel}\text{que}\\ \underset{{\mathrm{B}}_{m}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\mathrm{Q}}_{m}^{\text{*}}{\text{AQ}}_{m}}}\mathrm{x}=\tilde{\lambda}\mathrm{x}\end{array}$

The search for the Ritz elements ($\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{u}$) which interest us therefore amounts to finding the eigenmodes of the Rayleigh matrix ${\mathrm{B}}_{m}={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}^{T}\mathrm{A}{\mathrm{Q}}_{m}$.

The eigenvalues remain unchanged in the two formulations; on the other hand, knowing an eigenvector $x$ of ${\mathrm{B}}_{m}$ one must go back up to the whole space via the transformation $\tilde{\mathrm{u}}={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}\mathrm{x}$.

The approach can therefore be summarised in the form:

- Choice of a space $H$ and of a basis $({\mathrm{h}}_{1},{\mathrm{h}}_{2},\mathrm{...},{\mathrm{h}}_{m})$ represented by $H$.
- Orthonormalisation of the basis

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Shape7.gif)

- Computation of the Rayleigh matrix ${\mathrm{B}}_{m}:={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}^{\text{*}}{\text{AQ}}_{m}$
- Computation of the eigen elements of ${\mathrm{B}}_{m}:(\tilde{\lambda},\mathrm{x})$
- Computation of its Ritz elements: $(\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{\mathrm{u}}={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}\mathrm{x})$,
- Error test via the residual: $\mathrm{r}=\mathrm{A}\tilde{\mathrm{u}}-\tilde{\lambda}\tilde{\mathrm{u}}$.

**Algorithm 8. Rayleigh-Ritz procedure.**

Notes:

Lanczos and IRAM), is out of all proportion with that of a good QR ($O({n}^{2})$) or that of a Rayleigh quotient iteration encapsulated in a heuristic process such as the one developed in CALC_MODES with OPTION among ['PROCHE','AJUSTE','SEPARE']($\gg {\mathrm{pn}}^{3}$). where $\beta$ is a number depending on $\lambda$, $A$ and ${\mathrm{P}}_{m}$.

In the Hermitian case the numerator of this upper bound is squared. The associated eigenvector, $\tilde{u}$, for its part satisfies

$\sin(\mathrm{u},\tilde{\mathrm{u}})\le \beta \frac{{\mathrm{\parallel }(\mathrm{I}-{\mathrm{P}}_{m})-\mathrm{u}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}}{{\mathrm{\parallel }{\mathrm{P}}_{m}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}}$ .

*Proof*

Cf. [LT86] pp531-537.

One shows that for $m$ large enough, $\beta$ can be bounded by a number which depends only on $A$ and on $\underset{{\lambda}_{i}\ne {\lambda}_{j}}{\min}\mid {\lambda}_{i}-{\lambda}_{j}\mid$. The estimate of the second member ${\mathrm{\parallel }(\mathrm{I}-{\mathrm{P}}_{m})\mathrm{u}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}$ depends on the choice of the subspace.

Note:

- The number $\underset{{\lambda}_{i}\ne {\lambda}_{j}}{\min}\mid {\lambda}_{i}-{\lambda}_{j}\mid$ (and its variants) is omnipresent in the analyses of errors, of convergence or of spectral conditionings. One sees, once again, the importance of the separation of the working spectrum for the good numerical behaviour of the algorithms.

Of course, in order to take into account the spectral information already obtained, or even to improve it or filter it, one iterates this Rayleigh-Ritz procedure by modifying the projection subspace. One will then chain the construction of this new space (recurrent from the previous one) and this projection process. Two types of spaces (each attached to distinct methods) are the most often used.

### 5.3 Choice of the projection space

Two choices of projection space $H$ are the most often put in place:

- *The first, that of the* *Bathe and Wilson method (cf. §*  8 *METHODE='JACOBI')*, consists in choosing a subspace $H$ of dimension $m$ then in building successively:

$\begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{H}}_{1}=\mathrm{A}\mathrm{H}\\ {\mathrm{H}}_{2}=\mathrm{A}{\mathrm{H}}_{1}={\mathrm{A}}^{2}\mathrm{H}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\mathrm{H}}_{i}=\mathrm{A}{\mathrm{H}}_{i-1}={\mathrm{A}}^{i}\mathrm{H}\end{array}$

This method, which partakes both of the block generalisation of the power method and of the truncation of the QR algorithm, leads to an impoverishment of the working space: $\mathrm{dim}{H}_{i}\le \mathrm{dim}{H}_{i-1}$. In order not always to find the same dominant eigenmode one must insert into the process a reorthogonalisation (with all the problems of computational complexity and of roundings that this implies).

- *The second, that of the* *Lanczos method (cf. §*  6 *METHODE='TRI_DIAG') and of IRAM (cf. §*  7 *METHODE='SORENSEN')* consists in starting from an initial vector $y$, in building the sequence of Krylov spaces (${H}_{i}$) *i=1,m* where ${H}_{i}$ is the space generated by the family of vectors $(\mathrm{y},\text{Ay},...,{\mathrm{A}}^{i-1}\mathrm{y})$. The latter is called the Krylov space of $A$ and of order *i* initiated by $y$:

${H}_{i}={K}_{i}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{y})\mathrm{\equiv }\text{Vect}(\mathrm{y},\text{Ay},...,{\mathrm{A}}^{i-1}\mathrm{y})$ .

They satisfy the following property $\mathrm{dim}{H}_{i}\le \mathrm{dim}{H}_{i+1}$. Unlike the previous choice, one therefore has a certain enrichment of the working space. On the other hand, one will see that they make it possible to project in an optimal manner in the sense of the criteria defined previously.

Historically, the first type of space was very much used in structural mechanics. But in order to reduce the computational complexity linked to the size of the subspaces and to the orthogonalisations, one now prefers the methods of Lanczos/Arnoldi type.

Note:

- One encounters an impressive variety of algorithms using a space of the first type. They are called "subspace iterations", "orthogonal iterations" or else "simultaneous iterations". Beyond the various terms, one must above all remember that these adaptations concern the restart strategies [19]_ , the acceleration techniques [20]_ and the factorisations implemented in order to enrich the working space. There even exist versions based on inverse powers making it possible to compute the p smallest modes (cf. [LT86] pp538-45, [Vau00] pp49-54).

For these projection methods, assuming that the initial space $H$ is not too poor along the $p$ dominant directions, the convergence factor of the $i$ th mode after $k$ iterations is written (when they are arranged classically, that is to say in decreasing order of modulus):

$O({\mid \frac{{\lambda}_{m+1}}{{\lambda}_{i}}\mid }^{k})$

It clearly expresses two phenomena:

- The preferential convergence of the dominant modes.
- The improvement of these convergences (and therefore of their error norms for a fixed number of iterations) when the size of the subspace increases.

In order to transform the generalised modal problem into a standard problem one resorts to spectral transformations. They also make it possible to direct the search of the spectrum and to improve the convergence (cf. § 3.6).

### 5.4 Choice of the spectral shift

In order to compute the smallest eigenvalues neighbouring a given frequency or all the eigenvalues comprised in a given frequency band, one carries out a spectral shift of the generalised problem. Let $\sigma$ be the value of the shift; one transforms the initial problem $Au=\lambda Bu$ into a shifted standard problem.

${\mathrm{A}}^{\sigma}{\mathrm{B}}^{\text{-1}}\mathrm{u}=\mu \mathrm{u}$ with ${\mathrm{A}}^{\sigma}=\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B}$ and $\mu =\lambda -\sigma$

This spectral transformation, called "**simple shift**", is used in the Bathe and Wilson method. As the process detects the smallest eigenvalues in $\mu$, one progressively captures those which are the closest to $\sigma$ (in modulus if the shift is real and in real and imaginary part if it is complex).

By carrying out the inverse transformation ("**shift and invert**") it comes about that

${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}\mathrm{u}=\mu \mathrm{u}$ with ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}={(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{\text{-1}}\mathrm{B}$ and $\mu =\frac{1}{\lambda -\sigma }$

This is the problem treated with Lanczos and IRAM which makes it possible to compute the largest eigenvalues $\mu$ hence the eigenvalues closest to the shift $\sigma$ (in modulus). In the case of the IRAM method for matrices with complex coefficients, the shift is necessarily complex.

In the command CALC_MODES with OPTION among ['BANDE','CENTRE','PLUS_*'], there are four ways of choosing this shift (for buckling, the transposition to critical load levels is immediate):

- $\sigma =0$, one looks for the smallest eigenvalues of the initial problem. This corresponds to OPTION='PLUS_PETITE'.
- $\sigma ={\sigma}_{0}$ with ${\sigma}_{0}={(2\pi {f}_{0})}^{2}$, one looks for the frequencies close to the frequency FREQ= ${f}_{0}$ (OPTION='CENTRE').
- $\sigma =\frac{{\sigma}_{0}+{\sigma}_{1}}{2}$ with ${\sigma}_{0}={(2\pi {f}_{0})}^{2}$ and ${\sigma}_{1}={(2\pi {f}_{1})}^{2}$, one looks for all the frequencies comprised in the band $[{f}_{0,}{f}_{1}]$ (OPTION='BANDE' with FREQ={ ${f}_{0},{f}_{1}$ }).
- $\sigma ={\sigma}_{0}+j{\sigma}_{1}$ with ${\sigma}_{0}={(2\pi {f}_{0})}^{2}$ and ${\sigma}_{1}=\eta \frac{{(2\pi {f}_{0})}^{2}}{2}$ one looks for the frequencies close to the frequency FREQ= ${f}_{0}$ and to the reduced damping AMOR_REDUIT= $\eta$ (OPTION='CENTRE'). This approach is available only in dynamics with a GEP with complex modes: matrices $\mathrm{A}$ and/or $B$ complex symmetric or real non-symmetric (cf. table 1).

The number of frequencies to be computed is given in general by the user with the help of NMAX_FREQ under the factor keyword CALC_FREQ. But for the option 'BANDE', it is determined automatically by using corollary 3bis of R5.01.04.

## 6 Lanczos method (SOLVEUR_MODAL= _F(METHODE=‘TRI_DIAG’))

### 6.1 Introduction

This algorithm, developed by **Lanczos** [Lan50] **in 1950**, was hardly used until the mid-1970s. At first sight very simple and efficient, it is nevertheless the seat **of great numerical instabilities** which can cause the capture of spurious modes! These are linked mainly to **problems of maintaining orthogonality** between the vectors generating the Krylov space. The understanding of this type of behaviour in the face of the finite arithmetic of computers was long in being unearthed. Since then, numerous palliative solutions have seen the light of day and an abundant literature covers the subject. Let us cite for example the work of J.K. Cullum[CW85] which is entirely devoted to it, that of B.N.Parlett[Par80] and the updated and exhaustive survey of J.L. Vaudescal[Vau00] (pp55-78).

In the remainder of this paragraph, we shall first dwell on the theoretical framework of operation of the algorithm. Then, before detailing the variant put in place in *Code_Aster*, we shall apply ourselves to the realistic framework of finite arithmetic.

### 6.2 Theoretical Lanczos algorithm

#### 6.2.1 Principle

Its field of application covers the pairs working operator-(pseudo)inner product ensuring the **Hermitian character** of ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}$. It makes it possible to build iteratively a Rayleigh matrix ${\mathrm{B}}_{m}$ of **adjustable size, tridiagonal and Hermitian** (with a genuine inner product, otherwise it loses this last property). This particular form greatly facilitates the computation of the Ritz modes: with QR one then loses an order of magnitude, when one looks for $p$ eigenmodes by projecting onto a subspace of size $m$, going from $O(\mathrm{pm²})$ to $O(\mathrm{20pm})$. The algorithm consists in progressively building a family of Lanczos vectors ${\mathrm{q}}_{1,}{\mathrm{q}}_{2,}\mathrm{...},{\mathrm{q}}_{m}$ by projecting orthogonally, at iteration $k$, the vector ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}{\mathrm{q}}_{k}$ onto the two previous vectors ${\mathrm{q}}_{k}$ and ${\mathrm{q}}_{k-1}$. The new vector becomes ${\mathrm{q}}_{k+1}$ and thus, step by step, one structurally ensures the orthogonality of this family of vectors. The iterative process is summarised as follows.

$\begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{q}}_{0}=0,{\beta}_{0}=0.\\ \text{Calcul de}{\mathrm{q}}_{1}/\mathrm{\parallel }{\mathrm{q}}_{1}\mathrm{\parallel }=1.\end{array}\\ \text{Pour}\text{k=1,m}\text{faire}\\ \mathrm{z}={\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}{\mathrm{q}}_{k}-{\beta}_{\text{k-1}}{\mathrm{q}}_{\text{k-1}},\\ {\alpha}_{k}=(\mathrm{z},{\mathrm{q}}_{k}),\\ \mathrm{v}=\mathrm{z}-{\alpha}_{k}{\mathrm{q}}_{k},\\ \begin{array}{c}{\beta}_{k}=\mathrm{\parallel }\mathrm{v}\mathrm{\parallel },\\ \text{Si}{\beta}_{k}\mathrm{\ne }0\text{alors}\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{q}}_{\text{k+1}}=\frac{\mathrm{v}}{{\beta}_{k}},\\ \text{Sinon}\\ \text{Déflation};\\ \text{Fin si}.\end{array}\\ \text{Fin}\text{boucle}.\end{array}$ Algorithm 9. Theoretical Lanczos.

The **Rayleigh matrix** then takes the form (in complex with a Hermitian inner product)

${\mathrm{B}}_{m}=\left[\begin{array}{cccc}{\alpha}_{1}& {\stackrel{ˉ}{\beta}}_{1}& 0& 0\\ {\beta}_{1}& {\alpha}_{2}& ...& 0\\ 0& ...& ...& {\stackrel{ˉ}{\beta}}_{m-1}\\ 0& 0& {\beta}_{m-1}& {\alpha}_{m}\end{array}\right]$

By construction, this algorithm assures us of the following results (in exact arithmetic):

- The ${({\mathrm{q}}_{k})}_{k=1,m}$ constitute an orthonormal family.
- They generate the Krylov space of order $m$ initiated by ${\mathrm{q}}_{1}$, ${\mathrm{K}}_{m}({\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma},{\mathrm{q}}_{1})=\text{Vect}({\mathrm{q}}_{1},{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}{\mathrm{q}}_{1},...,{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{m-1}{\mathrm{q}}_{1})$.
- They make it possible to build a tridiagonal, Hermitian matrix ${\mathrm{B}}_{m}$ of adjustable size.
- The spectrum of ${\mathrm{B}}_{m}$ admits only the *m* dominant simple modes on which ${\mathrm{q}}_{1}$ has a component.

Notes:

- *The algorithm therefore does not allow, in theory, the capture of multiple modes. This can be explained by noting that any irreducible Hessenberg matrix*  [21]_ *(hence a fortiori a tridiagonal matrix) admits only simple modes.*
- The cost of one iteration is low, of the order of that of a matrix-vector product ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}{\mathrm{q}}_{k},$ that is for the $m$ first iterations a computational complexity of $O(\mathrm{nm}(c+5))$ (with c the mean number of non-zero terms on the rows of the working matrix). On the other hand, the memory complexity required is low because one does not need to build ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}$ in full; one only needs to know its action on a vector. This characteristic is very interesting for solving problems of large size[Vau00].
- In general the field of activity orients the choice of an initial Lanczos vector; the latter must for example belong to a space of admissible solutions (satisfying constraints, boundary conditions...) as well as to the range of the operator. This last point is important because it makes it possible to enrich the modal search more rapidly by not limiting oneself to the kernel.
- The Lanczos algorithm is only a means of approaching the Krylov subspaces. Its generalisation to non-symmetric configurations is called the Arnoldi algorithm (cf. § 7.2).

#### 6.2.2 Error and convergence estimates

Because of the particular form of ${\mathrm{B}}_{m}$, the extraction of the Ritz elements sought is simplified, and in addition, the following result allows us quickly (*via* a product of two reals!) to estimate their qualities.

**Property 2**

The Euclidean norm of the residual of the Ritz element $(\tilde{\lambda},\tilde{\mathrm{u}}={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}\mathrm{x})$ is equal to

${\mathrm{\parallel }\mathrm{r}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}={\mathrm{\parallel }(\sigma -\tilde{\lambda}\mathrm{I})\tilde{\mathrm{u}}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}=\mid {\beta}_{m}\mid \mid {\mathrm{e}}_{m}^{T}\mathrm{x}\mid$

*Proof:*

Trivial by taking the Euclidean norm of the Lanczos factorisation ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}{\mathrm{B}}_{m}\mathrm{x}={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}{\mathrm{B}}_{m}\mathrm{x}+{\beta}_{m}{\mathrm{q}}_{m+1}{\mathrm{e}}_{m}^{T}\mathrm{x}$ where ${\mathrm{q}}_{m+1}$ is normed to unity.

Notes:

- A more general result, independent of any solution method, assures us that for each eigenmode $(\tilde{\lambda},x)$ of ${\mathrm{B}}_{m}$, there exists an eigenmode $(\tilde{\lambda},u)$ of ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}$ such that:
- $\begin{array}{c}\mid \lambda -\tilde{\lambda}\mid \le \frac{{\mathrm{\parallel }\mathrm{r}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}^{2}}{\gamma}\\ \mid \sin(\mathrm{u},\tilde{\mathrm{u}})\mid \le \frac{{\mathrm{\parallel }\mathrm{r}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}}{\gamma}\end{array}\text{avec}\gamma =\underset{{\lambda}_{i}\mathrm{\ne }\tilde{\lambda}}{\min}\mid {\lambda}_{i}-{\tilde{\mathrm{u}}}^{T}{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}\tilde{\mathrm{u}}\mid$
- Therefore, more than the minimisation of the residual, the stopping criterion of the methods of Lanczos/Arnoldi type $\mid {\beta}_{m}\mid <\varepsilon$ makes it possible to approach the original spectrum as well as possible. These upper bounds are accessible only in the Hermitian case.
- In the general case (and in particular non-normal) they are more difficult or even impossible to build without complementary information (level of non-normality, of “defectiveness”..). The estimate of the residual is then no longer the right criterion for estimating the quality of the approximate modes.

Let us now take an interest in the quality of convergence of the algorithm. Let us particularise theorem 2 for this algorithm. By bounding the error norm of ${\mathrm{\parallel }({\text{I-P}}_{m})\text{u}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}$ one obtains the following upper bounds:

**Theorem 3**

Let $({\lambda}_{i},{\mathrm{u}}_{i})$ be the *i* th dominant eigenmode of the diagonalisable ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}$; there then exists a Ritz mode $(\tilde{{\lambda}_{i}},{\tilde{\mathrm{u}}}_{i})$ such that:

$\begin{array}{c}\mid {\lambda}_{i}-{\tilde{\lambda}}_{i}\mid \le \frac{{\alpha}^{2}}{\beta}{(\underset{j<i}{\Pi}\frac{{\lambda}_{j}-{\lambda}_{n}}{{\lambda}_{j}-{\lambda}_{i}})}^{2}{T}_{m-i}^{-2}({\gamma}_{i})\\ \mid \sin({\mathrm{u}}_{i},{\tilde{\mathrm{u}}}_{i})\mid \le \alpha (\underset{j<i}{\Pi}\frac{{\lambda}_{j}-{\lambda}_{n}}{{\lambda}_{j}-{\lambda}_{i}}){T}_{m-i}^{-1}({\gamma}_{i})\end{array}$

with ${\gamma}_{i}\text{=1+2}\frac{{\lambda}_{i}-{\lambda}_{\text{i+1}}}{{\lambda}_{\text{1+i}}-{\lambda}_{n}}$, ${T}_{m-i}(x)$ the Chebyshev polynomial of degree $m-i$, $\beta$ the constant of theorem 1 and $\alpha =\frac{\beta}{{\mathrm{\parallel }{\mathrm{P}}_{m}{\mathrm{u}}_{i}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}}\tan({\mathrm{q}}_{1},{\mathrm{u}}_{i})$.

*Proof:*

Cf. [LT86] pp554-557.

These upper bounds indicate that:

- If the initial vector has no contribution along the eigenvectors, one cannot capture the said modes ($\alpha \to +\infty$).
- One has **preferentially convergence of the extreme modes** and all the better as the spectrum is **separated** (these methods are less sensitive to clusters than the methods of iterated power type), without packing of eigenvalues (the famous "clusters").
- **The error decreases when** $m$ **increases** (like the inverse of the polynomial ${\mathrm{T}}_{\text{m-i}}(\mathrm{x})$, hence like the inverse of an exponential).
- The estimate on the eigenvalues is better than that of their associated eigenvectors.

Note:

- The convergence of the method was studied by P. Kaniel then improved (and corrected) by S.C.Paige; One will find a survey of these studies in the paper by Y. Saad[Saa80].

Let us now look at how the algorithm behaves in finite arithmetic. We shall see that it is the seat of surprising phenomena.

### 6.3 Practical Lanczos algorithm

#### 6.3.1 Orthogonality problem

During the effective implementation of this algorithm, one realises that very quickly **the Lanczos vectors lose their orthogonalities**. The Rayleigh matrix is then no longer the projected matrix of the initial operator and the spectrum captured is more and more tainted with errors. For a long time this inevitable phenomenon was wrongly attributed to the rounding effects of finite arithmetic. In 1980, C.C.Paige showed that the loss of orthogonality was above all **imputable to the convergence of an eigenmode**. Thus, as soon as one captures a dominant mode, one perturbs the arrangement of the previous Lanczos vectors. The following result clearly expresses this paradox.

**Property 4 (Paige's analysis)**

Taking up the notations of algorithm 9 and denoting by $\varepsilon$ the machine precision, at iteration $k+1$ the orthogonality of the new Lanczos vector is expressed in the form:

$\mid {\mathrm{q}}_{k+1}^{T}{\mathrm{q}}_{i}\mid =\frac{\mid {\mathrm{v}}^{T}{\mathrm{q}}_{i}\mid +\varepsilon {\mathrm{\parallel }{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}}{\mid {\beta}_{k}\mid }(i\le k)$

*Proof:*

Cf. [Pai00].

The problem in fact arises during the normalisation of the new Lanczos vector ${\mathrm{q}}_{k+1}$. When this mode converges, according to property 2, this comes from the smallness of the coefficient ${\beta}_{k}$ and therefore despite the theoretical orthogonality ($\mid {\mathrm{v}}^{T}{\mathrm{q}}_{i}\mid =0(i\le k)$), the effective orthogonality is far from being acquired ($\mid {\mathrm{q}}_{k+1}^{T}{\mathrm{q}}_{i}\mid \text{>>}\varepsilon (i\le k)$).

The numerical treatment of these problems has been the subject of numerous researches for thirty years and numerous palliative strategies have been developed. The choice of such or such a method depends on the type of spectral information required and on the computing resources available; the survey of JL.Vaudescal moreover proposes a very good overview of these variants:

- **Lanczos algorithm without reorthogonalisation**, developed by J.K.Cullum and R.A.Willoughby[CW85] which consists in purging the spectrum computed of its spurious modes by studying the interlacings of the eigenvalues of the working matrix and of one of its sub-matrices. This variant admits a low extra computational and memory cost for determining the eigenvalues, but it complicates (sometimes greatly) the search for the eigenvectors.
- **Lanczos algorithms with total reorthogonalisation** (B.N.Parlett) or **selective** (J.Scott) which consist at each step in reorthogonalising the last Lanczos vector obtained with respect to all the vectors already computed or simply with respect to the converged Ritz vectors (this variant thus makes it possible to drive dynamically the admissible loss of orthogonality). These variants are much more costly in computational complexity (the automatic reorthogonalisations) and in memory (storage of the previous vectors) but they prove more efficient and more robust.

In the Newmann-Pipano variant[NP77] (METHODE='TRI_DIAG') of *Code_Aster*, it is the **full reorthogonalisation strategy**  [22]_
which has been chosen: the vector ${q}_{k+1}$ is therefore not quite computed as in the theoretical algorithm.

Notes:

- These problems of loss of orthogonality arise with all the more acuity as the size of the subspace increases. This is an additional argument for putting in place an iterative process limiting this size.
- These strategies come in vector or block form; they are implemented in simple or iterative algorithms. The latter being able to benefit from explicit or implicit restarts, driven or automatic. The IRAM variant thus benefits from an implicit restart computed automatically.
- The central point of these algorithms is constituted by the orthogonalisation method put in place. The whole process depends on its success and on its robustness. To the orthogonal transformations of Householder or Givens type, very expensive but very robust, one now prefers the algorithms of Iterative Gram-Schmidt type (IGSM) which are a better compromise between efficiency and computational complexity (cf. Appendix 2). This is moreover the choice which has been made for the Lanczos/Arnoldi variants put in place in the code.
- The variant of selective reorthogonalisation with respect to the converged modes amounts to carrying out an implicit deflation by restriction on the working operator so as not to have to recompute them (cf. § 4.1).

#### 6.3.2 Capture of multiplicities

One has seen that in theory the Lanczos algorithm did not allow, whatever its reorthogonalisation strategy, the theoretical capture of multiple modes. **In practice**, for once, the **rounding effects come to the rescue** and "sprinkle" small components along almost all the eigenvectors. One can therefore **capture multiplicities**; however they can be erroneous and require a complementary verification post-processing.

Note:

- In fact, only a block version (G.Golub & R.Underwood 1979) can allow us a correct detection of the multiplicities, at least as long as the size of the blocks is sufficient. This version has been extended (M.Sadkane[Sad93] 1993) to the Arnoldi algorithm but it would be a pity to deprive oneself of the Sorensen variant (IRAM) which is more efficient and more robust.

#### 6.3.3 Lanczos phenomenon

The success of this algorithm rested at the outset on what is called the “**Lanczos phenomenon**”. This conjecture predicts that for a sufficiently large subspace size *(* $m\gg n$) one is capable of detecting the whole spectrum (it being the subsequent task to separate the “wheat from the chaff”) of the working operator. In view of the low memory pre-requisites of a “basic” Lanczos (*roughly* a tridiagonal matrix and a few vectors), this is particularly interesting for treating very large sparse systems (the other algorithms of iterated power type or QR themselves require the knowledge of the whole working matrix).

We shall now detail (a little) a few elements whose interest goes far beyond the framework of this algorithm.

### 6.4 Complementary treatments

#### 6.4.1 Detection of invariant subspaces

In algorithm 8, the vanishing of the coefficient ${\beta}_{l-1}$ prevents the normalisation of the new vector and requires an ancillary treatment called **deflation**. The Krylov subspace computed is then an **invariant subspace** and its spectrum is therefore exact. In other words, the $l-1$ first eigenvalues unearthed by the supporting algorithm (QR or QL) are those of ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}$.

In order to continue, one must then choose a new random vector respecting the boundary conditions, orthogonalise it with all the Lanczos vectors already computed and build a second family of Lanczos vectors which one orthogonalises with respect to the first family constituting the space:

${\mathrm{H}}_{l}={\mathrm{K}}_{l}({\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma},{\mathrm{q}}_{1})=\text{Vect}({\mathrm{q}}_{1},{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}{\mathrm{q}}_{1},...,{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}^{l-1}{\mathrm{q}}_{1})$

The tridiagonal matrix obtained has the following form:

${\mathrm{B}}_{m}=\left[\begin{array}{ccccccc}{\alpha}_{1}& {\stackrel{ˉ}{\beta}}_{1}& & & & & \\ {\beta}_{1}& {\alpha}_{2}& ...& & & 0& \\ & ...& ...& \mathrm{\simeq }0& & & \\ & & \mathrm{\simeq }0& {\alpha}_{l}& {\stackrel{ˉ}{\beta}}_{l}& & \\ & & & {\beta}_{l}& {\alpha}_{l+1}& ...& \\ & 0& & & ...& ...& {\stackrel{ˉ}{\beta}}_{m-1}\\ & & & & & {\beta}_{m-1}& {\alpha}_{m}\end{array}\right]$

The intermediate Lanczos factorisation (of order $l$) is written:

${A}_{\sigma}{Q}_{l}={Q}_{l}{B}_{l}$

The values ${({\alpha}_{k},{\beta}_{k})}_{k=1,l}$ are obtained from the first random vector and the following values ${({\alpha}_{k},{\beta}_{k})}_{k=l+1,m}$ *via* a second random vector. In order to detect the vanishing of the off-diagonal term one uses the numerical criterion

$\mid {\beta}_{l-1}\mid \le \text{PREC\_LANZOS}\mid {\alpha}_{l}\mid$ then ${\beta}_{l-1}=0$,

where PREC_LANCZOS is initialised under the factor keyword CALC_FREQ.

Notes:

- A more robust criterion retained by the mathematical libraries EISPACK, LAPACK and ARPACK uses the previous diagonal term, a modulable parameter $c$ and the machine precision $\varepsilon$,

$\mid {\beta}_{l-1}\mid <c\varepsilon (\mid {\alpha}_{l-1}\mid +\mid {\alpha}_{l}\mid )$

- it is this criterion which has been retained for IRAM (but the user cannot modify the parameter $c$; the latter is fixed at a standard value by the code), various messages moreover warning the user of the detection of an invariant subspace.
- Obtaining such a space is quite welcome since it assures us of the very good quality of the first modes. Moreover, one reduces the size of the problem by splitting it into two parts: one solved, the other to be solved. Numerous techniques seek to reproduce this phenomenon artificially (cf. § 4.1).

#### 6.4.2 Restart strategies

In order to **limit the endemic orthogonalisation problems**, **bound the memory pre-requisites** and take into account **dynamically the spectral information** already obtained, restart strategies have been coupled to the Lanczos algorithm. It loses its “simple” character and becomes “iterative”. One iterates the “restarts” until the desired convergence criteria are satisfied. The algorithm does not undergo the convergence of the modes imposed by theorem 8 and it can interactively favour that of certain modes.

In theory however, the larger the size of the subspace, the better the convergence. A compromise is therefore to be found between the size of the subspace and the frequency of the restarts. Various restart vectors can be used, generally written as a weighted sum of the $p$ eigenmodes sought (Y.Saad 1980)

${\mathrm{q}}_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}{\chi }_{i}{\mathrm{Q}}_{m}{\mathrm{x}}_{i}$

Indeed, if one restarts with an initial vector belonging to the invariant subspace generated by the modes sought, one is then sure of obtaining (with a good orthogonalisation algorithm) a residual norm of the order of the machine precision and almost exact eigenmodes. Besides the decision to trigger the restart, the whole problem resides in the search for these weights ${\chi }_{i}$. We shall see that with IRAM, the restarts put in place via implicit polynomial filters solve this question elegantly and automatically.

Notes:

- This philosophy of restarts was initiated by W.Karush(1951).
- The restarts based on the Schur vectors associated with the spectral decomposition sought seem more stable (J.Scott 1993).
- Efficiency criteria have been sought in order to decide on the appropriateness of a restart (B.Vital 1990).
- Instead of a simple linear combination of eigenvectors, one can determine a restart vector via polynomials (Chebyshev in real and Faber in complex) making it possible to focus the modal search in such or such a zone. One then speaks of explicit polynomial accelerations[Sad93].

The following paragraph will take up some of the concepts presented so far in order to explain the variant put in place in the code.

### 6.5 Implementation in Code_Aster

#### 6.5.1 Newmann & Pipano variant

This variant developed by M.Newmann & A.Pipano[CW85][NP77] in 1977 is a simple Lanczos method, in real arithmetic, with total reorthogonalisation (*via* a GSM). It uses the classical “shift and invert” blended with the pseudo-inner product introduced by the shifted matrix $(A-\sigma B)$

$\underset{{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{\text{-1}}\mathrm{B}}}\mathrm{u}=\underset{\lambda}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{1}{\mu -\sigma }}}\mathrm{u}$

with:

$\mid \begin{array}{c}(x,y)={y}^{t}(A-\sigma B)x,\\ \delta =\text{sign}(x,x),\\ \parallel x\parallel =\delta {\mid (x,x)\mid }^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{array}$

This choice makes the pair “working operator-inner product” symmetric and it is adapted to the particular matrices of structural mechanics. One can thus treat problems:

- Of free structure and of fluid-structure ($A$ may be singular).

#### 6.5.4 Detection of rigid-body modes

An **algebraic method computing the zero eigenvalues** of the generalised modal problem has been introduced. Physically these correspond to the motions with zero strain energy of a free structure (cf. [BQ00] tutorial no. 2). Apart from the numerical difficulties of handling quasi-zero quantities, because of their multiplicities, their correct capture had until now often been problematic for the Lanczos implemented in *Code_Aster*: spurious modes appeared corresponding to missed multiplicities!

This algorithm for detecting rigid-body modes, which one activates by initialising OPTION to ‘MODE_RIGIDE’ (default value ‘SANS‘), intervenes in pre-processing of the modal analysis proper (only with ‘TRI_DIAG’). It is based on the analysis of the stiffness matrix and breaks down into three phases:

- Detection of the zero pivots of this matrix.
- Blocking of these pivots.
- Solution of a linear system of which the associated eigenvectors are solutions.

During the Lanczos process it is then sufficient to orthogonalise, as they are determined, the basis vectors with these latter.

However, the introduction of the IRA method reduces the interest (if not for comparison purposes) of such an option (which is not free in computational complexity since it requires inversions of systems). Why deprive oneself of such an algorithm which is in no way affected by the presence of these somewhat particular multiple modes!

This method nevertheless remains useful as a fallback solution in the event of failure of IRAM. One can also envisage encapsulating it in an auxiliary operator (like INFO_MODE).

Note:

- This option ‘MODE_RIGIDE’ can be activated only with Lanczos for GEPs with real modes.

## 7 IRA algorithm(SOLVEUR_MODAL=_F( METHODE=‘SORENSEN’))

### 7.1 Introduction

We have seen that one of the crucial problems of the Lanczos method is the inevitable loss of orthogonality of its basis vectors. A **generalisation** of this algorithm **to the non-Hermitian case** imagined by **W.E.Arnoldi** [Arn51] **in 1951** makes it possible to solve this problem partially. It was brought back into fashion by Y.Saad[Saa80] in 1980 and an abundant literature covers the subject. Apart from the founding article and the papers of Y.Saad and M.Sadkane[Sad93], one recommends the updated and exhaustive survey of J.L.Vaudescal[Vau00].

This method being at the basis of **the IRAM algorithm called “of Sorensen”** (IRAM for ‘Implicit Restarted Arnoldi Method’), we shall first detail its operation, its behaviours and its limitations. Subsequently, we shall identify the issues which IRAM must answer (and it does so in most standard cases!) and we shall look into its theoretical and numerical inner workings. We shall conclude with the summary of its effective parameters in *Code_Aster* and with an example of a message file.

### 7.2 Arnoldi algorithm

#### 7.2.1 Principle

Its field of application covers **all the pairs “working operator-(pseudo)inner product”**. The counterpart of this openness is the fill-in of the Rayleigh matrix which becomes of **upper Hessenberg** form. This is not very prejudicial, because one can thus apply the QR algorithm to it **directly.** The first step of a good QR, apart from the balancing, consists precisely in reducing the working matrix to Hessenberg form. This makes it possible to gain an order of magnitude during the solution proper (cf. Appendix 1).

The algorithm is very similar to that of Lanczos; it consists in progressively building a family of Arnoldi vectors ${\mathrm{q}}_{1,}{\mathrm{q}}_{2,}\mathrm{...},{\mathrm{q}}_{m}$ by projecting orthogonally, at iteration $k$, the vector ${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}{\mathrm{q}}_{k}$ onto the $k$ previous vectors. The new vector becomes ${\mathrm{q}}_{k+1}$ and thus, step by step, one ensures the orthogonality of this family of vectors.

Unlike Lanczos, the orthogonality of the new vector with respect to all the previous ones is therefore ensured explicitly and not implicitly. The latter is managed by the Modified Gram-Schmidt algorithm (GSM) (cf. appendix 2) which proves sufficiently robust in most cases.

Denoting by ${\mathrm{e}}_{m}$ the $m$ th vector of the canonical basis, the **residual vector of the Arnoldi factorisation** is written ${\mathrm{R}}_{m}={b}_{m+1,m}{\mathrm{q}}_{m+1}{\mathrm{e}}_{m}^{T}$.

Armed with these lessons, we shall now summarise the issues which IRAM must answer.

### 7.3 The issues at stake

The IRA algorithm attempts to bring an elegant remedy to the recurrent problems raised by the other approaches:

- **Minimisation** of the **projection space**: it proposes at a minimum $m>p+1$ instead of the $m=4p$ of Lanczos.
- Optimal management of the extra costs of orthogonalisation establishing a **compromise** between the **size of the subspace** and the **frequency of the restarts**.
- Transparent, dynamic and efficient management of these restarts.
- Automatic taking into account of the **spectral information**.
- Fixing of the **memory pre-requisites** and of the **quality** of the **results**.

One therefore no longer has any question to ask oneself concerning the reorthogonalisation strategy, the frequency of the restarts, their implementations, the criteria for detecting possible spurious modes... “super-IRAM” takes care of everything!

In short, it provides:

- A better overall **robustness**.
- **Computational complexities** $O(\mathrm{4nm}(m-p))$ and **memory** complexities $O(\mathrm{2np}+{p}^{2})$ **improved** (especially compared with a simple Lanczos such as that of Newmann & Pipano) for **a fixed accuracy**.
- A more rigorous capture of the multiplicities, of the clusters and of the rigid-body modes (hence **fewer parasitic modes**!).

Notes:

- On this last point, only a block version of IRAM or a version incorporating “purge and lock” (capture and filtering techniques, cf. D.Sorensen & R.B.Lehoucq, 1997) can guarantee us a correct detection of the spectrum of a standard operator (i.e. not too poorly conditioned).
- It seems that, in practice in *Code_Aster*, the ratio in computational complexity between IRAM and Lanczos/Bathe & Wilson is at a minimum of the order of 2 in favour of the former. With reasonable problem sizes (a few tens of thousands of dofs and $p=O(100)$) the latter can rise up to 10 (without the encapsulation of CALC_MODES over several search sub-bands). In certain semi-industrial cases, it has made it possible to carry out a spectrum search which had failed with Jacobi and which was unapproachable with Lanczos (in view of the time allotted).
- A class of algorithm called “Jacobi-Davidson” (cf. R.B.Morgan 1990) seems even more promising for treating pathological cases. It uses an algorithm of Lanczos type which it preconditions via a Rayleigh method.

In the following paragraph we shall explain the operation of IRAM.

### 7.4 ‘Implicit Restarted Arnoldi’ (IRA) algorithm

This algorithm was initiated by D.C.Sorensen[Sor92] in 1992 and is enjoying a real boom for the solution of large modal systems on parallel supercomputers. Its field of application is entirely general. It treats both real and complex problems, Hermitian or not. It is summarised as a succession of Arnoldi factorisations whose results automatically drive static and implicit restarts, via polynomial filters modelled by implicit QRs.

First of all it carries out an **Arnoldi factorisation of order** $m=p+q$ (in theory $q=2$ is sufficient, in practice $q=p$ is preferable. It is moreover this last default value which has been retained (cf. §7.5) of the working matrix. Then once this pre-processing has been carried out it **iterates a process of filtering of the undesirable part of the spectrum** (numerically and methodologically, it is easier to exclude than to incorporate).

It begins by determining the spectrum of the Rayleigh matrix (*via* the unassailable QR) and it dissociates from it the undesired part (by referring to the criteria fixed by the user) which it then uses as a shift in order to put in place a series of $q$ implicit QRs with simple shift (cf. Appendix 1). The factorisation is then written:

${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}{\mathrm{Q}}_{m}^{+}={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}^{+}{\mathrm{B}}_{m}^{+}+{\mathrm{R}}_{m}\mathrm{Q}$

where ${\mathrm{Q}}_{m}^{+}={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}\mathrm{Q}$, ${\mathrm{B}}_{m}^{+}={\mathrm{Q}}^{T}{\mathrm{B}}_{m}\mathrm{Q}$ and $\mathrm{Q}={\mathrm{Q}}_{1}{\mathrm{Q}}_{2}\mathrm{....}{\mathrm{Q}}_{q}$ the unitary matrix associated with the QRs. After having updated the matrices, one truncates them down to order $p$

${\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}{\mathrm{Q}}_{p}^{+}={Q}_{p}^{+}{\mathrm{B}}_{p}^{+}+{\mathrm{R}}_{p}^{+}$

and thus, at the cost of $q$ new Arnoldi iterations, one can recover an Arnoldi factorisation of order $m$ which is viable. The whole subtlety of the process rests on this last chaining. Let us denote:

$\Phi ({\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma})=\prod_{i=1}^{q}({\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}-{\tilde{\lambda}}_{p+i}\text{Id})$ ,

the matrix polynomial of order $q$ generated by the working operator. In fact, the implicit QRs have acted on the $p$ first rows of the Arnoldi matrix, of the Rayleigh matrix and of the residual, **so that the complementary factorisation of order** $q$ **produces the same effect as a factorisation of order** $m$ **initiated by the vector**

${\tilde{\mathrm{q}}}_{1}=\Phi ({\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}){\mathrm{q}}_{1}$ .

The initial vector has been implicitly modified (there is no actual construction and application of the polynomial) so that it preferentially generates the modes desired, by subtracting the components judged “improper”. As one has already remarked, this type of restart makes it possible to decrease the residual by lessening the components of the initial vector along the undesirable modes. In exact arithmetic, one would immediately have ${\mathrm{R}}_{m}=0$ and the process would stop there!

**Iteration after iteration, one therefore improves the quality of the modes sought by relying on auxiliary modes**. Of course the latter is estimated at each step and conditions the appropriateness of simulating another restart or not. The process can be summarised (very macroscopically!) as follows:

$\begin{array}{c}\text{Factorisation d'Arnoldi d'ordre}m:{\text{AQ}}_{m}={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}{\mathrm{B}}_{m}+{\mathrm{R}}_{m}.\\ \text{Pour k= 1, NMAX\_ITER\_SOREN faire}\\ \text{Calculer}\underset{\begin{array}{c}\text{Conservées pour}\\ \text{amélioration}\end{array}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\tilde{{\lambda}_{1}},\tilde{{\lambda}_{2}}...\tilde{{\lambda}_{p}}}},\underset{\begin{array}{c}\text{Utilisées comme}\\ \text{shifts}\end{array}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\tilde{\lambda}}_{\text{p+1}}...{\tilde{\lambda}}_{\text{p+q}},}}\\ \text{QR avec shifts implicites,}\\ \text{Mise à jour}{\mathrm{Q}}_{m},{\mathrm{B}}_{m}\text{et}{\mathrm{R}}_{m},\\ \text{Troncature}\text{de}\text{ces}\text{matrices}à\text{l'ordre}p,\\ \begin{array}{c}{\text{AQ}}_{p}={\mathrm{Q}}_{p}{\mathrm{B}}_{p}+{\mathrm{R}}_{p}\mathrm{\Rightarrow }{\text{AQ}}_{m}={\mathrm{Q}}_{m}{\mathrm{B}}_{m}+{\mathrm{R}}_{m},\\ \text{Estimation qualité des}p\text{modes}.\end{array}\\ \text{Fin boucle}.\end{array}$

**Algorithm 14. IRA method (known as Sorensen's).**

The maximum number of iterations is driven by the keyword NMAX_ITER_SOREN of the factor keyword CALC_FREQ. One must note that the Arnoldi algorithm is prudently completed by a **total reorthogonalisation** (triggered only if this proves necessary). This extra cost is all the more acceptable as it is implemented via the IGSM algorithm of Kahan-Parlett (cf. Appendix 2) which is particularly efficient. All this allows us to be assured of the good behaviour of the projection with respect to the initial spectrum.

On the other hand, the evaluation of the **quality of the modes** is not carried out simply by building the $p$ **residuals** ${\mathrm{\parallel }{\mathrm{r}}_{i}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}={\mathrm{\parallel }({\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}-\tilde{\lambda}\mathrm{I}){\tilde{\mathrm{u}}}_{i}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}$ *via* property 10. One has already mentioned that in the non-Hermitian case, they would not suffice for this task, in particular in the event of a strong defect of normality. In order to acquit oneself of it, without having recourse to other information [23]_
*a priori*, one uses the previous property completed by a criterion due to Z.Bai et al[BDK93]

$\mid {b}_{m+1,m}\mid \mid {\mathrm{e}}_{m}^{T}\mathrm{x}\mid <\max(\varepsilon \mathrm{\parallel }{\mathrm{B}}_{m}\mathrm{\parallel },\text{PREC\_SOREN}\mid \tilde{\lambda}\mid )$

where $\varepsilon$ is the machine precision and PREC_SOREN is a keyword initialised under CALC_FREQ. The user therefore has a (partial) control of the quality of the modes, which he did not have with the other methods implemented in the code. In view of the various norms used, this error is different from that resulting from the global post-processing (cf. §3.6) which is displayed in the result columns.

Notes:

- The polynomial acceleration technique used is more efficient than that of Chebyshev, since the latter is explicit and requires m matrix-vector products.
- In order to avoid the deterioration of the Ritz vectors (and therefore of the approximate eigenvectors) by eigenvalues of very large moduli (associated with the kernel of $\mathrm{B}$ in “shift and invert”) a filtering of Ericsson & Ruhe type[ER80] has been implemented. More robust techniques exist but they require a priori information concerning in particular the Jordan blocks associated with the kernel of the operator (cf. Meerbergen & Spence, 1996).
- This algorithm can be seen as a truncated form of the implicit QR algorithm of J.C.F.Francis (cf. Appendix 1).

#### 7.5.2 Adaptations of the Sorensen algorithm

In order to treat generalised modal problems, this package proposes a whole series of spectral transformations, in real or in complex, Hermitian or not. In the Hermitian case, the Sorensen algorithm is based on the Lanczos-**QL** pair (IRLM for Implicit Restarted Lanczos Method). The two approaches (Hermitian or not) are moreover not provided for treating pseudo-inner products linked to indefinite matrices.

Precisely, in order both to **circumscribe numerical problems linked to their rather heterogeneous properties** in the code and, on the other hand, to ensure a **better overall robustness**, we have chosen to **work in non-symmetric mode** (**IRAM** with **Arnoldi** and **QR)**, on **the following working operator-inner product pair**:

$\begin{array}{c}\underset{{\mathrm{A}}_{\sigma}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})}^{-1}\mathrm{B}}}\mathrm{u}=\underset{\lambda}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{1}{\mu -\sigma }}}\mathrm{u}\\ (\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y})={\mathrm{y}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{x}\end{array}$

One could have treated the buckling problems in “buckling mode” *via* the same “shift and invert” and the pseudo-inner product introduced by $A$. But because of the quasi-systematic introduction of Lagranges, this matrix becomes indefinite or even singular, which greatly perturbs the process. The same causes produce the same effects when, for a dynamics computation, one resorts to the $B$-inner product. Rather than modifying the whole package by introducing a pseudo-inner product, we have therefore opted for a simple “Euclidean” inner product which is more robust and much less costly.

It was therefore necessary to modify the “*reverse-communication*” procedures of the package, because it did not provide for this option (with standard matrices one classically prefers to enrich the components with a matrix inner product, even in the non-symmetric case). Unlike the Newmann & Pipano variant introduced for Lanczos, we have deliberately placed ourselves in a non-symmetric configuration. But in order to avoid as far as possible the recurrent orthogonality problems, even in the symmetric case, we would have opted for the version of IRAM using Arnoldi.

The disadvantage of this approach is that one must, in a preliminary post-processing of IRAM, $B$-orthonormalise the approximate eigenvectors in order to recover numerically property 2 exploited by the modal recombinations. This step does not perturb the basis of eigenmodes unearthed and it is carried out very efficiently *via* the IGSM of Kahan-Parlett.

The **taking into account of the boundary conditions** and, in particular, of the double dualisations, has been conducted as for Lanczos according to the operating mode described in §3.2. In particular, once the initial vector is in the admissible space one applies the working operator to it. This classical process makes it possible to purge the Lanczos vectors (and therefore the Ritz vectors) of the components of the kernel.

On the other hand, in certain configurations for which the **number of frequencies requested** $p$ is **equal to the number of active degrees of freedom**, one has had to bluff the algorithm which stopped in fatal error! Indeed, it generally detected well the expected invariant space (of size $p$), but because of the particular structure of the eigenvectors associated with the Lagranges (cf. proof of property 4, § 3.5) it had a great deal of difficulty in generating an initial vector proportional to them.

Particular treatments taking into account the numbering of these Lagranges would have been necessary, which would have been all the more costly as they are not fundamentally necessary for solving the problem requested! All the spectral information already being present in the innermost part of the algorithm, it is therefore not worth completing the two remaining iterations (when the user requests $p={n}_{\mathrm{ddl}-\mathrm{actifs}}$, one automatically imposes $m=p+2$). It is sufficient to short-circuit the natural thread of the algorithm, to extract the interesting Ritz modes and to post-process them in order to return to the initial space.

Note:

- This type of case in which one looks for a number of eigenmodes very close to the number of degrees of freedom falls outside the “ideal” field of use of this type of algorithm. A good QR would without any doubt be more efficient, but it is a good means of testing the algorithm.

## 8 Bathe and Wilson method (SOLVEUR_MODAL= _F(METHODE=‘JACOBI’))

### 8.1 Principle

The Bathe and Wilson method is a **simultaneous iteration method** which consists in **extending the inverse iteration algorithm**. One works from the shifted problem ${\mathrm{A}}^{\sigma}\mathrm{x}:=(\lambda -\sigma )\mathrm{B}\mathrm{x}$ with ${\mathrm{A}}^{\sigma}:=\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B}$ (cf. §5.4). One distinguishes four parts in the algorithm[Bat71]:

- Choose $p$ independent initial vectors ${\mathrm{x}}_{1,}{\mathrm{x}}_{2,}\dots ,,{\mathrm{x}}_{p}$ and build the matrix $X$ which they generate.
- Compute the eigen elements, via the global Jacobi method (cf. Appendix 3), in the Ritz subspace by solving

$(\stackrel{ˉ}{\mathrm{A}}-\stackrel{ˉ}{{\lambda}_{i}}\stackrel{ˉ}{\mathrm{B}}){\mathrm{u}}_{i}=0$ .where $\stackrel{ˉ}{A}={Q}^{T}{A}^{\sigma}Q$ and $\stackrel{ˉ}{B}={Q}^{T}BQ$

- One then returns to the initial space (for the eigenvectors) via the transformation $X=QU$ .where $\mathrm{U}=[\lbrace  {\mathrm{u}}_{i}\rbrace  ]$
- Test the convergence of the eigenmodes ${\lambda}_{i}$.

### 8.2 Convergence tests

The Bathe and Wilson method converges towards the $p$ smallest eigenvalues provided that the $p$ initial vectors are not $B$-orthogonal to one of the eigenvectors. Moreover, the matrices $\stackrel{ˉ}{A}$ and $\stackrel{ˉ}{B}$ tend towards diagonal matrices. For this reason and as the matrices are full, one uses the Jacobi method (cf. Appendix 3) to find the eigen elements of the Ritz subspace.

In order to test the convergence of the eigenvalues, one ranks them after each iteration in increasing order in absolute value and one looks at whether, for each eigenvalue, the following test is satisfied

$\mid {\lambda}^{k+1}-{\lambda}^{k}\mid \le \text{PREC\_BATHE}\mid {\lambda}^{k+1}\mid$

where the exponent $k$ indicates the number of iterations. If after NMAX_ITER_BATHE iterations one has not converged for all the eigenvalues, an alarm message is issued in the message file.

### 8.3 Implementation in Code_Aster

#### 8.3.1 Dimension of the subspace

If one wishes to compute $p$ eigenvalues, it is recommended to use a subspace of higher dimension $q$. One will verify the convergence only for the $r$ smallest eigenvalues where $p\le r\le q$ . It seems that $r=p$ is not sufficient: one can find the right eigenvalues but the eigenvectors are not correct (the convergence is slower for the eigenvectors than for the eigenvalues). $r=(p+q)/2$ seems a good choice. For $q$ one usually takes[Bat71]

$q=\min(p+8,2p)$

#### 8.3.2 Choice of the initial vectors

In order to choose the $q$ initial vectors, one proceeds as follows:

- First vector such that ${{x}_{1}}_{i}=\frac{{\mathrm{A}}_{ii}^{\sigma}}{{\mathrm{B}}_{ii}}$,
- For the other vectors from 2 to $(q-1)$

${x}_{2}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ \\ 1\\ \\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace --\text{ligne}{i}_{1}\text{}{x}_{q-1}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ 1\\ 0\\ \\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace \begin{array}{c}\\ --\text{ligne}{i}_{2}\\ \\ \end{array},...$

> where the $i$ are the indices corresponding to the successive smallest values of $\frac{{\mathrm{A}}_{ii}^{\sigma}}{{\mathrm{B}}_{ii}}$,

- Last vector ${\mathrm{x}}_{q}$, random vector.

## 9 Global QZ method

This solver is selected with `SOLVEUR_MODAL= _F(METHODE='QZ'))`

### 9.1 Introduction

Most modal methods resort to a spectral transformation of “shift and invert” type in order to facilitate the search for a part of the spectrum and to transform the initial GEP into a classical SEP (r5.01.01-3.7.1). This approach is clever but it implies numerical factorisations of dynamic matrices of the type ${{A}}_{\sigma}={({A}-\sigma {B})}^{-1} {B}$ (r5.01.01-3.7.2).

The result of this factorisation is only a computational intermediate used many times in the algorithmics of the modal solvers. Now these factorisations are necessarily tainted with numerical errors linked to the conditionings of the matrices and to the backward error (the backward error measures the propensity of the algorithm to transmit/amplify rounding errors) of the direct solver (cf. [Generalities on direct linear solvers and use of MUMPS](doc:r6.02.03)). Hence the idea, in order to build a robust modal method, of avoiding this type of mechanism and of resorting only to:

- Transformations which produce/propagate few rounding errors (called “backward stable”) of Householder or Givens type,
- Canonical matrices easy to handle (triangular, diagonal or Hessenberg) and/or very well conditioned (orthogonal in real or unitary in complex).

### 9.2 GEPs and the QZ method

This is the principle of the QZ method which treats the GEP directly.

$$
\begin{array}{c}
    \text{Trouver }(\lambda, u)\text{ tel que }\\
    (A-\lambda B)u=0
\end{array}
$$

rather than an SEP resulting from its modification *via* a spectral transformation. This method in fact seeks to build the following generalised Schur decomposition (counterpart of the standard Schur decomposition).

**Theorem 12 (Generalised Schur Decomposition)**

Let $A$ and $B$ be two complex square matrices; then there exist two unitary square matrices $Q$ and $Z$ such that ${Q}^{\ast}AZ=T$ and ${Q}^{\ast}BZ=S$ are upper triangular matrices. The eigenvalues of the GEP eq:eq-r5.01.01-probGEP are then easily computed thanks to the (complex) diagonal terms of the matrices $T$ and $S$

For the proof, see [GL89] pp. 396 and [MS73].

This theorem therefore indicates that, once the Generalised Schur Decomposition has been unearthed, solving the GEP amounts to solving the equivalent GEP (and much simpler because triangular)

$$
\begin{array}{c}
    \text{Trouver }(\lambda, v)\text{ tel que }\\
    (T-\lambda S)v=0
\end{array}
$$

They have the same spectrum, $\lambda (A,B)=\lambda (T,S)$, and one passes from the eigenvectors of one to those of the other, *via* the canonical transformation

$$
u = Zv
$$

On the other hand, the handling of pairs of the type $({\alpha}_{i},{\beta}_{i})=({T}_{ii},{S}_{ii})$, rather than directly an eigenvalue ${\lambda}_{i}:=\frac{{\alpha}_{i}}{{\beta}_{i}}$, is a numerical trick making it possible to apprehend better the complexity induced by the direct solution of a GEP. Indeed, depending for example on the rank of the matrix $B$, the spectrum can be:

- finite: $\lambda (A,B)=\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{1}\dots {\lambda}_{n}\right\rbrace$, with possibly eigenvalues of very large values $\pm \infty$,
- empty: ($\lambda (A,B)=\varnothing$),
- infinite: ($\lambda (A,B)=\setR \text{ ou } \setC$).

In the first two cases, the system is said to be “regular” as opposed to the last situation where it is said to be “singular”. To convince oneself of this, the examples below illustrate the three cases:

The representation of the solutions in $(\alpha, \beta )$ then makes it possible to manage these cases more cleanly. For example, the value $\beta \approx 0$ can be interpreted as an infinite eigenvalue $\lambda \approx \infty$ (probably not to be retained in the working spectrum!). If in addition $\alpha \approx 0$, one has an indeterminacy for computing the corresponding eigenvalue and this illustrates a singular or very poorly conditioned system.

In the case of the GEPs, this situation appears when one treats the eigenvalues associated with the blocked dofs and with those of Lagrange (r5.01.01-3.2.1). The decomposition $(\alpha, \beta )$ then supplies a framework for filtering these numerical artefacts from the physical spectrum truly sought:

### 9.3 The QZ method

In the general case, the algorithm of the QZ method can be broken down into three steps:

- *Via* Givens transformations (cf. appendix 3) or Householder 2x2, the two complex matrices $A$ and $B$ (resp. real) are decomposed in a form called “Generalised Upper Hessenberg”. That is to say that one determines two unitary (resp. orthogonal) square matrices ${U}_{1}$ and ${V}_{1}$ such that $A={U}_{1}H{V}_{1}^{\ast}$ and $B={U}_{1}R{V}_{1}^{\ast}$ (resp. $A={U}_{1}H{V}_{1}^{T}$ and $B={U}_{1}R{V}_{1}^{T}$) with $H$ upper Hessenberg and $R$ upper triangular. For example, with $A:=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}10& 1& 2\\ 1& 2& -1\\ 1& 1& 2\end{array}\right]\text{ et }B:=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}1& 2& 3\\ 4& 5& 6\\ 7& 8& 9\end{array}\right]$, one finds

- By generalising Francis's implicit double-shift algorithm (based on Householder transformations, cf. appendix 1), one finalises the transformation. One then unearths two unitary (resp. orthogonal) square matrices ${U}_{2}$ and ${V}_{2}$ such that $H={U}_{2}{TV}_{2}^{\ast}$ and $R={U}_{2}S{V}_{2}^{\ast}$ (resp. $H={U}_{2}T{V}_{2}^{T}$ and $R={U}_{2}S{V}_{2}^{T}$) with $T$ and $S$ upper triangular (resp. $T$ upper quasi-triangular). By grouping these two steps, one establishes the result sought, as the product of unitary transformations remains unitary,

- By using the diagonal terms of the triangular matrices obtained $T$ and $S$, one deduces (after filtering and a few validity tests) the physical eigenvalues of the vibratory problem modelled. The right and left generalised Schur vectors (column vectors of $Q$ and $Z$) make it possible to go back up to the eigenvectors of the equivalent GEP then to the original eigenvectors (*via* the transformation).

This QZ method is recommended when one seeks to obtain in a robust manner the whole spectrum of a GEP. However, it is very costly in CPU time $\vartheta (30{n}^{3})$ against $\vartheta (nm(m-p))$ for IRAM with $n$ the size of the problem, $p$ the number of eigenmodes desired and $m$ the size of the projection space. Moreover, it is very greedy in memory capacity because it requires the full storage of the matrices. Hence a memory complexity in $\vartheta ({n}^{2})$ against, for example, $\vartheta (2 np+{p}^{2})$ for IRAM. In short, this algorithm is to be reserved for small GEPs (fewer than ${10}^{3}$ degrees of freedom), rather dense, of which one wishes to obtain a reliable estimate of the spectrum. As for the QR algorithm in Lanczos/IRAM or for Jacobi with Bathe & Wilson, it is rather to be exploited as an elementary brick of an algorithmic process which will have (considerably) reduced the size of the initial problem.

For further information on the method, one may consult G.H.Golub[GL89] or the founding paper of C.B.Moler and G.W.Stewart[MS73] (1973)

### 9.4 Implementation

#### 9.4.1 LAPACK

Routines of the excellent linear algebra library LAPACK [Lap] propose dedicated routines (and drivers encapsulating them) for the solution of a GEP directly *via* a QZ. And this, for a wide panel of problems: SPD, real non-symmetric, complex Hermitian or arbitrary, single/double precision, full or band storage. On the other hand, the library proposes a whole panoply of routines for decomposing the solution, pre- and post-processing the data handled or even estimating the numerical errors which taint the solutions. It nevertheless remains very much linked to the full storage of the matrices; it works only In-Core and exploits no parallelism.

On the other hand, it does not seem that there exists a public-domain package implementing a method of QZ type more efficiently than LAPACK. It is therefore on this library that the QZ method available in [CALC_MODES](doc:u4.52.02) with `OPTION` among `BANDE`, `CENTRE`, `PLUS_*` or `TOUT` is based. Let us note that many other products have made the same choice of LAPACK and of its QZ: `eigenvalues` module of Python/linear algebra, functions `eig` and `qz` for Matlab, `spec` for Scilab, MSC/Nastran, ...

#### 9.4.2 Integration and post-verifications

In the implementation which has been retained, the GEPs with real modes (real symmetric matrices) are treated in real arithmetic *via* the routines `DGGEV` and `DGGEVX`. The first routine is to be used during a standard run (`TYPE_QZ=’QZ_SIMPLE’`, default value). The second (`TYPE_QZ=’QZ_EQUI’`), more sophisticated, is to be reserved for difficult solutions. In particular, it balances and permutes the terms of the input matrices in order to improve the spectral conditionings and therefore to decrease the numerical error on the solutions. Sometimes this pre-processing can be prejudicial by excessively increasing terms close to the machine precision, thus falsifying the results. For this reason, this option is not activated by default.

In the very particular case of a real symmetric GEP with matrix $B$, in addition, positive definite (for example a dynamic computation without hysteretic damping with blocking boundary conditions modelled only by elimination without recourse to the double Lagrange), recourse to the LAPACK driver `DSYGV` is more efficient than the `DGGEV/X`. It must be activated explicitly *via* the keyword `TYPE_QZ=’QZ_QR’`. For certain situations (buckling, double Lagrange, complex or non-symmetric matrix, linearised QEP), the non-SPD character of $B$ can be detected *a priori*. A fatal error then stops the computation if the value `QZ_EQUI` has been chosen all the same.

In the third and last case of a GEP with complex modes (complex symmetric matrices as in the case of hysteretic damping and/or real non-symmetric), the solution is carried out directly in complex arithmetic *via* the LAPACK drivers `ZGGEV` and `ZGGEVX`. As in real, the first is the default standard mode (activated by `TYPE_QZ=’QZ_SIMPLE’`) and the second, the expert mode (`TYPE_QZ=’QZ_EQUI’`).

At the end of the modal analysis, QZ returns pairs $({\alpha}_{i},{\beta}_{i})$ and their associated eigenvector ${u}_{i}$, which one is going to sort and filter according to the following recommendations:

- Mechanical verifications: If $\vert {\beta}_{i}\vert$ is close to the machine precision, the mode is not retained (it doubtless corresponds to a blocked dof or to a blocking Lagrange) and an alarm message is issued (if `INFO=2`).

Once all the sorts have been carried out, one makes sure that the number of physically acceptable modes (cf. formula (9.1-6)) does indeed correspond to the number of active dofs ${n}_{\text{ddl}-\text{actifs}}$. The latter is determined in the initialisation phase and is independent of the modal solver (r5.01.01-3.2.1). If the GEP has real modes, the non-verification of this criterion entails a fatal error. In the other cases, it is only reported by a dedicated display. It is then not necessarily synonymous with malfunctions. Everything depends on the subsequent sorting of the complex conjugate eigenvalues!

- Numerical verifications: If $\vert {\alpha}_{i}\vert \ge \Vert A \Vert$ or $\vert {\beta}_{i}\vert \ge \Vert B \Vert$, one does not retain the mode and an alarm message is issued.

In the case of a real symmetric GEP (with `TYPE_QZ=’QZ_SIMPLE’` or `TYPE_QZ='QZ_EQUI’`), one moreover verifies that all the eigenvalues have a very small imaginary part (lower than the value ${f}_{\text{corrig}}$ supplied by `SEUIL_FREQ`, default value equal to $10^{-2}$). Otherwise, an alarm is issued.

In the case of a real symmetric positive definite GEP treated *via* `QZ_QR`, one excludes the eigenvalues lower than $-2{f}_{\text{corrig}}$ and greater than $0.5 \times {10}^{308}$ (0.5*maximum value representable on the machine). These arbitrary values result from numerical experiments on modal test cases of the verification base. When one finds an eigenvalue outside this band, an alarm is issued if `INFO=2`.

Then, once the modes coming from QZ have been filtered, they are going to be sorted and reordered according to the user's *desiderata*:

- `CALC_FREQ` with `OPTION='BANDE’`: option licit only with a GEP with real modes. One selects the eigenvalues situated in the frequency band desired by the user (keyword `FREQ` or `CHAR_CRIT`).
- `CALC_FREQ` with `OPTION=’PLUS_PETITE’`: one retains the eigenvalue smallest in modulus.
- `CALC_FREQ` with `OPTION=’CENTRE’`: one selects the eigenvalues closest in modulus to the shift parameterised by the keyword `FREQ` or `CHAR_CRIT`. One retains `NMAX_FREQ` of them.
- `CALC_FREQ` with `OPTION=’TOUTE’`: one keeps all the licit eigenvalues coming from the previous filters.

Finally, as for all the modal solvers of the code (cf. r5.01.01-3.7.2), the modes retained will undergo two last verifications:

- Test on the norm of the residual of the mode $(\lambda, u)$.
- In the standard case of a GEP with real modes, Sturm test for counting eigenvalues in the frequency band *ad hoc*.

### 9.5 Field of use

This solver can be used for a GEP with arbitrary real matrices (symmetric or not) or with matrix $A$ complex and $B$ real symmetric.

The user can specify the class of membership of the analysis (dynamics or buckling) by initialising the keyword `TYPE_RESU`. Depending on this value, one fills in the vector `FREQ` or `CHAR_CRIT`.

### 9.6 Display in the message file

In the message file are mentioned the pieces of information relating to the method chosen (here QZ), to its variant (here `QZ_SIMPLE`) and to the modes retained. In the most common case of a GEP with real eigenmodes, one specifies the list of frequencies ${f}_{i}$ retained (`FREQUENCE`) and their error norm (`NORME D’ERREUR`).

For a GEP with complex modes, unlike the QEPs one does not filter the conjugate eigenvalues. It keeps them all and displays them in increasing order of real part. Thus, the columns `FREQUENCE` and `AMORTISSEMENT` group, respectively, ${f}_{i}=\frac{{\lvert}{\lambda}_{i}{\rvert}}{2\pi}$ and ${\xi}_{i}=\frac{\text{Im}({\lambda}_{i})}{{ \lvert}{\lambda}_{i}{\rvert}}$.

## 10 Parallelism and high-performance computing

### 10.1 Integrated operators

**The operators** CALC_MODES **and** INFO_MODE **can benefit from a first level of parallelism: that intrinsic to the linear solver MUMPS.**

However, the parallel efficiency of MUMPS in a modal analysis is more limited than for other types of analyses. One observes, in general, a parallel efficiency in time of the order of 0.2 to 0.3 and this, for a small range of processors: from 2 to 16. Beyond that one no longer gains anything.

This is explained in particular by:

- the quasi-singularity of certain working dynamic matrices,
- a very unfavourable ratio “number of forward-back substitutions/number of factorisations”,
- the significant cost of the analysis phase compared with that of the factorisation,
- a very unfavourable ratio “time/memory cost of the linear solver”/“time/memory cost of the modal solver”.

For further technical and functional information, one can consult the documentations [R6.02.03](doc:r6.02.03), [U4.50.01](doc:u4.50.01) and [U2.08.03/06].

In order to progress in terms of performance, one proposes to decompose the initial computation into more high-performing and more accurate sub-computations: this is the object of one of the functionalities of the operator CALC_MODES with OPTION='BANDE' and a list of n>2 values given under CALC_FREQ=_F(FREQ), detailed in the following paragraph. Moreover, this algorithmic rewriting of the problem exhibits two levels of parallelism that are more relevant and more efficient for **“boosting” the modal computations of Code_Aster**.

### 10.2 Operator CALC_MODES, option 'BANDE' split into sub-bands

**In order to treat large modal problems efficiently** (in mesh size and/or in number of modes sought), one advises the use of the operator **CALC_MODES** with the option 'BANDE' split into sub-bands. It decomposes the modal analysis of a standard GEP (symmetric and real) into a succession of independent sub-computations, less costly, more robust and more accurate.

Even in sequential mode, the **gains can be appreciable**: factors of 2 to 5 in time, 2 or 3 in peak RAM and 10 to 104 on the mean error of the modes.

Moreover, **its multi-level parallelism,** by reserving about sixty processors, **can provide additional gains** of the order of 20 in time and 2 in peak RAM (cf. tables 10-1). And this, without loss of accuracy, nor restriction of field of use and with the same numerical behaviour.

**The principle of** **CALC_MODES** [U4.52.02](doc:u4.52.02) with the option 'BANDE' split into sub-bands rests on the fact that the computational and memory costs of the modal algorithms depend more than linearly on the number of modes sought. Therefore, as for domain decomposition, one is going to decompose the search for hundreds of modes into packets of more reasonable size.

A packet of the order of forty modes seems to be an empirical optimum in sequential mode. In parallel, one can continue to improve the performances by going down to about fifteen.

The example of figure 10-2 thus illustrates a global CALC_MODES computation in the band

$[{\mathit{freq}}_{min},{\mathit{freq}}_{max}]$

which is often advantageously replaced by ten CALC_MODES computations targeted on equivalent contiguous sub-bands

$[{\mathit{freq}}_{1}={\mathit{freq}}_{min},{\mathit{freq}}_{2}],[{\mathit{freq}}_{2,}{\mathit{freq}}_{3}],\mathrm{...}[{\mathit{freq}}_{10,}{\mathit{freq}}_{11}={\mathit{freq}}_{max}]$ .

On the other hand, this type of decomposition makes it possible to:

- reduce the robustness problems,
- improve and homogenise the modal errors.

In practice, **this modal operator dedicated to HPC** is broken down into four main steps:

1. **Modal pre-calibration** (*via* INFO_MODE) of the sub-bands parameterised by the user:

Hence potentially, a loop of nb_freq independent computations on each modal position of frequencies (cf. [R5.01.04](doc:r5.01.04)).

1. **Effective modal computation** (*via* CALC_MODES + OPTION='BANDE' + CALC_FREQ=_F(TABLE_FREQ)) of the modes contained in each non-empty sub-band (by pooling the modal calibrations of step no. 1):

Hence potentially, a loop of nb_sbande_nonvide < nb_freq independent computations.

1. **Post-verification** with a Sturm test on the extreme bounds of the modes computed (*via* INFO_MODE):

Hence potentially, a loop of 2 independent computations.

1. **Post-processings** on all the modes obtained: normalisation (*via* NORM_MODE) and filtering (*via* EXTR_MODE).

## 12 Appendix 1. Generalities on the QR algorithm

### 12.1 Principle

Algorithms of QR type were sensed by H.Rutishauser(1958) and formalised concurrently by J.C.Francis and V.N.Kublanovskaya(1961). This fundamental method is often involved in the other approaches better adapted to treating problems of large size (in particular the projection methods).

$\begin{array}{c}\text{Pour}k=1,...\text{faire}\\ {\mathrm{H}}_{k}={\mathrm{Q}}_{k}{\mathrm{R}}_{k}(\text{factorisation}\text{QR}),\\ {\mathrm{H}}_{k+1}={\mathrm{R}}_{k}{\mathrm{Q}}_{k},\\ \text{Fin boucle}.\end{array}$

**Algorithm 1.1.** QR **theoretical.**

The **process leads iteratively** towards a matrix ${\mathrm{H}}_{k}$ **upper triangular** (or block triangular) whose diagonal terms are the eigenvalues of the initial operator $\mathrm{H}={\mathrm{H}}_{1}$. The notation $H$ is in fact not innocent, because one has every interest in orthogonally transforming beforehand (so as to modify only the form of the shifted operator and not its spectrum) the working operator $A$ into upper Hessenberg form, that is in real arithmetic

${\mathrm{H}}_{1}={\mathrm{Q}}_{{0}_{}}^{\mathrm{T}}{\text{AQ}}_{0}$

This can be carried out *via* various orthogonal transformations (Householder, Givens, Gram‑Schmidt...) and their cost (of the order of $O({\mathrm{10n}}^{3}/3)$) is negligible compared with the gain that they make it possible to achieve at each iteration of the global process: $O({n}^{2})$ (with Householder or Fast‑Givens) against $O({n}^{3})$. This gain of an order of magnitude can even be improved when the operator is symmetric tridiagonal (this is the case of Lanczos with a genuine inner product): $O(\mathrm{20n})$.

The convergence towards a simple triangular matrix is carried out only if all the eigenvalues have distinct moduli and if the initial matrix is not “pathologically” too poor in the eigen directions. The convergence of the $i$ th mode (arranged classically in decreasing order of modulus) is then carried out in:

$\underset{\mathrm{j¹i}}{\max}\frac{\mid {\lambda}_{j}\mid }{\mid {\lambda}_{i}\mid }$

which can prove very slow if no complementary process is implemented.

Notes:

- *The determination of the spectrum of the Rayleigh matrix with the Newmann & Pipano variant is carried out via a simple QR (or a* *QL in the symmetric case) of this type (with beforehand a balancing procedure). The only parameter accessible by the user is the maximum number of admissible iterations* *NMAX_ITER_QR.*
- *For IRAM, this computation is carried out via a* *QR method with explicit double shift whereas the polynomial filters managing the restarts use a* *QR with implicit double shift. By precaution, no parameter is accessible for the user in Code_Aster!*
- *One must not confuse the method, the* *QR algorithm, and one of its conceptual tools, the* *QR factorisation.*
- This class of algorithm is very much used for determining the complete spectrum of an operator, because it is very robust (it is the reference in this field). However it is very greedy in memory space which makes its use prohibitive on large systems.
- The field of application of the QR algorithm is much more general than that of Jacobi (it is the second standard algorithm supplying the whole spectrum of an operator provided that one is ready to store it entirely) which is limited to Hermitian matrices.

In order to accelerate the convergence of the simple algorithm which can be very slow (in the presence of clusters for example) a multitude of variants, based on the choice of shifts answering certain criteria, have seen the light of day.

### 12.2 The shift strategy

This strategy consists in **artificially bringing about a deflation phenomenon** (cf. §4.1, §6.4) within the working matrix. This offers the triple advantage:

- of being able to **isolate a real eigenvalue** or even two complex conjugate eigenvalues,
- while **reducing the size** of the problem to be treated,
- and while **accelerating the convergence**.

In its version with explicit simple shift, the method is then rewritten in the following form:

$\begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{c}\text{Pour k}=1,...\text{faire}\\ \text{Choisir le shift}\mu ,\\ {\mathrm{S}}_{k}={\mathrm{H}}_{k}-\mu \mathrm{I},\end{array}\\ {\mathrm{S}}_{k}={\mathrm{Q}}_{k}{\mathrm{R}}_{k}(\text{factorisation}\text{QR}),\\ {\mathrm{H}}_{\text{k+}1}={\mathrm{R}}_{k}{\mathrm{Q}}_{k}+\mu \mathrm{I},\\ \text{Fin boucle}.\end{array}$

**Algorithm 1.2.** QR **with explicit simple shift.**

Note:

- This process generalises intuitively to several shifts. One then builds, for each global iteration $k$, as many auxiliary matrices ${\mathrm{S}}_{k}^{i}$ as there are shifts ${\mu}_{i}$.

The convergence of the process is greatly improved in the sense that the sub-diagonal terms vanish asymptotically in

$\underset{j\ne i}{\max}\frac{\mid {\lambda}_{j}-\mu \mid }{\mid {\lambda}_{i}-\mu \mid }$

In theory, if this shift $\mu$ is an eigenvalue of the problem, then the deflation is exact. In practice, the rounding effects perturb this phenomenon; this is what is called the **direct instability property of the algorithm**. The main difficulty resides in the choice of the shift(s). On the other hand, one does not keep the same shift for all the iterations. One must change it when it is associated with a converged eigenvalue. Indeed, it will have numerically caused its eviction from the working spectrum by bringing about a deflation at the previous iteration.

Since the 1960s a **whole zoology of shifts has developed**. While the simplest uses the last diagonal term ${\mathrm{H}}_{n,n}$ that of **J.H. Wilkinson** [WR71] consists in determining analytically the eigenvalue $\mu$ of the diagonal block

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\mathrm{H}}_{n-1,n-1}& {\mathrm{H}}_{n-1,n}\\ {\mathrm{H}}_{n,n-1}& {\mathrm{H}}_{n,n}\end{array}\right]$

closest to this term. This technique makes it possible to obtain a quadratic or even cubic convergence (in the symmetric case) and it proves particularly efficient for capturing double modes or distinct eigenvalues of the same modulus. However this strategy can prove ineffective in the presence of complex conjugate eigenmodes.

The same author then proposed a **variant including a double shift** corresponding to the two complex eigenvalues ${\mu}_{1}$ and ${\mu}_{2}$ (determined beforehand) of the block in question. But besides the numerical difficulties in curbing the appearance of invasive complex components (which in theory have no reason to be), one has on the other hand a great deal of difficulty in preserving the “upper Hessenberg” character of the working matrix. At best, this slows the convergence of the QR algorithm; at worst, it completely falsifies its operation.

In order to remedy these numerical disadvantages, J.C.Francis developed a version with **implicit double shift**. It is very well explained in [GL89](pp377-81) and one will content oneself here with summarising it.

In order to minimise the rounding effects, one would have to constitute directly the auxiliary matrix ${\mathrm{S}}_{k}$ resulting from the simultaneous application of the two shifts ${\mu}_{1}$ and ${\mu}_{2}$

${\mathrm{S}}_{k}={\mathrm{H}}_{k}^{2}-({\mu}_{1}+{\mu}_{2}){\mathrm{H}}_{k}+({\mu}_{1}{\mu}_{2})\mathrm{I}$

before factorising it in QR form and building the new iterate ${\mathrm{H}}_{k+1}$

$\begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{S}}_{k}={\mathrm{Q}}_{k}{\mathrm{R}}_{k},\\ {\mathrm{H}}_{k+1}={\mathrm{Q}}_{k}^{T}{\mathrm{H}}_{k}{\mathrm{Q}}_{k}.\end{array}$

But the sole cost of the initial assembly (of the order of $O({n}^{3})$) makes the tactic inoperative. This variant, based on the implicit $Q$ theorem, consists in applying to the working matrix particular Householder transformations making it possible to recover a Hessenberg matrix “essentially” equal to ${\mathrm{H}}_{k+1}$, that is to say of the type:

. ${\tilde{\mathrm{H}}}_{k+1}={\mathrm{E}}^{-1}{\mathrm{H}}_{k+1}\mathrm{E}\text{avec}\mathrm{E}\text{=diag}(\pm 1,...\pm 1)$

The **working matrix** thus **preserves**, at **least cost**, **its particular structure and its spectrum**.

All these variants are in fact very sensitive to the balancing techniques implemented in order to precondition the initial operator. The following paragraph will summarise this technique of “scaling” (“balancing” for English speakers) of the terms of the working matrix which is very much used in modal computation.

### 12.3 Balancing

It is a matter of **attenuating the rounding effects** by curbing the **range of expansion of the terms of the working operator**, that is to say of avoiding that they become too small or, on the contrary, too large. On this subject, E.E.Osborne [Osb60] (1960) noted that generally the error on the computation of the eigen elements of $A$ is of the order of $\varepsilon {\parallel A\parallel }_{2}$ where $\varepsilon$ is the machine precision. He then proposed to transform the initial matrix into a matrix

$\tilde{\mathrm{A}}={\mathrm{D}}^{\text{-1}}\mathrm{A}\mathrm{D}$ (with $D$ a diagonal matrix),

such that:

${\mathrm{\parallel }\tilde{\mathrm{A}}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}\ll {\mathrm{\parallel }\mathrm{A}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}$

In fact one computes iteratively a succession of matrices

${\mathrm{A}}_{k}={\mathrm{D}}_{k-1}^{\text{-1}}{\mathrm{A}}_{k-1}{\mathrm{D}}_{k-1}$

such that:

${\mathrm{A}}_{k}\underset{k\to \infty }{\to }{\mathrm{A}}_{f}$

satisfying ${\mathrm{\parallel }{\mathrm{a}}^{i}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}={\mathrm{\parallel }{\mathrm{a}}_{i}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{2}$ with ${\mathrm{a}}^{i}$ and ${\mathrm{a}}_{i}$, respectively, the $i$ th column and row of ${\mathrm{A}}_{f}$.

Notes:

- This technique has been generalised to any matrix norm induced by a discrete norm of [33]_ ${l}^{\mathrm{q.}}$
- Its use is very widespread in scientific computation and in particular among the direct solvers of systems of linear equations.

The computation base of the computer, denoted $\beta$, intervenes in the determination of the terms of the matrices ${\mathrm{D}}_{k}$. In order to minimise the rounding errors, one chooses the elements of ${\mathrm{D}}_{k}$ so that they are powers of this base.

Let ${\mathrm{R}}_{k}$ and ${\mathrm{C}}_{k}$ be the $p$ norms (in practice one often takes $p=2$), respectively of row and column $i$ of the matrix ${A}_{k-1}$ ($i$ is chosen in such a way that $i-1\equiv k-1[n]$). Assuming that ${\mathrm{R}}_{k}{\mathrm{C}}_{k}\mathrm{\ne }0$, one then shows that there exists a unique signed integer $\sigma$ such that:

${\beta}^{2\sigma -1}<\frac{{\mathrm{R}}_{k}}{{\mathrm{C}}_{k}}\le {\beta}^{2\sigma +1}$

Let $f={\beta}^{\sigma}$; one defines the matrix $\stackrel{ˉ}{{D}_{k}}$ such that:

${\stackrel{ˉ}{\mathrm{D}}}_{k}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{I}+(f-1){\mathrm{e}}_{i}{\mathrm{e}}_{i}^{T}& \text{si}{({\mathrm{C}}_{k}f)}^{p}+{({R}_{k}/f)}^{p}<\gamma ({\mathrm{C}}_{k}^{p}+{\mathrm{R}}_{k}^{p})\\ \mathrm{I}& \mathit{sinon}\end{array}$

where $0<\gamma \le 1$ is a constant and ${\mathrm{e}}_{i}$ the $i$ th canonical vector. One then builds iteratively:

$\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{\mathrm{D}}_{k}={\stackrel{ˉ}{\mathrm{D}}}_{k}{\mathrm{D}}_{k-1},\\ {\mathrm{A}}_{k}={\stackrel{ˉ}{\mathrm{D}}}_{k}^{\text{-1}}{\mathrm{A}}_{k-1}{\stackrel{ˉ}{\mathrm{D}}}_{k}\end{array}$

by initialising ${\mathrm{D}}_{0}=\mathrm{I}$. The process stops as soon as ${\stackrel{ˉ}{\mathrm{D}}}_{k}=\mathrm{I}$.

Notes:

- Before carrying out the balancing, a search for the isolated eigenvalues is carried out by detecting the presence of quasi-zero rows and columns (all the terms are zero, except the one placed on the diagonal). When some exist one can, by carrying out suitable permutations, put the working matrix into the following block form:

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Object_427.svg)

- It is then necessary to carry out the balancing only on the central block $X$ because the diagonal terms of the two upper triangular matrices are eigenvalues of the working matrix.
- In Code_Aster, one has chosen $p=1$ and $\gamma =0.95$ (cf. [PR71]).
- Before applying the QR method, one begins by balancing the working matrix and then one transforms it into upper Hessenberg form. Once the QR computation has been carried out, one goes back up from the Ritz vectors to the eigenvectors via the inverse of the orthogonal transformations due to the putting into Hessenberg form and to the balancing. It is this process which is put in place both in Lanczos and in IRAM. However besides the fact that it requires the storage of these orthogonal matrices, it is also and above all extremely sensitive to rounding effects. Thus, it would be greatly preferable to estimate the eigenvectors via an inverse power method initialised by the eigenvalues unearthed.

### 12.4 The QL method

The QL factorisation of a matrix $A$ consists in orthonormalising its column vectors by beginning with the last (unlike the QR algorithm which begins with the first) thus giving a regular lower triangular matrix $L$. One moreover shows that the QL algorithm applied to the invertible operator $A$ is equivalent to the QR algorithm applied to ${A}^{\text{-*}}$ (conjugate transpose matrix of the inverse of $A$).

The method put in place in *Code_Aster* is identical to the simple shift method of J.H.Wilkinson seen previously by adapting the search for this shift to the uppermost $2\times 2$ minor.

Note:

- Unlike QR which captures the eigenvalues in increasing order of modulus, one here obtains preferentially the dominant modes, then the others, in decreasing order of modulus.

The functional space:math:{l}^{q} is the set of complex sequences ${({u}_{n})}_{n}`telles que :math:`{\sum}_{n}{\mid {u}_{n}\mid }^{q}<\infty$.

## 13 Appendix 2. Gram-Schmidt orthogonalisation

### 13.1 Introduction

One has seen on several occasions in this document that the **quality of orthogonalisation** of a **family of vectors** is **crucial** for the good **course of the algorithms** and the **quality of the modes** obtained. This task is moreover to be carried out permanently, hence the importance of a fast algorithm.

The simple orthonormalisation algorithms are deduced from the classical Gram-Schmidt process but they are often “conditionally stable” (the quality of their work depends on the matrix conditioning of the family to be orthonormalised). This lack of robustness can prove problematic for treating particularly poorly conditioned situations. One then prefers more expensive but robust algorithms, based on projections or rotations: the spectral transformations of Householder and of Givens.

In practice, for the implementation of the IRA method in *Code_Aster*, we have retained an **iterative version of the Gram-Schmidt process** (the IGSM of Kahan-Parlett[Par80]). It achieves a **good compromise between robustness and computational complexity** since it is unconditionally stable and makes it possible to orthogonalise to near machine precision, in, at most, twice as much time as a classical Gram-Schmidt (GS).

In the following paragraphs we shall detail the operation of the basic algorithm, as well as that of these two main variants. The first has been put in place in CALC_MODES with OPTION among ['PROCHE','AJUSTE','SEPARE'] and the second is used in CALC_MODES with OPTION among ['BANDE','CENTRE','PLUS_*','TOUT']. A very striking comparison of these methods is set out in [Vau00] (pp33-36) from a very simple example.

### 13.2 Gram-Schmidt algorithm (GS)

Given $k$ **independent vectors** of ${ℝ}^{n}$, ${({\mathrm{x}}_{i})}_{i=1,k}$ one wishes to obtain $k$ **orthonormal vectors** (with respect to an arbitrary inner product) ${({\mathrm{y}}_{i})}_{i=1,k}$ of the space which they generate. In other words, one wishes to obtain another orthonormal family generating the same space. The classical Gram-Schmidt orthonormalisation process is the following:

$\begin{array}{c}\text{Pour}\text{i =}1,k\text{faire}\\ \text{Pour}\text{j =}1,i\text{-1 faire}\\ \text{calcul de}{r}_{ji}=({\mathrm{y}}_{j},{\mathrm{x}}_{i})\\ \text{Fin boucle.}\\ \text{calcul de}{\stackrel{ˆ}{\mathrm{y}}}_{i}={\mathrm{x}}_{i}-\sum_{j=1,i-1}{r}_{ji}{\mathrm{y}}_{j},\\ \text{calcul de}{r}_{ii}=\mathrm{\parallel }{\stackrel{ˆ}{\mathrm{y}}}_{i}\mathrm{\parallel },\\ \text{calcul de}{\mathrm{y}}_{i}=\frac{{\stackrel{ˆ}{\mathrm{y}}}_{i}}{{r}_{ii}}\\ \text{Fin boucle}.\end{array}$

**Algorithm 2.1. Gram-Schmidt algorithm (GS).**

This process is simple but very unstable because of rounding errors, which has the effect of producing non-orthogonal vectors. In particular when the initial vectors are almost dependent this creates significant gaps in magnitude in the second step of the process

${\stackrel{ˆ}{\mathrm{y}}}_{i}={\mathrm{x}}_{i}-\sum_{j=1,i-1}{r}_{ij}{\mathrm{y}}_{j}$

From a numerical point of view, the management of these gaps is very difficult.

Note:

- Denoting by $Q$ the matrix generated by the ${({\mathrm{y}}_{i})}_{i=1,k}$, one has therefore explicitly built a QR factorisation of the initial matrix $X$ linked to the ${({\mathrm{x}}_{i})}_{i=1,k}$. This is in fact the aim of any orthogonalisation process.

### 13.3 Modified Gram-Schmidt algorithm (GSM)

In order to evacuate these numerical instabilities, one reorganises the previous algorithm. Mathematically equivalent to the previous process, the latter is then much more robust because it avoids the significant gaps in magnitude between the vectors handled in the algorithm.

In the initial process, the orthogonal vectors ${\mathrm{y}}_{i}$ are obtained without taking into account the $i-1$ previous orthogonalisations. With the Modified Gram-Schmidt, one **orthogonalises more progressively by taking into account the previous alterations** according to the process below.

$\begin{array}{c}\text{Pour}\text{i =}1,k\text{faire}\\ \text{}{\stackrel{ˆ}{\mathrm{y}}}_{i}={\mathrm{x}}_{i}\\ \text{Pour}\text{j =}1,i\text{-1 faire}\\ \text{calcul de}{r}_{ji}=({\mathrm{y}}_{j},{\mathrm{x}}_{i}),\\ \text{calcul de}{\stackrel{ˆ}{\mathrm{y}}}_{i}={\mathrm{x}}_{i}-{r}_{ji}{\mathrm{y}}_{j},\\ \text{Fin boucle.}\\ \text{calcul de}{r}_{ii}=\mathrm{\parallel }{\stackrel{ˆ}{\mathrm{y}}}_{i}\mathrm{\parallel },\\ \text{calcul de}{\mathrm{y}}_{i}=\frac{{\stackrel{ˆ}{\mathrm{y}}}_{i}}{{r}_{ii}}\\ \text{Fin boucle}.\end{array}$

**Algorithm 2.2. Modified Gram-Schmidt algorithm (GSM).**

The orthonormality of the basis vectors is much better with this process and it can even be obtained to machine precision and to within a constant (depending on the conditioning of $X$). However, in order to treat particularly poorly conditioned situations, this “conditional” stability can quickly prove problematic, hence recourse to the following iterative algorithm.

Note:

- GSM is twice as efficient as a Householder method for obtaining a QR factorisation of the initial matrix $X$. It requires only $O({\mathrm{2nk}}^{2})$ operations (with $n$ the number of rows of the matrix).

### 13.4 Iterative Gram-Schmidt algorithm (IGSM)

In order nevertheless to **make sure** of the **orthogonality to near machine precision**, one recommends carrying out a **second orthogonalisation**. And if, at the end of the latter, the orthogonality is not assured it is no longer worth starting again; the quantities handled are then certainly very close and their gaps oscillate around zero. This approach is based on a theoretical analysis due to W.Kahan and taken up by B.N.Parlett[Par80] (cf. pp105-110).

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Object_27.svg)

**Algorithm 2.3. Iterative Gram-Schmidt algorithm of Kahan-Parlett type (IGSM).**

When using IRAM in *Code_Aster*, the criterion $\alpha$ is parameterised by the keyword PARA_ORTHO_SOREN (cf. §7.5). One shows that its interval of validity is $[1.2\varepsilon ,0.83-\varepsilon ]$ with $\varepsilon$ the machine precision and one generally assigns it the value 0.717 (by default).

Notes:

- The larger the value of the parameter , the less the reorthogonalisation is triggered, but this affects the quality of the process.
- Unlike the “home-grown” version developed for Lanczos, here the stopping criteria concentrate on the norms of the orthogonalised vectors rather than on the inter-vector inner products which have more of a tendency to reflect the rounding effects. This, together with the suppression of the iterations greater than two, hence useless, can explain the extra efficiency of the Kahan-Parlett version.
- According to D.C.Sorensen the paternity of this method is rather to be attributed to J.Daniel (paper of 1976 submitted to Mathematics of Computation, vol.30, pp772-795).

## 14 Appendix 3. Jacobi method

### 14.1 Principle

The Jacobi method[LT86] makes it possible to compute all the eigenvalues of a generalised problem whose matrices are positive definite and symmetric (the matrices obtained at each iteration by the Bathe & Wilson method satisfy these properties; cf. § 8). It consists in transforming the matrices $A$ and $B$ of the GEP $\mathrm{A}\mathrm{u}=\lambda \mathrm{B}\mathrm{u}$ into diagonal matrices, by using successively similar orthogonal transformations (Givens rotation matrices). The process can be schematised in the following manner:

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Object_437.svg)

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Object_438.svg)

**Algorithm 3.1. Jacobi process**

The eigenvalues are given by $\lambda ={\mathrm{A}}^{d}{({\mathrm{B}}^{d})}^{\text{-1}}$ that is $\lambda =\frac{{\mathrm{A}}_{ii}^{d}}{{\mathrm{B}}_{ii}^{d}}$ and the matrix of the eigenvectors satisfies:

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.01/Object_441.svg)

Each matrix ${\mathrm{Q}}_{k}$ is chosen so that a diagonal and non-zero term $(i,j)$ of ${\mathrm{A}}_{k}$ or of ${\mathrm{B}}_{k}$ is zero after the transformation.

### 14.2 Some choices

In this algorithm, one realises that the important points are the following:

- How to choose the terms to be cancelled?
- How to measure the diagonal character of the matrices when $k$ tends towards infinity?
- How to measure the convergence?

#### 14.2.1 Off-diagonal terms to be cancelled

For the choice of the terms to be cancelled, several methods exist:

- The first consists, at each step $k$, in choosing the largest off-diagonal element in modulus of the matrix ${\mathrm{A}}_{k}$ or ${\mathrm{B}}_{k}$ and in cancelling it by carrying out a rotation. This choice assures the convergence of the Jacobi method but is relatively expensive (search for the maximum element).
- The second solution consists in cancelling successively all the off-diagonal elements of these matrices by following the natural order ${\mathrm{A}}_{13}^{k},\mathrm{....},{\mathrm{A}}_{\mathrm{1n}}^{k},{\mathrm{A}}_{23}^{k},\mathrm{....}$. When one arrives at ${\mathrm{A}}_{n-1,n}^{k}$, one starts the cycle again. This method converges slowly.

A variant of this method consists, while following the natural order of the terms, in cancelling only those which are greater than a given precision ${\varepsilon}_{k}$. At the end of a cycle, one decreases the value of this criterion and one starts again. It is this strategy which is used in *Code_Aster*.

#### 14.2.2 Convergence test

In order to test the convergence and the diagonal character of the matrices, one proceeds thus. One verifies that all the coupling factors defined by:

${f}_{{\mathrm{A}}_{ij}}=\frac{∣{\mathrm{A}}_{ij}∣}{\sqrt{∣{\mathrm{A}}_{ii}{\mathrm{A}}_{jj}∣}}$ ${f}_{{\mathrm{B}}_{ij}}=\frac{∣{\mathrm{B}}_{ij}∣}{\sqrt{∣{\mathrm{B}}_{ii}{\mathrm{B}}_{jj}∣}}$ $i\ne j$

are lower than a given precision (diagonal character of the matrices). One also checks the convergence of the eigenvalues via the indicator

${f}_{\lambda}=\underset{i}{\max}\frac{∣{\lambda}_{i}^{k}-{\lambda}_{i}^{k-1}∣}{{\lambda}_{i}^{k-1}}$

so that it remains lower than a given precision ${\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{jaco}}$.

#### 14.2.4 Givens rotation matrix

One seeks at each step to cancel the terms located in position $i$ and $j$ $(i\ne j)$ of the matrices ${A}^{k,l}$ and ${B}^{k,l}$ by multiplying them by a rotation matrix which has the following form:

${\mathrm{G}}_{ll}=1l=1,n;{\mathrm{G}}_{ij}=a;{\mathrm{G}}_{ji}=b$

the other terms being zero. One wishes to have ${\mathrm{A}}_{ij}^{k,l+1}={\mathrm{B}}_{ij}^{k,l+1}=0$ which leads to:

$\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}a{\mathrm{A}}_{ii}^{k,l}+(1+\mathit{ab}){\mathrm{A}}_{ij}^{k,l}+b{\mathrm{A}}_{jj}^{k,l}=0\\ a{\mathrm{B}}_{ii}^{k,l}+(1+\mathit{ab}){\mathrm{B}}_{ij}^{k,l}+b{\mathrm{B}}_{jj}^{k,l}=0\end{array}$

because ${\mathrm{A}}^{k,l+1}={\mathrm{G}}^{T}{\mathrm{A}}^{k,l}\mathrm{G}$ and ${\mathrm{B}}^{k,l+1}={\mathrm{G}}^{T}{\mathrm{B}}^{k,l}\mathrm{G}$. If the two equations are proportional one has:

$a=0$ and $b=\frac{-{\mathrm{A}}_{ij}^{k,l}}{{\mathrm{A}}_{jj}^{k,l}}$

otherwise:

$a=\frac{{c}_{2}}{d}b=\frac{-{c}_{1}}{d}$

with:

${c}_{1}={\mathrm{A}}_{ii}^{k,l}{\mathrm{B}}_{ij}^{k,l}-{\mathrm{B}}_{ii}^{k,l}{\mathrm{A}}_{jj}^{k,l}$ ${c}_{2}={\mathrm{A}}_{jj}^{k,l}{\mathrm{B}}_{ij}^{k,l}-{\mathrm{B}}_{jj}^{k,l}{\mathrm{A}}_{ij}^{k,l}$

${c}_{3}={\mathrm{A}}_{ii}^{k,l}{\mathrm{B}}_{jj}^{k,l}-{\mathrm{B}}_{ii}^{k,l}{\mathrm{A}}_{jj}^{k,l}$ $d=\frac{{c}_{3}}{2}+\mathrm{sign}({c}_{3})\sqrt{{(\frac{{c}_{3}}{2})}^{2}+{c}_{1}{c}_{2}}$

Note:

- If $d=0$ then one is in the proportional case.
