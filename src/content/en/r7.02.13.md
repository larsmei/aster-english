# r7.02.13 Crack propagation algorithms with X-FEM

**Summary:**

This document presents the crack propagation algorithms within the framework of the X-FEM [R7.02.12](doc:r7.02.12) method. The crack is then represented by level-sets. The propagation of the crack therefore consists of updating the level-sets taking into account the advancement of the crack front.

## 1 Introduction

A significant advantage of the X-FEM method being the possibility of studying several crack geometries on the same mesh, we can easily study the evolution of the crack over time. The operator PROPA_FISS [U4.82.11](doc:u4.82.11) calculates the crack resulting from a fatigue propagation according to a local Paris law.

From the data of a crack at a given time and the corresponding stress intensity coefficients (Operator CALC_G [U4.82.03](doc:u4.82.03)), the operator calculates the level-sets after advancement of the crack, then the data structure fiss_xfem is enriched in the same way as with the operator DEFI_FISS_XFEM [U4.82.08](doc:u4.82.08).

An introduction to the Level-sets method is given in [R7.02.12](doc:r7.02.12). The crack propagation phase simply translates into the propagation of the level-sets

Currently we can calculate the new level-sets after advancement by three different approaches: by updating the level-sets following a fast marching type method, by updating after a direct geometric calculation or by updating from a reconstructed propagated mesh (the crack mesh is reconstructed from the propagation data and the level-sets are calculated in a second step by distance to the mesh). The different stages of X-FEM crack propagation in *Code_Aster* change depending on the approach used.

However, the step of calculating the crack front advancement speed is common to all methods (see [§ 2 ]). We define the level-sets reflecting the new crack geometry and we ensure that these keep definitions close to signed distance functions (cf. [§ 4 ]). The propagation of a level-set thus requires the following three successive steps (bib 2):

- extension of the known speed on iso-zero towards the entire domain,
- propagation of the level-set from this speed field,
- resetting the level-set function in order to maintain a signed distance function.

On the contrary, in the case where we use direct calculation, we immediately recalculate the new values of the two level-sets (cf. [§ 6 ]).

## 2 Computation of the propagation velocity field

The first steps consist of calculating two velocity fields, normal and tangential, defining the dissociated evolution of the two level-sets. We place ourselves within the framework of mixed mode propagation.

### 2.1 Advance rate of the crack front

The advancement of the crack front at each of its points evolves in the local basis according to a Paris law. Its direction can be determined by several criteria detailed in paragraph [§ 2.7]. We can generally write:

$$
V={V}_{N}{e}_{1}+{V}_{T}{e}_{2}
$$

Or again:

$$
V=C{G}^{m}\left(\sin\beta {e}_{1}+\cos\beta {e}_{2}\right)
$$

And where $C$ and $m$ are characteristics of the fatigue behaviour of the material, $G$ is the local energy release rate resulting from the $G$ -Theta method, and $\beta$ is the branching angle. This angle is:

$$
\beta =2\arctan\left[\frac{1}{4}.\left(\frac{{K}_{I}}{{K}_{\text{II}}}-\text{sign}\left({K}_{\text{II}}\right).\sqrt{{\left(\frac{{K}_{I}}{{K}_{\text{II}}}\right)}^{2}+8}\right)\right]
$$

Or again:

$$
\beta =2\arctan\left[\frac{1}{4}.\left(\frac{1+{x}_{I}-{(1-{x}_{\mathit{III}})}^{p}}{\text{}{x}_{\mathit{II}}}-\mathit{sign}({K}_{\mathit{II}}).\sqrt{{(\frac{1+{x}_{I}-{(1-{x}_{\mathit{III}})}^{p}}{\text{}{x}_{\mathit{II}}})}^{2}+8}\right)\right]
$$

With:

| :math:{x}_{i}=frac{{K}_{i}}{{K}_{I}+left | {K}_{mathit{II}}right | +left | {K}_{mathit{III}}right | }` and $p=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}-5\nu }{4}$ |  |

### 2.2 Velocity field extended to the mesh

The normal and tangent velocities are known only for the points of intersection between the crack front and the faces of the mesh elements. These points are not mesh nodes. For updating the level-sets after a propagation, the speed must be known for each node of the mesh [§ 3.1]. It is therefore necessary to extend the speed to all the nodes of the mesh from the values on the crack front. To do this, we project each node onto the crack front in the normal direction and we assign to the node the speed of the point which is the projection of the node.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000201000001E7000001984DC509B37B4D43D4.png)

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000201000001E7000001984DC509B37B4D43D4.png)

**Figure** 2.2-1 **: projection of the node** $M$ **on the crack front**
The projection is implemented according to the following algorithm ():

- Loop on the nodes $\text{M}$ of the mesh

  - Initialisation: ${d}_{min}=\text{réel maxi}$
  - Loop on segments $\overrightarrow{\text{IJ}}$: between two consecutive points $I$ and $J$ of the crack front

    - We calculate $s=\frac{\vec{\text{IJ}}\cdot \vec{\text{IM}}}{{\Vert \vec{\text{IJ}}\Vert }^{2}}=\frac{\Vert \vec{\text{IP}}\Vert \cdot \Vert \vec{\text{IJ}}\Vert }{{\Vert \vec{\text{IJ}}\Vert }^{2}}=\frac{\Vert \vec{\text{IP}}\Vert }{\Vert \vec{\text{IJ}}\Vert }$
    - If $s<0$, then $s=0$
    - If $s>1$, then $s=1$
    - If we project the node $M$ onto $\overrightarrow{\text{IJ}}$, we obtain the point $P$. The position of this point is determined by the vector $\overrightarrow{\text{IP}}$: $\overrightarrow{\text{IP}}=s\cdot \overrightarrow{\text{IJ}}$
    - We calculate the distance between the node $M$ and its projection $P$: $d=∥\overrightarrow{\text{MP}}∥$
    - If $d<{d}_{min}$, then we store ${d}_{min}$, ${\text{I}}_{min}=\text{I}$, ${\text{J}}_{min}=\text{J}$ and ${s}_{min}=s$
  - End of loop
- End of loop

The parameter ${s}_{min}$ gives the position of the projection $P$ of each node $M$ of the mesh with respect to the length of the vector $\overrightarrow{{\text{I}}_{min}{\text{J}}_{min}}$ of the crack front to which the projection $P$ belongs. The position of the projection $P$ can therefore be viewed as a parametric position on the vector $\overrightarrow{{\text{I}}_{min}{\text{J}}_{min}}$. This can be used to calculate the value of the velocity vector $V$ to assign to node $M$.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/100002010000024B000001C186049669B6834D84.png)

**Figure** 2.2-2 **: determination of the speed of the point** $N$
We reduce this problem to the determination of the velocity vector ${V}_{P}$ of a point $P$ of the segment $\overline{\text{IJ}}$ when the velocity vectors are known at the points $I$ and $J$ ().

The method used to calculate ${V}_{P}$ is arbitrary. If we look at the physical meaning of the speed, we can say that it changes gradually between the two limit vectors ${V}_{I}$ and ${V}_{J}$ with the position of the point $P$. For the value of $\Vert {V}_{P}\Vert$ we can therefore use a linear interpolation between the two values $∥{V}_{\text{I}}∥$ and $∥{V}_{\text{J}}∥$:

$$
∥{V}_{\text{P}}∥=(∥{V}_{\text{J}}∥-∥{V}_{\text{I}}∥)\cdot {s}_{min}+∥{V}_{\text{I}}∥
$$

For the direction of ${V}_{\text{P}}$, we can determine the angle between ${V}_{\text{I}}$ and ${V}_{\text{J}}$ and once again use a linear interpolation on the calculated angle, according to the following algorithm:

  - we calculate the vector normal to the plane formed by the vectors ${V}_{\text{I}}$ and ${V}_{\text{J}}$: ${v}_{3}={V}_{\text{I}}\mathrm{\wedge }{V}_{\text{J}}$
  - we return to a local basis formed by the vectors ${V}_{\text{I}}$ and ${v}_{3}$. We calculate the last axis of the base: ${v}_{2}={v}_{3}\mathrm{\wedge }{V}_{\text{I}}$. This vector is in the plane formed by the vectors ${V}_{\text{I}}$ and ${V}_{\text{J}}$.
  - we calculate the unit vectors of the base in this plane: ${u}_{1}=\frac{{V}_{\text{I}}}{\Vert {V}_{\text{I}}\Vert }$ and ${u}_{2}=\frac{{v}_{2}}{\Vert {v}_{2}\Vert }$
  - we calculate the angle between the two speed vectors ${V}_{\text{I}}$ and ${V}_{\text{J}}$: $\alpha =\mathit{acos}\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{3}{V}_{\text{I}i}\cdot {V}_{\text{J}i}}{\Vert {V}_{\text{I}}\Vert \cdot \Vert {V}_{\text{J}}\Vert }\right)$
  - we calculate the unit vector of the speed at the point $P$ by linear interpolation:
  - ${u}_{\text{P}}=\cos(\alpha {s}_{min})\cdot {u}_{1}+\sin(\alpha {s}_{min})\cdot {u}_{2}$
  - finally we calculate the speed vector at the point $P$ using the value $∥{V}_{\text{P}}∥$ already calculated: ${V}_{\text{P}}=∥{V}_{\text{P}}∥\cdot {u}_{\text{P}}$

### 2.3 Computation of the velocities at each node of the mesh

To make the normal level-set evolve properly [§ 2.5 ] and to integrate the two level-set updating equations [§ 3 ], we must know the normal velocity ${V}_{N}$ and tangent ${V}_{T}$ vectors for each node of the mesh. To do this, it is enough to calculate the normal and tangent directions of the level-sets using the gradients of the level-sets.

Unfortunately this solution is not applicable to all nodes. Indeed, after the update, the orthogonality property of the level-sets is not guaranteed [§ 4.1] for all the nodes. This property is very important to guarantee the orthogonality of the velocity vectors ${V}_{N}$ and ${V}_{T}$. We must therefore use another solution.

Looking at the way used to extend the velocity field to the mesh nodes [§ 2.2, § 2.5], we consider the following solution:

1. if the level-set tangent to the node is negative: it evolves in a direction parallel to the surface of the crack. We therefore use the gradients of the level-sets to calculate the normal and tangent directions. Indeed, this means that the node is in the space of the existing crack and that the value of the normal level-set does not change during the updating of the level-sets unlike the value of the tangent level-set [§ 2.5].
2. if the level-set tangent to the node is positive, we use the local basis of the projection of the node on the crack front. Indeed, the evolution of the level-sets must be consistent with the modifications of the crack front.

For the first case, the calculation of the normal and tangent directions does not pose a problem because the gradients of the level-sets $\varphi$ aux nœuds sont toujours connus: :math:`{V}_{N}={V}_{N}^{\text{P}}\cdot \nabla {\varphi}_{n}$ and ${V}_{T}={V}_{T}^{\text{P}}\cdot \nabla {\varphi}_{t}$. However, the normal ${V}_{N}^{\text{P}}$ and tangent ${V}_{T}^{\text{P}}$ components of the speed ${V}_{\text{P}}$ must be calculated with respect to the local basis of the crack front at point $P$ using the following solution described for the second case.

In this case, the calculation is more difficult because the local basis of the crack front is known only for the points of intersection between the crack front and the faces of the mesh elements. The situation is visible in the figure: we know the local basis at points $I$ and $J$ and we want to calculate the local basis at point $P$, projection of a node on the crack front. The orientation of this base must be between the orientations of the base at point $I$ and of the base at point $J$ according to the position of point $P$. The latter is well described by the parameter ${s}_{min}$ already calculated [§ 2.2].

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/100002010000024B000001D3AF0E58B3943B2044.png)

**Figure** 2.3-1 **: determination of the local basis at point** $P$

If we do not consider the translation of the origin, the change in orientation of the local basis can be seen as a rotation of the base from the orientation at the point $I$, for which ${s}_{min}=0$. We have the maximum rotation for ${s}_{min}=1$, that is to say at the point $J$. The orientation of the base at point $P$ is obtained using a lower rotation proportional to the value of ${s}_{min}$.

Using Euler's theorem, we can always describe any rotation as a single rotation (Euler angle) around a single axis (Euler axis). The direction of the latter and the value of the rotation change for the segment $\overline{\text{IJ}}$ along the crack front.

The local basis at the point $P$ can therefore be obtained by a rotation around the Euler axis calculated for the rotation which transforms the base at the point $I$ into the base at the point $J$. The rotation angle value can be calculated simply by multiplying the Euler angle by ${s}_{min}$. In the following paragraph [§ 2.4 ], we describe the equations that we use for this calculation.

After determining the local basis ${t}_{\text{P}}$ and ${n}_{\text{P}}$ at the point $P$, we can calculate the value of the components of the speed ${V}_{\text{P}}$. Either:

$$
{V}_{N}^{\text{P}}={V}_{\text{P}}\cdot {n}_{\text{P}}
$$

Finally we calculate the two vectors ${V}_{N}$ and ${V}_{T}$:

$$
{V}_{N}={V}_{N}^{P}\cdot {n}_{\text{P}}
$$

In summary, for each node $M$ of the mesh we calculate the projection $P$ on the crack front. Then we calculate the local basis ${t}_{\text{P}}$ and ${n}_{\text{P}}$ at the point $P$ and the components of the speed ${V}_{\text{P}}$ (which have already been calculated [§ 2.2 ]):

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{V}_{N}^{\text{P}}={V}_{\text{P}}\cdot {n}_{\text{P}}\\ {V}_{T}^{\text{P}}={V}_{\text{P}}\cdot {t}_{\text{P}}\end{array}
$$

Finally we calculate the vectors ${V}_{N}$ and ${V}_{T}$ at the node $M$ according to the sign of the tangent level-set:

$$
{\varphi}_{t}\le 0\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{V}_{N}={V}_{N}^{\text{P}}\cdot \nabla {\varphi}_{\text{n}}\\ {V}_{T}={V}_{T}^{\text{P}}\cdot \nabla {\varphi}_{t}\end{array}
$$

Where ${\varphi}_{n}$ and ${\varphi}_{t}$ are the normal level-set and the tangent level-set respectively.

### 2.4 Computation of the local basis

As already described in the previous paragraph, we know the local basis at points $I$ and $J$ and we want to calculate the local basis at point $P$ using a rotation.

The first step is the calculation of the axis and the Euler angle which describe the rotation that must be applied to the base $({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})$ at the point $I$ to obtain the base $({t}_{J},{n}_{J},{b}_{J})$ at the point $J$. The algorithm used is as follows:

- We calculate the third axis $b$ of the local basis at the points $I$ and $J$. In fact, only the unit vectors $t$ and $n$ are stored:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{b}_{\text{I}}={t}_{\text{I}}\wedge {n}_{\text{I}}\\ {b}_{\text{J}}={t}_{\text{J}}\wedge {n}_{\text{J}}\end{array}
$$

- We calculate the Euler rotation matrix between the two bases:

$$
\underline{\underline{{R}_{E}}}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{t}_{\text{J}}\cdot {t}_{\text{I}}& {n}_{\text{J}}\cdot {t}_{\text{I}}& {b}_{\text{J}}\cdot {t}_{\text{I}}\\ {t}_{\text{J}}\cdot {n}_{\text{I}}& {n}_{\text{J}}\cdot {n}_{\text{I}}& {b}_{\text{J}}\cdot {n}_{\text{I}}\\ {t}_{\text{J}}\cdot {b}_{\text{I}}& {n}_{\text{J}}\cdot {b}_{\text{I}}& {b}_{\text{J}}\cdot {b}_{\text{I}}\end{array}\right]
$$

Each column of the matrix represents a vector from the basis at point $J$ $({t}_{J},{n}_{J},{b}_{J})$ into the local basis at point $I$.

- We calculate the Euler angle using the elements of the matrix $\underline{\underline{{R}_{E}}}$:

$$
{\vartheta}_{E}=\text{acos}\left(\frac{{R}_{11}+{R}_{22}+{R}_{33}-1}{2}\right)
$$

- We calculate the Euler axis $e={\left[{e}_{1}{e}_{2}{e}_{3}\right]}^{\text{T}}$:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{e}_{1}=\frac{{R}_{23}-{R}_{32}}{2\cdot \sin{\vartheta}_{E}}\\ {e}_{2}=\frac{{R}_{31}-{R}_{13}}{2\cdot \sin{\vartheta}_{E}}\\ {e}_{3}=\frac{{R}_{12}-{R}_{21}}{2\cdot \sin{\vartheta}_{E}}\end{array}
$$

The calculated Euler angle and axis are the same for all points $P$ on the edge defined by points $I$ and $J$. The second step is the calculation of the local basis for a point $P$ for which the position is determined by the parameter ${s}_{min}$ [§ 2.2]. The algorithm used is as follows:

- We calculate the effective rotation angle to use:

$$
{\vartheta}_{P}={\vartheta}_{E}\cdot {s}_{min}
$$

- We calculate the rotation matrix between the local basis at point $I$ and the local basis at point $J$ for a rotation around the Euler axis $e$ equal to ${\vartheta}_{P}$:

$$
\underline{\underline{{T}_{P}}}=\cos({\vartheta}_{P})\cdot \underline{\underline{I}}+(1+\cos({\vartheta}_{P}))\cdot e\cdot {e}^{T}-\sin({\vartheta}_{P})\cdot \underline{\underline{\epsilon}}
$$

where the matrix $\underline{\underline{\epsilon}}$ is the following:

$$
\underline{\underline{\epsilon}}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& -{e}_{3}& {e}_{2}\\ {e}_{3}& 0& -{e}_{1}\\ -{e}_{2}& {e}_{1}& 0\end{array}\right]
$$

- We calculate the axes ${t}_{\text{P}}$ and ${n}_{\text{P}}$ from the local basis at point $P$ in the local basis at point $I$:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{t}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}=\underline{\underline{{T}_{\text{P}}}}\cdot {[100]}^{T}\\ {n}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}=\underline{\underline{{T}_{\text{P}}}}\cdot {[010]}^{T}\end{array}
$$

The two vectors are therefore coincident with the first and second column of the matrix $\underline{\underline{{T}_{P}}}$. Using the equation above to calculate this matrix, we can therefore write:

$$
{t}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}=\left[\begin{array}{c}\cos({\vartheta}_{\text{P}})+(1-\cos({\vartheta}_{\text{P}}))\cdot {e}_{1}^{2}\\ (1-\cos({\vartheta}_{\text{P}}))\cdot {e}_{1}\cdot {e}_{2}-\sin({\vartheta}_{\text{P}})\cdot {e}_{3}\\ (1-\cos({\vartheta}_{\text{P}}))\cdot {e}_{1}\cdot {e}_{3}+\sin({\vartheta}_{\text{P}})\cdot {e}_{2}\end{array}\right]
$$

And:

$$
{n}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}=\left[\begin{array}{c}(1-\cos({\vartheta}_{\text{P}}))\cdot {e}_{1}\cdot {e}_{2}+\sin({\vartheta}_{\text{P}})\cdot {e}_{3}\\ \cos({\vartheta}_{\text{P}})+(1-\cos({\vartheta}_{\text{P}}))\cdot {e}_{2}^{2}\\ (1-\cos({\vartheta}_{\text{P}}))\cdot {e}_{2}\cdot {e}_{3}-\sin({\vartheta}_{\text{P}})\cdot {e}_{1}\end{array}\right]
$$

- Finally we can express the vectors ${t}_{\text{P}}^{\text{I}}$ and ${n}_{\text{P}}^{\text{I}}$ in the global basis of the mesh, which is used for all calculations. If we denote the component $i$ of the vector ${t}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}$ by ${t}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}(i)$ and the same component of the vector ${n}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}$ by ${n}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}(i)$, we can write:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{t}_{\text{P}}={t}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}(1)\cdot {t}_{\text{I}}+{t}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}(2)\cdot {n}_{\text{I}}+{t}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}(3)\cdot {b}_{\text{I}}\\ {n}_{\text{P}}={n}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}(1)\cdot {t}_{\text{I}}+{n}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}(2)\cdot {n}_{\text{I}}+{n}_{\text{P}({t}_{I},{n}_{I},{b}_{I})}(3)\cdot {b}_{\text{I}}\end{array}
$$

### 2.5 Adjustment of the normal velocity field

In order to prevent the displacement of the pre-existing crack, and to make the normal level-set evolve properly, it is necessary to adjust the normal velocity field (bib 1). We want to have a relatively smooth crack trajectory, such that:

$$
{\overline{V}}_{N}(\mathit{lst})=\lbrace  \begin{array}{ccc}0& \text{si}& \mathit{lst}\le 0(\text{et notamment en}\mathit{lst}=0)\\ V& \text{si}& \mathit{lst}=V\cdot \Delta t\end{array}
$$

The value of $\Delta t$ is equal to the total integration time of the level-set updating equations [§ 3.1]. Thus, the crack front advances well according to ${V}_{T}$ and ${V}_{N}$ calculated previously. We make a linear approximation in $\mathit{lst}$.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/100000000000017E0000011869A3EE89FCD4CB23.png)

**Figure** 2.5-1 **: Normal speed adjustment**

The formula applied to all the nodes of the mesh is as follows:

$$
{\overline{V}}_{N}=h(\text{lst}).\frac{{V}_{N}.\text{lst}}{{V}_{T}.\mathrm{\Delta t}}
$$

Where ${V}_{N}$ and ${V}_{T}$ are the nodal velocity fields, and $\Delta t$ the chosen time step (see paragraph [§ 3 ]).

### 2.6 Taking twisting into account

In the case of a three-dimensional crack, two rotations are possible during crack propagation. Let a point at the crack front $(P)$ be associated with a local basis (**t**, **n**, **b**). [bib 21] thus describes the *branching* rotation as the rotation of the speed vector $V$ in the plane $(P,t,n)$ and $\beta$ its associated angle, and the *twisting* rotation as being the rotation of the local basis around the speed vector, of angle $\gamma$.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000000000002E400000255E3CB78DEC83D2BBF.png)

Figure 2.6-1: Definition of the branching and twisting angles

Taking twisting into account therefore consists of rotating the local bases before the stage of updating the level-sets. The propagation step therefore follows the following sequence:

1. application of *branching* ($\beta$) and *twisting* ($\gamma$) rotations to the local bases of the crack front points,
2. projection of the mesh points on the crack front,
3. attribution of a local basis and a propagation speed $V$ to all the points projected by interpolation between the points of the crack front,
4. update of level-sets in the local bases with speed $V$.

The local bases having undergone the rotation before the level-set updating step, the speed vector $V$ is therefore carried only by the vector $t'$ of the new local basis. There is therefore no longer a normal component to the speed in the new local basis.

**Note**:

*The twisting rotation can only be activated in 3D and when the keyword* *CRIT_ANGL_BIFURCATION of the propagation operator* *PROPA_FISS is worth* *SITT_MAX_DEVER.*

*By default twisting rotation is forced to* $0$ *.*

### 2.7 Determination of bifurcation angles

In the case where twisting is not taken into account, the branching angle is conventionally determined by the principle of the “Maximum Hoop Stress criterion” [bib 4] as a function of the stress intensity coefficients ${K}_{I}$ and ${K}_{\mathit{II}}$ *:*

$$
\beta =2\arctan\left[\frac{1}{4}.\left(\frac{{K}_{I}}{{K}_{\text{II}}}-\text{sign}\left({K}_{\text{II}}\right).\sqrt{{\left(\frac{{K}_{I}}{{K}_{\text{II}}}\right)}^{2}+8}\right)\right]
$$

**Note**:

*The branch angle can be calculated by the operator* CALC_G [R7.02.05](doc:r7.02.05) [U4.82.03](doc:u4.82.03). In this case it is imposed on PROPA_FISS *in the same table as G and the SIF.*

*It can also be calculated by the operator* *POST_RUPTURE* [U4.82.04](doc:u4.82.04) inside the operator *PROPA_FISS* [U4.82.11](doc:u4.82.11). This option allows you to choose other criteria *than* the “Maximum Hoop Stress criterion”.

In the case where twisting is taken into account, the branching angle and the twisting angle are both calculated with the “Maximum Hoop Stress criterion” [bib 21] using ${K}_{I}$, ${K}_{\mathit{II}}$ and ${K}_{\mathit{III}}$:

$$
{\beta}_{c}=2\arctan\left[\frac{1}{4}.\left(\frac{1+{x}_{I}-{(1-{x}_{\mathit{III}})}^{p}}{{x}_{\mathit{II}}}-\mathit{sign}({K}_{\mathit{II}}).\sqrt{{(\frac{1+{x}_{I}-{(1-{x}_{\mathit{III}})}^{p}}{{x}_{\mathit{II}}})}^{2}+8}\right)\right]
$$

And for the twisting angle:

$$
\gamma =\frac{1}{2}\arctan\left[\frac{2{\sigma}_{\theta z}({\beta}_{c})}{{\sigma}_{\theta \theta }({\beta}_{c})-{\sigma}_{zz}({\beta}_{c})}\right]
$$

With:

| :math:{x}_{i}=frac{{K}_{i}}{{K}_{I}+left | {K}_{mathit{II}}right | +left | {K}_{mathit{III}}right | }` and $p=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}-5\nu }{4}$ |  |

## 3 Update of the level-sets

After propagation, the geometry of the crack changes and the level-sets must evolve to properly describe this new geometry. Even if we can recalculate the level-sets using their signed distance definition, in practice this solution should be avoided for performance reasons.

We can recalculate the level-sets more quickly by using the level-set updating equations which describe their evolution following the propagation of the crack.

In the following equations, we will use the symbols ${\varphi}_{n}$, ${\varphi}_{t}$ and $\varphi$ pour indiquer respectivement la level-set normale ($\mathit{lsn}$), the tangent level-set ($\mathit{lst}$) and a level-set in general ($\mathit{lsn}$ or $\mathit{lst}$).

### 3.1 Update equations

The level-sets are updated on all nodes according to the following Hamilton-Jacobi equation (bib 1):

$$
\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial t}=-{V}_{\varphi}\cdot \Vert \nabla \varphi \Vert
$$

Where $\varphi$ est la level-set, $\nabla \varphi$ est son gradient et ${V}_{\varphi}$ la vitesse de propagation de la level-set $\varphi$ (:math:`{V}_{T}$  ou ${V}_{N}$ ). This equation is known as the level-set evolution equation. In (bib 13) and (bib 14) it is shown that an error is present in this formulation of the equation and that the correct form to use is instead the following:

$$
\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial t}+\nabla \varphi \cdot V=0
$$

The integration of the equation poses several problems (bib 14):

- The number of integration iterations is important if an explicit scheme is used (for example Runge-Kutta) because of the limitation on the time step usable at each iteration (CFL condition). The adjustment of the normal speed field ([§ 2.5 ]) is at the origin of the very strong reduction in the maximum value of the usable time step and consequently of the increment in the number of necessary iterations. To avoid this problem, an implicit schema can be used but this complicates programming. As a third alternative, we can use a “fast marching method” type method (bib 3) which calculates the solution directly and without iterations (bib 15, bib 16). However, errors may be present in the solution calculated by this last method at the edges of the calculation domain (bib 15, bib 16).
- In the presence of a high value of the propagation angle $\beta$ ([§ 2.1 ]), the solution obtained by integration of the level-set equation may present instabilities which will disrupt the convergence of the integration algorithms during successive propagation iterations.
- The above-mentioned instabilities result in a high numerical sensitivity of the method to rounding errors, which leads to a significant variation in results between machines. This behaviour is not desirable in industrial use of the method.

An alternative updating equation is therefore proposed in (bib 14). It is based on the geometric interpretation of the modification of the level-sets resulting from the propagation of the crack. If we look at the iso-zero of the tangent level-set ($\mathit{lst}=0$ in the figure), we can say that each point of this surface moves by the quantity ${V}_{T}\cdot \Delta t$. Therefore, the value of the level-set at the point decreases by the same amount. If we look at the iso-zero of the normal level-set ($\mathit{lsn}=0$ in the figure), we can say that in the part of the volume of healthy material ($\mathit{lst}>0$) the level-set is subject to a rotation around the crack front and that each point moves in the direction normal to the iso-zero by a quantity ${V}_{N}\cdot \Delta t$. It is the linear variation of the speed ${V}_{N}$ with $\mathit{lst}$ ([§ 2.5 ]) which makes it possible to obtain the correct displacement at each point. By generalizing to the 3D case as in (bib 14), we can therefore write the following updating equations:

$$
\Delta {\varphi}_{n}=-\left({V}_{N}\cdot \nabla {\varphi}_{n}\right)\cdot \Delta t
$$

The advantage of these equations is obvious: they are explicit algebraic equations which do not require the use of any iterative integration scheme. The solution can be calculated directly with very low calculation time. Furthermore, no instability problem is present even for high propagation angles $\beta$ as shown in (bib 14).

The value of $\Delta t$ to use in the level-set updating equations can be easily calculated from the maximum advancement $\Delta {a}_{max}$ of the crack given by the user:

$$
\Delta {t}_{\mathit{tot}}=\frac{\Delta {a}_{max}}{\underset{i\in \mathit{fond}}{max}∥V\left({X}_{i}\right)∥}
$$

Where the vector $V({x}_{i})$ is the vector of the propagation speed ($V={V}_{N}+{V}_{T}$) of a point ${x}_{i}$ of the crack front. It should be observed that, in this context, we take as the point of the crack front the physical points at the intersection between the crack front and the faces of the elements of the mesh, for which we know the speed and the angle of propagation, but that we also take the geometric points of the front which are the normal projection of the nodes of the mesh on the front itself [§ 2.2]. However, the propagation speed is calculated for these last points using linear interpolation [§ 2.2] and for each edge which forms the crack front the maximum speed is obtained at one of its two ends. We can therefore limit the search for the maximum speed to the points of intersection between the crack front and the faces of the mesh elements.

**Note**:

*In the description above, the symbols *$t$ *and* $\Delta t$ *have been used to indicate time. However, the quantity described by these symbols is effectively the number of fatigue cycles which cause the crack to advance. In fact, if we look at the equation used above for the calculation of * $\Delta t$ *, * $\Delta t$ * is obtained by dividing between the distance of advance of the crack (for example: millimeters) and the speed of advance. The speed calculated using a propagation law (for example the Paris law) is given as an advance of the crack per fatigue cycle (for example: millimeters/cycle). We therefore deduce that* $\Delta t$ *represents the number of fatigue cycles that must be applied to obtain the desired maximum progress. The link between the number of fatigue cycles and time is given by the frequency of application of the loading.*

*This is true for all the equations used and we can say that all the quantities which are reduced to time (* $t$ *,* $\Delta t$ *, …) in fact express a number of fatigue cycles.*

## 4 Reinitialization and reorthogonalization of level-sets

### 4.1 Introduction

During their evolution, the level-sets must imperatively keep a relatively close definition of signed distance functions.

The first reason is the calculation of the stress intensity factors at the crack front by the $G$ -Theta method, for which the polar coordinates $(r,\theta )$ are defined in the local basis as $r=\sqrt{{\text{lsn}}^{2}+{\text{lst}}^{2}}$ and $\theta =\arctan(\text{lsn}/\text{lst})$; the use of signed distances for the calculation of polar coordinates is therefore necessary.

Furthermore, during crack propagation, the updating equation presented above (paragraph [§ 3 ]) brings into play the norms of the gradients of the level-sets, which must remain sufficiently close to unity, because if $|\nabla \varphi |$ is not worth $1$, the crack does not advance with the defined speed ${V}_{\varphi}$.

After the update phase, it happens that the level-sets lose their meaning as a signed distance function, particularly in the case of out-of-plane propagation (case of a request where ${K}_{\mathit{II}}\mathrm{\ne }0$), which introduces a normal speed (see Figure 4.1-1).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000000000003E70000012F71A69D40485413BE.png)

**Figure** 4.1-1 **: Propagation of level-sets and loss of signed distance and orthogonality function**

After propagation it is therefore necessary to go through a so-called “reinitialization” step whose aim will be to minimize $∣∣\nabla \mathrm{lsn}∣-1∣$ and , $∣∣\nabla \mathrm{lst}∣-1∣$ as well as a “reorthogonalization” step whose objective will be to minimize $\mid \nabla \text{lsn}.\nabla \text{lst}\mid ` . En effet, les level-sets ne sont rigoureusement définies que si, en tout point de l’espace, $mid \nabla \text{lsn}mid =1$ , $\mid \nabla \text{lst}\mid =1$ and $\nabla \text{lsn}.\nabla \text{lst}=0$ .

However, in the case of a non-planar crack, it is impossible to have two functions whose gradients are orthogonal to each other and have unitary norms at any point in space. The antagonism between the two properties appears in Figure 4.1-2 on which are defined, from an iso-zero of $\mathit{lsn}$, a function $\mathit{lst}$ either standardized or orthogonal.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/1000000000000299000000F1599BAD0917AFC85E.png)

**Figure** 4.1-2 **: Antagonism Initialisation - Orthogonalization**

It is therefore necessary to favor one or the other of these properties. Given the main use of these functions for the $G$ -Theta method, it appears more important that the gradients are well normalised, rather than orthogonal, so that the polar coordinates $(r,\theta )$ make sense. In order to have an orthonormal local basis, we also impose on the crack front that the gradients are normalised and orthogonal. The sequence of steps proposed in (bib 1) is therefore:

- reset of $\mathit{lsn}$:

The iso-zero of $\mathit{lsn}$, of major importance for the crack definition, is unchanged;

- reorthogonalization of $\mathit{lst}$ with respect to fixed $\mathit{lsn}$:

$\mathit{lst}$ is straightened relative to $\mathit{lsn}$ without modifying the crack front $(\text{lsn}=0)\cap (\text{lst}=0)$;

- reset of $\mathit{lst}$:

The level-set $\mathit{lst}$ is initialised with respect to its iso-zero having been orthogonalized.

### 4.2 Resolution using the fast marching method

In the following we will analyze two methods for reinitializing and reorthogonalizing the level-sets. The first method (“Upwind” method) can only be applied to rectangular elements in 2D and hexahedrons in 3D, an extension to all types of mesh being possible with the use of an auxiliary grid. The second method (“Simplex” method) can only be applied to simplex elements (triangles in 2D and tetrahedra in 3D), a cutting of the mesh cells leaving this framework allows this method to be used with all types of mesh cells.

#### 4.2.1 “Upwind” method

We saw previously that after the propagation phase of a level-set, it was necessary to reset it in order to maintain a signed distance function. In other words, if we assume that the function $\varphi (\vec{x},t=0)={\varphi}_{0}(\vec{x})$ verifies for each node of the mesh the property of a signed distance function given by $\left|\nabla \varphi \right|=1$, the level-set function at $t>0$ no longer has this property. It is therefore necessary to put in place a reset process allowing $\varphi$ to once again become a signed distance function.

We propose here a fast-marching method introduced by Sethian [19], which solves the eikonal equation of the form:

| :math:begin{array}{c}left | nabla varphi (x)right | =F(x),xin Omega \ varphi (x)=0,xin partial Omega end{array}` |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

With $\Omega \in {\mathrm{ℝ}}^{n}$, $n\text{}\text{}\in \lbrace 2,3\rbrace$ and $F$ a function with positive values.

The point that interests us here is only a particular case of this equation: when $F=1$. The solution thus gives the function $\varphi$ signed distance.

The principle of the fast-marching method consists of gradually propagating the data on the distance of the points from the lowest values, that is to say close to the interface, to the most distant zones.

For the propagation of level-sets, we use the values around $\mathit{ls}=0$ to propagate the information. Two calls to the method are necessary to process all the nodes of the mesh: the first call will process the positive points of the level-set and the second the negative points.

To do this, the method divides the mesh nodes into three categories:

- **Frozen points:** region of the mesh where the points have already been calculated (black circles);
- **Narrow band:** region close to the points already calculated (gray band);
- Far away: set of remaining points (white circles).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/100002010000024400000189CF2343834BA8C6A2.png)

Figure 4.2-1: distribution of mesh nodes
The fast-marching method can do without the reorthogonalization step because once the reinitialization of $\mathit{lsn}$ has been carried out, we can initialise the values of lst on its non-reorthogonalized iso-zero by taking the values at the nodes obtained by a geometric calculation. We can therefore use these values as an initial condition of the method to propagate this exact information on $\mathit{lst}$ to the entire domain.

##### 4.2.1.1 Discretisation of the problem

In the level-set reinitialization phase we seek to numerically solve a particular case of the eikonal equation:

$$
∣\nabla \phi ∣=1
$$

If we denote ${\Delta}_{x}$ ${\Delta}_{Y}$ and ${\Delta}_{Z}$ the space steps in $x$, $y$ and $z$, an upwind finite difference discretisation of this equation is:

$$
{\left(\frac{{\varphi}_{i,j,k}-{\varphi}_{1}}{{\Delta}_{x}}\right)}^{2}+{\left(\frac{{\varphi}_{i,j,k}-{\varphi}_{2}}{{\Delta}_{y}}\right)}^{2}+{\left(\frac{{\varphi}_{i,j,k}-{\varphi}_{3}}{{\Delta}_{z}}\right)}^{2}=1
$$

With:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{\varphi}_{1}=min({\varphi}_{i-1,j,k},{\varphi}_{i+1,j,k})\\ {\varphi}_{2}=min({\varphi}_{i,j-1,k},{\varphi}_{i,j+1,k})\\ {\varphi}_{3}=min({\varphi}_{i,j,k-1},{\varphi}_{i,j,k+1})\end{array}
{\varphi}_{n}\in \mathrm{ℝ}
$$

We seek the solutions of the quadratic trinomial which approximates the equation () in the form:

$$
p({\varphi}_{i,j,k})=0
$$

with:

$$
p({\varphi}_{i,j,k})={\left(\frac{{\varphi}_{i,j,k}-{\varphi}_{1}}{{\Delta}_{x}}\right)}^{2}+{\left(\frac{{\varphi}_{i,j,k}-{\varphi}_{2}}{{\Delta}_{y}}\right)}^{2}+{\left(\frac{{\varphi}_{i,j,k}-{\varphi}_{3}}{{\Delta}_{z}}\right)}^{2}-1
$$

The two solutions of () are:

$$
{\varphi}_{i,j,k}=\frac{{\varphi}_{1}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\varphi}_{2}{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\varphi}_{3}{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}+\sqrt{{({\varphi}_{1}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\varphi}_{2}{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\varphi}_{3}{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2})}^{2}-({\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}+{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\Delta}_{y}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2})({\varphi}_{1}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\varphi}_{2}^{2}{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\varphi}_{3}^{2}{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}-{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2})}}{({\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}+{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\Delta}_{y}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2})}
$$

The distance increases as we move away from the interface. The solution ${\varphi}_{i,j,k}$ is evaluated at a point further from the interface than the points at distances ${\varphi}_{1}$, ${\varphi}_{2}$ and ${\varphi}_{3}$. So we necessarily have that ${\varphi}_{i,j,k}>{\varphi}_{1}$, ${\varphi}_{i,j,k}>{\varphi}_{2}$ and ${\varphi}_{i,j,k}>{\varphi}_{3}$. But we have:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{\varphi}_{i,j,k}({\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}+{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\Delta}_{y}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2})-({\varphi}_{1}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\varphi}_{2}{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\varphi}_{3}{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2})\\ >{\varphi}_{i,j,k}({\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}+{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\Delta}_{y}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2})-(max({\varphi}_{1,}{\varphi}_{2,}{\varphi}_{3})({\Delta}_{y}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}))\end{array}
$$

This involves:

$$
({\varphi}_{i,j,k}-max({\varphi}_{1,}{\varphi}_{2,}{\varphi}_{3}))({\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{y}^{2}+{\Delta}_{x}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2}+{\Delta}_{y}^{2}{\Delta}_{z}^{2})>0
$$

So solution () is necessarily the only acceptable one.

##### 4.2.1.2 Implementation of the method

The numerical discretisation of the equation () by finite difference upwind is only applicable on hexahedral mesh cells (or rectangular in 2D) whose edges are well oriented in three directions $({j}_{1},{j}_{2},{j}_{3})$ forming a basis of ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{3}$. As for the upwind method, we use an auxiliary grid to extend the method to irregular mesh cells.

We present here only an algorithm for the case $\varphi$ positif ($\mathit{lsn}>0$ or $\mathit{lst}>0$ ), a simple multiplication by $-1$ of this function allowing us to process the other side of the level-set.

The algorithm implemented is as follows:

- **Initialisation**

- We identify the nodes for which $\mathit{ls}\ge 0$
- We locate the points close to $\mathit{ls}=0$ and $\mathit{ls}\ge 0$, that is to say the nodes (narrow band) of the mesh cells cut by $\mathit{ls}=0$, such as $\mathit{ls}\ge 0$

Note: *given that we wish to propagate the calculation towards distant areas, we must first ensure that these points provide a consistent initialisation from the point of view of a distance function.* *On* *performs a geometric calculation of the level-sets on the elements cut by* $\mathit{ls}=0$ . *We assign the other positive nodes with the value* $+\infty$. *They will belong to the set* **far**, *while the negative points will be included in a third set* **known.**

- **Loop**

- As long as a node has $\mathit{ls}\ge 0$, we locate the node with the smallest value of $\varphi$ in the narrowband.
- For each node around this minimum, we recover ${\varphi}_{1}$, ${\varphi}_{2}$ and ${\varphi}_{3}$ as defined by ().

We calculate for each node around the minimum its value by the explicit formula ().

- **End of loop**

To carry out the calculations using the fast-marching method, we discretised the equation () by finite differences. To calculate the value of a node, you must therefore have the information necessary to apply the explicit formula (). 1. **Resetting lst**

Once the reinitialization of $\mathit{lsn}$ has been carried out by the fast marching method, we move on to the reinitialization of $\mathit{lst}$ without going through the reorthogonalization step. The main problem for this step is that the iso-zero of $\mathit{lst}$ is not orthogonal to that of $\mathit{lsn}$. As fast marching is only applicable under the condition that the distance increases moving away from the interface, we can start the method around the iso-zero of the non-orthogonalized $\mathit{lst}$ by carrying out a geometric calculation for these nodes in order to recover the correct values and propagate the information as defined above. The method will thus reorthogonalize the $\mathit{lst}$ and assign it the signed distance function property in a single calculation. To increase the precision and localisation of the crack front, we will perform two lst resets in succession. The first reset allows the iso-zero to be moved so that it becomes orthogonal to $\mathit{lsn}$ and the second will increase the precision on the location of the crack front. The problem may come from the position of the iso-zero of $\mathit{lst}$ before the first reset. If this position is too far from the new position after resetting, we notice a less precise location of the crack front. With two steps, we ensure that the location of the iso-zero of $\mathit{lst}$ will not disturb the solution.

##### 4.2.1.4 Application of the upwind method to an irregular mesh

For the application of the finite difference method, we must use a very regular mesh, consisting of hexahedral mesh cells in 3D and rectangular mesh cells in 2D, whose edges are well oriented according to the directions of the basis of the space ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{3}$ in 3D and ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{2}$ in 2D. This represents a very strong restriction for the creation of the mesh and therefore excessively limits the cases that can be treated by the method.

A solution to this problem consists of using two different mesh cells (bib 12): one mesh is used for the physical structure (*physical mesh*) and one mesh is used for the representation of the level-sets (*mesh level-sets*). The restrictions of the finite difference method apply only to this last mesh. The values of the level-sets on the physical mesh can be calculated by projecting the known values onto the level-sets mesh. This is necessary for locating the crack front in the physical mesh. In fact, the fracture mechanics calculations are carried out only on this last mesh. We can therefore carry out the updating, reinitialization and reorthogonalization of the level-sets using only the level-sets mesh. Afterwards we can project the level-sets onto the physical mesh from the level-sets mesh. Finally, we can calculate the stress intensity factors and the direction of crack propagation using only the physical mesh.

The projection also makes it possible to obtain geometric independence between the two mesh cells: the mesh of the level-sets does not need to be oriented according to the directions of the base chosen for the physical mesh and it can only occupy the volume of the crack propagation zone in the structure.

The nodes of the level-set mesh, constructed as described above, therefore constitute a cloud of points aligned in three orthogonal directions. The grid formed by these points can be used to apply the “upwind” method. In the remainder of this document and in the syntax of *Code_Aster*, we will use the expression **auxiliary grid** to indicate all of these points. Also, to simplify the notations used and make them clearer, we will simply use the term **mesh** to indicate the physical mesh.

##### 4.2.1.5 Implementation of the projection between the auxiliary grid and the physical mesh

In *Code_Aster* the auxiliary grid is not given as a set of well-oriented points but as a mesh and therefore some intermediate steps are necessary for the application of the upwind finite difference method. Indeed, for this method all the points of the grid, taken to be coincident with the nodes of the given level-set mesh, must be arranged according to the directions of a local basis. We must therefore calculate this local basis and then we must order the nodes and calculate the distance between two successive nodes.

The *calculation of the local basis* is very easy because we can use the definition of the elements of the auxiliary grid mesh. Indeed, the edges of the elements are well oriented in relation to the axes of a local basis and we can define this base by taking edges which are orthogonal to each other. In addition we only search for the direction of the axes of the local basis. We can therefore use the edges of any element of the mesh. In 3D, the first element of the mesh of the auxiliary grid makes it possible to determine from one of its nodes, the three edges which lead there, and thus to calculate the three unit vectors according to the three directions of the selected edges. In 2D we do the same operation with two edges.

For *the classification of the nodes* according to the directions of the axes of the calculated local basis, we can once again use the edges of the elements. For each node of the mesh, we can select the elements which contain it. For each of the selected elements, we select the edges which start from the node. Finally for each of the selected edges, we order the points according to one of the directions of the local basis of the mesh using the scalar product between the edge and the unit vectors of the local basis and we can calculate the distance between the nodes of the edge. In 3D the algorithm implemented is as follows (all the vectors are represented in the base chosen to represent the nodes of the physical mesh):

- We locate the three unit vectors of the local basis: ${j}_{1}$, ${j}_{2}$ and ${j}_{3}$
- Loop on the nodes $N$ of the mesh

  - We initialise to zero the stored values of the six nodes which are connected (successive or previous) to the node $N$ according to the three unit vectors of the local basis
  - We identify the elements which include the node $N$ in their definition
  - Loop on selected elements

    - We locate the three edges which start from the node $N$
    - Loop over the three selected edges

      - We calculate the vector $\overrightarrow{\mathit{NA}}$ between the two end nodes of the edge. This vector is oriented in the direction which goes from the node $N$ to the other node $A$ end of the edge.
- We calculate the components of the vector $\overrightarrow{\mathit{NA}}$ according to the local basis:

${\mathit{NA}}_{1}=\vec{\mathit{NA}}\cdot {j}_{1}$

${\mathit{NA}}_{2}=\overrightarrow{\mathit{NA}}\cdot {j}_{2}$

${\mathit{NA}}_{3}=\overrightarrow{\mathit{NA}}\cdot {j}_{3}$

      - We calculate the tolerance which is used to check if a component is zero. This value is calculated as a percentage $p$ (set to 1% in *Code_Aster*) of the length of the vector:

${l}_{max}=p\cdot ∣\overrightarrow{\mathit{NA}}∣$

      - We locate the largest component $n$:

$\Delta =max(∣{\mathit{NA}}_{1}∣,∣{\mathit{NA}}_{2}∣,∣{\mathit{NA}}_{3}∣,)$

$n\in [1,2,3]/\Delta =∣{\mathit{NA}}_{n}∣$

      - We check that the other two components are zero ($<{l}_{max}$), otherwise we stop with an error message. In fact, this means that the edges of the mesh are not all aligned in the same three directions.
      - If ${\mathit{NA}}_{n}>0$, then we store the node $A$ as the **successive** node of the node $N$ according to the direction ${j}_{n}$. If ${\mathit{NA}}_{n}<0$, then we store the node $A$ as the node **preceding** the node $N$ according to the direction ${j}_{n}$. We also store $\Delta$ as the value of the distance between the nodes $A$ and $N$ according to the direction ${j}_{n}$. If ${\mathit{NA}}_{n}=0$ , then we stop with an error message because this means that several nodes of the element are coincident.
    - End of loop
  - End of loop
- End of loop

In 2D we can use the same algorithm by taking only two edges per node and therefore two components. It is very important that before the start of the first loop we initialise to zero the values stored at the six nodes which are connected to the node $N$. Indeed, if the stored number of one of these nodes is equal to zero, we can deduce that the node $N$ does not have a preceding or successive node in a well-defined direction.

The arrangement of the nodes according to the directions of the axes of the local basis of the grid must be done only once before using the upwind method.

In the description above, nothing has been said about the modifications that must be made to the code before the reset and reorthogonalization phases of the level-sets. In fact, there are no big changes to be made. The calculation of the stress intensity factors and the crack front advancement speed are not affected [§ 2.1]. The algorithms used for the phases of extension of the speed field to the grid mesh [§ 2.2] and the adjustment of the normal speed [§ 2.5] do not change but we must give as input to the corresponding utilities the nodes of the auxiliary grid and not those of the physical mesh. For this, the changes to the code to be made can be made very quickly because the values of the crack front advancement speed are independent of the physical mesh. In fact, the calculation of $G$, ${K}_{I}$ and ${K}_{\mathit{II}}$ is carried out on the physical mesh but the values of these quantities are linked to the points of the crack front which are stored independently with respect to the mesh used. This allows the extension of the velocity field and the adjustment of the normal velocity to be carried out directly on the auxiliary grid, by projection onto the crack front discretised by segments, no projection of the velocity field between the physical mesh and the grid being necessary because they both use the same discretisation of the crack front. Finally, for the update phase, nothing changes in the utilities except the information on the mesh used (physical or level-set): the data is the same and is stored at the mesh nodes.

In the presence of mesh cells consisting of a large number of elements, the projection between the auxiliary grid and the physical mesh can take a lot of calculation time. If we look at the use of level-sets for the physical mesh, we can say that it is not necessary that we know the value of the level-sets for all the nodes of this mesh. Indeed, in the physical mesh, we use the level-sets for three calculations:

1. the representation of the crack front,
2. calculation of the local basis at each point at the crack front,
3. the energy calculation of stress intensity factors.

For the first and second points, it is sufficient that the value of the level-sets is known for the nodes of the elements cut by the crack front. For the last point it is sufficient that the value of the level-sets is known for all the nodes which are included in the volume of the mesh used for the energy calculation. This volume coincides with a torus constructed around the crack front. The radius of this torus is specified by the user in the energy operator used for the calculation, for example CALC_G.

The three calculations above define three groups of nodes for which the value of the level-sets must be known. The group defined for the energy calculation (point 3) includes the others. It is therefore sufficient to select in each mesh (physics and auxiliary grid) the nodes which are included in the torus described above and to make the projection only between the two groups defined by these nodes.

We can select the nodes to project if the distance between each node of the mesh and the crack front is known. For the auxiliary grid this distance is already calculated by the routine which extends the known velocity field at the crack front to the nodes of the mesh [§ 2.2]. In this case, the selection of nodes to project is therefore quick. On the other hand, for the physical mesh this distance is not known and it must be calculated using the same algorithm as that described in [§ 2.2].

The projection can be implemented using the operator PROJ_CHAMP available in *Code_Aster.*

As described above, we have already selected the nodes of the physical mesh for which the value of the level-sets must be known. The selection is made by taking all the nodes that are included in the torus used for energy calculations (point 3 above). This makes it possible to immediately have the domain on which the field is projected with the form requested by PROJ_CHAMP. On the other hand, additional work is necessary to define the domain of the field to be projected. We can select the nodes of this domain (on the level-sets mesh) using the torus already used to define the other domain. Then we can define the domain of the field to be projected by looking for the elements which have at least one of the nodes selected with the previous technique.

The last problem to solve is the following: what value should we take for the radius of the torus used for the selection of the nodes to project? We have already said that the value used in the energy calculation operator (point 3 above) is the one that must be used. Unfortunately this value is not known to the operator PROPA_FISS. We can therefore use the value of the RAYON parameter of this operator which defines the torus around the crack front used for the calculation of the local residue. It must always be greater than the value of the radius used in the energy calculation operator because, after propagation, the properties of orthogonality and signed distance of the level-sets are guaranteed only in this torus. However, the distance to be used for node selection must be calculated between each node and the new position of the crack front after propagation. This position is defined by the new values of the level-sets after the propagation which are known only for the auxiliary grid. The new position of the background will be known to the physical mesh after the projection. The selection of the nodes interested in the projection must therefore necessarily be implemented from the position of the crack front before propagation. The torus to be used must therefore include all the possible new positions of the crack front and all the nodes of the torus used for the calculation of the local residue.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/100002010000020C0000018913FA25B1357C82EF.png)

**Figure** 4.2-4 **: selection of** nodes to project

We can look at the example given in the figure for a 2D crack. The maximum advancement of the crack is chosen by the user using the parameter DA_MAX of the operator PROPA_FISS. The position of the crack front after propagation is therefore included in the center circle coinciding with the position of the bottom before propagation and of radius equal to the value of DA_MAX (circle 1 in the figure). However, for the proper functioning of the other X-FEM routines of *Code_Aster*, it is necessary that the level-sets of the physical mesh be defined for all the nodes. For this we can simply preserve the value of the level-sets for all the nodes which are not affected by the projection. Each time, we can therefore take the level-sets of the physical mesh before the propagation and we can change the value of the level-sets only for the nodes affected by the projection. This solution is very simple to develop in code and is very fast. In addition it guarantees that the existing surface of the crack and the crack front are always correctly represented by the level-sets because the results of each projection are preserved behind the crack front. #### 4.2.2 “Simplex” method

The use of the auxiliary grid is not always convenient for the user, especially for complex structures. With this in mind, a method based on the same idea as the upwind method has been developed. Still with a fast marching algorithm, we will simply modify the calculation at the element level and work directly on simplex elements [:ref:` *22*  < *22* >`] (triangle, tetrahedron).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/1000020100000135000001467ED7DA61A734D9EA.png)

Figure 4.2-6: Simplex method on a triangle

To simplify things, let's take the example of a 2D case. We consider a triangle mesh with ${x}_{1}$, ${x}_{2}$ and ${x}_{3}$, the coordinates of the three nodes of the triangle (see ). Assuming the piecewise linear crack front, we can project each of the points onto it. $n$ is therefore the normal, unknown and constant by element.

Keeping in mind that the three points are projected onto a straight line with normal $n$, we have ${d}_{1}$, ${d}_{2}$ and ${d}_{3}$ which represent the values of the level-set on each of the three nodes. ${d}_{1}$ and ${d}_{2}$ being known, we therefore look for a way to calculate ${d}_{3}$. The idea used amounts to writing the equation of the projection of a point on a line for ${x}_{1}$, ${x}_{2}$ and ${x}_{3}$. We have:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{d}_{1}={x}_{1}\cdot n+d\\ {d}_{2}={x}_{2}\cdot n+d\\ {d}_{3}={x}_{3}\cdot n+d\end{array}
$$

Or again:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{d}_{1}-{d}_{3}=({x}_{1}-{x}_{3})\cdot n\\ {d}_{2}-{d}_{3}=({x}_{2}-{x}_{3})\cdot n\end{array}
\iff
$$

We therefore have a system of two equations and three unknowns. To obtain a solution, we will put the system in vector form:

$$
{V}^{t}n+{d}_{3}I=d
$$

With:

$$
\begin{array}{c}V=({x}_{1}-{x}_{3,}{x}_{2}-{x}_{3})\in {M}_{2}(\mathrm{ℝ})\\ d={({d}_{1},{d}_{2})}^{t}\\ I={(1,1)}^{t}\end{array}
$$

$V$ is invertible because the mesh cells are non-degenerate, which allows us to write the unknown $n$ in the following form:

$$
n={V}^{-t}(d-{d}_{3}I)
$$

$n$ being a unit vector, we can eliminate this equation from our system by writing that $1={n}^{t}n$ . Using the expression (), we end up with a quadratic equation where ${d}_{3}$ is the solution:

$$
1={n}^{t}n={({d}_{3})}^{2}{I}^{t}\mathit{QI}-2({d}_{3}){I}^{t}Qd+{d}^{t}Qd
$$

We end up with two solutions for the quadratic equation. For a consistent solution, you must choose the largest root of the equation.

**Choice of the solution of the quadratic equation**

Solving the system() leads to two solutions:

$$
{x}_{1}=\frac{{I}^{t}\mathit{Qd}+\sqrt{{({I}^{t}\mathit{Qd})}^{2}-{I}^{t}\mathit{QI}({d}^{t}Qd-1)}}{{I}^{t}\mathit{QI}}
$$

$$
{x}_{2}=\frac{{I}^{t}\mathit{Qd}-\sqrt{{({I}^{t}\mathit{Qd})}^{2}-{I}^{t}\mathit{QI}({d}^{t}Qd-1)}}{{I}^{t}\mathit{QI}}
$$

These two solutions are valid, but for our problem, we choose to keep () because it is the only one which respects the condition of obtaining increasing level-set values ${d}_{3}>{d}_{2}$ or ${d}_{3}>{d}_{1}$ .

We observe several possible configurations in our problem. Let's examine them all to convince ourselves of the choice of this solution.

Figure 4.2-7: First configuration

In the first configuration (see figure ()), we know the value of the level-sets at points $a$ and $b$ and we look for the value at point $c$. The solution ${x}_{1}$ of () gives a normal :math:`` and the solution in ${x}_{2}$ of () gives a normal $\vec{{n}_{2}}(0;-1)$.

The solution () is therefore the one which gives the exact solution of the value of the level-set at the point $c$, and which respects the condition $\mathit{ls}(c)>\mathit{ls}(b)$ or $\mathit{ls}(c)>\mathit{ls}(a)$. The value obtained with solution () in this case gives $\mathit{ls}(c)<\mathit{ls}(b)$ and $\mathit{ls}(c)<\mathit{ls}(a)$ .

The domain of validity of the solution () for our problem can be identified. We look at the case where the quadratic equation has only one root (double root), see figure ().

The idea of the method being to start from the lowest level-set values, close to iso-zero and to propagate these values, we see that the solution () is the best suited. We therefore choose to take the largest root of the quadratic equation in our process of resetting the level-sets, whatever the configuration. The strength of fast marching is to calculate the level-set value several times for the same node in different configurations around a node and to keep the best value. This therefore corrects the pathological cases where we had taken the solution () whereas it was () which calculated the exact solution (i.e. there exists at least one mesh containing the node in question for which the solution () is valid).

To guarantee that the solution is monotonically increasing, we can add a criterion to the choice of the solution.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/100000000000023700000189F25E998CEFF057B5.jpg)

Figure 4.2-10: Fourth configuration

Let ${d}_{1}$, ${d}_{2}$ and ${d}_{3}$ be the values of the level-set on any triangle mesh. We therefore hope that if ${d}_{1}$ and ${d}_{2}$ increase then ${d}_{3}$ also increases. The idea is to consider a level-set gradient:

$$
{\nabla}_{d}{d}_{3}=\left(\frac{\partial {d}_{3}}{\partial {d}_{1}};\frac{\partial {d}_{3}}{\partial {d}_{2}}\right)
$$

As the solution is monotonically increasing, we want ${({\nabla}_{d}{d}_{3})}_{i}>0,i\in ⟦1;2⟧$. By deriving the quadratic equation (), we have:

$$
2{d}_{3}{\nabla}_{d}{d}_{3}.{I}^{t}\mathit{QI}-2{\nabla}_{d}{d}_{3}.{I}^{t}\mathit{Qd}-2{d}_{3}\mathit{QI}+2\mathit{Qd}=0
$$

Hence:

$$
{\nabla}_{d}{d}_{3}=\frac{Q(d-{d}_{3}I)}{{I}^{t}Q(d-{d}_{3}I)}=\frac{{\mathit{QV}}^{t}n}{{I}^{t}Q{V}^{t}n}
$$

For ${({\nabla}_{d}{d}_{3})}_{i}>0$ , it is necessary that:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{({\mathit{QV}}^{t}n)}_{i}>0,i\in ⟦1;2⟧\\ {({\mathit{QV}}^{t}n)}_{i}<0,i\in ⟦1;2⟧\end{array}
$$

We saw previously that if ${d}_{3}>{d}_{2}$ and ${d}_{3}>{d}_{1}$, we have ${({V}^{t}n)}_{i}<0$. As $Q$ is symmetric positive definite, the only possibility for ${({\nabla}_{d}{d}_{3})}_{i}>0$ is that ${({\mathit{QV}}^{t}n)}_{i}<0,i\in ⟦1;2⟧$. In 2D, this means that $\vec{n}$ must come from inside the triangle.

**Proof**

We have ${\mathit{QV}}^{t}V=I$ and we put ${\mathit{QV}}^{t}=B=\left(\begin{array}{c}\vec{u}\\ \vec{v}\end{array}\right)$. We therefore have $\mathit{BV}=I\iff \lbrace \begin{array}{c}\vec{u}\cdot \vec{\mathit{ca}}=1\\ \vec{u}\cdot \vec{\mathit{cb}}=0\\ \vec{v}\cdot \vec{\mathit{ca}}=0\\ \vec{v}\cdot \vec{\mathit{cb}}=1\end{array}$

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/100000000000012B0000016B0E7304167F16D3F5.png)

To satisfy the problem, we set $\parallel \vec{u}\parallel =\frac{1}{\parallel \vec{\mathit{ca}}\parallel \cos(\alpha )}$ and $\parallel \vec{v}\parallel =\frac{1}{\parallel \vec{\mathit{cb}}\parallel \cos(\beta )}$ where $\cos(\alpha )\mathit{et}\cos(\beta )\ne 0$. We want ${(\mathit{Bn})}_{i}<0\Rightarrow \lbrace \begin{array}{c}\vec{u}\cdot \vec{n}<0\\ \vec{u}\cdot \vec{n}<0\end{array}\Rightarrow \vec{n}$ to come from inside the triangle.

##### 4.2.2.1 Simplex method algorithm

The path of the mesh cells in the structure remains similar to that of the upwind method. We select the node where the level-set reaches its minimum and we explore the triangles around it to calculate the unknown values.

- For each triangle in the mesh, we calculate the quadratic equation and keep the largest root
- The propagation direction $n={V}^{-t}(d-{d}_{3}I)$ is used to check a monotony criterion.

$$
{({\mathit{QV}}^{t}n)}_{i}<0,i\in ⟦1,2⟧
$$

By replacing $n$ in , we have:

$$
{(Q(d-{d}_{3}I))}_{i}<0,i\in ⟦1,2⟧
$$

This criterion means that $n$ must come from inside the triangle. This ensures the monotony of the solution.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000000000000FD000000D3B93C1ED9A76B9FB7.png)

- If this condition is not respected, we take the minimum value of one side of the triangle.

$$
{d}_{3}=min\lbrace {d}_{3},{\parallel \mathit{x1}-\mathit{x3}\parallel }_{2},{\parallel \mathit{x2}-\mathit{x3}\parallel }_{2}\rbrace
$$

**Note:** The generalization in 3D is very natural, the formulas being the same. The only difference comes when the condition ${(Q(d-{d}_{3}I))}_{i}<0,i\in ⟦1,3⟧$ is no longer respected: we then calculate the smallest distance on each face of the tetrahedron and we keep the smallest.

Unlike the upwind method, we no longer need the geometric method for the edge points. The geometric method is used only to obtain the initialisation values of the tangent level-set, always with the idea that the level-sets must remain orthogonal to each other.

### 4.3 Calculation of level-sets near iso-zeros

The methods presented previously only give exact results if the iso-zeros of the level-sets actually pass through nodes. When iso-zero passes between two nodes, it tends to be slightly distorted upon reset. An algorithm for calculating iso-zeros is therefore put in place. This geometrically calculates the distances signed on the nodes of the mesh cells cut or tangented by the iso-zero of the level-set and sets the value calculated for the reset.

Regarding reorthogonalization, the values of $\mathit{lst}$ located on the surface $(\mathit{lsn}=0)$ must not be modified; we will therefore take advantage of the calculation algorithm of $\mathit{lsn}$ to calculate the orthogonal *lst* values on the elements cut or tangent by $(\mathit{lsn}=0)$ and freeze these values during the reorthogonalization phase. For this, the value of $\mathit{lst}$ is interpolated on the triangles $\mathit{IJK}$ that can be formed with the intersection points $(\text{lsn}=0)\cap \text{arêtes}$. If for a node $M$, the orthogonal projection of $M$ is inside $\mathit{IJK}$, we interpolate the value of $\text{lst}(M)$ as $\mathit{lst}$ of the projected point. Otherwise, $\text{lst}(M)$ is calculated as $\mathit{lst}$ of the projection brought back inside the triangle $\mathit{IJK}$ (Figure 4.3-1).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000200000004090000025D2EB32178E141A6EF.png)

**Figure** 4.3-1 **: Calculation of distance and interpolation of lst on the elements cut by** $\mathit{lsn}=0$ **.**

In 3D, the algorithm implemented after the propagation of $\mathit{lsn}$ and before its reset is as follows:

- Loop on the mesh cells

  - If at least three nodes of the mesh are on $\mathit{lsn}=0$, we mark it as a cut mesh
  - If the mesh has an edge $[\mathit{AB}]$ such that $\text{lsn}(A).\text{lsn}(B)<0$, we locate the cut mesh
- End of loop
- Loop on nodes

  - If the node belongs to a cut mesh cell, it will be calculated
- End of loop
- Loop on the nodes $P$ to calculate

  - Loop on the cut mesh cells with the node at the top

    - Loop on the edges $[\mathit{AB}]$ of the mesh

      - If $\text{lsn}(A).\text{lsn}(B)\le 0$, we interpolate the point ${I}_{\text{AB}}$:

$\overrightarrow{{\text{AI}}_{\text{AB}}}=s.\overrightarrow{\text{AB}}$ with $s=\frac{\mid \text{lsn}(A)\mid }{\mid \text{lsn}(A)\mid +\mid \text{lsn}(B)\mid }$

and $\text{lst}({I}_{\text{AB}})=\text{lst}(A)+s.(\text{lst}(B)-\text{lst}(A))$

    - End of loop
    - Loop over all the triangles $\mathit{IJK}$ that can be formed with the points ${I}_{\text{AB}}$

      - We calculate the projection $M$ of $P$ on the triangle $\mathit{IJK}$
      - We identify whether the projected point $M$ is inside the triangle
      - We store the distance $d=\mathit{PM}$
      - We linearly interpolate $\text{lst}(M)$ from $\text{lst}(I)$, $\text{lst}(J)$ and $\text{lst}(K)$
    - End of loop
  - End of loop
  - Among the calculated distances $\mathit{PM}$ we search for the smallest distance with $M$ inside $\mathit{IJK}$. If no projection inside $\mathit{IJK}$ has been found, we just take the smallest calculated distance and the corresponding value of $\mathit{lst}$.
  - We store the corresponding values of $d$ and $\mathit{lst}$ for the node $P$.
- End of loop
- We replace $\mathit{lsn}$ and $\mathit{lst}$ on the nodes to be calculated by the new stored values of *d* and *lst*.

The nodes marked as “calculated nodes” have level-sets which are then frozen during the phases of reinitialization of $\mathit{lsn}$ and reorthogonalization of $\mathit{lst}$.

The algorithm launched after the propagation and reorthogonalization phases of $\mathit{lst}$ and before its reset is the same, but it only calculates the distances $\mathit{lst}$, in the same way as $\mathit{lsn}$ previously, without touching the values of $\mathit{lsn}$.

The algorithm described above is only valid in 3D because in 2D we cannot form triangles. In fact, only two intersection points can be detected for each element.

To use the same algorithm in 2D as for the 3D case, we can use the following solution. We form a triangle using the two intersection points ($A$ and $B$) and a virtual point ($C$). This is chosen so that the triangle formed is in a plane orthogonal to the plane of the model (plane $X-Y$). To avoid numerical problems, the coordinates of the virtual point ($C$) are calculated as follows:

    - $X,Y$ = coordinates of the midpoint of the edge connecting the two intersection points ($A$ and $B$),

    - $Z$ = distance between the two intersection points ($A$ and $B$).

## 5 Localisation of the level-set computation domain

In paragraph [§ 4.2.1.5 ] we said that, in the case of using an auxiliary grid for the upwind method, the values of the level-sets are updated, reset and re-orthogonalized for all the points of the grid but that only the values in a region around the crack front are projected onto the mesh of the structure. This shows that the updating of the level-sets, their reinitialization and their reorthogonalization are necessary only in the region around the crack front, independently of the use or not of an auxiliary grid, and that the domain already defined for the projection between auxiliary grid and mesh of the structure ([§ 4.2.1.5 ]) can also be used to locate the calculation domain. An advantage of this localisation is obviously that the calculation time necessary for updating and resetting the level-sets is lower compared to the case where all the nodes and elements of the mesh or the auxiliary grid are used. In fact, in the case where the level-sets are updated at nodes/points which are very far from the crack front in the presence of oscillations on the angle and the speed of propagation between successive points at the crack front, the iso-zeros of the normal level-set can present very marked irregularities because of the linear variation imposed on the normal speed ([§ 2.5 ]). An illustrative example is described in figure and figure. It shows a 3D crack propagating in mixed mode at a propagation angle which changes linearly along the crack front. Small disturbances were superimposed on the expected theoretical value, which means that the variation of the propagation angle with the position on the bottom is not perfectly linear (figure ). These disturbances simulate the presence of small errors in a numerical calculation of angle values.

### 5.1 Selection of nodes/points and elements of the localised calculation domain

The domain to be used for localisation is that already defined for the projection between the auxiliary grid and the mesh of the structure ([§ 4.2.1.5 ]). In fact this domain is extended to all the points used for the mechanical calculations and it makes it possible to preserve the surface of the existing crack, as already shown (figure). The methods will be applied only to nodes and elements contained in this domain, which is defined by circle 3 in Figure . In 3D this domain coincides with a torus constructed around the crack front and with a radius equal to that of circle 3. In all cases the radius of the localised domain is therefore equal to:

${R}_{\mathit{loc}}=\Delta a+{R}_{\mathit{calc}G}$

where $\Delta a$ is the advancement of the crack to impose (chosen by the user) and ${R}_{\mathit{calcG}}$ is the radius of the domain used by CALC_G.

We can therefore first select the nodes of the grid or the mesh which have a distance to the crack front less than or equal to ${R}_{\mathit{loc}}$ (figure on the left). This distance can be calculated very quickly because for each node we already know its projection on the crack front ([§ 2.2 ]). Then we take all the elements which have at least one of the nodes selected previously (gray elements in the figure on the right). This allows you to correctly select all elements that are cut by the domain boundary and are therefore partially contained within the domain. In this way, the entire surface (or 3D volume) of the domain is correctly covered by the selected elements. Finally the list of nodes in the domain must be updated by adding the nodes of the elements partially contained in the domain (nodes marked with a small circle in the figure on the right). The quickest and easiest way to do this is to rebuild the list by taking all the nodes of the selected elements. Once the list of nodes has been updated, the value of the radius ${R}_{\mathit{loc}}$ of the discretised domain, that is to say of the domain constituted by the nodes and the selected elements, must be updated by taking the value at the node of the discretised domain furthest from the background. This operation is necessary for the part which follows ([§ 5.3 ]).

### 5.2 Displacement of the localised domain following the propagation of the crack

The updating and resetting of level-sets are done exclusively at the nodes defining the localised domain (nodes marked with triangles and circles on the right figure). This means that the level-set values at nodes outside the localised domain are not changed, which causes problems during successive propagation steps. The situation is illustrated in the figure for a point ${P}_{1}$ in the plane $t-n$ (plane ${e}_{1}-{e}_{2}$ of the figure). At the start of the propagation the crack front coincides with the point ${P}_{1}$ and the localised domain with the circle ${\Omega}_{1}$. To simulate propagation, the level-sets are updated and reset only in this circle. The new position of the bottom after the first propagation step coincides with ${P}_{2}$. At the second propagation step, the crack front coincides with the point ${P}_{2}$ and a new domain ${\Omega}_{2}$ is constructed around this point for updating and reinitialization of the level-sets. The value of the level-sets was not updated at the previous propagation step for all the points of ${\Omega}_{2}$: only the level-sets of the points of ${\Omega}_{2}$ in common with ${\Omega}_{1}$ are up to date (points on the gray surface of the figure). For all other points, the value of the level-sets is that which we had at the start of the propagation. The correct values at these other points must therefore be calculated at the start of the second propagation step.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000201000001B200000163850D3B7B82B85CD1.png)

**Figure** 5.2-1 **: displacement of the localised domain between two successive propagation steps. The crack front moves from** ${P}_{1}$ **to** ${P}_{2}$ **.**

The calculation of the correct values of the level-sets for all the points in the white region of the domain ${\Omega}_{2}$ of the figure can be done using the definition of signed distance of the level-sets (bib 14). For each point $M$ in this region (figure), the level-set can be calculated like this:

${\varphi}_{n}(\text{M})=\overrightarrow{\text{M}{\text{P}}_{2}}\cdot {n}_{2}$

${\varphi}_{t}(\text{M})=\overrightarrow{\text{M}{\text{P}}_{2}}\cdot {t}_{2}$

These equations make it possible to calculate the correct value of the level-sets with the correct sign. It should be noted that, in the 3D case, the point ${P}_{2}$ is the projected point of the point $M$ on the crack front. For each point $M$, its projection and the base ${n}_{2}-{t}_{2}$ have already been calculated during the phase of extension of the velocity from the crack front to the points of the calculation domain ([§ 2.2 ]). All the ingredients of the calculation are therefore already available and for each point $M$ the evaluation of the correct level-set is limited to the calculation of two scalar products, which is very quick to do.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000201000001B40000016320EC52EB6D4091C0.png)

### 5.3 Limitations on the value of the radius of the domain located between two successive propagations

The equations given in paragraph 33 for the calculation of the correct values of the level-sets can be applied only to the points for which the base ${n}_{2}-{t}_{2}$ is valid for the calculation of the signed distance. This means that the point $M$ must belong to the half-plane shown in figure, which contains the base ${n}_{2}-{t}_{2}$, the crack front ${P}_{2}$ and which has as its edge a straight line normal to the edge connecting ${P}_{1}$ and ${P}_{2}$ parallel to ${n}_{2}$.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000201000002BC00000274393C920FCC03D03A.png)

**Figure** 5.3-1 **: definition of the half-plane to which the point** $M$ **of the figure** **must belong so that the equations for calculating the level-sets in paragraph** 33 **are valid.**

This condition on the position of the point $M$ translates into a condition on the maximum value of the radius ${R}_{2}$ of the domain ${\Omega}_{2}$: the maximum value is that for which the two points of intersection ($A$ and $B$) between the two domains ${\Omega}_{1}$ and ${\Omega}_{2}$ are on the edge of the half-plane of validity of the equations, as shown in figure . These two points are always symmetrical with respect to the edge connecting ${P}_{1}$ and ${P}_{2}$. The maximum value of radius ${R}_{2}$ can therefore be easily calculated:

${R}_{2}\le \sqrt{\Delta {a}^{2}+{R}_{1}^{2}}$

The value of the radius of the current domain is therefore bounded by the values of the advancement of the crack and the radius of the domain used at the previous propagation step. Normally there is no interest in changing the value of the domain radius between two successive propagations. In this case, as ${R}_{1}$ is equal to ${R}_{2}$, the inequality on ${R}_{2}$ is necessarily verified whatever the value of the chosen advance.

It should however be noted that the value of the radius of the domain can change compared to the theoretical value given by the user due to the discretisation of the domain ([§ 5.1 ] and figure on the right). So, even if we give ${R}_{2}={R}_{1}$ to the current propagation step, the effective value of the domain radius will be the following:

${R}_{2\text{eff}}={R}_{2}+\Delta {R}_{2}={R}_{1}+\Delta {R}_{2}$

where $\Delta {R}_{2}$ is the increment of the radius ${R}_{2}$ due to the discretisation of the domain, which is of the order of the average length of the edges of the elements around the boundary of the localised domain (see figure).

For the same reason, the effective value of the radius ${R}_{1}$ at the previous propagation step may be different from the theoretical value given by the user:

${R}_{1\text{eff}}={R}_{1}+\Delta {R}_{1}$

where $\Delta {R}_{1}$ is the increment of the radius ${R}_{1}$ due to the discretisation of the domain, which is of the order of the average length of the edges of the elements around the boundary of the domain located at the previous propagation step.

So the above inequality can be written in the following form:

${R}_{1}+\Delta {R}_{2}\le \sqrt{\Delta {a}^{2}+{({R}_{1}+\Delta {R}_{1})}^{2}}$

In the case where the length of the edges of the auxiliary grid used is uniform in the domain, $\Delta {R}_{2}\approx \Delta {R}_{1}$, and the inequality is always verified. On the other hand, if this length changes in the domain, we could have $\Delta {R}_{2}>\Delta {R}_{1}$ and the inequality could not be verified.

In the latter case we must necessarily refine the mesh (or the auxiliary grid) to reduce the difference between $\Delta {R}_{1}$ and $\Delta {R}_{2}$ or use a higher crack advance $\Delta a$.

## 6 Direct computation of the new level-sets after advance

An alternative method to the level-set updating methods is that described in (bib 18). It is derived from the analysis of the level-set updating equations described in paragraphs 3.1: we manage to show that the level-set updating process can always be reduced to a 2D process in mode $I+\mathit{II}$ in the plane formed by the tangent and the normal to the surface of the crack at the crack front (plane ${n}_{P}-{t}_{P}$ in the figure). This makes it possible to enormously simplify the direct calculation of the new values of the level-sets after progress. This calculation has actually already been explained in paragraph § 5.2 with regard to the evaluation of the level-sets for the points outside the calculation domain which are added. The equations described there can be used in the case where the new position of the bottom is known. Knowing that the problem of updating the level-sets can always be reduced to a 2D problem, the new position of the bottom can be easily calculated from the advancement of the crack and the propagation angle at the point of the bottom considered.

### 6.1 Level-set calculation method

These ideas are taken up in figure , which shows the configuration of the iso-surfaces of the two level-sets around a point $P$ at the bottom of a 3D crack obtained by the updating process described in paragraph § 3.1

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/1000000000000391000003382755E7F80E03F0A8.png)

**Figure** 6.1-1 **: iso-surfaces of the two level-sets obtained by the standard updating process, with reinitialization and reorthogonalization.** $P$ **is a point at the bottom of a 3D crack.** $Q$ **is the new position of this point after an advance of** $\Delta a$ **with a propagation angle** $\beta$ **.**

We consider a point $M$ of the calculation domain where we must calculate the new value of the two level-sets. We project this point onto the crack front using the same algorithm as that described in paragraph 2.2 and we obtain the point $P$ (figure ). The local basis $({t}_{P},{n}_{P})$ and forward speed ${V}_{P}$ at point $P$ can be calculated as described in paragraphs 2.2, 2.3 and 2.4. For the propagation angle, we use the same linear interpolation as that used for the speed (see paragraph § 2.2 and figure:

$$
\beta =({\beta}_{\text{J}}-{\beta}_{\text{I}})\cdot {s}_{min}+{\beta}_{\text{I}}
$$

The advancement of the crack at the point $P$ of the bottom can therefore be easily calculated:

$$
\Delta \overrightarrow{a}=∥{V}_{\text{P}}∥\cdot \Delta t\cdot (\sin\beta \cdot {n}_{\text{P}}+\cos\beta \cdot {t}_{\text{P}})
$$

Where $\Delta t$ is the time or, in equivalent terms, the number of fatigue cycles to simulate at the current propagation step (see discussion in paragraph 3.1).

The new position $Q$ of the bottom point $P$ can therefore be calculated like this:

$$
\overrightarrow{\mathit{OQ}}=\overrightarrow{\mathit{OP}}+\Delta \overrightarrow{a}
$$

Where $O$ is the origin point of the global base in which the coordinates of the points are expressed.

Finally the base at the point $Q$ can be calculated by a rotation of angle  of the base at the point P around the axis normal to the plane ${t}_{P}-{n}_{P}$:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{t}_{\text{Q}}=\cos\beta \cdot {t}_{\text{P}}+\sin\beta \cdot {n}_{\text{P}}\\ {n}_{\text{Q}}=-\sin\beta \cdot {t}_{\text{P}}+\cos\beta \cdot {n}_{\text{P}}\end{array}
$$

All the ingredients for calculating level-sets are now known. We can therefore use the same equations as those used in paragraph 5.2:

$$
{\varphi}_{n}(\text{M})=\vec{\text{QM}}\cdot {n}_{\text{Q}}
$$

In order to preserve the existing surface of the crack (iso-surfaces in gray in the figure), the calculation of the normal level-set with the equation above is done only at the points in the domain where ${\varphi}_{t}(\text{M})>0$.

### 6.2 Treatment of problem points

The equations written in the previous paragraph apply if the vector $\overrightarrow{\text{PM}}$ is in the plane ${t}_{P}-{n}_{P}$ (figure) (bib 18). This is not the case for all points which do not have a point of normal projection on the crack front. In this case an end point of the background is selected as projected point P by the projection algorithm described in paragraph 2.2.

These problematic points can be treated by a rotation of the base $({t}_{P},{n}_{P})$ which makes it possible to reestablish the condition of coplanarity of the plane ${t}_{P}-{n}_{P}$ and the vector $\overrightarrow{\text{PM}}$ or $\overrightarrow{\text{QM}}$ (bib 18).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/1000000000000439000001DC1FD7321FA131123D.png)

**Figure** 6.2-1 **: a) example of problem point. b) rotation of the base at the point** $P$ **to restore the condition of coplanarity between the plane** ${t}_{P}-{n}_{P}$ **and the vectors** $\overrightarrow{\text{PM}}$ or $\overrightarrow{\text{QM}}$ **.**

An example of a problem point can be seen in figure a. To restore the coplanarity condition, we can rotate $\theta$ of the base $({t}_{P},{n}_{P})$ around the vector ${n}_{P}$ in such a way that the plane ${t}_{P}^{\text{*}}-{n}_{P}$ contains the point $M$ (figure b). Indeed, in this way the new position of the point $P$ after propagation (point $Q$ in the figure) is contained in the plane ${t}_{P}^{\text{*}}-{n}_{P}$, which makes it possible to verify the coplanarity condition.

The new vector ${t}_{\text{P}}^{\text{*}}$ can be calculated from the projection ${M}^{\text{*}}$ of the point $M$ on the plane normal to the axis ${n}_{P}$ (figure b). We first calculate:

$$
\vec{{\text{M}}^{\text{*}}\text{M}}=\left(\vec{\text{P}\text{M}}\cdot {n}_{\text{P}}\right)\cdot {n}_{\text{P}}
$$

Which allows us to obtain the new vector:

$$
{t}_{\text{P}}^{\text{*}}=\frac{\overrightarrow{\text{PM}}-\overrightarrow{{\text{M}}^{\text{*}}\text{M}}}{\mathrm{\parallel }\overrightarrow{\text{PM}}\mathrm{\parallel }-\mathrm{\parallel }\overrightarrow{{\text{M}}^{\text{*}}\text{M}}\mathrm{\parallel }}
$$

To correctly take into account the sign of the level-settangent, the vector ${t}_{\text{P}}^{\text{*}}$ must be reoriented following the direction given by ${t}_{\text{P}}$ (bib 18):

$$
{t}_{\text{P}}^{'}=\frac{{t}_{\text{P}}^{\text{*}}\cdot {t}_{\text{P}}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{t}_{\text{P}}^{\text{*}}\cdot {t}_{\text{P}}\mathrm{\parallel }}\cdot {t}_{\text{P}}^{\text{*}}
$$

If the two vectors ${t}_{\text{P}}^{\text{*}}$ and ${t}_{\text{P}}$ are orthogonal, the equation above cannot be applied but we can immediately write that:

$$
{t}_{\text{P}}^{'}={t}_{\text{P}}^{\text{*}}
$$

The use of the vector ${t}_{\text{P}}^{'}$ instead of ${t}_{\text{P}}$ in the equations of paragraph § 6.1 makes it possible to correctly calculate the level-sets for the problem points.

### 6.3 Discontinuity of the normal level-set and calculation limitations

Calculating the normal level-set only at the points $M$ for which ${\varphi}_{t}(\text{M})>0$ results in a discontinuity of the normal level-set, as demonstrated in (bib 18). The situation is illustrated in Figure . The normal level-set was not recalculated in the area colored in gray implying a discontinuity of iso-surfaces between the area in gray and the area where the level-set was recalculated.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000000000003BA000003423DC1670700D5866B.png)

**Figure** 6.3-1 **: discontinuity of the iso-surfaces of the normal level-set and maximum radius** ${R}_{max}$ **of the calculation domain of** CALC_G **to use.**
As demonstrated in (bib 18), this discontinuity does not cause any problem for the X-FEM calculations (identification of the new cut mesh cells in particular). In addition, the continuity of the iso-zero of the normal level-set is preserved, which ensures the continuity of the crack surface. The only condition to respect to manage this discontinuity concerns the maximum value of the calculation radius of CALC_G (see U4.82.03). This condition aims to prevent the calculation domain of CALC_G from containing the discontinuity (gray circle in the figure):

$$
{R}_{max}=\Delta {a}_{max}\cdot \cos{\beta}_{max}
$$

Where we take the maximum value of $\Delta a$ and $\beta$ along the crack front.

If it restricts the propagation angle to values strictly lower than $\pi /2$ (under penalty of an increasingly important refinement as we approach $\pi /2$), this condition is not very restrictive because it is advisable to respect a similar relationship even in the case of updating the level-sets by the simplex or upwind method.

### 6.4 Localisation of the domain

As shown in (bib 18), the localisation of the domain described in paragraph § 5 is no longer necessary if we use the direct calculation of the level-sets.

First of all, localizing the domain would not improve the robustness of the algorithm. In (bib 14), it is shown that small errors in the calculation of the propagation angle (which is always the case during numerical simulations) are amplified at points located far from the crack front (due to the linear interpolation used to create the velocity field ${V}_{N}$). The iso-surfaces of the normal level-set are then no longer regular and this can lead to the failure of the level-set reset phases. The localisation of the domain thus makes it possible to reduce the level-set updating domain to a domain encompassing the crack front, which leads to a limitation of the amplification effect. However, the geometric method successfully handles non-regular iso-surfaces since it is based on explicit polynomial equations and not on differential equations. Furthermore, the geometric method does not use a process of reinitialization and reorthogonalization of the level-sets (which are calculated at each propagation step). For all these reasons, the location of the domain does not affect the robustness of the geometric method.

Locating the domain when using the geometric method would also not necessarily reduce the calculation time necessary for updating the level-sets. It turns out that the calculation of the level-sets at each point of the domain is fast and that the time necessary for this calculation can be lower than the time necessary to select the points of the localised domain. The algorithm used for the selection of points belonging to the restricted domain is based on the calculation of the distance between a point $M$ of the domain and its projected $P$ on the crack front. As the point $P$ must also be calculated for the calculation of the level-sets, the additional calculation cost for the selection algorithm is reduced to the cost of calculating the distance $∣∣\vec{\mathit{PM}}∣∣$ and the complexity of the algorithm is $O(n)$. Even if the complexity of the algorithm for updating the level-sets by the geometric method is $O(n)$, it appears clear that the sum of the calculations carried out by the selection algorithm will be less than that carried out by the algorithm for updating the level-sets. This leads us to think at first glance that the localisation of the domain would be advantageous in terms of calculation time compared to the calculation of level-sets throughout the domain. However, the selection (based on the distance $∣∣\vec{\mathit{PM}}∣∣$) of points inside the restricted domain is not sufficient. Indeed, for the calculation of the local basis at each point, it is a question of determining the gradient of the level-sets which requires the selection of the elements containing the selected points (the shape functions of the elements being used to calculate the gradients). Even if the selection algorithm does not necessarily change, the number of operations performed increases and this can make the calculation time of the selection higher than the calculation of level-sets by the geometric method throughout the domain. Note that, at the same time, the time to calculate the gradients of the level-sets is also higher if the location of the domain is not taken into account.

Finally, the advantage of domain localisation in terms of calculation time strongly depends on the algorithms and implementations chosen and the number of points in the calculation domain. The localisation of the domain could thus be advantageous in the case of “very large” finite element mesh cells. In this case, limiting the updating of the level-sets to a group of nodes (fixed and defined by the user) defining the potential propagation surface of the crack can be a more efficient way to reduce the calculation time rather than the choice of a domain localisation algorithm for which a new restricted domain must be calculated at each propagation step in order to follow the movement of the crack front.

## 7 Mesh method for updating level-sets

The most intuitive method for updating the level-set values at each crack propagation step is to use their signed distance property. This involves an explicit representation of the crack (crack lips and crack front) propagated in order to calculate the distances from the nodes to the crack. This method thus uses two distinct mesh cells (crack and structure) and only the crack mesh is modified during propagation. Updating the crack mesh at each propagation step concretely consists of creating a new row of surface mesh cells to represent the advancement of the crack and a new row of linear mesh cells to represent the new front. Once this mesh has been updated, we recalculate the new level-sets by direct calculation of the distance to the crack (orthogonal projection on the crack mesh).

### 7.1 Presentation of the method

The mesh of the crack is carried out in *Code_Aster* by the PROPA_FISS command. The input data is the value of the propagation vector on the crack front, determined from the stress intensity factors and the Paris law. The principle of the mesh method is to calculate the nodes of the new front from the propagation speeds and the nodes of the current front. It comes down to the following three steps:

  - Calculation by linear interpolation of the propagation vector at each node of the discretised crack front. The propagation vector is orthogonal to the crack front: the direction of propagation at a node corresponds to the average of the normal vectors of the two linear elements sharing this node. This step gives the position of each propagated point.
  - Addition of a node at each of these points, creation of a new surface (addition of linear elements for the new crack front and quadrangle elements for the new surface) and meshing process of this created surface.
  - Specific treatment of edge nodes which, depending on the case, can “come out” of the structure. They are then moved to the borders of the structure.

For 2D modelling, the method is even simpler. Only one linear element needs to be added at each propagation step and no edge point correction is necessary.

### 7.2 Algorithm

The algorithm implemented for propagation by the mesh method is the following (as described in (bib 20) and in the figure). At iteration $i$:

    - Recovery of the crack mesh,
    - Recovery of the end points of the crack front,
    - Fixed crack mesh:
- We modify the coordinates of the two end nodes of the crack front so that they correspond to the end points of the crack front
- We modify the coordinates of all the other bottom nodes so that they are equally distributed between the two ends (number of bottom nodes $\mathit{NbNf}$ ).
- Loop on the bottom nodes: $1⩽k⩽\mathit{NbNf}$

  - Calculation of $\Delta G(k)$ by linear interpolation,
  - Calculation of the bifurcation angle $\beta$ from ${K}_{I}(k)$ and ${K}_{\mathit{II}}(k)$,
  - Calculation of the propagation vector by linear interpolation:
  - $\Delta a(k)=C{\left(\Delta G(k)\right)}^{m/2}$ if $C$ and $m$ are specified,
  - $\Delta a(k)={\mathit{DA}}_{\mathit{MAX}}{\left(\Delta G(k)/{max}_{k}(\Delta G(k))\right)}^{m/2}$ if ${\mathit{DA}}_{\mathit{MAX}}$ and $m$ are specified,
  - Calculation of the propagated position of the node ${N}_{k}$,
  - Added a new node.
- end of loop
- Loop between $1$ and $\mathit{NbNf}-1$

  - addition of a new SEG2 mesh between each of the bottom nodes and the next node,
  - addition of a QUA4 mesh between two successive nodes from the bottom to iteration $i-1$ and their propagated,
  - in 2D: addition of a POI mesh for the background and SEG2 between the previous background and the new front.
- End of loop,
- Creation of mesh cells $\mathit{FOND}$ (new front) and $\mathit{LEVRE}$ (previous lip + new surface mesh cells),
- Update of the mesh.

## 8 Separation and fusion of crack fronts

Different methods have been presented to follow the propagation of a crack with X-FEM (ie to update the level-set values at each propagation step):

- The mesh method (or by projection), see paragraph § 7,
- The geometric method, see paragraph§ 6.
- Upwind and simplex methods based on fast marching algorithms

The aim here is to explain the modifications to be made to these different methods to account for the behaviour of a crack front when it encounters an obstacle (a hole for example). How to manage the separation of this crack front into two, or even several other pieces? Likewise, what about the merger of two independent crack fronts into one? These are the level-sets which determine the crack front and therefore the presence or not of several crack fronts. But in certain cases it is a question of making improvements to the previous methods so that they adapt to the separation of the crack front (modification of the creation of the crack mesh for the mesh method, continuity of the level-sets in the hole for the upwind method with use of an auxiliary grid).

Depending on the methods used for managing the updating of the level-sets, the strategy implemented to account for the separation or merger of crack fronts differs.

### 8.1 Mesh method

For the mesh method, a first option is to extend what is done in paragraph 7.2 to the framework of several crack fronts. The level-sets give information on the number and position of the ends of the crack fronts (intersection between the mesh of the structure and the iso-zero of the two level-sets). While retaining the nodes which constitute the ends already existing at time $i-1$, the nodes constituting the new ends are selected (figure a.).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/1000000000000300000000C424EE78662709CE5A.png)

**Figure** 8.1-1 **: a. nodes determining the ends of the crack fronts (in blue: nodes selected for the already existing ends, in red: nodes selected for the new ends), b. repositioning of the selected nodes on the different fronts, c. calculation of propagated nodes and formation of new mesh cells.**

As in paragraph 7.2, a procedure for repositioning the nodes of the different fronts (figure b.) is carried out. All that remains is to calculate the propagated nodes and form the mesh cells (figure c.).

Although this method works correctly, it has a disadvantage: the loss of nodes when two backgrounds are separated. The mesh cells can, moreover, deform significantly during the repositioning phase of the nodes outside the structure. This can in particular lead to poor discretisation of the out-of-plane propagation crack.

To overcome these difficulties, the idea was to increase the number of nodes on each crack front. There remains the question of creating these nodes and the mesh. Noting that the mesh cells representing the crack are only used to make projections, it is possible to create a row of mesh cells for the advancement of cracks that does not conform to the previous row of mesh cells (figure). In this way, we can increase the number of nodes between two propagation steps without modifying the discretisation induced by the propagation speeds.

The algorithm is therefore summarized as follows: search for the nodes closest to the ends of the background, repositioning on the edge, creation of new nodes on the current front positioned equidistant from each other, calculation of the nodes of the new front. As there are the same number of nodes between the two backgrounds, quadrangles respecting the surfaces of the background are created.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/1000000000000149000000E5F639150493A4B0B7.jpg)

**Figure** 8.1-2 **:** non-compliant mesh (in red; nodes created for the new row of mesh cells).
The total number $N$ of nodes on all crack fronts must remain constant between two propagation steps. To do this, we distribute the ${N}_{i}$ nodes per background proportionally to the ratio between the curvilinear length ${s}_{i}$ of the background $i$ and the total curvilinear length of the $n$ backgrounds:

$$
{N}_{i}=E\left(\frac{{s}_{i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}{s}_{j}}N\right)
$$

Where $E(x)$ represents the integer part of $x$. As there may be cases for which the sum of ${N}_{i}$ is less than $N$, we distribute the remaining nodes to the funds with the largest decimal part.

**Note:**

The mesh method can be problematic when the crack is concave. This case can be encountered in particular when two crack fronts come together (figure a.) and is absolutely to be avoided because significant problems are created by the calculation of the level-sets (false values, appearance of false crack fronts).

### 8.2 Upwind method: introduction of a virtual front

As noted in paragraph 4.2.1.4, the use of the upwind method is very generally accompanied by the use of an auxiliary grid (whose mesh is regular) on which the updating of the level-sets is carried out which are subsequently projected onto the physical mesh of the part (generally much less regular). However, part of the grid can be located outside the mesh of the part, as for example in the case of a structure containing a hole.

The grid nodes located outside the part mesh are then projected onto the same point of the crack front when calculating the level-sets propagation speed. All these nodes are therefore assigned the same propagation speeds and angles as well as the same local bases (as described in paragraph 2.3). This results in the appearance of a discontinuity of the level-sets on the grid at the level of the free edge of the part (see figure).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/1000000000000397000002468BE50840C7E32197.png)

**Figure** 8.2-1 **: example of discontinuity of level-sets on the grid at the free edge.**
This discontinuity, although located outside the physical mesh, implies a poor definition of the gradient of the level-sets on the free edge and therefore a poor definition of the local basis during the next propagation step. It can also influence the definition of the level-sets inside the mesh, particularly during the propagation of cracks in parts having an irregular section and/or holes. To avoid these problems and consistently define the level-sets on the grid in the vicinity of the physical mesh, an interpolation of the crack front outside the mesh by a line tangent to the crack front is proposed. For this, the last segment of the crack front is extended by a straight line segment at the end of which a new node of the crack front, described as virtual, is introduced. This point is then considered as a point of the crack front. The same local basis as that of the last point of the physical crack front is assigned to it. The propagation speed and angle are calculated by linear interpolation from the propagation speeds and angles of the last two points (figure).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/100000000000026B0000016C3B002298ABCC347D.png)

**Figure** 8.2-2 **: representation of the linear approximation of the speed at the virtual point** ${V}_{v}$ **(in red) from the speeds of the last two points of the crack front** ${V}_{i}$ **and** ${V}_{j}$ **(in blue).**

To create the points of the virtual front, we proceed as follows (figure):

- we set ${P}_{i}$ and ${P}_{j}$ two points located at the end of the crack front and $d$ the distance from the virtual point ${P}_{v}$ to the last point of the crack front ${P}_{j}$,
- we determine the line extending the last segment of the crack front at the virtual point: $\overrightarrow{{P}_{\mathit{jv}}}=\mathit{d.}\overrightarrow{{b}_{j}}$ where $\overrightarrow{{P}_{\mathit{jv}}}$ is the vector connecting ${P}_{j}$ to ${P}_{v}$ and $\overrightarrow{{b}_{j}}$ is the unit vector tangent to the crack front at point ${P}_{j}$,
- we calculate the spatial derivatives of the speed and the angle of propagation on the last segment of the virtual front:

$$
\frac{\mathit{dV}}{\mathit{ds}}=\frac{{V}_{j}-{V}_{i}}{∣∣\overrightarrow{{P}_{ij}}∣∣},\frac{d\beta }{\mathit{ds}}=\frac{{\beta}_{j}-{\beta}_{i}}{∣∣\overrightarrow{{P}_{ij}}∣∣}
$$

- we deduce the speed and the angle of propagation at the virtual front:

$$
{V}_{v}={V}_{j}+\mathit{d.}\frac{\mathit{dV}}{\mathit{ds}},{\beta}_{v}={\beta}_{j}+\mathit{d.}\frac{d\beta }{\mathit{ds}}
$$

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/1000020000000154000000ECE084E705D6690605.png)

**Figure** 8.2-3 **: creation and location of the virtual crack front.**
We place the virtual point at a distance $d$ from the physical front equal to the radius of the torus used for the propagation of the level-sets (see paragraph 5). We choose this distance in order to obtain smoothed level-sets over the entire propagation zone. If localisation is not used, the virtual point is placed at a distance of seven times the maximum crack advancement. For propagation speeds and angles (defined on the virtual points) that are too high or too low, the virtual point is brought closer to the physical front so as to satisfy the imposed conditions. We note for example that we have a significant discontinuity of the level-sets if the propagation angle is greater (respectively less) than 90 degrees (respectively -90 degrees). We also note that if the distance from the virtual point to the physical crack front is less than the localisation radius, we can maintain a discontinuity of the gradients. However, this discontinuity is located far from the mesh of the part. It therefore does not influence the definition of the local basis on the free edge.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000000000003980000011FF029E2DAE4D73A9C.png)

**Figure** 8.2-4 **: creation of a virtual front (in red) inside a circular hole.** ${P}_{1}$ **is created by extension of** $[{P}_{i}{P}_{j}]$ **and** ${P}_{2}$ **by extension of** $[{P}_{l}{P}_{k}]$ **. The last segment of the virtual bottom connects** ${P}_{1}$ **and** ${P}_{2}$ **and connects the crack fronts.**
The virtual front method can then be immediately extended to the case of holes. If the part has a hole, it is also important to clearly define the level-sets inside it. We then proceed as described previously by defining a virtual crack front inside the hole by creating two virtual points ${P}_{1}$ and ${P}_{2}$, one for each crack front delimited by the hole (figure). Two of the three segments of the virtual front are extensions of the two physical crack fronts. The third segment connects the two virtual points in order to connect the crack fronts. It is not used to interpolate the level-sets but to ensure their continuity in the hole. The two virtual points having different propagation speeds, two neighboring nodes located in the center of the hole can be projected onto two different crack front nodes and have different speeds.

The propagation angles and speeds as well as the local basis on the segment $[{P}_{1}{P}_{2}]$ are calculated by linear interpolation (as described in paragraph 2.4) from the values assigned to the two virtual points.

In the case of creating a virtual bottom in a hole, we must ensure that there is no crossing of the crack fronts. The distance from a virtual point to the crack front must not exceed one third of the distance between the two tips. Either:

$$
d\le \frac{∣∣{P}_{k}-{P}_{j}∣∣}{3}
$$

In addition, the propagation speed at the virtual point ${V}_{v}$ must not be too small or too large. It is required that it be at least equal to two thirds of the speed Vj of the last crack front and that it must not exceed four thirds of this value. Thus, we impose that:

$$
\frac{2{V}_{j}}{3}\le {V}_{v}\le \frac{4{V}_{j}}{3}
$$

Likewise:

$$
\textrm{Si}
{V}_{v}<\frac{2{V}_{j}}{3}
$$

And:

$$
\textrm{Si}
{V}_{v}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}>\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}\frac{4{V}_{j}}{3}
$$

We deduce the expression for the distance $d$ in these two cases:

$$
d=∣\frac{{V}_{j}}{3\frac{\mathit{dV}}{\mathit{ds}}}∣
$$

However, creating a virtual front is not enough. The values of the level-sets defined inside the hole, resulting from the projection onto the middle segment of the virtual front, can in certain cases influence the definition of the level-sets in the mesh. Furthermore, the approximation of the speed is more precise if the level-sets are initially straight and if the intersection of the two iso-zeros is confused with the virtual front. In order to guarantee the straightening of the level-sets in the hole during the fusion of the crack fronts after the passage of the latter, we recalculate the level-sets for the nodes projected onto a virtual segment of the crack front, and this before the propagation of the level-sets. We calculate the level-sets geometrically (see paragraph 6) by projecting the node onto the crack front (figure). We place $\mathit{XM}$ a node of the mesh. We then define its projection $\mathit{XN}$ on the crack front, the vectors $\vec{\mathit{tp}}$ and $\vec{\mathit{np}}$ defining the local basis at the crack front in $\mathit{XN}$. We obtain the normal and tangent level-sets at the nodes $\mathit{XM}$ simply by: $\mathit{lsn}(\mathit{XM})=\overrightarrow{\mathit{XM}\mathit{XN}}.\overrightarrow{\mathit{np}}$ and $\mathit{lst}(\mathit{XM})=\overrightarrow{\mathit{XM}\mathit{XN}}.\overrightarrow{\mathit{tp}}$ .

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.13/10000000000001FB000000D0277CA578995FA12D.png)

**Figure** 8.2-5 **: Calculation of the level-sets at the** nodes **projected onto the crack front.**
This calculation of the level-sets makes it possible to correct the influence of the non-physical definition of the level-sets at the center of the hole during the fusion of the two crack fronts. Once the virtual front has been created and the level-sets have been corrected, the calculation of the propagation speeds by projection onto the crack front is carried out as described in paragraph 2.3, without making a distinction between the physical front and the virtual front.

**Note**:

- The introduction of a virtual front requires a strategy for verifying the numbering of the crack fronts as well as their direction of travel, which was not previously necessary: the connection of the crack fronts by a virtual front no longer makes them independent of each other. The following method was adopted to check whether the nodes of the different crack fronts are correctly numbered (figure):

  - we construct a new crack front on the auxiliary grid which by definition of the grid is only made up of a single piece with a single direction of travel. We then project the ends ${E}_{1}^{i}$ and ${E}_{2}^{i}$ of each of the pieces $i$ of the real crack front onto the continuous crack front associated with the grid. These ends are identified on the crack front of the grid by their respective curvilinear abscissa. As an example, at the ends ${E}_{1}^{i}$ and ${E}_{2}^{i}$ correspond the curvilinear abscissa ${s}_{{P}_{1}^{i}}$ and ${s}_{{P}_{2}^{i}}$ (where ${P}_{1}^{i}$ and ${P}_{2}^{i}$ are respectively the projections of ${E}_{1}^{i}$ and ${E}_{2}^{i}$ on the crack front associated with the auxiliary grid);
  - the sequence of curvilinear abscissas thus obtained allows a renumbering of the pieces of the fronts of the real crack;
  - we evaluate the scalar product $({E}_{2}^{i}{E}_{1}^{i}).({P}_{2}^{i}{P}_{1}^{i})$ to verify that each of the pieces is traveled in the same direction as the crack front on the grid. Otherwise, we reorient the poorly oriented piece(s).

### 8.3 Geometric and simplex method

The geometric method and the simplex method do not require any auxiliary grid, the management of the separation and fusion of crack fronts is automatic and does not require any particular modification.
