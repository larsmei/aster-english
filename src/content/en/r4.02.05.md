# r4.02.05 Absorbing boundary elements

**Summary:**

This document describes the implementation in code_aster of absorbing boundary elements. These elements, of paraxial type, whose theory is described here, are assigned to boundaries of elastic or fluid domains in order to treat 2D or 3D problems of soil–structure or soil–fluid–structure interaction. They make it possible to satisfy the Sommerfeld condition verifying the anechoic assumption: the elimination of non-physical diffracted elastic or acoustic plane waves coming from infinity.

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Problem of a semi-infinite medium for SSI

Standard problems of seismic response and of soil–structure or soil–fluid–structure interaction lead one to consider domains that are infinite or assumed to be so. For example, in the case of dams subjected to an earthquake, one often deals with reservoirs of large size which allow us to make the anechoic assumption: the waves that travel towards the far end of the reservoir do not "come back". The aim is to reduce the size of the structure to be meshed and to make it possible to run complex analyses with present computing resources. One proposes in r4.02.05-domaine_isfs a diagram that describes the type of situations considered.

![Figure](/figures/r4.02.05/Probleme_ISFS.svg)

*Domain for soil–fluid–structure interaction.*

Throughout the document, one considers that the boundary of the finite-element mesh of the soil lies in a domain with elastic constitutive behaviour. The theory of elliptic systems simply ensures the existence and uniqueness of the solution of acoustic or elastoplastic problems in bounded domains, under the assumption of boundary conditions ensuring closure of the problem. It is different for infinite domains. One must resort to a particular condition, called the Sommerfeld condition, formulated in the infinite directions of the problem. This condition ensures in particular, in the case of the diffraction of a plane wave (elastic or acoustic) by a structure, the elimination of the non-physical diffracted waves coming from infinity, which the classical conditions on the edges of the domain at a finite distance are not sufficient to ensure.

### 1.2 State of the art of numerical approaches

The preferred method for treating infinite domains is that of boundary elements (or integral equations). The fundamental solution used automatically satisfies the Sommerfeld condition. However, the use of this method is conditional on knowledge of this fundamental solution, which is impossible in the case of a soil with complex geometry, for example, or when the soil or the structure are non-linear. One must then resort to finite elements. From then on, particular conditions at the boundary of the finite-element mesh are necessary in order to forbid the reflection of the outgoing diffracted waves and thus to reproduce artificially the Sommerfeld condition.

Several methods make it possible to identify boundary conditions meeting our requirements. Some lead to an exact solution of the problem: they are called "consistent boundaries". They are based on a precise taking into account of wave propagation in the infinite domain. For example, if this domain can be assumed elastic and with a simple stratigraphy far from the structure, one may consider a finite-element–integral-equation coupling. One of the problems of this solution is that it is not local in space: one must take stock over the whole boundary separating the finite domain from the infinite domain, which necessarily leads us to a substructuring problem. This non-locality in space is characteristic of consistent boundaries.

To arrive at boundary terms that are local in space, one may use the theory of infinite elements. These are elements of infinite dimension whose basis functions reproduce as well as possible the propagation of elastic or acoustic waves at infinity. These functions must be close to the solution because the classical mathematical theorems no longer ensure the convergence of the computed result towards the solution with such elements. In fact, one can find an analogy between the search for satisfactory basis functions and that for a fundamental solution for the integral equations. The geometric constraints are fairly similar but, above all, this search has a major drawback: it depends on the frequency. Consequently, such boundaries, whether local in space or not, can only be used in the Fourier domain, which rules out a certain category of problems, with constitutive non-linearities or large displacements for example.

One therefore comes to having to find efficient absorbing boundaries that are local in space and in time in order to treat, with finite elements, transient problems posed on infinite domains. In what follows we shall present the theory of paraxial elements, which achieve the desired absorption with an efficiency inversely proportional to their simplicity of implementation, as well as the description of the implementation constraints in code_aster. The developments for treating 3D problems are presented. Those for the 2D cases have been carried out and their theory is deduced simply from the 3D modelling.

## 2 Theory of paraxial elements

This part presents the principle of the paraxial approximation in the case of linear elastodynamics. Two theoretical approaches make it possible to grasp the spirit and the practical implementation of elastic paraxial elements: the first is due to Cohen and Jennings and the second to Modaressi. The application of the theory of paraxial elements to the fluid case will be made in the following part.

In all that follows, as shown in r4.02.05-domaine_isfs, one assumes that the boundary of the soil mesh is located in a domain with elastic constitutive behaviour. The approach of Modaressi implemented in code_aster makes it possible both to build absorbing boundaries and to introduce the incident seismic field.

### 2.1 Spectral impedance of the boundary

To obtain the paraxial equation, we must first determine the form of the diffracted displacement field in the neighbourhood of the boundary. To that end, one starts from the equations of 3D elastodynamics:

$$
\frac{{\partial}^{2} \disp }{\partial {t}^{2}}
-
{\tensTwo{E_{11}}}{\soundSpeed}^{2}\frac{\partial^2\disp}{\partial \posiTang^{2}}
-
{\tensTwo{E_{12}}}{\soundSpeed}^{2}\frac{\partial^2\disp}{\posiTang\partial \posiNorm}
-
{\tensTwo{E_{22}}}{\soundSpeed}^{2}\frac{\partial^2\disp}{\partial \posiNorm^{2}}
=
\vectorZero
$$

With the displacement $\disp$ which is decomposed into a normal part and a tangential part:

$$
\begin{split}\disp
=
\left\lbrace\begin{array}{cc}
    \dispTang \\
    \dispNorm
\end{array}\right\rbrace\end{split}
$$

and

$$
\begin{split}{\tensTwo{E_{11}}}
=
\frac{1}{\soundSpeed^{2}}
\left[\begin{array}{cc}
    \soundSpeedComp^{2}& 0\\
    0& \soundSpeedCisa^{2}
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

$$
\begin{split}{\tensTwo{E_{12}}}
=
\frac{1}{\soundSpeed^{2}}
\left(\soundSpeedComp^{2}-\soundSpeedCisa^{2}\right)
\left[\begin{array}{cc}
    0& 1\\ 1& 0
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

$$
\begin{split}{\tensTwo{E_{22}}}
=
\frac{1}{\soundSpeed^{2}}
\left[\begin{array}{cc}
    \soundSpeedCisa^{2}& 0\\
    0& \soundSpeedComp^{2}
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

The constant $\soundSpeed$, homogeneous to a velocity, is introduced in order to make certain quantities dimensionless. The equations and their solutions are of course independent of this constant.

One calls $\posiTang$ and $\dispTang$ the directions and the components of the displacement in the tangent plane and $\posiNorm$ and $\dispNorm$ along $\basisVector{3}$, the direction normal to the boundary.

One performs two Fourier transforms, one with respect to time, the other with respect to the space variables in the plane at the boundary. One restricts oneself to the case of a plane boundary without a corner:

The equations are then written. Along the tangential component:

$$
\left(\soundSpeedComp^{2}-\soundSpeedCisa^{2}\right)
\left[
    -\vector{\xi}_{\tau} \cdot \widehat{\dispTang}
    +
    i \, \frac{\partial \widehat{\dispNorm}}{\partial \posiNorm}
\right] \, \vector{\xi}_{\tau}
+
\soundSpeedCisa^{2}
\left[
    -
    \normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}^{2}
    +
    \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \posiNorm^{2}}
\right] \, \widehat{\dispTang}
+
{\omega}^{2} \, \widehat{\dispTang}
=
\vectorZero
$$

and along the normal component

$$
\left(\soundSpeedComp^{2}-\soundSpeedCisa^{2}\right)
\left[
    -i
    \,
    \vector{\xi}_{\tau}
    \cdot
    \frac{\partial \widehat{\dispTang}}{\partial \posiNorm}
    +
    \frac{\partial^2 \widehat{\dispNorm}}{\partial \posiNorm^{2}}
\right]
+
\soundSpeedCisa^{2}
\left[
    -
    \normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}^{2}
    +
    \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \posiNorm^{2}}
\right] \, \widehat{\dispNorm}
+
{\omega}^{2} \, \widehat{\dispNorm}
=
0
$$

where $\widehat{\disp}$ and $\widehat{\dispNorm}$ denote the Fourier transforms and $\vector{\xi}_{\tau}$ the wave vector associated with $\posiTang$.

This is a differential system in $\posiNorm$ which one knows how to solve by diagonalising it. One deduces from it:

$$
\begin{split}\left\lbrace
    \begin{array}{ll}
        \frac{\left(\widehat{\dispTang}\wedge \vector{\xi}_{\tau}\right)\,\basisVector{3}}
        {\normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}} &=& A \, \exp\left(-i \, {\xi}_{S} \, \posiNorm\right) \\
        {\dispTang \cdot \vector{\xi}_{\tau}} &=& \normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}
           \left[
                {A}_{P} \, \exp( -i{\xi}_{P}\posiNorm)+{A}_{S}\exp(-i{\xi}_{S}\posiNorm)
            \right] \\
        \normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}} \widehat{\dispNorm} &=&
        - {A}_{P} \, {\xi}_{P} \, \exp\left( -i {\xi}_{P} \posiNorm \right)-{A}_{S} \, {\xi}_{S} \, \exp\left(-i{\xi}_{S}\posiNorm\right)\\
    \end{array}
\right .\end{split}
$$

With $\vector{\xi}_P=\sqrt{\frac{{\omega}^{2}}{\soundSpeedComp^{2}}-{\normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}}^{2}}$ and $\vector{\xi}_S=\sqrt{\frac{{\omega}^{2}}{\soundSpeedCisa^{2}}-{\normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}}^{2}}$.

To determine the constants $A$, ${A}_{S}$ and ${A}_{P}$, one assumes $\dispTang \left(\vector{\xi}, 0\right)$ known on the boundary of the finite-element domain. One expresses them as a function of $\widehat{\dispTang}\left(\vector{\xi}_{\tau}, 0\right)={\widehat{\dispTang}}_{,0}$ and $\widehat{\dispNorm}\left(\vector{\xi}_{\tau},0\right)={\widehat{\dispNorm}}_{,0}$.

One will now evaluate the stress vector $\sigmVector$ on a facet of normal $\basisVector{3}$ at $\posiNorm=0$, which will give us the impedance of the boundary. One subjects $\sigmVector(\posiTang, \posiNorm)$ to the same Fourier transform in space as for the equations of elastodynamics, so that:

$$
\sigmVectorTang(\vector{\xi}_{\tau},\posiNorm)
=
\left[
    i \, \lameLambda \, \widehat{\dispTang} \cdot \vector{\xi}_{\tau}
    +
    (\lameLambda +2\lameMu ) \frac{\partial \widehat{\dispNorm}}{\partial \posiNorm}
\right] \basisVector{3}
+
\lameMu \left(
    \frac{\partial \widehat{\dispTang}}{\partial \posiNorm}
    +
    i \, \widehat{\dispNorm} \, \posiTang
\right)
$$

$\lameLambda$ and $\lameMu$ are the elastic Lamé coefficients.

One wishes to eliminate at $\posiNorm=0$ the terms containing derivatives with respect to $\posiNorm$ . The system obtained previously allows us to do so as a function of ${\widehat{\dispTang}}_{,0}$ and ${\widehat{\dispNorm}}_{,0}$.

$$
\begin{split}\left\lbrace
    \begin{array}{ll}
        \frac{\partial \widehat{\dispTang}_{,0}}{\partial \posiNorm} \,\vector{\xi}_{\tau}
        &=&
        i{\normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}}^{2}{\widehat{\dispNorm}_{,0}} \\
        \left( \frac{\partial \widehat{\dispTang}_{,0}}{\partial \posiNorm}\wedge \vector{\xi}_{\tau}\right)\,\basisVector{3}
        &=&
        -i\vector{\xi}_S\left(\widehat{\dispTang}_{,0}\wedge \vector{\xi}_{\tau} \right)\,\basisVector{3}\\
        \frac{\partial {\widehat{\disp}}_{30}}{\partial \posiNorm}
        &=&
        i\left[-\vector{\xi}_P\vector{\xi}_S\frac{\widehat{\dispTang}_{,0}\,\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}{{\normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}}^{2}}+\left(\vector{\xi}_P+\vector{\xi}_S\right){\widehat{\dispNorm}_{,0}}\right]\\
    \end{array}
\right .\end{split}
$$

One thus obtains the spectral impedance of the boundary:

$$
\widehat{\sigmVector}_{0}
=
{a}^{0}\basisVector{3}
+
{b}^{0}\vector{\xi}_{\tau}
+
{c}^{0}\vector{\xi}_{\tau} \wedge \basisVector{3}
$$

where ${a}^{0}$, ${b}^{0}$ and ${c}^{0}$ are functions of $\normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}$ and of $\omega$ which depend linearly on $\widehat{\dispTang}_{,0}$ and ${\widehat{\dispNorm}_{,0}}$ .

One may then write:

$$
\widehat{\sigmVector}_{0}:
=
A \left(
    \normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}} , \omega
\right)
\,
{\widehat{\disp}}_{0}
\left( \vector{\xi}_{\tau},\omega\right)
$$

where $A$ denotes the global spectral impedance operator. One returns to physical space by two inverse Fourier transforms.

### 2.2 Paraxial approximation of the impedance

The spectral impedance computed previously is local neither in space nor in time, since it involves $\left( \vector{\xi}_{\tau},\omega\right)$, the Fourier transform of ${\disp}_{0} \left(\posiTang, t\right)$ for every $\posiTang$ and every $t$ .

The idea is then to expand ${\vector{\xi}}_{P}$  and ${\vector{\xi}}_{S}$ in powers of $\frac{\normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}}{\omega}$. This approximation will be good either at high frequency, or for $\normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}$ small.

Let us examine the dependence on $\posiNorm$, for example of $\widehat{\dispNorm}$. One will have, for $\dispNorm\left(\posiTang, \posiNorm,t\right)$, terms of the form $\exp\left[i\left(\vector{\xi}_{\tau}\posiTang+\omega t-{\vector{\xi}}_{P}\posiNorm\right)\right]$.

With the expansion of $\vector{\xi}_P$:

$$
\vector{\xi}_P
=
\frac{\omega}{\soundSpeedComp}
\left[
    1
    -
    \left( \frac{ \soundSpeedComp \normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}} }{\omega} \right) ^{2}
    +
    \dots
\right]
$$

One shows that for $\normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}$ small, one will have waves propagating along directions close to the normal $\basisVector{3}$ to the boundary, because the exponential is written:

$$
\exp\left\lbrace
    i \, \omega
    \left[
        \left(
            t-\frac{\posiNorm}{\soundSpeedComp}\right)
            +
            i \, \mathcal{O} \left(
                \frac{\normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}}{\omega}
                \right)
    \right]
\right\rbrace
$$

From then on, with an asymptotic expansion of ${\vector{\xi}}_{P}$ and ${\vector{\xi}}_{S}$, multiplying by a suitable power of $\omega$ in order to remove this quantity from the denominator, one obtains:

$$
{A}_{0}\left(\vector{\xi}_{\tau},\omega \right) \widehat{\sigmVector}_{0}
=
{A}_{1}\left(\vector{\xi}_{\tau},\omega \right) {\widehat{\disp}}_{0}
$$

where ${A}_{0}$ and ${A}_{1}$ are polynomial functions of $\vector{\xi}_{\tau}$ and $\omega$.

That is, after the two inverse Fourier transforms:

$$
{A}_{0}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \posiTang},\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right){\sigmVector}_{0}
=
{A}_{1}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \posiTang},\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right){\disp}_{0}
$$

One thus obtains the final form of the approximate transient local impedance as a function of the last term in $\frac{\normEucl{\vector{\xi}_{\tau}}}{\omega}$ retained. The detailed computation of the ${A}_{i}$ can be found in .

For example, for order 0:

$$
{\sigmVector}_{0}
=
C^{a}
\left(
    \rho \, \soundSpeedComp \,\frac{\partial \dispNorm}{\partial t} \, \basisVector{3}
    +
    \rho \, \soundSpeedCisa \,\frac{\partial \dispTang}{\partial t}
\right)
$$

This corresponds to viscous dampers distributed along the boundary of the finite-element domain, with the corrective coefficient $C^{a}$ supplied under the keyword COEF_AMOR assigned by [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01) to the material of the absorbing boundary elements.

By analogy, for order 0, one may add a displacement term corresponding to stiffnesses distributed along the boundary of the finite-element domain:

$$
{\sigmVector}_{1}
=
\frac{\lameLambda+2\lameMu}{L}
\, \dispNorm \, \basisVector{3}
+
\frac{\lameMu}{L}\dispTang
$$

This additional formulation comprises the Lamé coefficients $\lameLambda$ and $\lameMu$ , as well as a characteristic dimension $L$ .

At order 1:

$$
\begin{split}\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial {\sigmVector}_{0}}{\partial t} =
    \rho\soundSpeedComp\frac{\partial^2 \dispNorm}{\partial {t}^{2}}\basisVector{3} +
    \rho\soundSpeedCisa\frac{\partial^2 \dispTang }{\partial {t}^{2}} + \\
    \rho\soundSpeedCisa\left[
        \left( 2\soundSpeedCisa-\soundSpeedComp \right) \,
            \frac{\partial^2\dispNorm}{\partial \posiTang\partial t}\basisVector{3}
        +
        \left(\soundSpeedComp-2\soundSpeedCisa\right) \,
            \frac{\partial^2\dispTang}{\partial \posiTang\partial t} \right] + \\
        \rho \soundSpeedComp^{2} \left( \soundSpeedCisa-\frac{\soundSpeedComp}{2} \right)
            \frac{\partial^2 \dispNorm\,\basisVector{3}}{{\partial \posiTang}^{2}}  +
        \rho \soundSpeedCisa^{2} \left( \soundSpeedComp-\frac{\soundSpeedCisa}{2} \right)
            \frac{\partial^2\dispTang}{{\partial \posiTang}^{2}}
\end{aligned}\end{split}
$$

The derivative with respect to time of the stress vector $\sigmVector$ appears. In the numerical treatment, one will have to resort to an integration of this term over the elements of the boundary.

To conclude, one will retain that the paraxial approximation leads to a transient local impedance involving essentially only derivatives in time and in the plane tangent to the boundary, as well as possibly an additional term without a time derivative.

Symbolically, one writes at order 0:

$$
{\sigmVector}_{0}
=
{A}_{0}\left(\frac{\partial \disp}{\partial t}\right)
+
{A}_{1}\left(\disp\right)
$$

and at order 1:

$$
\frac{\partial {\sigmVector}_{0}}{\partial t}
=
{A}_{1}\left(
    \frac{\partial^2\disp}{\partial {t}^{2}} ,
    \frac{\partial \disp}{\partial t} ,
    \disp
\right)
$$

### 2.3 Taking the incident seismic field into account

One recalls that the constitutive behaviour of the soil is assumed elastic at least in the neighbourhood of the boundary.
One therefore introduces the diffracted field ${\disp}_r$ such that:

$$
\disp
=
{\disp}_{i}
+
{\disp}_{r}
$$

At infinity, the total field $\disp$ must be equal to the incident field ${\disp}_{i}$ (one of the consequences of the Sommerfeld radiation condition), that is:

$$
\lim_{x \to + \infty}
{ {\disp}_{r} }
=
\vectorZero
$$

At the boundary of the finite-element mesh, one writes the absorption condition for the diffracted field at order 0:

$$
{\sigmVector}_{0}({\disp}_{r})
=
{A}_{0} \, \left(\frac{\partial {\disp}_{r}}{\partial t}\right)
$$

One deduces from it the total stress vector on the boundary of the finite-element mesh:

$$
{\sigmVector}_{0}\left(\disp\right)
=
{\sigmVector}_{0}\left({\disp}_{i}\right)
+
{\sigmVector}_{0}\left({\disp}_{r}\right)
=
{\sigmVector}_{0}\left({\disp}_{i}\right)
+
{A}_{0}\left(\frac{\partial \disp}{\partial t}\right)
-
{A}_{0}\left(\frac{\partial {\disp}_{i}}{\partial t}\right)
$$

One thus obtains the variational formulation of the problem in the neighbourhood of the boundary for order 0:

$$
\rho \int_{\domain}{ \frac{\partial^2\disp}{\partial {t}^{2}} \dispVirt \measDomain}
+
\int_{\domain} { \stress \, \left(\disp\right) : \strain \left(\dispVirt\right) \measDomain}
-
\int_{\bound} { {A}_{0} \, \left(\frac{\partial \disp}{\partial t}\right) \dispVirt \measBound}
=
\int_{\bound} {
    \left[
        \sigmVector \left({\disp}_{i}\right)
        -
        {A}_{0}\left(\frac{\partial {\disp}_{i}}{\partial t}\right)
    \right] \, \dispVirt \measBound}
$$

For every field $\dispVirt$ that is kinematically admissible.

The absorption condition for the diffracted field at order 1 is written:

$$
\frac{\partial {\sigmVector}_{0} ({\disp}_{r})}{\partial t}
=
{A}_{1} \, \left(
    \frac{\partial^2{\disp}_{r}}{\partial {t}^{2}},
    \frac{\partial {\disp}_{r}}{\partial t},
    {\disp}_{r}
\right)
$$

For order 1, one keeps the classical formulation:

$$
\rho \int_{\domain} {\frac{\partial^2\disp}{\partial {t}^{2}} \dispVirt \measDomain}
+
\int_{\domain} { \stress\left(u\right):\strain\left(\dispVirt\right) \measDomain}
-
\int_{\bound} {  \sigmVector  \left(\disp\right) \dispVirt  \measBound}
=
0
$$

where $\sigmVector  \left(\disp\right)$ follows the following evolution law:

$$
\frac{\partial \sigmVector\left(\disp\right)}{\partial t}
=\
\frac{\partial \sigmVector\left({\disp}_{i}\right)}{\partial t}
+
{A}_{1}\left(\frac{\partial^2\disp}{\partial {t}^{2}},\frac{\partial \disp}{\partial t},\disp\right)
-
{A}_{1}\left(\frac{\partial^2{\disp}_{i}}{\partial {t}^{2}},\frac{\partial {\disp}_{i}}{\partial t},{\disp}_{i}\right)
$$

The loading due to the incident field appears explicitly in the case of order 0, but it is contained in the evolution law of $\sigmVector  \left(\disp\right)$ for order 1.

## 3 Anechoic fluid elements in transient analysis

This part presents the essentials of the general constraints on the implementation of anechoic absorbing-boundary fluid elements with the paraxial approximation of order 0 in code_aster. For reasons of simplicity linked to the handling of scalar quantities such as the pressure or the displacement potential, as opposed to vector quantities such as the displacement, one is interested first in the fluid elements.

### 3.1 Standard formulation

One takes up here the reasoning of Modaressi, adapting it to an acoustic fluid domain. At first, one is interested only in the pressure quantity in this fluid. One will then return to this modelling in order to adapt it to the constraints of code_aster, pointing out the adjustments to be made.

Consider therefore the following configuration, taking up the conventions of the previous part in the neighbourhood of the boundary:

![Figure](/figures/r4.02.05/Shape1.gif)

The definition of a local frame at the level of the element allows us to reduce systematically to such a situation.

#### 3.1.1 Finite-element formulation

The pressure $p$ satisfies the Helmholtz equation throughout the domain $\Omega$ modelled with finite elements, which gives, for every virtual pressure field $q$ :

$-\underset{\Omega}{\int}\nabla p.\nabla q-\frac{1}{{c}^{2}}\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{\partial {t}^{2}}\underset{\Omega}{\int}\text{pq}+\underset{\Sigma}{\int}\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}q=0$

$\Sigma$ represents the boundary of the domain $O$ .

The quantity to be estimated on $\Sigma$ by means of the paraxial approximation is here $\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}$ .

#### 3.1.2 Paraxial approximation

In the proposed configuration, the term $\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}$ corresponds to $\frac{\partial p}{\partial {x}_{3}}$ .

Consider therefore a harmonic plane wave propagating in the fluid:

$p=A\exp\left[i({k}_{1}{x}_{1}+{k}_{2}{x}_{2}+{k}_{3}{x}_{3}-\omega t)\right]$

Substituting into the Helmholtz equation, one obtains:

${k}_{3}=\frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{1-\frac{{c}^{2}}{{\omega}^{2}}({k}_{1}^{2}+{k}_{2}^{2})}$

One then obtains the following expansion, for high frequencies ($\omega$  large) or in the neighbourhood of the boundary (${k}_{1}$  and ${k}_{2}$ small):

${k}_{3}=\frac{\omega}{c}(1-\frac{{c}^{2}}{2{\omega}^{2}}({k}_{1}^{2}+{k}_{2}^{2}))$

That is, multiplying by $\omega$ in order to remove this quantity from the denominator and after an inverse Fourier transform in space and in time:

$\frac{{\partial}^{2}p}{\partial {x}_{3}\partial t}=-\frac{1}{c}\frac{{\partial}^{2}p}{\partial {t}^{2}}+\frac{1}{2}c(\frac{{\partial}^{2}p}{\partial {x}_{1}^{2}}+\frac{{\partial}^{2}p}{\partial {x}_{2}^{2}})$

As Modaressi had presented, this equation involves the derivative with respect to time of the surface term. Within the framework of this part, one is interested only in the term of order 0, that is, after an integration in time, which removes the troublesome derivative:

$\frac{\partial p}{\partial {x}_{3}}=-\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}$ or, more generally: $\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}=-\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}$

It is this impedance relation that we are going to discretise on the boundary of the finite-element domain.

**Note:**

*Taking into account the disappearance of the order-1 term in the expansion of the square root, the minimum order of approximation for fluid paraxial elements is in fact 1 and not 0. We shall keep the name of order-0 elements for consistency with the solid. However, one speaks of fluid elements of order 2 when considering elements of strictly positive order.*

### 3.2 Impedance of the vibro-acoustic elements in code_aster

code_aster has vibro-acoustic elements. This paragraph recalls the formulation choices made at the time of their implementation. In presenting what exists, one draws on the reference documentation [r4.02.02](doc:r4.02.02).

#### 3.2.1 Limits of the formulation in p

In the framework of fluid–structure interaction in harmonic analysis, the formulation of the acoustic fluid in pressure only leads to non-symmetric matrices. Indeed, the global system is expressed, in variational form, in the following way:

$\underset{{\Omega}_{s}}{\int}{C}_{ijkl}.{u}_{k,l}{v}_{i,j}-{\omega}^{2}\underset{{\Omega}_{s}}{\int}{\rho}_{s}{u}_{i}{v}_{i}-\underset{\Sigma}{\int}p{v}_{i}.{n}_{i}=0$ for the structure

$\frac{1}{{\rho}_{f}{\omega}^{2}}\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\nabla p.\nabla q-{k}^{2}\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\mathrm{pq}-\underset{\Sigma}{\int}{u}_{i}.{n}_{i}q=0$ for the fluid

with $k=\frac{\omega}{c}$ , the wave number for the fluid, $v$ and $q$ two virtual fields in the structure and in the fluid respectively.

After discretisation by finite elements, one obtains the following matrix system:

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}K& -C\\ 0& H\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}u\\ p\end{array}\right]-{\omega}^{2}\left[\begin{array}{cc}M& 0\\ {\rho}_{f}{C}^{T}& \frac{Q}{{c}^{2}}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}u\\ p\end{array}\right]=0$

| where: | $K$ and $M$ are the stiffness and mass matrices of the structure |
| --- | --- |
|  | $H$ and $Q$ are the fluid matrices obtained respectively from the bilinear forms: $\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\nabla p.\nabla q$ and $\underset{{O}_{f}}{\int}\mathrm{pq}$ |

$C$ is the coupling matrix obtained from the bilinear form: $\underset{\Sigma}{\int}p{u}_{i}.{n}_{i}$

The non-symmetric character of this system does not allow the use of the classical solution algorithms of code_aster. This motivates the introduction of an additional variable in the description of the fluid.

#### 3.2.2 Symmetric formulation in p and phi

The new quantity introduced is the displacement potential $\phi$ , such that $u=\nabla \varphi$ . According to , one obtains the new variational form of the coupled fluid–structure system:

$\underset{{\Omega}_{s}}{\int}{C}_{ijkl}.{u}_{k,l}{v}_{i,j}-{\omega}^{2}\underset{{\Omega}_{s}}{\int}{\rho}_{s}{u}_{i}{v}_{i}-{\rho}_{f}{\omega}^{2}\underset{\Sigma}{\int}\phi p{v}_{i}.{n}_{i}=0$ for the structure

$\frac{1}{{\rho}_{f}{c}^{2}}\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\text{pq}-{\rho}_{f}{\omega}^{2}\left[\frac{1}{{\rho}_{f}{c}^{2}}\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\left(\varphi q+p\chi \right)-\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\nabla \varphi .\nabla \chi +\underset{S}{\int}\chi {\mathrm{u}}_{i}{\mathrm{n}}_{i}\right]=0$ for the fluid

With: $p={\rho}_{f}{\omega}^{2}f$ in the fluid and $\chi$ a virtual displacement-potential field

This leads us to the symmetric matrix system:

$\left[\begin{array}{ccc}K& 0& 0\\ 0& \frac{{M}_{f}}{{\rho}_{f}{c}^{2}}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}u\\ p\\ \phi \end{array}\right]-{\omega}^{2}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}M& 0& {\rho}_{f}{M}_{\Sigma}\\ 0& 0& \frac{{M}_{\text{fl}}}{{c}^{2}}\\ {\rho}_{f}{M}_{\Sigma}^{T}& \frac{{M}_{\text{fl}}^{T}}{{c}^{2}}& {\rho}_{f}H\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}u\\ p\\ \phi \end{array}\right]=0$

where: $K$ and $\mathrm{M}$ are the stiffness and mass matrices of the structure

${\mathrm{M}}_{\Sigma}$ is the coupling matrix obtained from the bilinear form $\underset{\Sigma}{\int}\varphi {\mathrm{u}}_{i}{\mathrm{n}}_{i}$

${\mathrm{M}}_{\mathrm{f}},{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{fl}}$ and $\mathrm{H}$ are the fluid matrices obtained from the bilinear forms: $\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\text{pq}$ , $\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}pq$ (or $\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\varphi q$ ) and $\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\nabla \varphi .\nabla \chi$

#### 3.2.3 Formulation in psi

There exists a third formulation defined in $\psi$ , that is a velocity potential. For further details one refers to the documentation . This formulation is also available in code_aster. Its negative point is that it does not give direct access to post-processing in fluid pressure.

#### 3.2.4 Imposition of an impedance of order 0

In a general way, an impedance relation at the boundary of the fluid is expressed as follows:

$p=Z\mathrm{v}.\mathrm{n}$

where:

- $Z=\rho c{q}_{\alpha}$ is the imposed impedance ($\rho$  is the density of the fluid, $c$ is the wave speed of the fluid waves and ${q}_{\alpha}$ is the reflection coefficient);
- $\mathrm{v}.\mathrm{n}$ is the outgoing normal velocity of the fluid particles.

One deduces from it, according to the constitutive law of the fluid, which relates the pressure to the displacement of the fluid particles for an acoustic fluid $\nabla p-{\rho}_{f}\frac{{\partial}^{2}u}{\partial {t}^{2}}=0$ :

$\frac{{\rho}_{f}}{Z}\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}$

The discretisation of such an equation leads to a non-symmetric term in a formulation in $p$ and $\varphi$ . One prefers to formulate the condition with respect to the displacement potential, that is:

$\nabla \varphi +\frac{{\rho}_{f}}{Z}\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial t}=0$

One then obtains as the expression for the boundary term associated with the impedance relation:

${\rho}_{f}\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{\partial {t}^{2}}\underset{\Sigma}{\int}\varphi \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial n}=\frac{{\partial}^{3}}{\partial {t}^{3}}\underset{\Sigma}{\int}\frac{{\rho}_{f}^{2}}{Z}\varphi \psi$

One then observes the appearance (somewhat artificial) of a term in the third derivative with respect to time. In harmonic analysis, this poses no problem. One treats a term in ${\omega}^{3}$ without difficulty. This third-order term will be computed using the option IMPE_MECA in CALC_MATR_ELEM (or ASSEMBLAGE) and was used as the input of MATR_IMPE_PHI in DYNA_VIBRA (TYPE_CALCUL = ‘HARM’). For the transient computation, rather than introducing an approximation of a third derivative in the Newmark scheme implemented in the operators of direct integration in dynamics in code_aster DYNA_VIBRA (or DYNA_LINE) and DYNA_NON_LINE, one can transform the third-order term into a first-order term and treat this as a classical damping term (for further details refer to ). This term will be computed using the option AMOR_MECA in CALC_MATR_ELEM (or ASSEMBLAGE). This option is available for the three fluid formulations (for the details on the formulation $u-\psi$ refer to ).

#### 3.2.5 Imposition of an impedance of order 1

The radiation condition is exact for a plane wave. However, in order to approximate asymptotically the behaviour at infinity of a spherical wave, for a given radius $R$ , one uses an approximation of order 1. This condition is recalled in , as well as the complete description of the terms imposed by this condition. Depending on the formulation chosen, one affects the mass or the stiffness of the model:

- If one chooses the formulation $u-p-\varphi$ , one adds a mass term computed using the option MASS_MECA in CALC_MATR_ELEM (or ASSEMBLAGE);
- If one chooses the formulations $u-p$ or $u-\psi$ , one adds a stiffness term computed using the option RIGI_MECA in CALC_MATR_ELEM (or ASSEMBLAGE).

The radius $R$ will be given via the option LONG_CARA in DEFI_MATERIAU(FLUIDE…). This parameter is zero by default (in this case the impedance of order 1 is not taken into account).

#### 3.2.6 The reflection ratio

If one wants to represent a reflection ratio $\alpha$ , for example in the case of the presence of alluvium at the bottom of the reservoir, one must then correct the value of the wave speed $c$ by a new value $c'$ such that $c'=c\frac{1+\alpha }{1-\alpha }$ . The parameter $\alpha$ will be defined via the option COEF_AMOR in DEFI_MATERIAU (FLUIDE…). This parameter is zero by default (in this case the boundary is perfectly absorbing). This parameter influences both the impedance of order 0 and of order 1.

#### 3.2.7 Detailed formulation

One proposes here the precise formulation for an acoustic fluid modelled on a domain $\Omega$ avec une condition anéchoïque sur une partie :math:`{\Sigma}_{a}$ of the boundary $\Sigma$ du domaine. En dehors de cela, on décompose la frontière en une surface libre et une partie en contact avec un solide rigide. L’introduction de sollicitations extérieures ou la présence d’une structure élastique se modélise aisément par les méthodes courantes. Les éléments de volume et de surface sont formulés en :math:`p$ and $\varphi$ .

The equations in the fluid are:

${\rho}_{f}\Delta \varphi +\frac{1}{{c}^{2}}p=0$ in the volume $\Omega$                                         eq 3.2.4-1

$p={\rho}_{f}\frac{{\partial}^{2}\varphi }{\partial {t}^{2}}$ in the volume $\Omega$                                             eq 3.2.4-2

$p=0$ on the free surface                                                    eq 3.2.4-3

$\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial n}=0$ on the rigid wall                                                eq 3.2.4-4

$\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}=-\frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}$ on the part of the boundary with an anechoic condition            eq 3.2.4-5

One multiplies the equation [eq 3.2.4-1] by a virtual potential field $y$ and one integrates in $\Omega$ :

$\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\left[\frac{1}{{c}^{2}}p\psi +{\rho}_{f}\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{\partial {t}^{2}}\left(\nabla \varphi .\nabla \psi \right)\right]+\underset{\Sigma}{\int}\psi {\rho}_{f}\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{\partial {t}^{2}}\left(\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial n}\right)=0$ according to Green's formula

That is, with the boundary conditions on $\Sigma$ et l’équation [:ref:`éq 3.2.4-2 <éq 3.2.4-2>$]:

$\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\left[\frac{1}{{c}^{2}}p\psi +{\rho}_{f}\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{\partial {t}^{2}}\left(\nabla \varphi .\nabla \psi \right)\right]+\underset{\Sigma}{\int}\psi {\rho}_{f}\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}=0$

One may then apply the impedance condition formulated in pressure:

$\underset{{\Sigma}_{a}}{\int}\psi {\rho}_{f}\frac{\partial p}{\partial n}=-\frac{1}{c}\underset{{\Sigma}_{a}}{\int}\psi {\rho}_{f}\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}$

Furthermore, in order to arrive at a symmetric formulation of the volume terms, one multiplies the equation [eq3.2.4-2] by a virtual pressure field $q$ and one integrates in $\Omega$ :

$\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\frac{\text{pq}}{{\rho}_{f}{c}^{2}}-\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{\partial {t}^{2}}\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\frac{\varphi q}{{c}^{2}}=0$

Summing the two variational equations, one obtains:

$\frac{1}{{\rho}_{f}{c}^{2}}\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\text{pq}+{\rho}_{f}\frac{{\partial}^{2}}{\partial {t}^{2}}\left[\frac{1}{{\rho}_{f}{c}^{2}}\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\left(\varphi q+p\psi \right)-\underset{{\Omega}_{f}}{\int}\nabla \varphi .\nabla \psi \right]-\frac{1}{c}\underset{{\Sigma}_{a}}{\int}\psi {\rho}_{f}\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=0$

In matrix form:

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\mathrm{M}}_{\mathrm{f}}& 0\\ 0& 0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}p\\ \varphi \end{array}\right]-\frac{1}{c}\left[\begin{array}{cc}0& 0\\ \mathrm{A}& 0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}\dot{p}\\ \dot{\varphi}\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}{cc}0& \frac{{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{fl}}}{{c}^{2}}\\ \frac{{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{fl}}^{\mathrm{T}}}{{c}^{2}}& {\rho}_{f}\mathrm{H}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}\ddot{p}\\ \ddot{\varphi}\end{array}\right]=0$

where the sub-matrices ${\mathrm{M}}_{\mathrm{f}},{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{fl}}$ and $\mathrm{H}$ discretise the same previous bilinear forms.

The sub-matrix $\mathrm{A}$ discretises the term $\underset{{\Sigma}_{a}}{\int}\psi {\rho}_{f}\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}$ . One notes that the damping matrix obtained is not symmetric.

### 3.3 Use in code_aster

Taking anechoic fluid elements into account and computing their impedance requires a specific modelling on the absorbing boundaries:

- in 2D with the modelling '2D_FLUI_ABSO' or 'AXIS_FLUI_ABSO' on the absorbing edges with $n$ nodes;
- in 3D with the modelling '3D_FLUI_ABSO' on the absorbing faces with $n$ nodes.

In harmonic analysis for the formulation $u-p-\varphi$ with the operator DYNA_VIBRA, one computes beforehand a mechanical impedance by the option IMPE_MECA of the operator CALC_MATR_ELEM and one supplies it in DYNA_VIBRA (keyword MATR_IMPE_PHI). In all the other cases, the impedance of order 0 will be taken into account via the option AMOR_MECA of the operator CALC_MATR_ELEM. The impedance of order 1 will be taken into account via stiffness or mass terms (depending on the formulation) as explained previously. The material characteristics of the fluid absorbing boundaries will be defined in DEFI_MATERIAU (FLUIDE…).

## 4 Elastic absorbing elements in code_aster

This part presents the essentials of the general constraints on the implementation of elastic absorbing-boundary elements with the paraxial approximation of order 0 in code_aster. One recalls the paraxial impedance relation of order 0 as it was established by Modaressi for a linear elastic domain:

$\mathrm{t}\left(\mathrm{u}\right)=\rho \left({c}_{p}\frac{\partial {\mathrm{u}}_{\phantom{.}\perp \phantom{.}}}{\partial t}+{c}_{s}\frac{\partial {\mathrm{u}}_{\text{//}}}{\partial t}\right)$

${\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{T}}$ becomes ${\mathrm{u}}_{3}$ and ${\mathrm{u}}_{\text{//}}$ becomes ${\mathrm{u}}^{\text{/}}$

### 4.1 Adaptation of the seismic loading to the paraxial elements

The first part presented the principle of taking the incident field into account by means of the paraxial elements. It is appropriate here to present the methods of modelling the seismic loading in code_aster in order to be able to adapt the data to the requirements of the paraxial elements.

The fundamental equation of dynamics associated with an arbitrary 2D or 3D model discretised into finite elements of a continuum or of a structure and in the absence of external loading is written in the absolute frame:

$\mathrm{M}{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{\mathrm{a}}+\mathrm{C}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{\mathrm{a}}+{\text{KX}}_{\mathrm{a}}=0$

One decomposes the motion of the structures into an entrainment motion ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{e}}$ and a relative motion ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}$ .

![Figure](/figures/r4.02.05/10000000000004190000025E890601B85B05EC78.png)

**Figure 4.1-a: Decomposition of the motion of the structures**

Thus, ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{a}}={\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}+{\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{e}}$

- ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{a}}$ is the vector of the displacements in the absolute frame,
- ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}$ is the vector of the relative displacements, that is the vector of the displacements of the structure with respect to the deformed shape that it would have under the static action of the displacements imposed at the level of the supports ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{S}}$ . ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{r}}$ is therefore zero at the anchorage points,
- ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{e}}$ is the vector of the entrainment displacements of the structure produced statically by the imposed displacement of the supports ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{S}}$ : ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{e}}=\Psi {\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{s}}$ ,
- $\Psi$ est la matrice des modes statiques. Les modes statiques représentent la réponse de la structure à un déplacement unitaire imposé à chaque degré de liberté de liaison (les autres étant bloqués), en l’absence de forces extérieures. Ainsi, $\mathrm{K}\Psi =0$ , that is, ${\text{KX}}_{e}=0$ .

In the case of the single support (all the supports undergo the same imposed motion), $\Psi$ is a rigid-body mode.

**Assumption in code_aster:**

*One assumes that the damping dissipated by the structure is of viscous type, that is that the damping force is proportional to the relative velocity of the structure. Thus,* $\mathrm{C}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{e}=0$ *.*

The fundamental equation of dynamics in the relative frame is then written:

$\mathrm{M}{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{\mathrm{r}}+\mathrm{C}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{\mathrm{r}}+{\text{KX}}_{\mathrm{r}}=-\mathrm{M}\Psi \ddot{{\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{S}}}$

The operator CALC_CHAR_SEISME [U4.63.01](doc:u4.63.01) computes the term $-\mathrm{M}\Psi ` , ou plus exactement , $-\mathrm{M}\Psi \mathrm{d}$ where $\mathrm{d}$ is a unit vector such that ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{s}}=\mathrm{d}.f\left(t\right)$ with $f$ a scalar function of time.

One distinguishes two types of seismic loading introduced in code_aster by means of the operator CALC_CHAR_SEISME:

1. The loading of type MONO_APPUI, for which $\Psi$ is the identity matrix (the static modes are rigid-body modes),
2. The loading of type MULTI_APPUI, for which $\Psi$ is arbitrary.

According to the method of taking the incident field into account with the paraxial elements presented in the first part, we need to know on the boundary the displacement and the stresses due to the incident field. For the loading of type MULTI_APPUI, only the displacement is directly accessible at every time instant. It therefore seems difficult to allow the use of such a loading mode with paraxial elements in the soil. Moreover, if such a loading models the imposed displacements of the supports, it does not require a modelling of the soil since the whole influence is taken into account by these displacements.

The MONO_APPUI case can be perceived differently. It represents an overall acceleration applied to the model. From then on, wave propagation in the soil may have a role to play in the behaviour of the structure, since the motions of the soil–structure interface are not imposed. Furthermore, the paraxial elements can be used with this type of loading because it does not create stresses at the boundary of the mesh (a rigid-body mode does not create strains). From then on, one has all the data necessary for the computation of the absorbing impedance on the boundary.

**Note 1:**

*In the case of a seismic loading* *MONO_APPUI, the dynamic computation is done in the relative frame. If one returns to the term to be discretised on the paraxial elements (see the first part), one notes that* ${u}_{i}$ *corresponds exactly to the entrainment displacement* ${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{e}}$ *presented above. Thus,* $\mathrm{u}-{\mathrm{u}}_{i}$ *corresponds to the relative displacement computed during the analysis. From then on, the relation to be taken into account on the paraxial elements in such a configuration is simply:*

$t(u)={A}_{0}(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t})$

**Note 2:**

*In the case of a soil–fluid–structure interaction analysis with an infinite fluid, the pressure to be taken into account for the computation of the anechoic impedance in the fluid is indeed the absolute pressure, if one has no incident field in the fluid (which is often the case). The correction that one could dispense with for the soil must then be made for the fluid paraxial elements.*

### 4.2 Implementation of the elements in transient and harmonic analysis

#### 4.2.1 Implementation in transient analysis

The mode of implementation of the elastic paraxial elements in transient analysis is very close to that presented for the fluid elements. The difference comes essentially from the need to decompose the displacement into a component along the normal to the element, corresponding to a wave $P$ , and a component in the plane of the element, corresponding to a wave $S$ . One is then in a position to discretise the impedance relation introduced in the first part:

$\mathrm{t}\left(\mathrm{u}\right)=\rho {C}_{p}\frac{\partial {\mathrm{u}}_{3}}{\partial t}+\rho {C}_{s}\frac{\partial {\mathrm{u}}^{\text{/}}}{\partial t}$

One does not return to the time-integration scheme that has already been described in the previous part, knowing that one considers the impedance relation by a damping operator added to the left-hand side.

For taking into account the additional term:

${\mathrm{t}}_{1}\left(\mathrm{u}\right)=\frac{\lambda +2\mu }{L}{u}_{3}{\mathrm{e}}_{3}+\frac{\mu}{L}{\mathrm{u}}^{'}$

one uses this time a stiffness operator added to the left-hand side.

#### 4.2.2 Implementation in harmonic analysis

The fluid acoustic elements of code_aster already offer the possibility of taking into account an impedance imposed at the boundary of the mesh in harmonic analysis. This corresponds to the treatment of a term in ${\omega}^{3}$ in the equations, as mentioned above. It is tempting to introduce the possibility of imposing an absorbing impedance for an elastic problem in harmonic analysis.

For a computation of the harmonic response of an infinite structure, taking the absorbing impedance into account as a correction of the right-hand side is obviously not applicable. However, the impedance relation at order 0 expresses the surface terms as a function of the velocity of the nodes of the element. One can therefore build a pseudo-matrix of viscous damping expressing the presence of the infinite domain.

In the same way, one builds (cf. 4.1.1) a pseudo-matrix of stiffness completing the role of the damping matrix defined previously.

The decomposition of the impedance relation according to the normal or tangential components of the displacement on the element forces us to build the impedance matrix in a local frame on the element. One defines this local frame in the elementary routine as well as the transformation matrix that allows the return to the global basis.

### 4.3 Seismic loading mode by plane wave

In addition to the methods of taking the seismic loading into account that are already available, and because of the unsuitability of the MULTI_APPUI mode with the paraxial elements, it seems interesting to introduce a principle of loading by plane wave. This corresponds to the loadings classically encountered in soil–structure interaction computations by integral equations.

#### 4.3.1 Characterisation of a plane wave in transient analysis

In harmonic analysis, an elastic plane wave is characterised by its direction, its angular frequency and its type (wave $P$ for compression waves, waves $\mathrm{SV}$ or $\mathrm{SH}$ for shear waves). In transient analysis, the datum of the angular frequency, corresponding to a wave that is stationary in time, must be replaced by the datum of a displacement profile whose propagation over time in the direction of the wave will be taken into account.

```text
Les directions des ondes :math:`P`, :math:`\mathit{SV}`et :math:`\mathit{SH}`sont déterminées à partir du vecteur :math:`V`renseigné par le paramètre DIRECTION. A savoir :

*   :math:`P`est colinéaire à :math:`V`et normé à 1,

*   :math:`\mathit{SH}`est l'intersection du plan horizontal et du plan normal à :math:`V`, et normé à 1,

*   :math:`\mathit{SV}`est le produit vectoriel de :math:`\mathit{SH}`et de :math:`P`. Il existe un cas d'indétermination avec cette règle quand le plan horizontal et le plan normal sont confondus. Dans ce cas, si :math:`V=Z`purement vertical, on impose :math:`\mathit{SH}=Y`, et :math:`\mathit{SV}=X`.
```

More precisely, one will consider a plane wave in the form:

$u(x,t)=f(k.x-{C}_{p}t)k$ for a wave $P$ (with $k$ a unit vector)

$\mathrm{u}\left(\mathrm{x},t\right)=f\left(k.\mathrm{x}-{C}_{S}t\right)\wedge k$ for a wave $S$ (with again $k$ a unit vector)

$f$ then represents the profile of the wave given along the direction $k$ .

![Figure](/figures/r4.02.05/Object_213.svg)

$H$ is the distance from the origin to the main wave front.

#### 4.3.2 User data for loading by plane wave

In accordance with the theory set out in the first part, we need to compute the stress at the boundary of the mesh due to the incident wave and the impedance term corresponding to the incident displacement, that is:

$\mathrm{t}\left({\mathrm{u}}_{i}\right)$ and ${A}_{0}\left(\frac{\partial {\mathrm{u}}_{i}}{\partial t}\right)+{A}_{1}\left({\mathrm{u}}_{i}\right)$

To express the stresses, we need to have the strains due to the incident wave, the constitutive law of the material allowing us to pass from the ones to the others.

On the boundary elements, one can express the linearised strain tensor at each node by the classical formula:

$\epsilon \left(x,t\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla \mathrm{u}\left(x,t\right)+{}^{t}\text{}\nabla \mathrm{u}\left(x,t\right)\right]$

Finally, to estimate the stresses due to the incident field, we therefore need to determine the derivatives $\frac{\partial {\left({\mathrm{u}}_{i}\right)}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{k}}$ for $j$ and $k$ running through the three directions of space. One obtains these quantities from the definition of the incident plane wave:

$\frac{\partial {\left({\mathrm{u}}_{i}\right)}_{j}}{\partial {x}_{k}}={k}_{k}f'\left(k.\mathrm{x}-{C}_{m}t\right){k}_{j}$ with $m=S$ or $P$

As regards the impedance term, we need $\frac{\partial {\mathrm{u}}_{i}}{\partial t}=-{C}_{m}\dot{f}\left(k.\mathrm{x}-{C}_{m}t\right)k$ , again with $m=S$ or $P$ .

One then sees that the important function for a loading by plane wave with paraxial elements of order 0 is not the profile of the wave $f$ , but its derivative, either $f'$ or $\dot{f}$ . The wave being plane, the wave front is characterised by the planes $k.\mathrm{x}-{C}_{m}t=\mathit{cte}$ , whence the relation: $k.d\mathrm{x}={C}_{m}\mathit{dt}$ . There is therefore the following equivalence between the two derivatives of $f$ : $f'=\frac{1}{{C}_{m}}\dot{f}$ . One chooses to ask the user for the function $\dot{f}$ as a datum of the analysis.

However, in the optional case where one adds distributed stiffnesses along the boundary of the finite-element domain , the user must in addition supply the profile of the wave $f$ *.*

On the other hand, in order to compute correctly the time shift due to the passage of the wave, one must also indicate the entry point of the plane-wave loading. For taking the reflected wave into account, the latter is activated if one gives the position of the exit point. For further details on the use of a plane-wave loading in code_aster, one must consult the dedicated section of the command AFFE_CHAR_MECA.

### 4.4 The inclined plane-wave loading

In taking seismic waves into account from a deep source, the resulting waves may take an inclined direction. Now, in most modellings, only the pressure or shear plane waves in the vertical direction are taken into account. Generally, in soil–structure interaction (SSI) studies, the propagation of a seismic wave in the vertical direction is considered as the standard approach. Indeed, in most cases, one expects a verticalisation of the waves in the sedimentary basin in connection with the variation of the propagation speed of the shear waves ($v_S$). In the case where the structures are founded on rock (thus without the verticalisation effect by Snell's law due to superficial layers with a lower $v_S$) and for shallow seismic scenarios, the assumption of propagation in the vertical direction is not very realistic. Modelling with vertical propagation makes it possible to represent wave propagation fairly simply from analytical solutions or simplified models. However, this approach does not make it possible to take into account certain phase shifts due to the passage of an inclined wave. That is why it is important to adapt the modes of computation of the propagation of vertical waves to inclined waves. In this part, we present:

- The theoretical elements on elastic waves in isotropic solids
- The setting up of a modelling exclusively for homogeneous undamped media. For this specific case, the analytical formulation is known and makes it possible to impose directly the oblique plane-wave loading on the boundaries
- The setting up of a methodology generalising the application of inclined plane waves to the boundaries of the models in heterogeneous and/or damped media

##### 4.4.1.1 Longitudinal incident wave

In the case of isotropic solids, the speeds of the longitudinal and transverse waves are the same in all directions. It follows that the slownesses, that is the inverse of the speeds, are also the same in all directions. The Snell–Descartes laws can then be easily interpreted by observing the slowness surfaces presented in u2.06.05-longitu. Reading these surfaces quickly shows that the angle $\theta_T$ is always less than the angle of incidence $\theta_L$ and therefore that the reflected vertical transverse waves are always propagating plane waves.

### 4.5 Use in code_aster

Taking elastic absorbing elements into account and computing their impedance requires a specific modelling on the absorbing boundaries:

- in 2D with the modelling 'D_PLAN_ABSO' on the absorbing edges.
- in 3D with the modelling '3D_ABSO' on the absorbing faces.

The formulation of these elements being rudimentary enough for them precisely to be likened to discrete dampers and therefore to be used in harmonic analyses, consequently, on the one hand, one must not block them during dynamic analyses, and on the other hand, a counterpart is that the quality of their use depends on the quality of the shape of the boundary. A good test of this quality is inspired by the test cases SDLV120 and SDLV121 and can be based on the more or less total absorption, at the level of this boundary, of the passage of a wave of imposed displacement at the top of the structure. As for these tests, one can check that the wave does not come back by looking at a velocity degree of freedom on a node near the boundary.

In harmonic analysis with the operator DYNA_VIBRA [U4.53.03](doc:u4.53.03), one computes beforehand a mechanical damping by the option AMOR_MECA of the operator CALC_MATR_ELEM [U4.61.01](doc:u4.61.01) and one supplies it in DYNA_VIBRA (keyword MATR_AMOR).

In transient analysis, the computation of the mechanical damping is automatic with the modellings of absorbing elements in the operator DYNA_NON_LINE [U4.53.01](doc:u4.53.01). With the operator DYNA_VIBRA [U4.53.03](doc:u4.53.03), one computes beforehand this mechanical damping explicitly by the option AMOR_MECA of the operator CALC_MATR_ELEM [U4.61.01](doc:u4.61.01) and one supplies it by the keyword MATR_AMOR.

For these two analyses, the computation of the added mechanical stiffness is automatic with the modellings of absorbing elements when one systematically computes the option RIGI_MECA whatever the analysis operator.
