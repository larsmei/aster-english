# 1 **r5.03.52** Contact elements derived from a continuous hybrid formulation

**Summary:**

This document describes the manner in which frictional contact elements are derived from a continuous hybrid formulation of contact problems between solids (2D or 3D) in large transformations and specifies the solution strategy used.

An original formulation, called the stabilised Lagrangian formulation, is used, which makes it possible to recover the classical cases of the literature (Lagrangian, augmented Lagrangian, penalisation) by a judicious choice of its parameters.

This formulation is available in the command DEFI_CONTACT under the name 'CONTINUE'.

## 1 Introduction

There exists, for the treatment of the frictional-contact problem, a significant gap between the so-called “discrete” formulations and the so-called “continuous” formulation. Implementations in analysis codes are based on models such as those developed by Hallquist [], Alart and Curnier [], Laursen and Simo ([]).

The two essential objects of this document are, on the one hand, the description of the derivation of contact elements from a mixed variational formulation of a contact problem between three-dimensional solids, deformable and undergoing large transformations, and, on the other hand, the detail of the solution strategy.

This document progressively constructs the frictional-contact problem implemented in *Code_Aster* under the name of “continuous method for frictional contact”, which can be activated via the keyword FORMULATION='CONTINUE' in DEFI_CONTACT, initially proposed by Ben-Dhia and Zarroug [].

An abundant bibliography will be found at the end of the document. In particular, the four theses defended on the subject by M.Zarroug [], C.Zammali [], M.Torkhani [] and D.Kudawoo [].

## 2 Kinematics

### 2.1 Kinematics of the solids

One considers two deformable solids ${B}^{i}(i=1,2)$ in frictional contact. These two solids occupy, in their initial configuration, the closure of two domains ${O}_{0}^{1}$ and ${\Omega}_{0}^{2}$ of ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{3}$ and, in their current configuration at time instant $t$ , the closure of ${O}_{t}^{1}$ and ${O}_{t}^{2}$ (also contained in ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{3}$ ), respectively. One assumes that, in their initial configuration, these two solids are in a natural state, that is to say without residual stresses or pre-strains. Consider a material particle ${M}^{i}$ of the solid ${B}^{i}$ , located by the vector ${p}^{i}$ in the initial configuration. One denotes by ${\varphi}_{t}^{i}$ the transformation at time $t$ of the solid ${B}^{i}$ ; this mapping transports the particle ${M}^{i}$ towards the position ${x}_{t}^{i}$ in the current configuration. One has:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{\varphi}_{t}^{i}({p}^{i},t):& {\Omega}_{0}^{i}\times \left[0,t\right]\to {\Omega}_{t}^{i}\subset {\mathrm{ℝ}}^{3}\\ & {p}^{i}\to {x}_{t}^{i}={\varphi}_{t}^{i}({p}^{i},t)\end{array}
$$

During their motion, the solids may come into contact, as indicated on the . The boundary ${\Gamma}^{i}=\partial {\Omega}^{i}$ of each solid ${B}^{i}$ is decomposed into parts ${\Gamma}_{u}^{i}$ , ${\Gamma}_{g}^{i}$ and ${\Gamma}_{c}^{i}$ in the initial configuration, whose intersections are pairwise empty:

$$
{\Gamma}_{u}^{i}\cap {\Gamma}_{g}^{i}=\mathrm{\varnothing }
{\Gamma}_{u}^{i}\cap {\Gamma}_{c}^{i}=\varnothing
{\Gamma}_{c}^{i}\cap {\Gamma}_{g}^{i}=\varnothing
{\Gamma}_{u}^{i}\cup {\Gamma}_{g}^{i}\cup {\Gamma}_{c}^{i}={\Gamma}^{i}
$$

These boundaries deform in the current configuration into ${\gamma}_{u}^{i}$ , ${\gamma}_{g}^{i}$ and ${\gamma}_{c}^{i}$ . The solid ${B}^{i}$ is clamped on ${\Gamma}_{u}^{i}$ and subjected to a nominal surface-force density denoted ${g}^{i}$ on the part ${\Gamma}_{g}^{i}$ . Furthermore, one denotes by ${f}^{i}$ the body-force density field applied on the solids ${B}^{i}$ . The parts of the surfaces ${\Gamma}^{i}$ liable to come into contact during the deformation of the two solids are denoted ${\Gamma}_{c}^{i}$ . These surfaces are not known *a priori* (this is one of the non-linearities of the problem to be solved, as will be seen later).

The coordinates of the contact surfaces ${\Gamma}_{c}^{i}$ are transported by application of the deformation, at time $t$ *.* They are therefore constructed by composition of the two functions:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{\Phi}_{t}^{i}(\zeta ,t):& {\Gamma}_{c}^{i}\times \left[0,T\right]\to {\gamma}_{c}^{i}\\ & (\zeta ,t)\to {\Phi}_{t}^{i}(\zeta )={\varphi}_{t}^{i}\mathrm{°}{\Phi}^{i}(\zeta )\end{array}
$$

One defines the gradient tensor of the transformation $\underline{\underline{{F}_{t}^{i}}}$ by:

$$
\underline{\underline{{F}_{t}^{i}}}=\frac{\partial {x}_{t}^{i}}{\partial {p}^{i}}
$$

If one denotes by ${u}^{i}$ the displacement field of the solid ${B}^{i}$ :

$$
{x}_{t}^{i}={p}^{i}+{u}^{i}
$$

Then the gradient tensor of the transformation is rewritten:

$$
\underline{\underline{{F}_{t}^{i}}}=\nabla \otimes {u}^{i}+\underline{\underline{{I}_{}}}
$$

Using the function ${\varphi}_{t}^{i}$ , one may write:

$$
\underline{\underline{{F}_{t}^{i}}}=\frac{\partial {\varphi}_{t}^{i}({p}^{i},t)}{\partial {p}^{i}}={\nabla}_{{p}^{i}}\otimes {\varphi}_{t}^{i}
$$

Where $\otimes$ is the tensor product and ${\nabla}_{{p}^{i}}$ the gradient operator (in space). One introduces the Jacobian of the transformation:

$$
{J}_{t}^{i}=det(\underline{\underline{{F}_{t}^{i}}})
$$

One will denote by $\overline{{\Omega}^{i}}$ the strict interior of ${\Omega}^{i}$ and by ${\Gamma}^{i}$ its boundary:

$$
{\Omega}^{i}=\overline{{\Omega}^{i}}\cup {\Gamma}^{i}
$$

#### 2.1.1 Construction of the local bases

The two mappings ${\Phi}^{i}$ and ${\varphi}_{t}^{i}$ make it possible to define the local frame on the solid $i$ . This local frame is established by construction of the tangent plane at the point considered and therefore by the data of the two tangent vectors ${t}_{\alpha =1,2}^{i}$ on the deformed configuration of the surface ${\gamma}^{i}$ :

$$
{t}_{\alpha}^{i}({\zeta}_{c}^{i})=\frac{\partial {\Phi}_{t}^{i}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}=\frac{\partial {\varphi}_{t}^{i}({p}^{i},t)}{\partial {p}^{i}}.\frac{\partial {\Phi}^{i}(\zeta )}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}
$$

One may define in the same way the basis vectors of the initial surface ${\Gamma}_{c}^{i}$ :

$$
{T}_{\alpha}^{i}(\zeta )=\frac{\partial {\Phi}^{i}(\zeta )}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}
$$

Using the definition of the gradient tensor of the transformation (), one explicitly expresses the relation between ${T}_{\alpha}^{i}$ and ${t}_{\alpha}^{i}$ :

$$
{t}_{\alpha}^{i}=\underline{\underline{{F}_{i}^{}}}{T}_{\alpha}^{i}
$$

There is no reason for the ${t}_{\alpha}^{i}$ to be unit vectors and for the frame $({t}_{1}^{i},{t}_{2}^{i})$ to be orthogonal. We obtain the normals by a simple cross product:

$$
{n}^{i}=\frac{{t}_{1}^{i}\times {t}_{2}^{i}}{∥{t}_{1}^{i}\times {t}_{2}^{i}∥}
$$

In *Code_Aster* , the normal is always obtained according to the algorithm below:

- Computation of the tangent vectors on the current configuration;
- Modification of the tangent vectors (according to the options VECT_MAIT, VECT_ESCL);
- Computation of the normal vector by cross product at the point considered.

By construction, the tangent vectors ${t}_{\alpha}^{i}$ are orthogonal to the normal ${n}^{i}$ .

#### 2.1.2 Regularisation of the local bases

It is sometimes necessary to use a field of “smoothed” normals in order to minimise the risks of instabilities of the contact problem when the surface is described by polynomial fields (typically the case of finite elements). For this, a very simple strategy has been implemented in the code (which can be activated via the option LISSAGE). The algorithm is described below:

- For each element $e$ of the surface, for each node $s$ of this element shared by $k(s)$ elements, one computes the unit normal ${n}^{e}(s)$ at this node $s$ ;
- For each node $s$ , one takes the average ${n}_{L}(s)$ of the normals of each element sharing this common node:

$$
{n}_{L}(s)=\frac{{\sum}_{e=1}^{k\left(s\right)}{n}^{e}\left(s\right)}{∥{\sum}_{e=1}^{k\left(s\right)}{n}^{e}\left(s\right)∥}
$$

- One smooths the normal obtained by an interpolation consistent with the degree of the surface (linear or quadratic). If one denotes by ${N}_{e}^{s}$ the $\mathit{nno}(e)$ shape functions of the element $e$ , then the smoothed normal ${n}_{\text{lis}}(\overline{\zeta})$ at a point $\overline{\zeta}$ of the element is written simply:

$$
{n}_{\text{lis}}\left(\overline{\zeta}\right)={\sum}_{s=1}^{\mathit{nno}\left(e\right)}{N}_{e}^{s}\left(\overline{\zeta}\right).{n}_{L}(s)
$$

**Note:** given that one takes an unweighted arithmetic mean of the normals, if two adjacent elements are of very different size and normals, the smoothing obtained may be of poor quality.

#### 2.1.3 Some remarkable identities

In this paragraph, we shall present a certain number of remarkable identities relating to the properties of the basis $({t}_{1},{t}_{2},n)$ . We first recall the definition of the tangent vectors:

$$
{t}_{\alpha}=\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}
$$

The vectors ${t}_{\alpha}$ and $n$ are orthogonal to one another, so their scalar product is zero:

$$
{t}_{\alpha}\cdot n=0
$$

The vector $n$ is unit by definition; its product by itself is a constant (which equals $1$ ):

$$
n\cdot n=1
$$

One defines the matrix of the first fundamental form, or metric tensor $\underline{\underline{m}}$, whose components are:

$$
{m}_{\alpha \beta }={t}_{\alpha}\cdot {t}_{\beta}
$$

If the basis were orthonormal, this tensor would reduce to the identity tensor.

The inverse of the metric tensor is also the first fundamental form of the dual space; its components are:

$$
\frac{1}{{m}_{\alpha \beta }}={m}^{\alpha \beta }={t}^{\alpha}\cdot {t}^{\beta}=\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}\cdot \frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\beta}}
$$

To pass from the tangent vectors of the covariant basis to the contravariant basis, one uses the metric tensor 1
:

$$
{t}^{\alpha}={m}^{\alpha \beta }{t}_{\beta}
$$

With:

$$
{m}_{\alpha \beta }{m}^{\alpha \beta }=1
$$

One also defines the tensor of the second fundamental form, related to the curvature, whose components are the following:

$$
{\kappa}_{\alpha \beta }=\frac{{\partial}^{2}\overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}\partial {\zeta}_{\beta}}\cdot n
$$

### 2.2 Kinematic condition of non-interpenetration

To express non-interpenetration, one proceeds as follows:

- One couples the points of the contact surfaces two by two: this is the *pairing;*
- One imposes between the two paired points a condition of non-interpenetration along a given direction (generally the normal to one of the two surfaces at the point concerned).

#### 2.2.1 Pairing problem

By convention, one will call slave surface the contact surface defined on the solid $1$ and master surface the paired surface defined on the solid $2$ . One carries out the pairing by seeking, for every point $x$ of the boundary ${\gamma}_{c}^{1}$ , the point $\overline{x}$ of ${\gamma}_{c}^{2}$ that is closest. This amounts to solving the following minimisation problem:

$$
\forall x\in {\gamma}_{c}\text{et}\forall t\ge 0
{x}_{t}={\varphi}_{t}^{1}({p}^{1},t)
\textrm{trouver}
\overline{\zeta}\in \omega \times \left[0,t\right]
\overline{\zeta}=\underset{\zeta \in \omega }{\text{ArgMin}}\left\lbrace \frac{1}{2}.{∥{\varphi}_{t}^{1}({\Phi}^{1}(\zeta ),t)-{\varphi}_{t}^{2}({\Phi}^{2}(\zeta ),t)∥}^{2}\right\rbrace
$$

The solution $\bar{\zeta}$ is the position in the parametric reference space of the projection $M$ of the slave node $P$ onto the master mesh cell.

#### 2.2.2 Definition of the gap

Pairing makes it possible to define the point $\overline{p}$ of the surface ${\Gamma}_{c}^{2}$ , paired with $p$ at time instant $t$ in the original configuration, and the point $\overline{x}$ paired with $x$ at time instant *t* in the current configuration. The distance vector $d$ between the paired points is quite simply:

$$
d=x-\overline{x}={\Phi}_{t}^{1}(\zeta )-{\Phi}_{t}^{2}(\overline{\zeta})
$$

It is the result of the minimisation described in the system ():

$$
\overline{d}=\underset{\zeta \in \omega }{min}\left\lbrace \frac{1}{2}.{∥{\varphi}_{t}^{1}({\Phi}^{1}(\zeta ),t)-{\varphi}_{t}^{2}({\Phi}^{2}(\zeta ),t)∥}^{2}\right\rbrace
$$

The first optimality condition is written:

$$
\left[{\Phi}_{t}^{1}(\zeta )-{\Phi}_{t}^{2}(\overline{\zeta})\right]\cdot {t}_{\alpha}(\overline{\zeta})=0
$$

With the definition of the tangent vectors on the deformed surface ${\gamma}_{c}^{2}$ :

$$
{t}_{\alpha}(\overline{\zeta})=\frac{\partial {\Phi}_{t}^{2}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}=\frac{\partial {\varphi}_{t}^{2}}{\partial \overline{p}}.\frac{\partial {\Phi}^{2}(\overline{\zeta})}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}
$$

One can go back to the basis vectors of the initial surface ${\Gamma}_{c}^{2}$ :

$$
{T}_{\alpha}({\overline{\zeta}}_{t})=\frac{\partial {\Phi}^{2}({\overline{\zeta}}_{t})}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}
$$

Using the definition of the gradient tensor of the transformation, one explicitly expresses the relation between ${T}_{\alpha}$ and ${t}_{\alpha}$ :

$$
{t}_{\alpha}=\underline{\underline{{F}_{2}^{}}}{T}_{\alpha}
$$

We obtain the normal by a simple cross product:

$$
n({\overline{\zeta}}_{t})=\frac{{t}_{1}\times {t}_{2}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{t}_{1}\times {t}_{2}\mathrm{\parallel }}
$$

The basis constructed by the three vectors $({t}_{1},{t}_{2},n)$ has no reason *a priori* to be orthonormal. On the other hand, by construction, the normal $n$ is unit and normal to the plane defined by the vectors $({t}_{1},{t}_{2})$ which define the tangent plane to the deformed surface ${\gamma}_{c}^{2}$ . Taking up equality () one necessarily has:

$$
\left[{\Phi}_{t}^{1}(\zeta )-{\Phi}_{t}^{2}(\overline{\zeta})\right]={d}_{n}n
$$

${d}_{n}$ is a signed algebraic quantity. Since $x={\Phi}_{t}^{1}(\zeta )$ , one obtains:

$$
x={\Phi}_{t}^{2}(\overline{\zeta})+{d}_{n}n
$$

${d}_{n}$ is called the **gap** between the two solids at the point $x$ . This gives us the relation between the basis $({t}_{1}^{1},{t}_{2}^{1},{n}^{1})$ defined on the tangent plane at the slave point and the basis $({t}_{1},{t}_{2},n)$ constructed by the projection of the point $x$ onto the deformed (master) surface ${\gamma}_{c}^{2}$ :

$$
{t}_{\alpha}^{1}(\overline{\zeta})={t}_{\alpha}(\overline{\zeta})+{d}_{n}\frac{\partial n}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}(\overline{\zeta})
{n}^{1}=n
$$

The two frames coincide if the points are in contact (if ${d}_{n}=0$ ). The distance is decomposed into a normal part ${d}_{n}$ and a tangential part ${d}_{\tau}$ :

$$
d={d}_{n}n+{d}_{\tau}
$$

One obtains for the expression of ${d}_{\tau}$ :

$$
{d}_{\tau}={\underline{\underline{{P}_{}}}}^{\tau}d={\underline{\underline{{P}_{}}}}^{\tau}(x-\overline{x})
$$

With ${\underline{\underline{{P}_{}}}}^{\tau}$ the projection matrix onto the tangent plane:

$$
{\underline{\underline{{P}_{}}}}^{\tau}=\underline{\underline{{I}_{}}}-\underline{\underline{{n}_{}\otimes {n}_{}}}
$$

One may write the condition of non-interpenetration between $x\in {\gamma}_{c}^{1}$ and $\overline{x}\in {\gamma}_{c}^{2}$ along the search direction $n=-{n}^{2}(\overline{x})$ , the inward unit normal at the point $\overline{x}$ (directed towards the interior of ${\Omega}_{t}^{2}$ ):

$$
{d}_{n}=(x-\overline{x})\cdot n\le 0
$$

This quantity is objective.

**Note:**

- Given the choice of the normal, the gap is “reversed” with respect to the case of contact in discrete formulation in *Code_Aster* : there is interpenetration if ${d}_{n}\ge 0$ ;

#### 2.2.3 Relative velocity

To write Coulomb’s friction law, we shall need the notion of relative slip velocity between the two solids. For this we take up the definition of the normal gap ():

$$
\left[{\Phi}_{t}^{1}(\zeta )-{\Phi}_{t}^{2}(\overline{\zeta})\right]={d}_{n}n
$$

One begins by differentiating the right-hand term with respect to time:

$$
\frac{d}{\mathit{dt}}\left({d}_{n}n\right)=\dot{{d}_{n}}n+{d}_{n}\left(\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial n}{\partial \overline{{\zeta}^{\alpha}}}\frac{\partial \overline{{\zeta}^{\alpha}}}{\partial t}\right)
$$

With the usual convention of summation on the dummy indices $\alpha$ . Then one differentiates the left-hand term:

$$
\frac{d}{\mathit{dt}}({\Phi}_{t}^{1}(\zeta )-{\Phi}_{t}^{2}(\overline{\zeta}))=\frac{\partial {\Phi}_{t}^{1}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial {\Phi}_{t}^{2}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial {\Phi}_{t}^{2}}{\partial \overline{{\zeta}^{\alpha}}}\frac{\partial \overline{{\zeta}^{\alpha}}}{\partial t}
$$

One recognises the definition of ${t}_{\alpha}$ in the term $\frac{\partial {\Phi}_{t}^{2}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}$ (see ()). One equates the two terms () and ():

$$
\dot{{d}_{n}}.n+{d}_{n}.(\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial n}{\partial \overline{{\zeta}^{\alpha}}}.\frac{\partial \overline{\zeta}}{\partial t})=\frac{\partial {\Phi}_{t}^{1}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial {\Phi}_{t}^{2}}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial \overline{{\zeta}_{\alpha}^{}}}{\partial t}{t}_{\alpha}
$$

Setting $v(x,t)$ the velocity of the point $x$ and $\overline{v}(\overline{x},t)$ the velocity of the paired point $\overline{x}$ :

$$
v(x,t)=\frac{\partial {\Phi}_{t}^{1}}{\partial t}
$$

One finally obtains, using ():

$$
\dot{{d}_{n}}n+{d}_{n}\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial \overline{{\zeta}_{\alpha}^{}}}{\partial t}{t}_{\alpha}^{1}={v}^{1}-\overline{v}
$$

One denotes by ${v}_{\text{gliss}}$ the slip velocity:

$$
{v}_{\text{gliss}}=v-(\bar{v}+{d}_{n}\frac{\partial n}{\partial t})
$$

This quantity is objective because it is invariant under a change of frame. The relative slip velocity is identified with the velocity difference of the two solids only when they are in contact (${d}_{n}=0$ ).

#### 2.2.4 Discontinuity operator

One denotes by $\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}(p)$ the discontinuity of the quantity $g$ between solid $1$ and solid $2$ . This discontinuity is written at the point $p$ on $1$ which projects onto $\bar{p}$ on $2$ according to the pairing mapping:

$$
\mathrm{〚}g(p)\mathrm{〛}={g}^{1}(p)-{g}^{2}(\overline{p})
$$

In what follows one will abandon the notation $\mathrm{〚}g(p)\mathrm{〛}$ in favour of $\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}$ , making the implicit assumption that the discontinuity is written on the pair $(p,\overline{p})$ of paired points. One may decompose this discontinuity into a normal part ${\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}}_{n}$ and a tangential part ${\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}$ :

$$
\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}={\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}}_{n}n+{\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}
$$

With:

$$
{\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}}_{n}=\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}\cdot n
$$

Where $n$ is the normal to the master surface, directed towards the interior of solid $2$ .

The tangential part:

$$
{\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}={\underline{\underline{{P}_{}}}}^{\tau}\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}
$$

With the notations introduced, the gap ${d}_{n}$ may be written:

$$
{d}_{n}={\mathrm{〚}x\mathrm{〛}}_{n}={\mathrm{〚}p+u\mathrm{〛}}_{n}
$$

It should be noted that the quantities projected onto the normal are necessarily scalars, whence the notation ${\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}}_{n}$ and not ${\mathrm{〚}g\mathrm{〛}}_{n}$ .

### 2.3 Variations of the kinematic quantities

The contact surfaces are described by a set of four parameters $({\zeta}_{1}^{1},{\zeta}_{2}^{1},{\zeta}_{1}^{2},{\zeta}_{2}^{2})$ , in the reference (parametric) space of the finite elements. The master/slave approach makes it possible to reduce this number by describing the kinematic variables $\zeta$ with respect to a body taken as reference (in this case, the body defined as “master”). The adequate choice of the reference surface is not immediate and remains a modelling assumption to be taken into consideration, especially in the cases of a large relative slip of the two surfaces. It is necessary to distinguish Lagrangian derivatives from Eulerian derivatives. This paragraph aims to evaluate the different variations. One defines the variation $\delta f({x}_{0})$ of a function $f(x)$ of one variable at a given point ${x}_{0}$ by the quantity:

$$
\delta f({x}_{O})=\underset{\delta x\to 0}{lim}(f({x}_{0}+{\delta}_{x})-f({x}_{0}))={\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\mid }_{{x}_{0}}\delta x
$$

In the case of a function of several variables, one distinguishes the total variation (with respect to all its variables), denoted $\tilde{\delta}f$ , and the *partial* variation with respect to a variable $x$ , denoted ${\delta}_{x}f$ . The total variation is the sum of all the partial variations. Let $\tilde{\delta}$ denote the total variation of a kinematic quantity. If one denotes by $t$ the time variable, one will have, in the case of an Eulerian quantity $\bar{a}$ :

$$
\tilde{\delta}\bar{a}=\frac{\partial \bar{a}}{\partial t}\delta t+\frac{\partial \bar{a}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}
$$

It is therefore the sum of a variation ${\delta}_{t}$ with respect to time $t$ and of the variation ${\delta}_{\zeta}$ with respect to the parametric coordinates:

$$
\tilde{\delta}\bar{a}={\delta}_{t}\bar{a}+{\delta}_{{\zeta}_{\alpha}}\bar{a}
$$

If one now considers a Lagrangian quantity $a$ , its variation with respect to the parametric coordinates $\zeta$ is zero:

$$
\tilde{\delta}a={\delta}_{t}a\text{car}{\delta}_{{\zeta}_{\alpha}}a=0
$$

The quantities linked to the slave surface are Lagrangian quantities; those linked to the master surface are Eulerian quantities.

#### 2.3.1 The Weingarten formulae

One first uses the fact that the variation of a constant is zero whatever the parameter that varies. Now, according to () the product $(n\cdot n)$ is constant, so:

$$
\frac{1}{2}\delta (n\cdot n)=0
$$

Expanding () one obtains:

$$
\delta n\cdot n=0
$$

The product is zero if, and only if, $\delta n$ is orthogonal to $n$ , and therefore $\delta n$ belongs to the tangent plane:

$$
\delta n=a{t}_{1}+b{t}_{2}
\forall a,b\in \mathrm{ℝ}\times \mathrm{ℝ}
$$

One notes that the variation of the normal belongs to the tangent plane, using the constants ${c}_{\beta}$ :

$$
\delta n={c}_{\beta}{t}_{\beta}
\forall {c}_{\beta}\in \mathrm{ℝ}
$$

We shall now establish some very practical formulae which will be needed later. The first thing to do is to give the formula establishing the value of the coefficients ${c}_{\beta}$ . One begins by establishing the following scalar product:

$$
\delta n\cdot {t}_{\alpha}={c}_{\beta}{t}_{\beta}\cdot {t}_{\alpha}={c}_{\beta}{m}_{\alpha \beta }
$$

Next, one starts from the property ():

$$
\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\cdot n=0
$$

One computes its first variation; the variation of a constant is zero whatever the parameter that varies:

$$
\delta (\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}})\cdot n+\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\cdot \delta n=0\to \frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\cdot \delta n=-\delta (\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}})\cdot n=-{\kappa}_{\alpha \beta }
$$

One recovers the components of the second fundamental form, up to the sign. By identification with (), one has the following relation:

$$
{c}_{\beta}{m}_{\alpha \beta }=-{\kappa}_{\alpha \beta }\to {c}_{\beta}=-{m}^{\alpha \beta }{\kappa}_{\alpha \beta }
$$

The variation of the normal is therefore:

$$
\delta n=-{m}^{\alpha \beta }{\kappa}_{\alpha \beta }{t}_{\beta}
$$

By application of (), one has:

$$
\delta n=-{\kappa}_{\alpha \beta }{t}^{\alpha}
$$

Using (), one has:

$$
\delta n=-(\delta (\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}})\cdot n)\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}
$$

This is the Weingarten formula.

#### 2.3.2 Variation of the normal gap

Starting from the definition of the normal gap ():

$$
x-\overline{x}={d}_{n}n
$$

One applies the first total variation:

$$
\tilde{\delta}x=\tilde{\delta}\overline{x}+\tilde{\delta}{d}_{n}n+{d}_{n}\tilde{\delta}n
$$

By definition $\overline{x}$ is an Eulerian quantity; its variation is therefore expressed by the formula ():

$$
\tilde{\delta}\overline{x}={\delta}_{t}\overline{x}+\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}
$$

Likewise for the variation of the normal:

$$
\tilde{\delta}n={\delta}_{t}n+\frac{\partial n}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}
$$

Since $x$ is a Lagrangian quantity, $\tilde{\delta}x={\delta}_{t}x$ . Finally:

$$
{\delta}_{t}x={\delta}_{t}\overline{x}+\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}+{\delta}_{t}{d}_{n}n+{d}_{n}({\delta}_{t}n+\frac{\partial n}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\delta {\zeta}^{\alpha})
$$

If one takes the product of this identity with the normal $n$ :

$$
{\delta}_{t}x\cdot n={\delta}_{t}\overline{x}\cdot n+\delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\cdot n+\tilde{\delta}{d}_{n}n\cdot n+{d}_{n}({\delta}_{t}n+\delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}\frac{\partial n}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}})\cdot n
$$

One considers the properties (), () and (), that is to say:

$$
\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\cdot n=0
$$

Which simplifies the expression:

$$
\tilde{\delta}{d}_{n}=n\cdot ({\delta}_{t}x-{\delta}_{t}\overline{x})={\delta}_{t}{d}_{n}
$$

The first variation of the gap does not bring about the appearance of transport terms (as in the equation ). ${d}_{n}$ is therefore a purely Lagrangian quantity.

#### 2.3.3 Variation of the parametric coordinates

One starts again from the first variation of the expression() (taking into account the fact that ${d}_{n}$ is a Lagrangian quantity):

$$
{\delta}_{t}x={\delta}_{t}\overline{x}+\frac{\partial \overline{x}}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}+\delta {d}_{n}n+{d}_{n}({\delta}_{t}n+\frac{\partial n}{\partial {\zeta}_{\alpha}}\delta {\zeta}^{\alpha})
$$

## 3 Contact and friction laws

In this chapter, we shall write the different forms equivalent to the standard frictional-contact laws (Signorini law and Coulomb law). The proof of equivalence between the different forms (Signorini, Coulomb, augmented Lagrangian, stabilised Lagrangian) is available in [].

In *Code_Aster* , it is the “stabilised Lagrangian” forms which are used.

### 3.1 Contact law

#### 3.1.1 Signorini law

The *Signorini law* for unilateral contact is written:

$$
\left\lbrace {P}^{C}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\equiv }\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{n}\le 0& (a)\\ {d}_{n}\le 0& (b)\\ {\lambda}_{n}{d}_{n}=0& (c)\end{array}
$$

where ${d}_{n}$ is the algebraic gap defined by equation () and ${\lambda}_{n}$ is the contact pressure. The graph of the Signorini law is shown on the . The first condition restates the non-adhesion condition and the second condition expresses impenetrability.

The contact problem thus posed introduces a *multi-valued* relation (${\lambda}_{n}$ is not a function of ${d}_{n}$ ), *positive semi-definite* and *non-differentiable* at ${\lambda}_{n}={d}_{n}=0$ . The Signorini contact law is equivalent to a constitutive law. It is analogous to the case of perfect rigid plasticity. It should therefore be noted that the gap ${d}_{n}$ is equivalent to the measure of a strain and that it must therefore be objective.

#### 3.1.2 Contact law – Convex analysis

Using the tools of convex analysis, one may write the Signorini law in the following form:

$$
\left\lbrace {P}_{\mathit{cvxe}}^{C}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\equiv }\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{n}\in \partial {I}_{{\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}}({d}_{n})& (a)\\ {d}_{n}\in \partial {I}_{{\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}}({\lambda}_{n})& (b)\end{array}
$$

$\partial$ denoting the subdifferential and ${I}_{K}$ the indicator function of the convex set $K$ :

$$
{I}_{K}(x)=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}0& \text{si}\end{array}x\in K\\ \begin{array}{cc}\infty & \text{si}\end{array}x\mathrm{\notin }K\end{array}
$$

The Signorini contact law consists of inequalities (search for a solution in a given convex set); one uses this writing when one solves the contact problem by constrained optimisation methods: this is the methodology favoured by the *discrete* formulations in *Code_Aster* . In the continuous method, one uses a form of semi-regularisation by using projection operators onto a given convex set.

#### 3.1.3 Contact law – Projection

The augmented-Lagrangian method applied to contact consists in using the projection onto the convex set ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}$ of a quantity called the *augmented* contact multiplier ${g}_{n}$ , defined by:

$$
{g}_{n}={\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{d}_{n}
$$

where ${\rho}_{n}$ is a strictly positive real number, with the dimension of a force per unit volume:

$$
\left[{\rho}_{n}\right]=\frac{[F]}{{[L]}^{3}}
$$

The Signorini problem ${P}^{C}$ is then rewritten:

$$
\left\lbrace {P}_{{\mathit{Proj}}_{{\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}}({g}_{n})}^{C}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\equiv }\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{n}={\mathit{Proj}}_{{\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}}({g}_{n})& (a)\\ {g}_{n}={\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{d}_{n}& (b)\end{array}
$$

The idea of this formulation is to replace the non-differentiable energetic part of the contact by the square of the projection of ${g}_{n}$ onto an appropriate convex set, which makes the energy functional of the augmented Lagrangian differentiable. The projection operator is explicitly:

$$
{\mathit{Proj}}_{{\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}}({g}_{n})=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{g}_{n}\text{si}{g}_{n}\le 0\\ 0\text{si}{g}_{n}>0\end{array}
$$

The *augmented* contact multiplier ${g}_{n}$ is a straight line in the space $({d}_{n},{\lambda}_{n})$ . With the projection operator ${\mathit{Proj}}_{{\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}}(x)$ , one is brought back onto the Signorini graph. ${\mathit{Proj}}_{{\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}}(x)$ is continuous on ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}$ and differentiable almost everywhere.

#### 3.1.4 Contact law – Stabilised Lagrangian

The idea of the sign-field formulation (also called the *stabilised* formulation) is to dissociate the status of a contact point from the value of the augmented Lagrangian. One introduces the same multiplier ${g}_{n}$ as in the case of the augmented Lagrangian (equation ). One defines the sign field (“level-set”) denoted ${S}_{u}^{x}$ by the following expression:

$$
{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}{g}_{n}\le 0\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{si}{g}_{n}>0\end{array}\end{array}
$$

This operator is the characteristic function of the variable ${g}_{n}$ on the convex set ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}$ . It is not differentiable. The Signorini problem ${P}^{C}$ is then rewritten:

$$
\left\lbrace {P}_{\mathit{stab}}^{C}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\equiv }\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{n}-{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}({\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{d}_{n})=0& (a)\\ {g}_{n}={\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{d}_{n}& (b)\\ {S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}{g}_{n}\le 0\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{si}{g}_{n}>0\end{array}\end{array}& (c)\end{array}
$$

The *augmented* contact multiplier ${g}_{n}$ is a straight line. The non-regular character of the contact law is the non-differentiable character coming from the sign field. ${S}_{u}^{x}$ is continuous almost everywhere (except at $x=0$ ) and differentiable almost everywhere. The two operators ${S}_{u}^{x}$ and ${\mathit{Proj}}_{{\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}}(x)$ are linked by a simple relation:

$$
x{S}_{u}^{x}={\mathit{Proj}}_{{\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}}(x)
$$

It is this form which is used in *Code_Aster* .

### 3.2 Friction law

#### 3.2.1 Coulomb law

To represent friction phenomena, one uses the *Coulomb law* which is written as follows:

$$
\left\lbrace {P}^{F}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\equiv }\begin{array}{cc}& \lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}\text{Si}\mathrm{\parallel }{\lambda}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }<\mu \mid {\lambda}_{n}\mid \text{alors}{v}_{\tau}=0& (a)\\ \text{Si}\mathrm{\parallel }{\lambda}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }=\mu \mid {\lambda}_{n}\mid \text{alors}\frac{{v}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{v}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}=-\frac{{\lambda}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{\lambda}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}& (b)\\ {v}_{\tau}(\mathrm{\parallel }{\lambda}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }-\mu \mid {\lambda}_{n}\mid )=0& (c)\end{array}\end{array}
$$

where $\mu$ is called the *Coulomb friction coefficient* (assumed constant and isotropic), and ${v}_{\tau}$ is the *tangential relative velocity* , defined as the projection, into the plane tangent to the surface at the contact point, of the velocity jump of solid $1$ with respect to solid $2$ . When the contact is sliding, since ${\lambda}_{n}$ is always negative by assumption, one may write:

$$
{\lambda}_{\tau}=\mu {\lambda}_{n}\frac{{v}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{v}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}
$$

The quantity $\frac{{v}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{v}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}$ corresponds to the slip direction, unknown, but of which one knows that it takes place in the tangent plane constituted by the two vectors $({t}_{1},{t}_{2})$ . The third law, analogous to that of equation (c) introduced for contact, imposes the vanishing of the friction forces when there is no contact. Friction induces the notion of a threshold. The relation introduced by Coulomb friction is *multi-valued* and *non-differentiable* . The expression of ${v}_{\tau}$ is given by:

$$
{v}_{\tau}({p}^{1},t)={\mathrm{〚}v\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}=\mathrm{〚}v\mathrm{〛}-{\mathrm{〚}v\mathrm{〛}}_{n}n
$$

The tangential velocity jump ${v}_{\tau}$ **is not objective** (see []) *.* Strictly speaking, one should use ${v}_{\mathrm{gliss}}$ (see p. 13 ). However, these two definitions are identical when there is contact (${d}_{n}=0$ ). The Coulomb friction law is written in *velocity* and not in *displacement* , unlike the Signorini law. This constitutes one of the difficulties to be considered in the case of a quasi-static solution of the frictional-contact problem. One cannot directly replace the velocity by the displacement without making the assumption that the loading in the contact zone varies in a monotonic manner, which, in practice, is an assumption impossible to satisfy for an arbitrary loading of the structure.

Nevertheless, one may write the Signorini law in incremental form, by considering the *displacement increment* ; this way of proceeding is correct from the point of view of respecting the Coulomb law. But the difficulty is that, not knowing *a priori* the value of the total quantity of the contact pressure, the Coulomb problem must be solved in two stages.

#### 3.2.2 Friction law – Convex analysis

Using the tools of convex analysis, one may write the Coulomb law in the following form:

$$
\left\lbrace {P}_{\mathit{cvxe}}^{F}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\equiv }\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{\tau}\in \partial {I}_{C({\lambda}_{n})}({v}_{\tau})& (a)\\ {v}_{\tau}\in \partial {I}_{{C}^{\text{*}}({\lambda}_{n})}({\lambda}_{\tau})& (b)\end{array}
$$

With the convex set $C({\lambda}_{n})$ which represents the Coulomb cone:

$$
C({\lambda}_{n})=\left\lbrace \forall {\lambda}_{\tau}\text{tel que}\mathrm{\parallel }{\lambda}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }<\mu \mid {\lambda}_{n}\mid \right\rbrace
$$

#### 3.2.3 Friction law – Projection

The augmented-Lagrangian method applied to friction is analogous to the case of contact, except that the convex set considered changes. Consider the *augmented* friction multiplier ${g}_{\tau}$ , defined by:

$$
{g}_{\tau}={\lambda}_{\tau}+{\rho}_{\tau}{v}_{\tau}
$$

where ${\rho}_{\tau}$ is a strictly positive real number, with the dimension of a force per unit volume:

$$
\left[{\rho}_{\tau}\right]=\frac{[F]}{{[L]}^{3}}
$$

The Coulomb problem ${P}^{F}$ is then rewritten:

$$
\left\lbrace {P}_{{\mathit{Proj}}_{C({\lambda}_{n})}}^{F}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\equiv }\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{\tau}={\mathit{Proj}}_{C({\lambda}_{n})}({g}_{\tau})& (a)\\ {g}_{\tau}={\lambda}_{\tau}+{\rho}_{\tau}{v}_{\tau}& (b)\end{array}
$$

The projection operator is not orthogonal:

$$
{\mathit{Proj}}_{C({\lambda}_{n})}({g}_{\tau})=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{g}_{\tau}\text{si}{g}_{\tau}\in C({\lambda}_{n})\\ \frac{\mu ∣{\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{d}_{n}∣{g}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{g}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}\text{si}{g}_{\tau}\mathrm{\notin }C({\lambda}_{n})\end{array}
$$

#### 3.2.4 Friction law – Normalised projection

In contact, we have used the same definition of the augmented multiplier between the augmented Lagrangian formulation and the stabilised Lagrangian formulation. On the other hand, for friction, we shall proceed otherwise. In the first place, one introduces a quantity $\Lambda$ called the **Lagrange semi-multiplier** :

$$
{\lambda}_{\tau}=\mu {\lambda}_{n}\Lambda
$$

This normalised quantity belongs to the unit ball:

$$
\Lambda \in B(0,1)\subset {\mathrm{ℝ}}^{2}
$$

One calls it a Lagrange **semi** -multiplier because this quantity has two meanings:

1. In the case of sticking friction, $\Lambda$ represents the sticking-force density. It is therefore formally the Lagrange multiplier associated with the sticking condition (zero tangential relative velocity);
2. In the case of sliding, $\Lambda$ indicates the slip direction (normalised) and one has $\Lambda =\frac{-{\lambda}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{\lambda}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}=\frac{{v}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{v}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}$ ; which is not the definition of a Lagrange multiplier in the “classical” sense (see p. 3.2.3 );

One notes that ${\lambda}_{n}$ being always negative or zero (usual pressure convention), ${\lambda}_{\tau}$ is indeed of opposite sign to $\Lambda$ , which indicates the slip direction. This change of variable makes it possible to pass from the unknown in tangential force to the direction of this force. From then on, one introduces the augmented friction semi-multiplier ${h}_{\tau}$ (in order to distinguish it from the “classical” ${g}_{\tau}$ , see () such that:

$$
{h}_{\tau}=\Lambda +{\rho}_{t}{v}_{\tau}
$$

It should be noted that the augmentation coefficient ${\rho}_{t}$ has a unit different from ${\rho}_{\tau}$ in the case of the augmented Lagrangian. Indeed, it is written as the ratio of a time to a length:

$$
\left[{\rho}_{t}\right]=\frac{[T]}{[L]}
$$

In the augmented Lagrangian formulation, the augmentation coefficient ${\rho}_{\tau}$ is of the same unit as the contact augmentation coefficient (see ). The Coulomb problem ${P}^{F}$ is rewritten:

$$
\left\lbrace {P}_{{\mathit{Proj}}_{B(0,1)}}^{F}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\equiv }\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}\Lambda ={\mathit{Proj}}_{B(0,1)}({h}_{\tau})& (a)\\ {h}_{\tau}=\Lambda +{\rho}_{t}{v}_{\tau}& (b)\end{array}
$$

This projection operator is orthogonal:

$$
{\mathit{Proj}}_{B(0,1)}({h}_{\tau})=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{h}_{\tau}\text{si}{h}_{\tau}\in B(0,1)\\ \tau =\frac{{h}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{h}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}\text{si}{h}_{\tau}\mathrm{\notin }B(0,1)\end{array}
$$

One will denote:

$$
\tau =\frac{{h}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{h}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}
$$

One will proceed to the computation of the variation with respect to the variable $x$ :

$$
{\delta}_{{h}_{\tau}}{\mathit{Proj}}_{B(0,1)}({h}_{\tau})=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\underline{\underline{1}}\text{si}{h}_{\tau}\in B(0,1)\\ {\underline{\underline{P}}}^{B(0,1)}\text{si}{h}_{\tau}\mathrm{\notin }B(0,1)\end{array}
$$

One will denote the projection matrix onto the unit ball:

$$
{\underline{\underline{P}}}^{B(0,1)}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{\parallel }{h}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}(\underline{\underline{1}}-\frac{\underline{\underline{{h}_{\tau}\otimes {h}_{\tau}}}}{{\mathrm{\parallel }{h}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}^{2}})
$$

#### 3.2.5 Friction law – Stabilised Lagrangian

One defines the sign field (“level-set”) denoted ${S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}$ by the following expression:

$$
{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}{h}_{\tau}\in B(0,1)\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{sinon}\end{array}\end{array}
$$

This operator is the characteristic function of the variable ${h}_{\tau}$ on the convex set $B(0,1)$ . It is not differentiable. The Coulomb problem ${P}^{F}$ is then rewritten:

$$
\left\lbrace {P}_{\mathit{stab}}^{F}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\equiv }\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}(1-{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}})\Lambda +{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}\left\lbrace (1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\Lambda -{\rho}_{t}{v}_{\tau}{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}-(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau \right\rbrace =0& (a)\\ {h}_{\tau}=\Lambda +{\rho}_{t}{v}_{\tau}& (b)\\ {S}_{f}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}{h}_{\tau}\in B(0,1)\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{sinon}\end{array}\end{array}& (c)\end{array}
$$

The stabilised Lagrangian form is an **exact** formulation of the friction problem. It is this form which is used in *Code_Aster* .

### 3.3 Frictional-contact law

#### 3.3.1 Augmented Lagrangian

We write the augmented Lagrangian of the frictional-contact problem. Consider the convex set $K$ such that:

$$
K=\left\lbrace \lambda ;\lambda ={\lambda}_{\tau}+{\lambda}_{n}n\text{avec}{\lambda}_{n}\in {\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}\text{et}{\lambda}_{\tau}\in C({\lambda}_{n})\right\rbrace
$$

Then the augmented Lagrangian of the contact/friction problem is written:

$$
\left\lbrace {P}_{\mathit{augm}}^{\mathit{CF}}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\equiv }\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}\lambda -{\mathit{Proj}}_{K}(\hat{\lambda})=0& (a)\\ \hat{\lambda}={g}_{\tau}+{g}_{n}n& (b)\\ {g}_{n}={\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{d}_{n}& (c)\\ {g}_{\tau}={\lambda}_{\tau}+{\rho}_{t}{v}_{\tau}& (d)\end{array}
$$

The projection onto the convex set $K$ is explicitly:

$$
{\mathit{Proj}}_{K}(\hat{\lambda})=\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}\hat{\lambda}\text{si}\hat{\lambda}\in K& (a)\\ {g}_{n}n+\mu {\lambda}_{n}\frac{{g}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{g}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}\text{si}{g}_{n}\le 0\text{et}\mathrm{\parallel }{g}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }>\mu {\lambda}_{n}& (b)\\ 0\text{si}{\lambda}_{n}>0& (c)\end{array}
$$

Case (a) corresponds to sticking contact, case (b) is sliding contact and case (c) is the case without contact (nor friction).

#### 3.3.2 Stabilised Lagrangian

We write the stabilised Lagrangian of the contact/friction problem:

$$
\left\lbrace {P}_{\mathit{stab}}^{\mathit{CF}}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\equiv }\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{n}-{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}({\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{d}_{n})=0& (a)\\ (1-{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}})\Lambda +{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}\left\lbrace (1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\Lambda -{\rho}_{t}{v}_{\tau}{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}-(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau \right\rbrace =0& (b)\\ {g}_{n}={\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{d}_{n}& (c)\\ {h}_{\tau}=\Lambda +{\rho}_{t}{v}_{\tau}& (d)\\ {\lambda}_{\tau}=\mu {\lambda}_{n}\Lambda & (e)\\ \tau =\frac{{h}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{h}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}& (f)\end{array}
$$

With the two sign fields:

$$
{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}{g}_{n}\le 0\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{si}{g}_{n}>0\end{array}\end{array}
$$

It is this form which is used in *Code_Aster* .

#### 3.3.3 Differences between the augmented Lagrangian and the stabilised Lagrangian

The two writings belong to the category of unconstrained optimisation methods. The main differences come from the use of a “sign field” (or level-set) and, for friction, from a change of variable reducing the Coulomb disk to a disk of unit radius.

- By dissociating the writing of the augmented Lagrangian from that of the status (contact/separation and sticking/sliding), this allows us to introduce the status unknowns *explicitly* in the case of the stabilised Lagrangian;
- In the stabilised Lagrangian, one uses a friction semi-multiplier, which has as its principal effect a different unit between the augmented friction Lagrangian ${\rho}_{t}$ and the augmented contact Lagrangian ${\rho}_{n}$ ; It is this form which is used in *Code_Aster* .

The proof of the equivalence between these two writings will be found in [].

## 4 Continuous hybrid formulation of contact/friction

To express the equilibrium of the structure, taking frictional contact into account, we have two ways of proceeding:

1. One expresses the classical form of the principle of virtual work by considering contact as a surface interaction force (one integrates it into the expression of the work of the internal forces); To complete the system, one adds to it the weak expression of the frictional-contact laws;
2. One writes directly the energy of the system in the form of a mixed variational inequality (without constraining the space of the solutions by the contact/friction conditions). The expression of the optimality criteria by the use of the augmented Lagrangian (saddle-point problem) makes it possible to recover the system established from the PVW;

### 4.1 Spaces

The Hilbert space ${H}^{1}$ , a complete vector space, usually the Sobolev space equipped with an appropriate norm (the scalar product), is the space of the square-integrable functions ${L}^{2}$ and whose derivative in the weak sense also belongs to ${L}^{2}$ . The dual space of ${H}^{1}$ will be denoted ${H}^{-1}$ . One denotes by ${\mathit{CA}}^{i}$ the space containing the kinematically admissible functions such that:

$$
{\mathit{CA}}^{i}=\left\lbrace {u}^{i}\in {H}^{1}({\Omega}^{i});{u}^{i}=0\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{u}^{i}\right\rbrace
$$

${H}^{1/2}(\Gamma )$ is the space of the traces of functions (belonging to ${H}^{1}$ ) on the boundary $\Gamma$ and ${H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma )$ is its dual space. For simplicity, one will denote $H={H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma )$ and $H={({H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma ))}^{2}$ its two-dimensional counterpart (for friction).

### 4.2 Principle of virtual work

The equations for the equilibrium of the body ${B}^{i}$ are:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{ccc}{div\underline{\underline{\Pi}}}^{i}+{f}_{v}^{i}=0& \text{dans}{\Omega}_{0}^{i}& (a)\\ {\underline{\underline{\Pi}}}^{i}{N}^{i}={f}_{s}^{i}& \text{sur}{\Gamma}_{g}^{i}& (b)\\ {u}^{i}=0& \text{sur}{\Gamma}_{u}^{i}& (c)\\ {\underline{\underline{\Pi}}}^{i}{N}^{i}={\lambda}^{i}& \text{sur}{\Gamma}_{c}^{i}& (d)\end{array}
$$

Where ${\underline{\underline{P}}}^{i}$ denotes the first *Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor* (non-symmetric), ${u}^{i}$ the displacement field and ${\lambda}^{i}$ the force density due to the frictional-contact interactions between the two solids.

#### 4.2.1 Virtual work of the internal forces

The principle of virtual work (PVW) makes it possible to express the bilinear form of the internal forces:

$$
{G}_{int}^{i}={\int}_{{\Omega}_{0}^{i}}({\underline{\underline{F}}}^{i}{\underline{\underline{S}}}^{i}:{\underline{\underline{\mathit{grad}}}\tilde{u}}^{i})d\Omega
$$

Where ${\tilde{u}}^{i}$ denotes the virtual displacement field. ${\underline{\underline{S}}}^{i}$ is the second Piola–Kirchhoff stress tensor, symmetric and purely Lagrangian, linked to the first tensor ${\underline{\underline{\Pi}}}^{i}$ by the relation:

$$
{\underline{\underline{S}}}^{i}={({\underline{\underline{F}}}^{i})}^{-1}{\underline{\underline{\Pi}}}^{i}
$$

#### 4.2.2 Virtual work of the external forces

The density of the body forces ${f}_{v}^{i}$ and surface forces ${f}_{s}^{i}$ applied to the two solids produces a work ${G}_{\text{ext}}^{i}$ :

$$
{G}_{\text{ext}}^{i}={\int}_{{\Omega}_{0}^{i}}{f}_{v}^{i}({u}^{i}){\tilde{u}}^{i}d\Omega +{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{g}^{i}}{f}_{s}^{i}({u}^{i}){\tilde{u}}^{i}d\Gamma
$$

#### 4.2.3 Virtual work of the frictional-contact forces

${G}_{\text{cf}}^{i}$ is the work of the frictional-contact forces on the solid ${B}^{i}$ :

$$
{G}_{\text{cf}}^{i}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}^{i}}{\lambda}^{i}({p}^{i},t){\tilde{u}}^{i}d\Gamma
$$

${\lambda}^{i}$ is the density of the frictional-contact forces undergone by the solid ${B}^{i}$ . Using the pairing procedure, described in sub-paragraph § 2.2.1 , the *principle of action and reaction* is written locally, on the initial configurations, in the following form:

$$
{\lambda}^{1}({p}^{1},t)d{\Gamma}_{c}^{1}+{\lambda}^{2}(\bar{p},t)d{\Gamma}_{c}^{2}=0
$$

One sets ${\Gamma}_{c}={\Gamma}_{c}^{1}$ and ${\lambda}^{1}=\lambda$ and one may therefore take:

$$
{\lambda}^{1}d{\Gamma}_{c}^{1}=-{\lambda}^{2}d{\Gamma}_{c}^{2}=\lambda d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

Which gives us a new form of the contact reaction term:

$$
{G}_{\text{cf}}^{i}\to {G}_{\text{cf}}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\lambda ({\tilde{u}}^{1}-{\tilde{u}}^{2})d{\Gamma}_{c}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\lambda \mathrm{〚}\tilde{u}\mathrm{〛}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

${\tilde{u}}^{i}$ are the virtual displacement fields, kinematically admissible on each of the solids. Furthermore, one has set ${\Gamma}_{c}={\Gamma}_{c}^{1}$ and ${\lambda}^{1}=\lambda$ . One points out that the force density ${\lambda}^{2}$ is extended by zero at the points of ${\Gamma}_{c}^{2}$ without a counterpart on ${\Gamma}_{c}^{1}$ . One decomposes the contact force density $\lambda$ into a normal part ${\lambda}_{n}.n$ and a tangential part ${\lambda}_{\tau}$ such that:

$$
\lambda ={\lambda}_{\tau}+{\lambda}_{n}n
$$

Projecting onto the unit ball (see () in § 3.2.4 ), one has:

$$
{\lambda}_{\tau}=\mu {\lambda}_{n}\Lambda
$$

The reaction is discontinuous and takes two values (contact or not). One uses the sign field ${S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}$ introduced in § 3.1.4 :

$$
\lambda ={S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}({\lambda}_{n}n+\mu {\lambda}_{n}\Lambda )
$$

The friction semi-multiplier is also discontinuous and takes two values (sliding or sticking). Using the sign field ${S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}$ introduced in § 3.2.5 :

$$
\Lambda ={S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}\Lambda +(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau
$$

By substituting the equation back into the equation :

$$
\lambda ={S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{n}n+\mu {\lambda}_{n}({S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}\Lambda +(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau )\right\rbrace
$$

We shall add here the so-called “stabilisation” term, which will make it possible to recover the behaviour of a standard augmented Lagrangian. The idea is the following: in the case of contact with sticking (${S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}=1$ and ${S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}=1$ ), the frictional-contact reaction () is written:

$$
\lambda ={\lambda}_{n}n+\mu {\lambda}_{n}\Lambda
$$

One may modify the writing of the contact/friction reaction:

$$
\lambda ={S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}\left\lbrace ({\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{d}_{n})n+\mu {\lambda}_{n}\left\lbrace {S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}{h}_{\tau}+(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau \right\rbrace \right\rbrace
$$

This addition changes nothing in the case of sticking contact, because, in this case, ${d}_{n}=0$ and ${v}_{\tau}=0$ , which makes it possible to recover (). On the other hand, in the other cases (no contact or sliding contact), the parameters ${\rho}_{n}$ and ${\rho}_{t}$ make it possible to avoid a zero on the diagonal of the matrix. Finally, the frictional-contact reaction term in the PVW may be decomposed into a component for contact:

$$
{G}_{\text{c}}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{g}_{n}{\mathrm{〚}\tilde{u}\mathrm{〛}}_{n}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

And a component for friction:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{G}_{\text{f}}=& {\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}{h}_{\tau}\cdot {\mathrm{〚}\tilde{u}\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}d{\Gamma}_{c}+\\ & {\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau \cdot {\mathrm{〚}\tilde{u}\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}d{\Gamma}_{c}\end{array}
$$

#### 4.2.4 Weak formulation of the contact law

We introduce the weak form of the contact law. For this it suffices to take up equation (a) by multiplying it by the test field of the contact Lagrange multiplier:

$$
{\tilde{G}}_{\text{c}}=-\frac{1}{{\rho}_{n}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{n}-{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{g}_{n}\right\rbrace {\tilde{\lambda}}_{n}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

#### 4.2.5 Weak formulation of the friction law

We introduce the weak form of the friction law. For this it suffices to take up equation (b) by multiplying it by the test field of the friction Lagrange multiplier:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{\tilde{G}}_{\text{f}}=& \frac{1}{{\rho}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}\Lambda \cdot \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}-\\ & \frac{1}{{\rho}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}{h}_{\tau}\cdot \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}-\\ & \frac{1}{{\rho}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau \cdot \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}+\\ & {\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}(1-{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}})\Lambda \cdot \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}\end{array}
$$

#### 4.2.6 Application of the principle of virtual work

If one considers the quasi-static problem, one seeks to solve the equilibrium in weak form by application of the principle of virtual work:

$$
\textrm{Trouver les champs}
({u}^{1},{u}^{2},{\lambda}_{n},\Lambda )\in {\text{CA}}^{1}\times {\text{CA}}^{2}\times H\times H
\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[{G}_{\text{ext}}^{i}(W,\tilde{W})-{G}_{int}^{i}(W,\tilde{W})\right]-{G}_{\text{c}}(W,\tilde{W})-{G}_{\text{f}}(W,\tilde{W})=0& (a)\\ {\tilde{G}}_{\text{c}}(W,\tilde{W})=0& (b)\\ {\tilde{G}}_{\text{f}}(W,\tilde{W})=0& (b)\end{array}
\forall \tilde{W}=({\tilde{u}}^{1},{\tilde{u}}^{2},{\tilde{\lambda}}_{n},\tilde{\Lambda})\in {\text{CA}}^{1}\times {\text{CA}}^{2}\times H\times H
$$

### 4.3 Mixed variational inequality

Overall, it is a matter of finding the solution of the following problem (mixed variational inequality):

$$
\textrm{Trouver les champs}
W=({u}^{1},{u}^{2},{\lambda}_{n},\Lambda )\in {\text{CA}}^{1}\times {\text{CA}}^{2}\times H\times H
({u}^{1},{u}^{2},{\lambda}_{n},\Lambda )=\underset{({\tilde{u}}^{1},{\tilde{u}}^{2})}{\text{argmin}}\underset{({\tilde{\lambda}}_{n},\tilde{\Lambda})}{\text{argmax}}\left\lbrace \sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[{W}_{int}^{i}-{W}_{\text{ext}}^{i}\right]-{W}_{\text{c}}-{W}_{\text{f}}\right\rbrace
$$

${W}_{int}^{i}$ and ${W}_{\text{ext}}^{i}$ correspond to the internal energy and to the external energy. We shall write the expression of the contact energy and of the friction energy. Let ${l}_{n}$ be the continuous and differentiable density of the contact energy:

$$
{W}_{\text{c}}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{l}_{n}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

And ${l}_{t}$ the continuous and differentiable density of the friction energy:

$$
{W}_{\text{f}}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{l}_{t}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

With respect to a classical augmented Lagrangian formulation, one has carried out the change of variable ${\lambda}_{\tau}=\mu {\lambda}_{n}\Lambda$ (see § 3.2.4 ). For the contact energy density:

$$
{l}_{n}=\frac{1}{2{\rho}_{n}}({S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{g}_{n}^{2}-{\lambda}_{n}^{2})
$$

One recalls that the normal gap ${d}_{n}$ is written:

$$
{d}_{n}={\mathrm{〚}x\mathrm{〛}}_{n}=(x-\bar{x})\cdot n
$$

Likewise, the sign field depends on the displacement fields and on the contact Lagrangian:

$$
{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}{g}_{n}\le 0\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{si}{g}_{n}>0\end{array}\end{array}
$$

For the friction energy density:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{l}_{t}=& \frac{1}{2{\rho}_{\tau}}(1-{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}})\Lambda \cdot \Lambda +\\ & \frac{1}{2{\rho}_{\tau}}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}\left[{h}_{\tau}\cdot {h}_{\tau}-\Lambda \cdot \Lambda \right]+\\ & \frac{1}{2{\rho}_{\tau}}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau \cdot \tau +\\ & \frac{1}{2{\rho}_{\tau}}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})(-2\mathrm{\parallel }{h}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }+\Lambda \cdot \Lambda )\end{array}
$$

To recover the expression of equilibrium in the weak sense, it suffices to apply the optimality condition () to () and (). By variation ${\delta}_{u}$ of the contact energy ${W}_{\text{c}}$ , one obtains:

$$
{\delta}_{{u}^{i}}{W}_{\text{c}}={\delta}_{{u}^{i}}({\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{l}_{n}d{\Gamma}_{c})={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{g}_{n}\delta {d}_{n}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

One recovers the expression of the virtual work of the contact forces (). Likewise, by variation ${\delta}_{{u}^{i}}$ of the contact energy ${W}_{\text{f}}$ , one obtains the expression of the virtual work of the friction forces ():

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{\delta}_{{u}^{i}}{W}_{\text{f}}& ={\delta}_{{u}^{i}}({\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{l}_{t}d{\Gamma}_{c})\\ & ={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}{h}_{\tau}\cdot {\delta v}_{\tau}d{\Gamma}_{c}+\\ & {\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau \cdot {\delta v}_{\tau}d{\Gamma}_{c}\end{array}
$$

To recover the expression of the virtual work of the contact law (equation ), one computes the variation of ${W}_{c}$ with respect to the contact Lagrangian ${\lambda}_{n}$ and, for the virtual work of the friction law (equation ), one expresses the variation of ${W}_{f}$ with respect to the normalised friction Lagrangian $\Lambda$ .

### 4.4 Penalised variational formulation

To write the penalised variational formulation, one regularises the contact and friction laws by writing:

$$
{\lambda}_{n}=-{({\kappa}_{n}{d}_{n})}^{+}
\Lambda ={P}^{B(0,1)}({\kappa}_{\tau}{v}_{\tau})
$$

The two parameters ${\kappa}_{n}$ and ${\kappa}_{\tau}$ are strictly positive penalisation parameters. ${(.)}^{+}$ denotes the positive part and ${\mathit{Proj}}_{B(0,1)}$ the projection onto the unit ball (see the definition in § 3.2.4 ). The weak form of the contact reaction is written:

$$
{G}_{\text{c}}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{\kappa}_{n}{d}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{\mathrm{〚}\tilde{u}\mathrm{〛}}_{n}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

For the friction reaction:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{G}_{\text{f}}=& -{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{\kappa}_{\tau}{\tilde{S}}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{\tilde{S}}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}{v}_{\tau}\cdot {\mathrm{〚}\tilde{u}\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}d{\Gamma}_{c}\\ & -{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{\tilde{S}}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}(1-{\tilde{S}}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\frac{{\kappa}_{t}{v}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{\kappa}_{t}{v}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}\cdot {\mathrm{〚}\tilde{u}\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}d{\Gamma}_{c}\end{array}
$$

The sign fields are modified:

$$
{\tilde{S}}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}-{d}_{n}\le 0\text{(contact)}\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{si}-{d}_{n}>0\text{(décollement)}\end{array}\end{array}
{\tilde{S}}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}\mathrm{\parallel }{\kappa}_{t}{v}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }\le 1\text{(adhérence)}\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{si}\mathrm{\parallel }{\kappa}_{t}{v}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }>1\text{(décollement)}\end{array}\end{array}
$$

In the penalised formulation, the contact multipliers ${\lambda}_{n}$ and the friction semi-multipliers $\Lambda$ theoretically no longer have any reason to exist, but one keeps them for reasons of software architecture. One therefore writes a weak form corresponding to the contact/friction law: one thus has access to the contact pressures in penalised formulation:

$$
{\tilde{G}}_{\text{c}}=-\frac{1}{{\kappa}_{n}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}({\lambda}_{n}+{\tilde{S}}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{\kappa}_{n}{d}_{n}){\tilde{\lambda}}_{n}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

As well as to the sticking-force density thanks to the weak form of the Coulomb law:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{\tilde{G}}_{\text{f}}=& \frac{1}{{\kappa}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{\tilde{S}}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}\Lambda \cdot \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}-\\ & \frac{1}{{\kappa}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{\tilde{S}}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{\tilde{S}}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}{\kappa}_{t}{v}_{\tau}\cdot \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}-\\ & \frac{1}{{\kappa}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{\tilde{S}}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}(1-{\tilde{S}}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\frac{{\kappa}_{t}{v}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{\kappa}_{t}{v}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}\cdot \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}+\\ & {\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}(1-{\tilde{S}}_{u}^{{g}_{n}})\Lambda \cdot \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}\end{array}
$$

### 4.5 Dynamics – Velocity/impulse writing

The Signorini model is unsuited to contact-impact problems, that is to say to contact problems in dynamics. Since the displacement fields are irregular (sign field), their time integration by classical finite-difference schemes of Newmark type causes spurious (non-physical) oscillations at the moment of the impacts. These oscillations are all the more important as the order of the scheme is high.

#### 4.5.1 Signorini–Moreau law for contact

Moreau rewrote the Signorini model in order to adapt it to the case of dynamics:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{ccc}{\lambda}_{n}=0& \text{si}{d}_{n}<0& (a)\\ {\mathrm{〚}v\mathrm{〛}}_{n}\le 0\text{et}{\lambda}_{n}\le 0\text{et}{\lambda}_{n}v=0& \text{si}{d}_{n}\ge 0& (b)\end{array}
$$

With the normal velocity defined by:

$$
{\mathrm{〚}v\mathrm{〛}}_{n}=\mathrm{〚}v\mathrm{〛}\cdot n
$$

The Moreau model is justified by writing that if the Signorini model is true at the initial time instant ${t}_{0}$ , then respecting the Moreau conditions amounts to solving the Signorini problem for every $t>{t}_{0}$ . For $t={t}_{0}$ , the Signorini–Moreau laws are strictly equivalent to those introduced in § 3.1.1 :

$$
{\lambda}_{n}={S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{g}_{n}
$$

Still with the augmented contact multiplier ${g}_{n}$ defined by:

$$
{g}_{n}={\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{d}_{n}
$$

For $t>{t}_{0}$ , the Signorini laws are written:

$$
{\lambda}_{n}={S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}{S}_{v}^{{\dot{g}}_{n}}{\dot{g}}_{n}
$$

with the following two sign fields:

$$
{S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}{-d}_{n}\le 0\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{si}{-d}_{n}>0\end{array}\end{array}
$$

And the augmented contact multiplier *in velocity* ${\dot{g}}_{n}$ , defined by:

$$
{\dot{g}}_{n}={\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{\mathrm{〚}v\mathrm{〛}}_{n}
$$

One will note that the constraint on the gap has partly moved onto the normal velocity ${\mathrm{〚}v\mathrm{〛}}_{n}$ . One must also note the change in nature of the augmentation parameter ${\rho}_{n}$ , which now has the dimension of a force per velocity (and no longer of a force per displacement).

#### 4.5.2 Coulomb law for friction

The Coulomb model for friction is already naturally written in velocity:

$$
(1-{S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}})\Lambda +{S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}\left\lbrace (1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\Lambda -{\rho}_{\tau}{v}_{\tau}{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}-(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\frac{{h}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{h}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}\right\rbrace =0\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

#### 4.5.3 Application of the principle of virtual work

For the problem in dynamics, one adds the inertia term ${G}_{\text{dyn}}^{i}$ to the equilibrium equation. The system to be solved is therefore:

$$
\textrm{Trouver les champs}
({u}^{1},{u}^{2},{\lambda}_{n},\Lambda )\in {\text{CA}}^{1}\times {\text{CA}}^{2}\times H\times H
\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[{G}_{\text{dyn}}^{i}(W,\tilde{W})+{G}_{int}^{i}(W,\tilde{W})-{G}_{\text{ext}}^{i}(W,\tilde{W})\right]-{G}_{\text{cf}}(W,\tilde{W})=0& (a)\\ {\tilde{G}}_{\text{cf}}(W,\tilde{W})=0& (b)\end{array}
\forall \tilde{W}=({\tilde{u}}^{1},{\tilde{u}}^{2},{\tilde{\lambda}}_{n},\tilde{\Lambda})\in {\text{CA}}^{1}\times {\text{CA}}^{2}\times H\times H
$$

${G}_{\text{dyn}}^{i}$ is the work of the inertia forces:

$$
{G}_{\text{dyn}}^{i}={\int}_{{\Omega}_{0}^{i}}({\rho}_{p}^{i}\frac{{\partial}^{2}{u}^{i}}{\partial {t}^{2}}\cdot {\tilde{u}}^{i})d{\Omega}^{i}
$$

Initial conditions should be added to the system:

$$
{u}_{t}^{i}={u}_{0}^{i}\text{dans}{\Omega}_{0}^{i}
{v}_{t}^{i}={v}_{0}^{i}\text{dans}{\Omega}_{0}^{i}
$$

To complete the system, one must also describe the time-integration scheme, which relates the displacements to the velocities:

$$
{u}_{t}^{i}={u}_{0}^{i}+{\int}_{{t}_{0}}^{t}{v}_{s}^{i}d\tau
$$

Relying on the work of Jean [], we shall use a first-order theta-scheme:

$$
{u}_{k+1}^{i}={u}_{k}^{i}+\Delta {t}_{k}\left[(1-\theta ){v}_{k}^{i}+\theta {v}_{k+1}^{i}\right]
$$

If $\theta =0$ , one recovers a purely explicit scheme. If $\theta =1$ , one recovers a purely implicit scheme of Euler type (one indeed recovers the velocity approximation carried out in quasi-statics):

$$
{v}_{k+1}=\frac{1}{\Delta {t}_{k}}({u}_{k+1}-{u}_{k})
$$

The scheme is stable if:

$$
\theta \ge 0\text{et}1-\theta \le \frac{2}{{\omega}_{m}\Delta {t}_{k}}
$$

where ${\omega}_{m}$ is the maximum angular frequency of the dynamic system. In practice, one recommends choosing $\theta$ between $0.5$ and $1$ .

#### 4.5.4 Virtual work of the frictional-contact forces

We shall now write the virtual work of the frictional-contact forces in strong form. One has a component for contact:

$$
{G}_{\text{c}}=-{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}{S}_{v}^{{\dot{g}}_{n}}{\mathrm{〚}\tilde{u}\mathrm{〛}}_{n}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

And a component for friction:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{G}_{\text{f}}=& {\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}\Lambda \cdot {\mathrm{〚}\tilde{u}\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}d{\Gamma}_{c}+\\ & {\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau \cdot {\mathrm{〚}\tilde{u}\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}d{\Gamma}_{c}\end{array}
$$

One does not see the velocity sign field ${S}_{v}^{{\dot{g}}_{n}}$ in the expression of the strong form of the reaction due to friction, but it is present implicitly in the expression of the contact multiplier ${\lambda}_{n}$ .

#### 4.5.5 Weak formulation of the contact law

We now write the weak form of the contact law established by equation ():

$$
{\tilde{G}}_{\text{c}}=-\frac{1}{{\rho}_{n}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{\lambda}_{n}-{S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}{S}_{v}^{{\dot{g}}_{n}}({\lambda}_{n}-{\rho}_{n}{\mathrm{〚}v\mathrm{〛}}_{n}){\tilde{\lambda}}_{n}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

#### 4.5.6 Weak formulation of the friction law

We now write the weak form of the friction law established by equation ():

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{\tilde{G}}_{\text{f}}=& \frac{1}{{\rho}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}\Lambda \cdot \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}-\\ & \frac{1}{{\rho}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}{h}_{\tau}\cdot \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}-\\ & \frac{1}{{\rho}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\frac{{h}_{\tau}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{h}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }}\cdot \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}+\\ & {\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}(1-{S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}})\Lambda \tilde{\Lambda}d{\Gamma}_{c}\end{array}
$$

#### 4.5.7 Conservation of energy

The stabilised Lagrangian formulation for contact conserves the total energy of the system, the linear momentum and the angular momentum. In the case of friction, the energy is conserved if the friction is sticking (${S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}{S}_{v}^{{\dot{g}}_{n}}=1$ and ${S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}=1$ ); on the other hand it decreases (dissipation) in the case of sliding friction (${S}_{u}^{{d}_{n}}{S}_{v}^{{\dot{g}}_{n}}=1$ and ${S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}=0$ ).

## 5 Algorithms for the solution of the non-linear system

### 5.1 Incremental formulation

We shall transform the continuous mixed variational formulations into an incremental form, because the problem is always solved in this form, whether in quasi-statics or in dynamics. One is given a discretisation of the time interval $I=\left[0,T\right]$ to be computed:

$$
0={t}_{0}<{t}_{1}<\cdots <{t}_{\mathrm{nT}}=T
$$

At a given loading stage $k$ , corresponding to the fictitious time instant ${t}_{k}$ , for $k\in \left[1,{n}_{T}\right]$ , one assumes known the fields at time instant ${t}_{k-1}$ denoted ${W}_{k-1}=({u}_{k-1}^{1},{u}_{k-1}^{2},{\lambda}_{n,k-1},{\Lambda}_{k-1})$ . One seeks the new solution fields at time instant ${t}_{k}$ denoted ${W}_{k}=({u}_{k}^{1},{u}_{k}^{2},{\lambda}_{n,k},{\Lambda}_{k})$ . The hybrid formulation is then written in the following generic form:

Find the fields
${W}_{k}\in {\text{CA}}^{1}\times {\text{CA}}^{2}\times H\times H$
such that:

$$
\left\lbrace
\begin{array}{cc}
\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[{G}_{int,k}^{i}({W}_{k},{\tilde{W}}_{k})-{G}_{\text{ext},k}^{i}({W}_{k},{\tilde{W}}_{k})\right]-{G}_{\text{c},k}({W}_{k},{\tilde{W}}_{k})-{G}_{\text{f},k}({W}_{k},{\tilde{W}}_{k})=0 & (a)\\ {\tilde{G}}_{\text{c},k}({W}_{k},{\tilde{W}}_{k})=0& (b)\\
{\tilde{G}}_{\text{f},k}({W}_{k},{\tilde{W}}_{k})=0& (c)
\end{array}
\right.
$$

$\forall {\tilde{W}}_{k} \in {\text{CA}}^{1} \times {\text{CA}}^{2} \times H \times H$

The variational formulation given by the system () is strongly non-linear. Indeed, besides the “classical” non-linearities due to large strains or to the non-linear constitutive behaviours of the materials, several levels of non-linearity inherent in frictional contact may furthermore be distinguished:

- A geometric non-linearity due to the *a priori* lack of knowledge of the pairs of points in contact between the two surfaces;
- A non-linearity due to the lack of knowledge of the contact state of the paired points;
- A non-linearity due to the dependence on ${\lambda}_{n}$ of the friction threshold ${\lambda}_{s}=\mu {\lambda}_{n}$ .

The whole is written in the form of a non-linear system:

$$
F({W}_{k},p(W))=0
$$

With ${W}_{k}$ which represent the unknowns of the system and $p(W)$ the parameters of the problem. The general idea is to solve this non-linear system by fixing certain frictional-contact parameters and then to linearise the system by a Newton method:

1. Fix the parameters of the analysis ${p}_{0}({W}_{0})$ ;
2. Solve the system by the Newton method $F(W,{p}_{0}({W}_{0}))=0$ ;
3. Loop back to 1 as long as ${p}_{0}\mathrm{\ne }p(W)$ ;

The Newton method consists in applying a first-order Taylor expansion:

$$
F({W}_{k-1}+\delta W)\approx F({W}_{k-1})+\frac{\partial F({W}_{k-1})}{\partial {W}_{k-1}}\delta W
$$

Depending on the parameters which will be fixed, the solution method comes in several variants:

- The fixed-point method;
- The partial Newton method;
- The generalised Newton method;

### 5.2 Time discretisation for the Coulomb law

It should be noted that the Coulomb friction law introduces the notion of relative slip velocity ${v}_{\tau}$ between the two solids. In quasi-statics, one uses an implicit Euler scheme. On the other hand, in dynamics, two situations arise:

- One uses the velocity/impulse formulation proposed in p. 4.5 ;
- One uses a standard Newmark scheme in displacement;

In this latter case, one will not use the velocity given by the scheme, but the incremental form of implicit Euler type, as in statics. This velocity is written in incremental form:

$$
{v}_{\tau}=\frac{1}{\Delta t}{⟦\Delta u⟧}_{\tau}
$$

where ${\mathrm{〚}\Delta u\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}$ represents the increment of the projection of the displacement jump $u$ onto the plane tangent to the contact surface at the contact point considered. This incremental writing in displacement of the Coulomb problem is equivalent to the original formulation in velocity. In practice, one integrates the $\Delta t$ into the parameter ${\rho}_{\tau}$ , that is to say one asks the user to choose them taking into account that one must integrate into them the “typical” $\Delta t$ of the problem. One must therefore take care that the parameter ${\rho}_{t}$ which one introduces *via* COEF_FROT equals ${\bar{\rho}}_{t}=\frac{{\rho}_{t}}{\Delta t}$ and not ${\rho}_{t}$ .

#### 5.3.1 Loop on the geometry

The problem of the geometric non-linearity comes from the fact that one does not know *a priori* which will be the pairs of points in contact. This lack of knowledge also entails that of the local frame (normal and tangent plane) at each potential contact point.

This non-linearity is particularly pronounced for problems with warped contact surfaces (that is to say non-planar) and/or potentially subjected to *large transformations (large relative slips)* . In practice, a single correction is often sufficient if the contact surfaces undergo little geometric variation (small displacements).

It is important to understand what geometric updating implies: it is, at each iteration, an entirely new frictional-contact problem which one solves, and in which one must start again from the determination of the pairs of paired points. This is why the geometry loop is the outermost.

The pairing non-linearity also entails lack of knowledge of the local frame. Now the current local frame (the normal $n$ and the two tangents ${t}_{1}$ and ${t}_{2}$ in 3D, in the deformed configuration) is present several times in the writing of the Signorini–Coulomb equations. If one takes the case of the normal, this means that it is written, in the course of the displacement ${u}^{1}$ :

$$
n(\bar{x})=n(\bar{p}+{u}^{1}(\bar{p}))
$$

The normal is therefore non-linear in displacement. This non-linearity may be differentiated in the equations, or one may make the assumption that the normal remains constant in the course of the loop *<J>* on the geometry:

$$
n(\bar{x})\approx n(\bar{p}+{u}_{j}^{1}(\bar{p}))
$$

In our case, one makes the assumption that the normal (and the tangents) remains constant during the loop on the geometry. The number of iterations of the loop is determined automatically by the code but one may fix it by NB_ITER_GEOM. It should be noted that there is always at least *one* pairing at the beginning of the time step, even if NB_ITER_GEOM=0.

#### 5.3.2 Loop on the friction threshold

The threshold non-linearity (Coulomb friction) comes from the fact that the friction threshold depends on the normal density of the contact force ${\lambda}_{n}$ , which is itself an unknown of the problem. The fixed-point loop <L> on the friction threshold ${\lambda}_{s}$ amounts to treating the frictional-contact problem with a *fixed* friction threshold, which amounts at each iteration to treating a *Tresca problem* . This strategy is very common for solving Coulomb friction, and is justified theoretically: by fixing the friction threshold, one may derive an *associated convex potential* for the Coulomb law, which makes it possible to relate the displacement unknowns to their associated dual variables, the friction forces.

#### 5.3.3 Loop on the contact statuses

The last non-linearity concerns the lack of knowledge of the effective contact state. We have adopted a method derived from the active-constraint method implemented in *Code_Aster* for discrete contact (see [R5.03.50](doc:r5.03.50)). By default, the initial status of the contact nodes is non-contacting in *Code_Aster* . Furthermore ${\lambda}_{s}$ is initialised to zero (unless one uses SEUIL_INIT). This amounts to beginning by solving a problem without contact at the first iteration, then with contact but without friction, and finally to activating friction with a fixed friction threshold ${\lambda}_{s}$ (Tresca problem). One may nevertheless start from an initially contacting state by using the option CONTACT_INIT. In this case, it is possible to solve hypostatic problems with the continuous formulation. This is an important advantage of the continuous formulation of contact with respect to the discrete formulations, which do not support a mechanical problem which is “held” only by the contact.

Moreover, the “slide” method makes it possible to simulate bilateral contact by assuming that the nodes which come into contact will remain so throughout the transient (functionality which can be activated by the keyword GLISSIERE).

### 5.4 Linearisations of the terms

The non-linear algebraic system () will be solved by linearisation of Newton type. We take up the expressions of equilibrium (PVW, see p. 4.2 ) and of the Signorini law and of the Coulomb law in weak form. The virtual quantities are then infinitesimal variations, so the quantities expressed in terms of updated coordinates (of the kind $\delta x$ ) transform into a variation on the displacements $\delta u$ :

$$
x=p+u\to \delta x=\delta u
$$

**Important note: the linearisation of the terms in the following paragraphs implies the use of a Newmark scheme in dynamics or the quasi-static case, which uses the incremental writing in displacement of the friction law.**

The PVW includes in particular the contact reaction, which will be written with the variation of the normal gap:

$$
{G}_{c}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{g}_{n}\tilde{\delta}{d}_{n}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

For the friction reaction in the PVW:

$$
{G}_{f}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}{h}_{\tau}\cdot {\tilde{\delta}d}_{\tau}d{\Gamma}_{c}+{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau \cdot {\tilde{\delta}d}_{\tau}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

It is important to understand the meaning of the notations. In the two previous cases, $\tilde{\delta}$ is understood as the total variation with respect to the displacements (expressions established on p. 2.3 ).

For the Signorini law in weak form one considers:

$$
{\tilde{G}}_{c}=-\frac{1}{{\rho}_{n}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{n}-{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{g}_{n}\right\rbrace {\delta \lambda }_{n}d{\Gamma}_{c}
$$

In this case, $\delta$ is understood as the variation with respect to the contact pressure ${\lambda}_{n}$ .

Finally for the Coulomb law in weak form:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{\tilde{G}}_{f}=& \frac{1}{{\rho}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}\Lambda \cdot \delta \Lambda d{\Gamma}_{c}-\\ & \frac{1}{{\rho}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}{h}_{\tau}\cdot \delta \Lambda d{\Gamma}_{c}-\\ & \frac{1}{{\rho}_{t}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\lambda}_{n}{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}(1-{S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}})\tau \cdot \delta \Lambda d{\Gamma}_{c}+\\ & {\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}(1-{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}})\Lambda \cdot \delta \Lambda d{\Gamma}_{c}\end{array}
$$

$\delta$ is understood as the variation with respect to the friction Lagrange multiplier $\Lambda$ .

By adding the terms corresponding to the equilibrium of the structure (${G}_{int}^{i}$ and ${G}_{\text{ext}}^{i}$ ), one has the following non-linear system to solve:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}{L}_{u}({W}_{k},{\delta W}_{k})={L}_{u,k}=0& (a)\\ {L}_{c}({W}_{k},{\delta W}_{k})={L}_{c,k}=0& (b)\\ {L}_{f}({W}_{k},{\delta W}_{k})={L}_{f,k}=0& (c)\end{array}
$$

With ${W}_{k}$ which represent the unknowns of the system and $\delta {W}_{k}$ the virtual quantities. The first equation represents the equilibrium of the structure with frictional contact; since the variation is therefore understood with respect to the displacements, one has:

$$
{L}_{u,k}={G}_{\text{ext},k}^{i}-{G}_{int,k}^{i}-{G}_{\text{c},k}-{G}_{\text{f},k}
\forall {\delta u}_{k}^{i}\in {\text{CA}}_{h}^{i}
$$

The following equation is the expression of the Signorini contact law. The variation is understood with respect to the contact Lagrangian:

$$
{L}_{c,k}={\tilde{G}}_{\text{c},k}
\forall {\delta \lambda }_{n,k}\in {H}_{h}({\Gamma}_{c})
$$

For the Coulomb friction law. The variation is understood with respect to the friction Lagrangian:

$$
{L}_{f,k}={\tilde{G}}_{\text{f},k}
\forall {\delta \Lambda }_{k}\in {H}_{h}({\Gamma}_{c})
$$

We have already made an assumption in these last two equations: the Lagrange multipliers of contact ${\lambda}_{n}$ and the semi-multipliers of friction $\Lambda$ have been written only on one of the two solids. One has therefore assumed that ${\Gamma}_{c}={\Gamma}_{c}^{1}$ or ${\Gamma}_{c}={\Gamma}_{c}^{2}$ . The privileged surface is called the slave surface. One will evaluate the integrals on this surface. The linearisation introduces in particular terms corresponding to the generalised Jacobian of frictional contact. For equilibrium:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{L}_{u,k}\approx & {G}_{\text{ext},k-1}^{i}-{G}_{int,k-1}^{i}-{G}_{\text{c},k-1}-{G}_{\text{f},k-1}+\\ & ({\frac{\partial {G}_{\text{ext}}^{i}}{\partial {u}^{i}}\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}}-{\frac{\partial {G}_{int}^{i}}{\partial {u}^{i}}\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}}-{\frac{\partial {G}_{\text{c}}}{\partial {u}^{i}}\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}}-{\frac{\partial {G}_{\text{f}}}{\partial {u}^{i}}\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}})\Delta {u}^{i}\\ & (-{\frac{\partial {G}_{\text{c}}}{\partial {\lambda}_{n}}\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}}-{\frac{\partial {G}_{\text{f}}}{\partial {\lambda}_{n}}\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}})\Delta {\lambda}_{n}\\ & (-{\frac{\partial {G}_{\text{c}}}{\partial \Lambda }\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}}-{\frac{\partial {G}_{\text{f}}}{\partial \Lambda }\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}})\Delta \Lambda \end{array}
$$

For the Signorini contact law:

$$
{L}_{c,k}\approx {\tilde{G}}_{\text{c},k-1}+{\frac{\partial {\tilde{G}}_{\text{c}}}{\partial {u}^{i}}\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}}\Delta {u}^{i}+{\frac{\partial {\tilde{G}}_{\text{c}}}{\partial {\lambda}_{n}}\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}}\Delta {\lambda}_{n}+{\frac{\partial {\tilde{G}}_{\text{c}}}{\partial \Lambda }\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}}\Delta \Lambda
$$

For the Coulomb friction law:

$$
{L}_{f,k}\approx {\tilde{G}}_{\text{f},k-1}+{\frac{\partial {\tilde{G}}_{\text{f}}}{\partial {u}^{i}}\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}}\Delta {u}^{i}+{\frac{\partial {\tilde{G}}_{\text{f}}}{\partial {\lambda}_{n}}\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}}\Delta {\lambda}_{n}+{\frac{\partial {\tilde{G}}_{\text{f}}}{\partial \Lambda }\mid }_{{X}_{k-1}}\Delta \Lambda
$$

In the end, we shall obtain the following linearised system:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{L}_{u,k}\\ {L}_{c,k}\\ {L}_{f,k}\end{array}\right\rbrace \approx \left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{L}_{u,k-1}\\ {L}_{c,k-1}\\ {L}_{f,k-1}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{J}_{uu}& {J}_{uc}& {J}_{uf}\\ {J}_{cu}& {J}_{cc}& {J}_{cf}\\ {J}_{fu}& {J}_{fc}& {J}_{ff}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\Delta {u}^{i}\\ \Delta {\lambda}_{n}\\ \Delta \Lambda \end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{L}_{u,k-1}\\ {L}_{c,k-1}\\ {L}_{f,k-1}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{c}J\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace {\Delta W}_{k}\right\rbrace
$$

We must evaluate all the terms of the Jacobian matrix $\left[J\right]$ .

#### 5.4.1 The variants for the linearisation of the Coulomb law

The Coulomb law relates the contact pressure to two quantities:

- The intensity of the tangential stresses (friction forces);
- The friction direction $\tau$ ;

The dependence of the friction direction with respect to the intensity of the contact pressure is an important point, and one which shows the difficulty of taking the Coulomb model into account. This dependence is essential in 3D, on curved surfaces.

If one takes up the expression of equilibrium, with integration of the friction reaction ${G}_{f}$ , one therefore has, strictly speaking, a dependence on the contact pressure ${\lambda}_{n}$ of $\mathrm{\parallel }{\lambda}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel }$ and of $\tau$ , but also, through the expression of the weak friction law, a dependence of ${\tilde{G}}_{f}$ on the contact pressure ${\lambda}_{n}$ . Three quantities are therefore variable:

$$
{\Delta}_{{\lambda}_{n}}(\mathrm{\parallel }{h}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel })\mathrm{\ne }0
$$

This is the most general case; however, one may strictly speaking distinguish 8 possible configurations:

| Variant | ${\Delta}_{{\lambda}_{n}}(\mathrm{\parallel }{\lambda}_{\tau}\mathrm{\parallel })=0$ | ${\Delta}_{{\lambda}_{n}}(\tau )$ | ${\Delta}_{{\lambda}_{n}}({\tilde{G}}_{f})$ | Matrix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0 | 0 | 0 | Symmetric |
| 2 | 0 | 0 | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | Non-symmetric |
| 3 | 0 | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | 0 | Non-symmetric |
| 4 | 0 | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | Non-symmetric |
| 5 | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | 0 | 0 | Non-symmetric |
| 6 | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | 0 | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | Non-symmetric |
| 7 | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | 0 | Non-symmetric |
| 8 | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | $\mathrm{\ne }0$ | Non-symmetric |

All these variants are numerically acceptable (they are Newton variants) but only a few of them have a real physical meaning. Variant 1 corresponds to the case where the contact pressure is constant: it is the Tresca model or the variant known as “fixed point on the friction threshold”; it is the only one which produces a symmetric tangent matrix. Variant 8 is the most general case (generalised Newton method). Apart from these two cases, only two other variants have a physical meaning:

- Variant 4 assumes that one neglects the dependence of the friction intensity with respect to the contact pressure but that one may have “abrupt” variations of direction of the tangential directions. The typical case is that of a curved surface in 3D with low contact-pressure intensities (grazing contact);
- Variant 6 assumes that one may neglect the dependence of the friction direction with respect to the contact pressure (2D or planar 3D case) but not the dependence of the friction force. The typical case is that of a planar 3D or 2D surface, with a high contact-pressure intensity;

In practice, variant 8 introduces numerically ill-conditioned terms which slow down convergence. The dependence on the friction direction couples geometric terms with pressure terms. We have therefore introduced variant 5, which seems to be a good compromise. The computation of the terms of the Jacobian is postponed to the appendix (see p. 63 )

In the simplification assumptions, one has neglected the second variation of the tangential gap (equation ) because the equations which follow from it are very complex and do not seem to bring a significant gain on the rate of convergence. Of course, one thus loses the theoretical quadratic convergence of the Newton algorithm.

## 6 Discretisation of the mixed variational formulation

One describes in this section the discretisation of the problem defined by the variational formulations given on p. 4 . The variational formulations used have the following points in common:

- The writing of the PVW for the equilibrium of the structure, using a weak formulation of the equilibrium equation;
- A vector field of unknowns: that of the primal variables representing either the displacements or the velocities (in dynamics, in the case of the theta-scheme);
- The geometry is represented by a regular triangulation (classical polynomial finite elements);
- An unknown scalar field representing the Lagrange multiplier of contact $\lambda$ ;
- An unknown vector field representing the Lagrange semi-multiplier of friction $\Lambda$ ;
- The Signorini contact model is applied in a weak manner, with an augmented Lagrangian formulation;
- The Coulomb friction model is applied in a weak manner, with an augmented Lagrangian formulation;
- Discontinuous sign fields, for contact and for friction, have been introduced into the formulation.

In this chapter, we shall introduce all the ingredients necessary for the construction of the discretised model, then, in the following chapter, we shall see the manner of solving the problem.

**Important: henceforth, we shall denote by** $\lambda$ **and no longer by** ${\lambda}_{n}$ **the contact pressure in order to lighten the notations. The friction pressure is always denoted** $\Lambda$ **.**

### 6.1 Theoretical elements

#### 6.1.1 Tessellation of the domain

The domains ${\Omega}^{i}$ are approximated by polygonal domains ${\Omega}_{h}^{i}$ . With each domain ${\Omega}^{i}$ is associated a family of triangulations ${T}_{{h}^{i}}$ with the parameter ${h}^{i}$ which is the mesh size of the domain ${\Omega}^{i}$ . Denoting by ${K}_{j}^{i}$ the ${\mathit{NE}}^{i}$ elements of each triangulation, we may write:

$$
{\Omega}_{h}^{i}=\underset{j=1}{\overset{{\mathit{NE}}^{i}}{\cup}}{K}_{j}^{i}
$$

For the discretisation of the contact boundary ${\Gamma}_{c}^{i}$ , we are faced with a first important assumption. Indeed, this boundary may be discretised in two manners:

- One takes the geometric trace of the mesh of the underlying domain ${\Omega}^{i}$ ;
- One creates a new independent mesh, with its own discretisation size.

In practice, the mesh of the boundary is almost always built on the first assumption, because otherwise this would imply meshing work difficult to carry out, particularly in 3D. One will therefore always use the geometric trace of the mesh of the domain ${\Omega}^{i}$ as the mesh for the contact boundary. The trace of ${T}_{{h}^{i}}$ forms a mesh ${T}_{{h}^{i}}^{\Gamma}$ of ${\Gamma}_{c}^{i}$ . The discretised boundary ${\Gamma}_{c,h}^{i}$ is composed of ${\mathit{NE}}_{c}^{i}$ elements ${k}_{j}^{i}$ :

$$
{\Gamma}_{c,h}^{i}=\underset{j=1}{\overset{{\mathit{NE}}_{c}^{i}}{\cup}}{k}_{j}^{i}
$$

#### 6.1.2 Discrete approximation spaces

The discrete version of the space ${H}^{1}({\Omega}^{i})$ , on the domain ${\Omega}^{i}$ , which one will denote ${H}_{h}^{i,q}$ , is such that:

$$
{H}_{h}^{i,q}=\left\lbrace {u}^{i}\in {(C({\bar{\Omega}}^{i}))}^{d};{u}_{\mid {K}_{j}^{i}}^{i}\in {({P}_{q}({K}_{j}^{i}))}^{d}\text{pour}1\le j\le {\mathit{NE}}^{i}\right\rbrace
$$

with ${\mathit{NE}}^{i}$ the number of elements coming from the triangulation (), $d$ the dimension of the problem, $C({\bar{\Omega}}^{i})$ the space of the continuous functions on the closure of ${\Omega}^{i}$ and ${P}_{q}({K}_{j}^{i})$ the space of polynomials of degree less than or equal to $q$ on the domain. One has denoted by ${\mathit{CA}}^{i}$ the space containing the kinematically admissible functions. The discrete version of this space, denoted ${\mathit{CA}}_{h}^{i,q}$ , is constructed as ():

$$
{\mathit{CA}}_{h}^{i,q}=\left\lbrace {u}^{i}\in {(C({\bar{\Omega}}^{i}))}^{d};{u}_{\mid {K}_{j}^{i}}^{i}\in {({P}_{q}({K}_{j}^{i}))}^{d}\text{pour}1\le j\le {\mathit{NE}}^{i}\text{et}{u}_{\mid {\Gamma}_{u}^{i}}^{i}=0\right\rbrace
$$

We shall write the discrete version of the space of the traces of functions on the boundary:

$$
{\mathit{CA}}_{h}^{\gamma ,i}=\left\lbrace \mathit{trace}({u}^{i})\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{c}^{i};{u}^{i}\in {\mathit{CA}}_{h}^{i,q}\right\rbrace
$$

Henceforth, one will forget the notation $q$ for indicating the order of the polynomials.

#### 6.1.3 The Inf-Sup condition or LBB condition

The mixed character of the formulation imposes a *dependence* between the finite-element spaces of the contact and friction multipliers and the finite-element space of the displacements. This choice falls under the compatibility condition (or Inf-Sup condition or LBB condition of Ladyzenskaya–Babuška–Brezzi). A mechanical problem generally reduces to solving a *variational equation* of the type:

$$
\text{Trouver}u\in U\text{tel que}\forall {u}^{\text{*}}\in U
\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}a(u,{u}^{\text{*}})=\langle l,{u}^{\text{*}}\rangle & \text{dans}U\\ u=g& \text{sur}\partial U\end{array}
$$

where $U$ is a Hilbert space. It is often more convenient to rewrite this problem in a so-called *mixed* form, with two unknowns $u\in U$ and $\lambda \in M$ :

$$
\text{Trouver}u\in U\text{et}\lambda \in M\text{tel que}\forall {u}^{\text{*}}\in U\text{et}\forall {\lambda}^{\text{*}}\in M
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}a(u,{u}^{\text{*}})+b({u}^{\text{*}},\lambda )=\langle l,{u}^{\text{*}}\rangle & \text{dans}U\\ b({u}^{\text{*}},\lambda )=\langle g,{\lambda}^{\text{*}}\rangle & \text{sur}\partial U\end{array}
$$

where $M$ is also a Hilbert space, dual of $U$ through the bilinear form $b$ . The problem is that the formulation given by () is not equivalent to the formulation given by (). Thus, in this very general framework, the *equivalence* of the two formulations, as well as a *necessary and sufficient condition of existence and uniqueness* (in addition, of course, to the classical assumptions on the *ellipticity* and the *coercivity* of the bilinear form $a$ ) of the solution of (), is given by the so-called condition of *Ladyzenskaya* , *Babuška* and *Brezzi* (LBB):

$$
\exists \alpha \in ℝ>0,\underset{{\lambda}^{\text{*}}\in M}{\inf}\underset{{u}^{\text{*}}\in U}{\sup}\frac{\mid b({u}^{\text{*}},{\lambda}^{\text{*}})\mid }{{\parallel {\lambda}^{\text{*}}\parallel }_{M}.{\parallel {u}^{\text{*}}\parallel }_{U}}\ge \alpha >0
$$

This condition indicates *how* one must choose the space $M$ , so that the formulations are equivalent. However, one must admit that from an operational point of view, one is not very much further forward, because this condition, very abstract, is also very technical to implement. All the more so as this condition is expressed in the continuous framework and its passage into the discrete framework is not guaranteed! One will content oneself with giving rules of application drawn from the literature.

The application of the LBB condition to our contact problem depends on the nature of the interfaces:

- If the interface is warped, obtaining an *Inf-Sup* condition with a discontinuous discretisation of the contact multipliers requires a very fine discretisation of these multipliers;
- If the interface is planar, the space ${\text{CA}}_{h}^{\gamma ,i}$ of the traces of the displacement fields on the contact interface as the discretisation space of the contact-multiplier field satisfies the LBB condition;

The continuous method uses the space of the traces of the displacement fields, knowing that stability (in the LBB sense) will not be guaranteed for non-planar interfaces.

One takes multiplier fields in the space ${\text{CA}}_{h}^{\gamma ,i}$ over the whole contact interface
. For *warped* surfaces (), this choice is convenient (since we discretise the multiplier field in a manner identical to the displacement field), but there is no *proof of stability* . Indeed, the problem comes from the fact that the choice of one of the two surfaces as the integration surface constitutes a more or less crude approximation, as illustrated by the . Experience shows however that this difficulty may be circumvented by the use of approximation fields that are as smooth as possible (smoothing of the normals, continuity of the multiplier fields). We shall see later that satisfaction of the Inf-Sup condition is also strongly linked to the numerical quadrature scheme used to evaluate the integrals.

#### 6.1.4 Sign fields

The choice of the approximation spaces (of polynomial finite-element type) for the displacement field, the contact Lagrange-multiplier field and the field of the friction semi-multipliers is not sufficient to construct the discrete problem. There remains the case of the sign fields (for contact, friction, the velocity field, etc.) which are naturally in infinite spaces. One uses a collocation method to approximate these sign fields. One therefore chooses a finite collection of points of the discretised contact surface ${\Gamma}_{c,h}^{i}$ :

$$
{({p}_{j}^{i})}_{1\le j\le {n}_{c}^{i}}\in {\Gamma}_{c,h}^{i}
$$

A sign field $S$ will therefore be approximated by ${S}_{h}$ , which is the following discrete sum:

$$
{S}_{h}=\sum_{j=1}^{{n}_{c}}{\omega}_{j}S({p}_{j})
$$

Warning! The number of collocation points ${n}_{c}^{i}$ is not necessarily the same as the number of points coming from the finite-element discretisation of the contact/friction multipliers (which rests, one recalls, on the geometric trace of the discretised volume).

#### 6.1.5 Numerical integration

To evaluate the integrals numerically, it is appropriate to choose a suitable numerical quadrature scheme. One bases oneself for this on the integration of the discontinuous terms (sign fields) which “guide” the best scheme to adopt. The question therefore shifts towards the choice of the optimal collocation points in order to obtain accurate contact forces.

Satisfaction of the Inf-Sup condition has been addressed in the framework of the choice of the approximation functions of the contact and friction multiplier fields. Still with a view to satisfying this condition, it has been said that it was strongly linked to the accuracy of the numerical integration which one is capable of obtaining. The phenomenon is understood when one draws the analogy with the methods of numerical under-integration sometimes employed to solve problems of numerical locking due to too great a richness of the approximation, by then “lowering” the degree of interpolation of the fields by under-integrating. The reasoning may therefore easily be reversed: if the numerical integration “distorts” (by enriching or by impoverishing) the polynomial interpolation of the multiplier fields, one risks leaving the stability domain of the LBB condition.

This is the case, notably in the event of incompatibility. Let us imagine that the elementary integration area on the slave surface covers several elements of the master surface. Since the test functions have as non-zero support a finite element, the fact of integrating numerically, by a Gauss quadrature, a non-polynomial function, but identically zero on part of the integration area, is clearly not exact, because the quadrature is quite inappropriate (see ). Yet this is what one does when one tries to integrate a functional field of a master element on a *much more extended slave element* (non-compatible). By reversing the choice of the surfaces, the result is clearly better ( on the right), but still without a guarantee of having a satisfactory integration. In what follows, we shall describe the method adopted for *Code_Aster* , based on a subdivision of the element *which serves only for the integration* . This method, which consists in refining the integration surface, limits the numerical integration error, and above all has the advantage of being extremely simple to implement.

Several avenues have been explored in an attempt to minimise these problems of incompatibilities between the surfaces. The one which has been retained in Code_Aster is to propose different numerical integration schemes, usable according to the needs. There are several types of schemes:

- Numerical integration at the nodes;
- Numerical integration at the *Gauss* points;
- Numerical integration by the *Simpson* method with 3, 5 or 9 integration points;
- Integration by the *Newton–Cotes* method with 4, 5 or 10 integration points;

Simpson integration makes it possible to improve the results in the event of mesh incompatibility between the two contact surfaces, thanks to the subdivision of the integration elements which we have already mentioned above.

Newton–Cotes integration makes it possible to integrate exactly polynomials of high order (greater than three). It proves very useful when the interpolation functions used on one of the two contact surfaces are of order greater than one ($\mathit{P1}-\mathit{P2}$ , $\mathit{P2}-\mathit{P2}$ …). Moreover, the Newton–Cotes quadratures also allow (like those of Simpson) a subdivision of the integration element, and therefore apply in the event of mesh incompatibility.

However, one will take care not to believe that a systematic use of a richer quadrature (Simpson, Newton–Cotes) is always a good choice. First because, in most cases, the quadrature at the nodes proves in practice sufficient ($\mathit{P1}-\mathit{P1}$ with sufficiently fine meshes). Next because the richer a quadrature is (in number of integration points), the greater the volume of the analysis, and one will very quickly realise that, for example, the use of the Newton–Cotes quadrature quickly becomes prohibitive. There is therefore no systematic rule a priori as to the choice of a quadrature, apart from the few indications which we have given: one must take into account the specificity of the model and of the mechanical problem studied, and make a compromise between the accuracy desired for the solution and the analysis time required.

### 6.2 Contact elements

After having discretised the mixed variational formulation and after having given the numerical integration schemes, all that remains for us is to set up finite elements for frictional contact.

The idea of using finite elements is not immediate. For example, in the discrete methods of frictional contact [R5.03.50](doc:r5.03.50), the kinematic contact conditions are imposed without using finite elements and by intervening directly on the algebraic system to be solved. Such a strategy has the drawback of making the programming and the architecture completely dependent on the formulation, which prevents contact from benefiting from improvements (architecture, performance) which would be made in the rest of the finite-element code. Conversely, we shall see that developing frictional-contact finite elements implies leaving the standard scheme of finite elements and that it is a sub-optimal strategy from the point of view of performance.

To determine the stiffness matrix (and the right-hand side) of the discretised system, one must compute the elementary terms of the finite elements before carrying out their assembly. This elementary (classical) level is not sufficient for the treatment of our frictional-contact problem. Several incompatibilities come to make the problem non-standard in the sense of finite elements.

One already notes a geometric incompatibility () between the discretisations of the two facing contact surfaces, a slave element being able for example to overlap several master elements.

We have placed the collocation points $(a,b,c,d)$ as Gauss points, but their location will of course depend on the integration scheme. Each contact element therefore represents an elementary matrix or vector on the degrees of freedom carried by the slave nodes and by the master nodes. In 3D, the largest surface mesh cell is the QUAD9 (nine-node quadrilateral), which makes $2\times 9\times 3=54$ degrees of freedom for the displacements. In the case of contact with friction in 3D, one adds on the slave surface three additional degrees of freedom per node: the Lagrange multiplier of the contact multiplier and the two Lagrange multipliers of the friction semi-multiplier, which gives $9\times 3=27$ additional degrees of freedom. Therefore, in 3D, for the contact element with friction, the maximum size of the elementary matrix is $81$ by $81$ degrees of freedom.

In practice the method is the following:

- Creation of the contact element on the master and slave nodes;
- For each contact element, loop over the collocation points;
- For each collocation point, computation of the elementary matrix (or vector).

It is because of the last point that the creation of the finite element is sub-optimal. Indeed, one calls the computation of the elementary matrix for each collocation point. Yet, for the classical finite elements, the loop over the integration points is inside the elementary computation.

We therefore see that the use of a collocation scheme makes it possible for different contact states to coexist within the same element. However, there remains a problem of compatibility between the FE approximation of the shape functions and the integration scheme. Let us take the example of a segment (figure ).

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.52/Shape10.gif)

| ![Figure](images/Shape10.gif) |

| **Figure** 12 **: FE/collocation incompatibility on a segment** |

On this segment, the displacement is approximated by a linear shape function and it equals 0 at the left-hand node and it is greater than zero at the right-hand node. From the point of view of contact, this means that a point situated in the middle of the segment will necessarily separate. Yet the integration scheme by collocation does not necessarily indicate this state. There is a decoupling between the polynomial approximation of the displacements and the discretisation by collocation of the sign fields. Which means that the collocation method is perfect for zones with contact states constant on the element but that it is much less relevant if the contact state varies. Such a situation generates oscillations in the transition zones. These oscillations cannot be reduced by using more collocation points but by refining the mesh in this zone.

**Notes:**

- The frictional-contact elements having no geometric meaning, they are not conforming, with no impact on the quality of the result: they are infinitely flattened when there is contact, they may be very distorted and have “strange” connectivities in the case of the joining between surfaces of different degrees;
- The fact of using the integration scheme at the nodes does not make the contact elements more efficient from the point of view of their use, since the principle described above is generic for all the integration schemes;
- There is no procedure dedicated to perfectly compatible meshes. Two meshes chosen to be compatible will not give faster results than two incompatible meshes;
- The fact of having to construct late finite elements results in a re-factorisation of the global matrix at each Newton iteration, since the topology of the matrix will be modified.

### 6.3 Preparation of the discretisation

#### 6.3.1 Discretisation in time

The (pseudo)-temporal discretisation will be indicated by the index $k$ . For example, the displacement ${u}_{k}$ at the current time step is written as the sum of the cumulated displacement ${u}_{k}^{\mathit{cum}}$ since the beginning of the Newton process of the current time step and of the displacement increment of the current Newton iteration $\Delta u$ which one is seeking:

$$
{u}_{k}={u}_{k-1}+{u}_{k}^{\mathit{cum}}+\Delta u
$$

This iterative writing is also valid for the other nodal unknowns (field of the Lagrange multipliers):

$$
{\lambda}_{k}={\lambda}_{k-1}+{\lambda}_{k}^{\mathit{cum}}+\Delta \lambda
$$

And:

$$
{\Lambda}_{k}={\Lambda}_{k-1}+{\Lambda}_{k}^{\mathit{cum}}+\Delta \Lambda
$$

In *Code_Aster* , one has chosen to work in total quantity (and not incremental) for the Lagrange multipliers; they are therefore initialised to zero at each beginning of a time step and one therefore has:

$$
{\lambda}_{k-1}=0\to {\lambda}_{k}={\lambda}_{k}^{\mathit{cum}}+\Delta \lambda
$$

And:

$$
{\Lambda}_{k-1}=0\to {\Lambda}_{k}={\Lambda}_{k}^{\mathit{cum}}+\Delta \Lambda
$$

One will use a notation which separates, at the current time step $k$ , the *known* quantities ${\hat{u}}_{k}$ and the *unknown* quantities $\Delta u$ :

$$
{u}_{k}={\hat{u}}_{k}+\Delta u
{\bar{u}}_{k}={\hat{\bar{u}}}_{k}+\Delta \bar{u}
$$

For the updated coordinates, since one has $x=p+u$ :

$$
{x}_{k}={\hat{x}}_{k}+\Delta x
{\bar{x}}_{k}={\hat{\bar{x}}}_{k}+\Delta \bar{x}
$$

For the contact-pressure field:

$$
{\lambda}_{k}={\hat{\lambda}}_{k}+\Delta \lambda
$$

For the friction-pressure field:

$$
{\Lambda}_{k}={\hat{\Lambda}}_{k}+\Delta \Lambda
$$

This notation remains valid for other quantities such as the augmented contact multiplier:

$$
{g}_{n,k}={\hat{g}}_{n,k}+{\Delta g}_{n}
$$

And the augmented friction semi-multiplier:

$$
{h}_{\tau ,k}={\hat{h}}_{\tau ,k}+{\Delta h}_{\tau}
$$

For the projection operator onto the unit ball:

$$
{\hat{\tau}}_{k}=\frac{{\hat{h}}_{\tau ,k}}{∥{\hat{h}}_{\tau ,k}∥}
$$

#### 6.3.2 Discretisation of the local frame

One does not specify the manner in which the normals and the tangents are interpolated, because everything will depend on the options specified in DEFI_CONTACT (activation of smoothing for example). One must simply know that one uses the same shape functions as the description of the geometry. One will write directly that the normal or the tangents are quantities expressed at the current integration point:

$$
n\stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {n}_{h}\right\rbrace
{t}^{1}\stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {t}_{h}^{1}\right\rbrace
{t}^{2}\stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {t}_{h}^{2}\right\rbrace
$$

One will need to transform unknowns from the usual 3D space towards the tangent plane. One uses for this the rectangular matrix $\left[{T}_{h}\right]$ such that:

$$
\left[{T}_{h}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\left\lbrace {t}_{h}^{1}\right\rbrace & \left\lbrace {t}_{h}^{2}\right\rbrace \end{array}\right]
$$

The discretisation of the normal projection matrix is denoted:

$$
{\underline{\underline{P}}}^{n}\stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left[{P}_{h}^{n}\right]
$$

Expanding the terms of the normal $n$ (discretised) and its three components in the global frame $(x,y,z)$ , one has:

$$
\left[{P}_{h}^{n}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{n}_{x}^{2}& {n}_{x}{n}_{y}& {n}_{x}{n}_{z}\\ {n}_{x}{n}_{y}& {n}_{y}^{2}& {n}_{y}{n}_{z}\\ {n}_{x}{n}_{z}& {n}_{y}{n}_{z}& {n}_{z}^{2}\end{array}\right]
$$

Finally, the discretisation of the tangent projection matrix is denoted:

$$
{\underline{\underline{P}}}^{\tau}\stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left[{P}_{h}^{\tau}\right]
$$

Take care not to confuse $\left[{P}_{h}^{\tau}\right]$ with the matrix $\left[{T}_{h}\right]$ () on the tangent plane. $\left[{P}_{h}^{\tau}\right]$ equals:

$$
\left[{P}_{h}^{\tau}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}1-{n}_{x}^{2}& -{n}_{x}{n}_{y}& -{n}_{x}{n}_{z}\\ -{n}_{x}{n}_{y}& 1-{n}_{y}^{2}& -{n}_{y}{n}_{z}\\ -{n}_{x}{n}_{z}& -{n}_{y}{n}_{z}& 1-{n}_{z}^{2}\end{array}\right]
$$

The matrices $\left[{P}_{h}^{\tau}\right]$ and $\left[{P}_{h}^{n}\right]$ are symmetric:

$$
\left[{P}_{h}^{\tau}\right]={\left[{P}_{h}^{\tau}\right]}^{T}
$$

#### 6.3.3 Discretisation of the solution fields

Consider the vector field describing the geometry (coordinates of the nodes) on the slave element ${p}^{e}$ . This field has two or three components according to the dimension of the space; we shall denote it in the following form:

$$
{p}^{e}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{p}_{x}^{e}\\ {p}_{y}^{e}\\ {p}_{z}^{e}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

The component ${p}_{x}^{e}$ is approximated by:

$$
{p}_{x}^{e}=\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathit{NN}}_{e}}{p}_{x,j}^{e}{N}_{j}^{e}
$$

With ${\mathit{NN}}_{e}$ the number of nodes on the slave element, ${p}_{x,j}^{e}$ the J-th nodal value of the field along the dimension $x$ and ${N}_{j}^{e}$ the shape functions. Projecting onto the finite-element bases, the shape functions being identical for the three dimensions of space:

$$
{p}^{e}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{p}_{x}^{e}\\ {p}_{y}^{e}\\ {p}_{z}^{e}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathit{NN}}_{e}}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{N}_{j}^{e}& 0& 0\\ 0& {N}_{j}^{e}& 0\\ 0& 0& {N}_{j}^{e}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{p}_{x,j}^{e}\\ {p}_{y,j}^{e}\\ {p}_{z,j}^{e}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

One will write in more compact form the nodal values $\left\lbrace {p}_{h}^{e}\right\rbrace$ :

$$
{p}^{e}=\left[{N}^{e}\right]\left\lbrace {p}_{h}^{e}\right\rbrace
$$

In an analogous manner for the master element:

$$
{p}^{m}=\left[{N}^{m}\right]\left\lbrace {p}_{h}^{m}\right\rbrace
$$

For the displacement fields:

$$
{u}^{e}=\left[{N}^{e}\right]\left\lbrace {u}_{h}^{e}\right\rbrace
$$

And for the current coordinate:

$$
{x}^{e}=\left[{N}^{e}\right]\left\lbrace {x}_{h}^{e}\right\rbrace
$$

The contact-multiplier field is scalar:

$$
\lambda =\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathit{NN}}_{e}}{\lambda}_{j}{\psi}_{j}
$$

With ${\psi}_{j}$ the shape functions used for the Lagrange multipliers. One will write in more compact form:

$$
\lambda =\langle \psi \rangle \left\lbrace {\lambda}_{h}\right\rbrace =\langle {\lambda}_{h}\rangle \left\lbrace \psi \right\rbrace
$$

The normal and the tangents must also be discretised. One starts from the definition of the tangent frame on the master mesh cell, and one interpolates the normals and the tangents thanks to the same shape functions. One will denote by $\left\lbrace {n}_{h}\right\rbrace$ , $\left\lbrace {t}_{h}^{1}\right\rbrace$ and $\left\lbrace {t}_{h}^{2}\right\rbrace$ these discretised quantities. When writing the different relations, we assumed that the normal friction force density was a global quantity, defined in the global frame:

$$
\Lambda =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\Lambda}_{x}\\ {\Lambda}_{y}\\ {\Lambda}_{z}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

The component ${\Lambda}_{x}$ on a boundary element is written as follows:

$$
{\Lambda}_{x}=\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathit{NN}}_{c}}{\Lambda}_{x,j}{\phi }_{j}
$$

With ${\mathit{NN}}_{c}$ the number of nodes on the boundary element, ${\Lambda}_{x,j}$ the J-th nodal value of the component $x$ of the friction-pressure field and ${\phi }_{j}$ the shape functions. In fact, since there naturally exists a close relation between contact and friction (that of the definition of the local frame), the basis functions ${\psi}_{j}$ and ${\phi }_{j}$ are not independent, and one will decompose the global friction Lagrange semi-multiplier $\Lambda$ into two components (in 3D) on the tangent plane defined at the contact point by $({t}^{1},{t}^{2})$ :

$$
\Lambda =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\Lambda}_{x}\\ {\Lambda}_{y}\\ {\Lambda}_{z}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathit{NN}}_{e}}\left[\begin{array}{cc}\left\lbrace {t}_{h}^{1}\right\rbrace & \left\lbrace {t}_{h}^{2}\right\rbrace \end{array}\right]\text{}\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\psi}_{j}& 0\\ 0& {\psi}_{j}\end{array}\right]\text{}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\Lambda}_{j}^{1}\\ {\Lambda}_{j}^{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

Which one will rewrite in more compact form:

$$
\Lambda =\left[{T}_{h}\right]\left[\psi \right]\left\lbrace {\Lambda}_{h}\right\rbrace
$$

If one considers the 3D case, the field of the friction semi-multiplier $\Lambda$ is therefore of dimension three times the number of slave nodes. Its discrete version $\left\lbrace {\Lambda}_{h}\right\rbrace$ , the projection onto the tangent plane, is of dimension twice the number of slave nodes. We therefore have only three a priori independent finite-element bases left:

- The basis describing the geometry of the structure;
- The basis describing the primal field of unknowns (displacement or velocity);
- The basis describing the Lagrange multiplier of contact and the projection of the friction semi-multiplier onto the tangent plane at the contact point.

#### 6.3.4 Numerical integration

The transformation of the continuous integral of a scalar quantity $f$ into a discrete integral is written:

$$
{\int}_{{\Omega}_{h}}fd\Omega \approx \sum_{c=1}^{\mathit{NP}}f({\zeta}_{c}){\omega}_{c}{J}_{c}
$$

With $\mathit{NP}$ the number of integration points, ${\zeta}_{c}$ the parametric coordinate of the integration point on the slave mesh cell, ${\omega}_{c}$ the integration weight of this point and ${J}_{c}$ the Jacobian of the transformation of the integration domain. This point is projected from the slave surface towards the master surface by the pairing mapping ${A}_{t}$ . The evaluation on the master surface of the “paired” quantities denoted $\bar{f}$ is therefore written simply:

$$
{\int}_{{\Omega}_{h}}\bar{f}d\Omega \approx \sum_{c=1}^{\mathit{NP}}\bar{f}({A}_{t}({\zeta}_{c})){\omega}_{c}{J}_{c}
$$

In the case of contact, it should be noted that each contact point is also an integration point (method of discretisation of the sign field by collocation). The whole of the continuous integrals will therefore be discretised by a numerical scheme coinciding with the collocation method of the sign fields.

#### 6.3.5 Discretisation of the jumps

The different quantities linked to the “jump” are functions of a quantity on the slave surface with its projection onto their master surface. Let us first consider the projection of the jump onto the normal:

$$
{\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{n}=\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}\cdot n=(u-\bar{u})\cdot n
$$

Recalling that $u$ is a quantity defined on the slave surface and $\bar{u}$ its projection onto the master surface. We shall also need to expand the terms of tangential type:

$$
{\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}=\left[{P}^{\tau}\right]\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}=\left[{P}^{\tau}\right](u-\bar{u})
$$

One recalls that the discretisation defined on the unknown $u$ by () uses the shape functions defined at the current integration point ${\zeta}_{c}$ on the slave surface. The projection $\bar{u}$ of this quantity onto the master surface will therefore use the transported integration point ${A}_{t}({\zeta}_{c})$ on the master surface:

$$
u=\left[{N}^{e}({\zeta}_{c})\right]\left\lbrace {u}_{h}^{e}\right\rbrace
$$

Which one will denote in a more compact manner by:

$$
u=\left[{N}^{e}\right]\left\lbrace {u}_{h}^{e}\right\rbrace
$$

One finally has:

$$
{\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{n}=\langle {n}_{h}\rangle (\left[{N}^{e}\right]\lbrace  {u}_{h}^{e}\rbrace  -\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]\lbrace  {u}_{h}^{m}\rbrace  )=(\langle {u}_{h}^{e}\rangle {\left[{N}^{e}\right]}^{T}-\langle {u}_{h}^{m}\rangle {\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]}^{T})\left\lbrace {n}_{h}\right\rbrace
$$

It is a scalar. For the tangential jump:

$$
{\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}=\left[{P}^{\tau}\right](\left[{N}^{e}\right]\lbrace  {u}_{h}^{e}\rbrace  -\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]\lbrace  {u}_{h}^{m}\rbrace  )
$$

If one now considers the normal displacement jump ${d}_{n}$ :

$$
{d}_{n}={\mathrm{〚}x\mathrm{〛}}_{n}=\mathrm{〚}x\mathrm{〛}\cdot n
$$

Therefore:

$$
{d}_{n}=\langle {n}_{h}\rangle (\left[{N}^{e}\right]\lbrace  {x}_{h}^{e}\rbrace  -\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]\lbrace  {x}_{h}^{m}\rbrace  )
$$

Likewise:

$$
{\mathrm{〚}x\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}=\left[{P}^{\tau}\right](\left[{N}^{e}\right]\lbrace  {x}_{h}^{e}\rbrace  -\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]\lbrace  {x}_{h}^{m}\rbrace  )
$$

Whence:

$$
{d}_{\tau}=\left[{P}^{\tau}\right](\left[{N}^{e}\right]\lbrace  {x}_{h}^{e}\rbrace  -\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]\lbrace  {x}_{h}^{m}\rbrace  )
$$

The quantities differentiated with respect to the parametric coordinates:

$$
\frac{\partial \bar{x}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}=\sum_{j=1}^{{\mathit{NN}}_{m}}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\frac{\partial {\bar{N}}_{j}^{m}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}& 0& 0\\ 0& \frac{\partial {\bar{N}}_{j}^{m}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}& 0\\ 0& 0& \frac{\partial {\bar{N}}_{j}^{m}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{x}_{j}^{m}\\ {y}_{j}^{m}\\ {z}_{j}^{m}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

Denoting by $\left[{\bar{B}}_{\alpha}^{m}\right]$ the matrix of the derivatives of the shape functions with respect to the parametric coordinate ${\zeta}^{\alpha}$ . That is:

$$
\frac{\partial \bar{x}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}=\left[{\bar{B}}_{\alpha}^{m}\right]\lbrace  {x}_{h}^{m}\rbrace
$$

#### 6.3.6 Discretisation of the (semi)-multipliers

Consider the augmented contact multiplier ${g}_{n}$ :

$$
{g}_{n}=\lambda -{\rho}_{n}{d}_{n}
$$

Its discretisation gives:

$$
{g}_{n}=\langle \psi \rangle \left\lbrace {\lambda}_{h}\right\rbrace -{\rho}_{n}\langle {n}_{h}\rangle (\left[{N}^{e}\right]\lbrace  {x}_{h}^{e}\rbrace  -\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]\lbrace  {x}_{h}^{m}\rbrace  )
$$

Now consider the augmented friction semi-multiplier ${h}_{\tau}$ :

$$
{h}_{\tau}=\Lambda +{\rho}_{t}{v}_{\tau}
$$

We consider the incremental writing in displacement (see § 5.2 ):

$$
{h}_{\tau}=\Lambda +{\bar{\rho}}_{t}{\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\tau}
$$

The discretisation of the semi-multiplier gives:

$$
{h}_{\tau}=\left[T\right]\left[\psi \right]\left\lbrace {\Lambda}_{h}\right\rbrace +{\bar{\rho}}_{\tau}\left[{P}^{\tau}\right](\left[{N}^{e}\right]\lbrace  {u}_{h}^{e}\rbrace  -\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]\lbrace  {u}_{h}^{m}\rbrace  )
$$

#### 6.3.7 Contact and friction statuses

One has defined the contact sign field denoted ${S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}$ by the following expression:

$$
{S}_{u}^{{g}_{n}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}{g}_{n}\le 0\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{si}{g}_{n}>0\end{array}\end{array}
$$

This operator is the characteristic function of the augmented contact multiplier ${g}_{n}$ on the convex set ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}$ . One has also defined the friction sign field denoted ${S}_{f}^{{h}_{\tau}}$ by the following expression:

$$
{S}_{f}({h}_{\tau})=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}{h}_{\tau}\in B(0,1)\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{sinon}\end{array}\end{array}
$$

This operator is the characteristic function of the augmented friction semi-multiplier ${h}_{\tau}$ on the convex set $B(0,1)$ . The discretisation of the sign fields is trivial because one uses collocation on these fields of discontinuous nature (see § 6.1.4 ). The discretised value of a multiplier (of contact or of friction) is equal to its value at the integration point. These operators being discontinuous, their linearisation has no meaning. One therefore writes directly the value of these fields at the current time $k$ but just before the current Newton iteration:

$$
{\hat{S}}_{u,k}^{{g}_{n}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{cc}1& \text{si}{\hat{g}}_{n,k}\le 0\end{array}\\ \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{si}{\hat{g}}_{n,k}>0\end{array}\end{array}
$$

#### 6.3.8 Discretisation of the virtual quantities

We shall now consider the writing of the discretised forms of the virtual quantities. For $\delta {d}_{n}$ :

$$
\delta {d}_{n}=\langle {n}_{h}\rangle (\left[{N}^{e}\right]\lbrace  \delta {u}_{h}^{e}\rbrace  -\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]\lbrace  \delta {u}_{h}^{m}\rbrace  )=(\langle \delta {u}_{h}^{e}\rangle {\left[{N}^{e}\right]}^{T}-\langle \delta {u}_{h}^{m}\rangle {\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]}^{T})\left\lbrace {n}_{h}\right\rbrace
$$

For $\delta {d}_{\tau}$ :

$$
{\delta d}_{\tau}=\left[{P}^{\tau}\right](\left[{N}^{e}\right]\lbrace  {\delta u}_{h}^{e}\rbrace  -\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]\lbrace  {\delta u}_{h}^{m}\rbrace  )
$$

The variation of the augmented friction semi-multiplier equals:

$$
{\delta h}_{\tau}=\left[T\right]\left[\psi \right]\left\lbrace {\delta \Lambda }_{h}\right\rbrace +{\bar{\rho}}_{\tau}\left[{P}^{\tau}\right](\left[{N}^{e}\right]\lbrace  {\delta u}_{h}^{e}\rbrace  -\left[{\bar{N}}^{m}\right]\lbrace  {\delta u}_{h}^{m}\rbrace  )
$$

### 6.4 Resulting discretised system

One starts again from the system (), which represents the equilibrium of the system (PVW) in incremental form:

$$
\textrm{Trouver les champs}
({u}_{k}^{1},{u}_{k}^{2},{\lambda}_{n,k},{\Lambda}_{k})\in {\text{CA}}^{1}\times {\text{CA}}^{2}\times H\times H
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[{G}_{int,k}^{i}\left({W}_{k},{\delta W}_{k}\right)-{G}_{\text{ext},k}^{i}\left({W}_{k},{\delta W}_{k}\right)\right]-{G}_{\text{c},k}\left({W}_{k},{\delta W}_{k}\right)-{G}_{\text{f},k}\left({W}_{k},{\delta W}_{k}\right)=0& (a)\\ {\tilde{G}}_{\text{c},k}\left({W}_{k},{\delta W}_{k}\right)=0& (b)\\ {\tilde{G}}_{\text{f},k}\left({W}_{k},{\delta W}_{k}\right)=0& (c)\end{array}
\forall {\delta W}_{k}\in {\text{CA}}^{1}\times {\text{CA}}^{2}\times H\times H
$$

After discretisation, we obtain the following linearised system:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[{G}_{int,k}^{i}-{G}_{\text{ext},k}^{i}\right]-{G}_{\text{c},k}-{G}_{\text{f},k}=0& \stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {L}_{u,k}\right\rbrace =0\\ {\tilde{G}}_{\text{c},k}=0& \stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {L}_{c,k}\right\rbrace =0\\ {\tilde{G}}_{\text{f},k}=0& \stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {L}_{f,k}\right\rbrace =0\end{array}
\forall {\delta W}_{k}\in {\text{CA}}^{1}\times {\text{CA}}^{2}\times H\times H
$$

This system is linearised (see § 5.4 ):

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{L}_{u,k}\\ {L}_{c,k}\\ {L}_{f,k}\end{array}\right\rbrace \approx \left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{L}_{u,k-1}\\ {L}_{c,k-1}\\ {L}_{f,k-1}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{J}_{uu}& {J}_{uc}& {J}_{uf}\\ {J}_{cu}& {J}_{cc}& {J}_{cf}\\ {J}_{fu}& {J}_{fc}& {J}_{ff}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\Delta {u}^{i}\\ \Delta {\lambda}_{n}\\ \Delta \Lambda \end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{L}_{u,k-1}\\ {L}_{c,k-1}\\ {L}_{f,k-1}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{c}J\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace {\Delta W}_{k}\right\rbrace
\forall \delta W\in {\text{CA}}^{1}\times {\text{CA}}^{2}\times H\times H
$$

After linearisation, one therefore finds oneself with a system of the following form:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{c}J\end{array}(\delta W)\right]\left\lbrace \Delta W\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {L}_{k-1}(\delta W)\right\rbrace \stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\langle \delta W\rangle (\left[K\right]\left\lbrace \Delta W\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace L\right\rbrace )=0
$$

With the vector of the nodal unknowns (real or virtual) $\langle \delta W\rangle$ :

$$
⟨\delta W⟩=⟨\begin{array}{cccc}\delta {u}^{e}& \delta {u}^{m}& \delta \lambda & \delta \Lambda \end{array}⟩
$$

The matrix $[K]$ :

$$
\left[K\right]=\left[\begin{array}{cccc}\left[{K}_{\mathit{ee}}^{t}\right]+\left[{K}_{\mathit{ee}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{em}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{ec}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{ef}}\right]\\ \left[{K}_{\mathit{me}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{mm}}^{t}\right]+\left[{K}_{\mathit{mm}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{mc}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{mf}}\right]\\ \left[{K}_{\mathit{ce}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{cm}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{cc}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{cf}}\right]\\ \left[{K}_{\mathit{fe}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{fm}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{fc}}\right]& \left[{K}_{\mathit{ff}}\right]\end{array}\right]
$$

The vector $\left\lbrace L\right\rbrace$ :

$$
\left\lbrace L\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{L}_{int}^{e}+{L}_{c}^{e}+{L}_{f}^{e}\\ {L}_{int}^{m}+{L}_{c}^{m}+{L}_{f}^{m}\\ {\tilde{L}}_{c}^{c}\\ {\tilde{L}}_{f}^{f}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

The matrices $\left[{K}_{\mathit{ee}}^{t}\right]$ and $\left[{K}_{\mathit{mm}}^{t}\right]$ are the tangent matrices coming from the linearisation of the internal forces (discretisation of the Jacobian matrices ${J}_{uu}^{u}$ , see p. 11.1.1 ). The other matrices come from the linearisation of the contact and friction terms (see § 5.4 ). The system to be solved will therefore be:

$$
\left[K\right]\left\lbrace \Delta W\right\rbrace =-\left\lbrace L\right\rbrace
$$

In the expanded expressions, one will always consider the case of the generalised Newton method, basing oneself on the “incremental-iterative” discretisation specified in § 6.3.1 . The discretised quantities **will be written at a single integration point** of coordinate ${\zeta}_{c}$ , in such a way that one will introduce the integration weight ${\omega}_{c}$ of this point and the Jacobian ${J}_{c}$ of the transformation of the integration domain:

$$
{\int}_{{\Omega}_{h}}fd\Omega \stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }f({\zeta}_{c})\omega (c)J
$$

**Important note: It should be noted that with the notations above it is in fact** $-\left[L\right]$ **which one finds on the right of the equals sign. However, since the frictional-contact forces are added in the code to the internal forces which are themselves subtracted from the right-hand side, one does not need to take this change of sign into account. The expressions which are written in the elementary terms of the code are therefore indeed identical to those presented.**

## 7 Management of the incompatibilities in the treatment of contact

The presence of contact and of Dirichlet boundary conditions imposed on points of a common surface may generate redundancy problems leading to the failure of the analysis (in general, the tangent stiffness matrix becomes singular). In what follows, we present a few typical cases of redundancy which we have identified, and the specific treatments proposed in [].

### 7.1 Redundancy between the boundary conditions and the frictional-contact conditions

#### 7.1.1 Example

The most common case is a redundancy of the contact conditions with the boundary conditions. Let us take the example of a block in frictional contact on a rigid plane (test case ssnv128, see ): one models half of this block, at the end of which one applies the symmetry conditions: ${u}_{x}=0$ . The loading is an imposed pressure $F$ on the side and top faces. The bottom face is, as has been said, in frictional contact with its support. On the segment $[\mathit{AC}]$ , one will have redundancy between the boundary condition and the slip condition.

When one discretised the mixed variational formulation, one wrote that there was coupling between the displacements and the friction semi-multipliers (matrices $\left[{A}_{u}^{\Lambda}\right]$ and $\left[{A}_{\Lambda}^{u}\right]$ ), which means that the non-slip condition will give ${u}_{x}=0$ , whereas this latter condition is already imposed in the global system (the matrix $[B]$ of the boundary conditions). One therefore imposes twice the relation ${u}_{x}=0$ . This situation leads to a zero-pivot error during the inversion of the tangent stiffness matrix.

#### 7.1.2 Choice of the approximation space

The solution retained is always to give precedence to the boundary conditions over the sticking-sliding conditions. For this, it suffices to decouple the friction multipliers and the displacements for the component of the node concerned by the redundancy (to take up our example, one will decouple ${u}_{x}$ and ${\Lambda}_{x}$ at the nodes $A$ and $C$ ). This amounts to modifying the approximation space of the friction-multiplier field by using a function which equals zero for the appropriate degree of freedom. This operation is illustrated on the figure in the 2D case where the redundancy condition is at the point $A$ .

$$

In 2D, one and only one slip direction exists. The removal of the sticking condition for the problematic points is therefore sufficient to eliminate any redundancy. It is done by decoupling the friction semi-multiplier associated with a constraint from the displacement unknowns.

In 3D on the other hand, the sliding or the sticking occurring in the tangent plane, one may wish to favour a particular slip direction (for example perpendicular to a restraint). For this, the user supplies under the keyword DIRE_EXCL_FROT the direction to be excluded. This direction allows the construction of a local frame which facilitates the decoupling of a single friction semi-multiplier. If no direction is supplied then this amounts to not treating friction on the nodes concerned (decoupling of the two multipliers).

One acts only on the slave-displacement/friction-multiplier coupling because the risk of redundancy will appear only in the case where the boundary conditions are imposed on the slave surface, owing to the fact that the Lagrange multipliers (contact and friction) are discretised on the slave surface.

## 8 Cycling during the Newton process

### 8.1 The problem

The use of a Newton algorithm on non-differentiable quantities sometimes brings about phenomena of numerical cycling. In a rigorous manner, one may use the Newton algorithm on quasi-differentiable quantities (in the classical sense); in this instance, one will speak here of *B-differentiability* [], that is to say that the points of non-differentiability belong to a space of zero measure. In practice, with the numerical approximations introduced by the discretisation and the passage to a floating-point algebra, it is “highly unlikely” that one will encounter these points of non-differentiability, but cycling problems are not excluded, particularly in the case where contact/friction couples with other non-linearities.

A cycling corresponds to the crossing of a point of non-differentiability during the Newton process (that is to say from one Newton iteration to the next).

In [], one distinguishes three kinds of them (see figure ):

- Cycling on the contact status: a point of the mesh passes alternately from a case of contact to a case of non-contact;
- Cycling on the friction status: a point of the mesh passes alternately from a case of sliding to a case of sticking;
- Cycling on the slip threshold: a point of the mesh passes alternately from a case of forward slip to a case of backward slip;

From a more heuristic point of view, one also shows that cycles of order 2 often cause non-convergence.

### 8.2 Detection

The three kinds of cycle are detected automatically.

### 8.3 Treatment

#### 8.3.1 Automatic treatment of cycling

There is the possibility of automatically treating cycles of contact/separation, sticking/sliding and forward-slip/backward-slip type (keyword ADAPTATION in DEFI_CONTACT).

For the keyword ADAPTATION='CYCLAGE', one constructs a basis of the generalised Jacobian over two successive iterations: one uses this basis to modify the current contact and friction matrices. This manipulation serves for contact/separation cycling. The cases of forward-slip/backward-slip cycling are treated so that the point is taken as sticking as soon as the norm of its slip is almost zero. In the case where the user requests a cycling-type treatment, one carries out an automatic switch of the non-converged points into penalisation with an automatic control of the penalisation coefficient (if the initial algorithm was the augmented Lagrangian). The variational formulation in ALGO_CONT='STANDARD' is thus modified. Indeed, in the expression of the initial variational formulation. Denoting by ${G}_{\text{c},k}^{\mathit{STD}}$ the weak contact force linked to ALGO_CONT='STANDARD' for the set of points which pose no problems, ${G}_{\text{c},k}^{\mathit{PENA}}$ for the points which have switched, one has:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left[{G}_{int,k}^{i}-{G}_{\text{ext},k}^{i}\right]-{G}_{\text{c},k}^{\mathit{STD}}+{G}_{\text{c},k}-{G}_{\text{f},k}=0& \stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {L}_{u,k}\right\rbrace =0\\ {\tilde{G}}_{\text{c},k}^{\mathit{STD}}=0& \stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {L}_{c,k}^{\mathit{STD}}\right\rbrace =0\\ {\tilde{G}}_{\text{c},k}^{\mathit{PENA}}=0& \stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {L}_{c,k}^{\mathit{PENA}}\right\rbrace =0\\ {\tilde{G}}_{\text{f},k}=0& \stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {L}_{f,k}^{\mathit{PENA}}\right\rbrace =0\end{array}
$$

Finally, in cycling mode, in order to reinforce robustness, one does not wait for the Newton convergence which ensures that the pressures and the gaps are indeed in the cone of the admissible values ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{-}$ .

For the keyword ADAPTATION='ADAPT_COEF', there are two cases:

- In the augmented Lagrangian (ALGO_CONT='STANDARD' and ALGO_FROT='STANDARD'), one will modify the augmentation coefficient in order to “break” the cycle between the sticking status and the sliding status at the same integration point. This modification of the coefficient is safe because it does not change the results, but only the rate of convergence. This mode serves to manage sticking/sliding cycling. For this, the following algorithm is employed:

  - For a given augmentation coefficient, one computes the sticking/sliding status at the previous cycle and at the current cycle of the point detected as “cyclic”: is one close to the point of discontinuity (tolerance fixed at 5%)?
  - If yes, one iteratively modifies the augmentation coefficient in order to leave this critical zone while not approaching it from the other side;
  - If no, one changes nothing;
  - One propagates the adapted coefficient onto the other points according to a specific algorithm (see § 8.3.2 );
- In the stabilised Lagrangian (ALGO_CONT='PENALISATION'), one will modify the regularisation coefficients in penalisation in order to detect automatically the cases of singular matrices and to treat automatically the maximum penetration computed in the course of the iterations: see § 8.3.3 .

Notes on sticking/sliding cycling:

- Owing to the scaling of the projection of the friction Lagrangian (see § 3.2.4 ), the tolerance given (here 5% on either side of the point of discontinuity) does not depend on the units of the problem;
- The cycling-detection procedure has exhibited a point which varies between the sliding status and the sticking status. The strategy retained here consists in considering the cases where one of the statuses is close to the point of discontinuity. If this is not the case (cycling between two zones far from the discontinuity or between two zones close to the discontinuity), the algorithm does nothing.
- The algorithm for adaptation of the augmentation coefficient is a simple loop which, in a first stage, increases the augmentation coefficient by multiplying it by two, and if one fails, starts off in the other direction by dividing the coefficient by two. The coefficient is chosen in the interval $[{10}^{-8}{,10}^{+8}]$ .

#### 8.3.2 Algorithm for propagation of the augmentation coefficient, sticking/sliding case

The previous algorithm is effective only if one sets up a procedure which will propagate (or not) the optimal coefficient found on a cyclic point. Indeed, in practice, such an optimal coefficient has a strong chance of also being so for other points. This method thus makes it possible to anticipate the risks of cycling of the points which are not yet in contact and makes the algorithm much more efficient.

For this, during the coefficient-adaptation procedure (algorithm of § 8.3.1 ), one stores the maximum and minimum values found over the whole contact zone and one applies the following algorithm:

- Loop over the contact points
- With the maximum coefficient on the contact zone, one evaluates on all the points whether or not one is close to the point of discontinuity; one then has four situations:

1. This new coefficient does not modify the situation of the point on the friction graph with respect to the old coefficient→ one propagates the new coefficient;
2. This coefficient modifies the situation of the point on the friction graph but the old situation was far from the discontinuity→ one does not propagate the new coefficient;
3. This coefficient modifies the situation of the point on the friction graph, the old situation was close to the discontinuity and the new position moves away from the discontinuity→ one propagates the new coefficient;
4. This coefficient modifies the situation of the point on the friction graph, the old situation was close to the discontinuity and the new position changes status (crossing of the discontinuity)→ one does not propagate the new coefficient;

- With the minimum coefficient on the contact zone, one evaluates on all the points whether or not one is close to the point of discontinuity; one evaluates the same situations as in the previous case;
- End of the loop over the contact points

#### 8.3.3 Algorithm for initialisation of the penalisation coefficient

We shall detail the use of the keyword ADAPTATION='ADAPT_COEF' in the case of penalised contact. In certain analyses with the penalisation method on contact, the matrix is singular from the first iteration of the first time step. This singularity is due to a poor choice of the initial penalisation coefficients. To detect this situation numerically and to treat it, one relies on the assembled matrix before the first factorisation. Indeed, one notes that this situation appears if the largest value on the diagonal (apart from the Lagrange terms due to the boundary conditions) is very far from the smallest value on this same diagonal.

The profile of the assembled matrix looking at the diagonal is:

$$
K=\left(\begin{array}{ccc}\tilde{K}& \mathrm{..}& {B}_{\mathit{lagr}}\\ \mathrm{..}& {K}_{\mathit{pena}}& \mathrm{..}\\ {B}_{\mathit{lagr}}& \mathrm{..}& \mathrm{..}\end{array}\right)
$$

With $\tilde{K}$ the global stiffness matrix reduced to the diagonal terms without the contact terms and ${K}_{\mathit{pena}}$ the contact penalisation matrix such that:

$$
{K}_{\mathit{pena}}={\left(\begin{array}{cc}{K}_{{\mathit{pena}}_{11}}={r}_{n}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{S}_{c}{g}_{n}(u)\delta {g}_{n}(u)d\Gamma & \mathrm{..}\\ \mathrm{..}& {K}_{{\mathit{pena}}_{22}}=\frac{1}{{r}_{n}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{g}_{n}(u)\delta {g}_{n}(u)d\Gamma \end{array}\right)}_{ii}
$$

One deliberately omits the friction terms for the sake of simplicity. In this last expression, one recalls that:

- ${S}_{c}$ denotes the contact status;
- ${g}_{n}$ denotes the current contact gap;
- $\delta {g}_{n}$ denotes the virtual contact gap;

The initialisation algorithm currently treats two cases:

- After having assembled the matrix $K$ , one computes ${r}_{min}=min(\tilde{K}\cup {K}_{\mathit{pena}})$ and ${r}_{max}=max(\tilde{K}\cup {K}_{\mathit{pena}})$
- First case: ${r}_{min}$ is very small but non-zero and $\left|(\log({r}_{max}))\right|/\left|(\log({r}_{min}))\right|<4$ .

This case corresponds to the case most often encountered when a singular matrix is detected. It reflects two assumptions observed in practice.

- The first assumption is that one is in the presence of materials that are not soft (global structural stiffness not negligible, so a very small ${r}_{min}$ comes from the contact) and **that there is at least one point initially in contact** (${K}_{{\mathit{pena}}_{11}}\ne 0$ ). If ${r}_{n}$ has been chosen too large (e.g.: ${r}_{n}\gg E$ Young's modulus of the hard body) then either ${K}_{{\mathit{pena}}_{22}}$ is zero or ${K}_{{\mathit{pena}}_{11}}$ is very much greater than $max(\tilde{K})$ : one must decrease ${r}_{n}$ . A value less than ${r}_{max}$ is sufficient to initialise the penalisation coefficient properly. Otherwise if ${r}_{n}$ has been chosen too small then either ${K}_{{\mathit{pena}}_{11}}$ is zero or ${K}_{{\mathit{pena}}_{22}}$ is very much greater than $max(\tilde{K})$ : one must increase ${r}_{n}$ . A value less than ${r}_{max}$ is sufficient to initialise the penalisation coefficient properly.
- The second assumption is that one is in the presence of materials that are not soft (global structural stiffness not negligible, so a very small ${r}_{min}$ comes from the contact) and that there is no node initially in contact. In this case only ${K}_{{\mathit{pena}}_{22}}$ is at the origin of the singularity. A value less than ${r}_{max}$ is sufficient to initialise the penalisation coefficient properly.
- In practice a single formula is sufficient for this case:

#### 8.3.4 Advice if the automatic treatment fails or is not possible

For the user, the appearance of these cyclings is the sign of a certain difficulty in converging and one may treat this problem in three manners:

- Use a more robust algorithm such as the partial Newton or the incomplete Newton (see keywords ALGO_RESO_* ). In this latter case, another type of cycling may appear: it is the flip-flop, a cycling of contact type but of order 15;
- Modify the augmentation parameters (keywords COEF_FROT and COEF_CONT). Here, there are no pre-established rules on the direction of the modification (decrease or increase the value) and on its amplitude. Nevertheless, it should be observed that the augmentation parameter ${\rho}_{t}$ is very sensitive.
- Modify one's model. Either by refining/coarsening the mesh (warning! refinement may amplify the cycling phenomena), or by degrading the model (passage to an elastic material, or even a rigid one).

## 11 Appendix A: computation of the terms of the Jacobian

### 11.1 Equilibrium equation

For the equilibrium equation the virtual quantities are total variations with respect to the displacements. The notation $\tilde{\delta}$ will therefore be employed. The first row of the Jacobian matrix $\left[J\right]$ consists of three terms:

$$
{J}_{uu}={J}_{uu}^{\text{ext}}+{J}_{uu}^{int}+{J}_{uu}^{c}+{J}_{uu}^{f}
{J}_{uc}={J}_{uc}^{c}+{J}_{uc}^{f}
{J}_{uf}={J}_{uf}^{c}+{J}_{uf}^{f}
$$

One already knows that the internal and external forces of the bodies will depend neither on the contact Lagrangian, nor on the friction Lagrangian:

$$
{J}_{uc}^{int}={J}_{uc}^{\text{ext}}=0
$$

But also that the contact pressure does not depend on the friction pressure:

$$
{J}_{uf}^{\text{c}}=0
$$

Whereas the friction pressure depends on the contact pressure (see § 5.4.1 ):

$$
{J}_{uc}^{\text{f}}\mathrm{\ne }0
$$

These are not simplification assumptions, but the logical consequence of the writing of the physical model.

#### 11.1.1 Second variation with respect to the displacements

In this paragraph, the second variation will be understood as the *total* variation with respect to the displacements ${u}^{i}$ . It will be denoted ${\Delta}_{{u}^{i}}=\tilde{\Delta}$ . We shall not return in detail to the computation of the non-linear terms ${J}_{uu}^{int}$ and ${J}_{uu}^{\text{ext}}$ corresponding to the internal forces ${G}_{int}^{i}$ and external forces ${G}_{\text{ext}}^{i}$ since they concern the constitutive behaviour and the kinematics of the solids without frictional contact. For example, the variation of the work of the internal forces introduces the tangent matrices ${\underline{\underline{K}}}_{t}^{i}$ such that:

### 11.2 Contact law

For the contact law (in weak form) the virtual quantities are variations with respect to the contact pressure ${\lambda}_{n}$ . The notation $\delta$ will therefore be employed in this sense. The second row of the Jacobian matrix $\left[J\right]$ consists of three terms:

$$

{J}_{cc}
$$

Since the contact pressure does not depend on the friction pressure, one already knows that:

$$

#### 11.2.1 Second variation with respect to the displacements

In this paragraph, the second variation will always be understood as the *total* variation with respect to the displacements ${u}^{i}$ . It will be denoted ${\Delta}_{{u}^{i}}=\tilde{\Delta}$ . We begin by evaluating ${J}_{cu}$ such that:

$$

One immediately observes that ${J}_{cu}\mathrm{\ne }{J}_{uc}$ because of (). In friction, the matrix is not symmetric.

\left[{K}_{\mathit{ec}}^{\text{c}}\right]=-{\omega}_{c}{J}_{c}{\hat{S}}_{u,k}^{{g}_{n}}{\left[{N}^{e}\right]}^{T}\lbrace  {n}_{h}\rbrace  \langle \psi \rangle
\left[{K}_{\mathit{mc}}^{\text{c}}\right]=+{\omega}_{c}{J}_{c}{\hat{S}}_{u,k}^{{g}_{n}}{\left[{N}^{m}\right]}^{T}\lbrace  {n}_{h}\rbrace  \langle \psi \rangle

From the friction reaction, that is to say from the Jacobians () and ():

$$
{J}_{uc}^{\text{f},1}\stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left[{K}_{\mathit{ec}}^{\text{f},1}\right],\left[{K}_{\mathit{mc}}^{\text{f},1}\right]
{J}_{uc}^{\text{f},2}\stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left[{K}_{\mathit{ec}}^{\text{f},2}\right],\left[{K}_{\mathit{mc}}^{\text{f},2}\right]

The first part concerns the sticking case (${\hat{S}}_{u,k}^{{g}_{n}}=1$ and ${\hat{S}}_{f,k}^{{h}_{\tau}}=1$ ):

The second part concerns the sliding case (${\hat{S}}_{u,k}^{{g}_{n}}=1$ and ${\hat{S}}_{f,k}^{{h}_{\tau}}=0$ ):

{J}_{uc}^{\text{f},2}=-{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu \left[{\underline{\underline{P}}}^{\tau}({\delta}_{t}{\hat{u}}_{k}^{e}-{\delta}_{t}{\hat{u}}_{k}^{m})\right]\cdot {\hat{\tau}}_{k}\Delta \lambda d{\Gamma}_{c}

Which is written, using in particular the discretisation of the projection onto the unit ball ():

\left[{K}_{\mathit{ec}}^{\text{f},2}\right]=-{\omega}_{c}{J}_{c}\mu {\left[{N}^{e}\right]}^{T}\left[{P}^{\tau}\right]\left\lbrace {\hat{\tau}}_{k}\right\rbrace \left\lbrace \psi \right\rbrace
\left[{K}_{\mathit{mc}}^{\text{f},2}\right]=+{\omega}_{c}{J}_{c}\mu {\left[{N}^{m}\right]}^{T}\left[{P}^{\tau}\right]\left\lbrace {\hat{\tau}}_{k}\right\rbrace \left\lbrace \psi \right\rbrace

The second part concerns the sliding case (${\hat{S}}_{u,k}^{{g}_{n}}=1$ and ${\hat{S}}_{f,k}^{{h}_{\tau}}=0$ ):

### 13.3 Quantities for the contact law

One considers the matrix terms coming from the linearisation of the contact law and therefore in the second row of the global matrix. The discretisation of the Jacobian () corresponding to the first column of the global matrix:

$$
{J}_{cu}\stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left[{K}_{\mathit{ce}}\right],\left[{K}_{\mathit{cm}}\right]

From the Jacobian:

$$
{J}_{cu}=-{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{\hat{S}}_{u,k}^{{g}_{n}}\delta \lambda ({\Delta}_{t}{u}^{e}-{\Delta}_{t}{u}^{m})\cdot {n}_{k}d{\Gamma}_{c}

One obtains two quantities:

$$
\left[{K}_{\mathit{ce}}\right]=-{\omega}_{c}{J}_{c}{\hat{S}}_{u,k}^{{g}_{n}}\left\lbrace \psi \right\rbrace \langle {n}_{h}\rangle \left[{N}^{e}\right]

$$
{J}_{cc}\stackrel{\text{Discrétisation}}{\to }\left[{K}_{\mathit{cc}}\right]

{J}_{cc}=-\frac{1}{{\rho}_{n}}{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\Delta \lambda (1-{\hat{S}}_{u,k}^{{g}_{n}})\delta \lambda d{\Gamma}_{c}

### 13.5 Writing of the infinitesimal slip: GRAND_GLIS

In the case where contact is established and the slip is active and the latter is constrained by the Coulomb law, one must linearise the weak form of the force inducing the slip, the Coulomb law remaining unchanged.

${G}_{f}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\mu {\widehat{\lambda}}_{k}{\widehat{S}}_{u,k}^{{g}_{n}}\left(1-{\widehat{S}}_{f,k}^{{h}_{\tau}}\right){\widehat{\tau}}_{k}\cdot {\delta d}_{\tau}d{\Gamma}_{c}$

Up to now, the relative slip has been approximated in the following way:

$$
\Delta {d}_{\tau}={v}_{\tau}\Delta t=\frac{1}{\Delta t}{⟦\Delta u⟧}_{\tau}\approx (I-{\mathit{nn}}^{T})⟦\Delta u⟧
$$

Note: in what follows, one may if need be replace $\Delta$ by $\delta$.

Strictly speaking, if one adopts a kinematic writing proper to continuum mechanics, the relative velocity at a contact point is:

$$
v=\frac{\partial [{y}_{\mathit{escl}}-{y}_{\mathit{mait}}]}{\partial t}\Delta t-\frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}\frac{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}{\partial t}\delta t=\frac{\partial [{y}_{\mathit{escl}}-{y}_{\mathit{mait}}]}{\partial t}\Delta t-\frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}\Delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}
$$

In what follows, one has:

$\frac{\partial [{y}_{\mathit{escl}}-{y}_{\mathit{mait}}]}{\partial t}\Delta t-\frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}\Delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}=\frac{\partial {g}_{n}n}{\partial t}\Delta t-\frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}\Delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}=\frac{\partial {g}_{n}}{\partial t}\delta tn+{g}_{n}\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}\Delta t-\frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}\Delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}$

One will denote:

${v}_{n}=\frac{\partial {g}_{n}}{\partial t}\Delta tn$ and ${v}_{\tau}={g}_{n}\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}\Delta t-\frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}\Delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}$

The continuous writing of the relative velocity is therefore:

$$
v={v}_{n}+{v}_{\tau}
$$

One is interested here in the writing of ${v}_{\tau}$ . As a first approximation, and assuming that the point is constantly in contact, one has:

$$
{v}_{\tau}\approx \frac{-\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\alpha}}\Delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}
$$

One may refer to equation (66) in order to take into account the variation due to the normal ${g}_{n}\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}$ . From the point of view of vocabulary in continuum mechanics, one says that the slave contact point is described according to a Lagrangian kinematics whereas its projected master point is described according to an Eulerian kinematics. The previous equation shows that, by neglecting the term ${g}_{n}\frac{\partial n}{\partial t}$ , all the information necessary for the computation of the slip in the tangent plane is contained in the variation of the projected master point provided that the status of the contact point equals 1 and remains so during the Newton process in the time step. One will make explicit the term $\Delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}$ .

$$
\Delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}={[\tilde{\tilde{A}}-{g}_{n}\tilde{\tilde{H}}]}_{\alpha \beta }^{-1}[\frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\beta}}(\Delta {y}_{\mathit{escl}}-\Delta {y}_{\mathit{mait}})+{g}_{n}n\Delta \frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\beta}}]
$$

For the linearisation of friction, one will also need the second variation of $\Delta \delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}$ .

$$
\Delta \delta {\zeta}^{\alpha}={[\tilde{\tilde{A}}-{g}_{n}\tilde{\tilde{H}}]}_{\alpha \beta }^{-1}[\frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\beta}}(\Delta {\zeta}^{\gamma}\delta \frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\gamma}}+\Delta \frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\gamma}}\delta {\zeta}^{\gamma})+\Delta {\zeta}^{\gamma}(\frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\beta}}.\frac{{\partial}^{2}{y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{{\partial}^{2}{\zeta}^{\beta}})\delta {\zeta}^{\gamma}]
-{g}_{n}n(\Delta {\zeta}^{\gamma}\delta \frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\gamma}}+\Delta \frac{\partial {y}_{\mathit{mait}}}{\partial {\zeta}^{\gamma}}\delta {\zeta}^{\gamma})
$$

One proposes to the user the two writings of the slip:

- either by projection of the displacement increment onto the tangent plane, equation (4339), via the keyword GRAND_GLIS='NON',
- or by adopting a continuous writing of the tangential slip relative to the master point: GRAND_GLIS='OUI'.

In theory, in small slips, the two writings give the same result. For the implementation, the discretisation technique remains the same for the kinematic unknowns as described in section 6.
