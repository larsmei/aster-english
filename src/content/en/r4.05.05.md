# r4.05.05 Generation of seismic signals

**Summary:**

In "best-estimate" seismic studies, one is often led to carry out transient dynamic analyses. The seismic signal is then modelled by a stochastic process. This process expresses the ground acceleration as a function of time.

One calls trajectories the time signals (accelerograms) which are realisations of the stochastic process. These signals may come from databases (ground accelerations measured during earthquakes) or be obtained by numerical simulation (cf. also [bib17] *)* . The simulation of artificial seismic signals, as carried out by the operator GENE_ACCE_SEISME, is described in this document. The operator proposes two modellings:

- Kanai-Tajimi PSD model (§2)
- PSD model compatible with a target response spectrum (§3)

In both cases, it is possible to introduce an evolution of the frequency content and of the amplitude (modulation) with the duration of the earthquake.

## 1 Modelling of the seismic excitation by a stochastic process

Gaussian stochastic processes are entirely characterised by their power spectral density (PSD). If the process is stationary, then one can simulate trajectories very easily with the aid of the spectral representation theorem as described in §4. This theorem also applies to non-stationary Gaussian processes under the condition that the process can be expressed in the form of an evolutive PSD with weak oscillation (that is to say that the properties change slowly with time).

In a simplified seismic analysis, one models only the strong phase of the seismic signal. The latter is then assumed stationary during this period. This is in particular the case within the framework of linear seismic analyses by modal combination (CQC, SRSS).

Nevertheless, studies and statistical analyses have shown that the seismic signal is non-stationary in amplitude and in frequency content. As for the frequency content, its evolution can be estimated from natural signals. Most of the time one observes a decrease of the central frequency with time [bib9]. This drop in the central frequency of the seismic excitation may go together with the drop in the natural frequencies of the structure subjected to the earthquake. This coincidence may have aggravating effects which it is appropriate to take into account in the modellings of non-linear structures.

### 1.1 Modelling of the earthquake by a non-stationary process with evolutive PSD

One models the seismic motion by realisations of a centred Gaussian process described by its evolutive power spectral density [bib8]:

${S}_{X}(\omega ,t)=q(t){S}_{Y}(\omega ,t)q(t)\in ℝ$                                 (1)

where ${S}_{Y}(\omega ,t)$ is the non-separable part of the spectral density which models the evolution of the frequency content of the seismic signal over time. The function $q(t)$ is a deterministic modulation function which expresses the amplitude of the signal. In the case where one has ${S}_{Y}(\omega ,t)\equiv {S}_{Y}(\omega )$ , that is to say that the PSD does not depend on time, $Y$ is a stationary process and $X(t)=q(t)Y(t),t\in T$ a quasi-stationary process of which only the amplitude varies with time. One then speaks of a separable PSD: ${S}_{X}(\omega ,t)={q}^{2}(t){S}_{Y}(\omega )$ .

In *Code_Aster* , one considers processes $Y(t)$ normalised so that the standard deviation is unity at each time instant. It is the modulation function which determines the appearance of the signals and their energy (and therefore the amplitude over a fixed duration of the strong phase). This is illustrated in **Figure 1a** . The amplitude of the signals to be generated is then determined by the datum of the Arias intensity ${I}_{a}$ (energy), of the standard deviation ${\sigma}_{X}$ over the duration of the strong phase, or else of the median maximum ${a}_{m}$ over the duration of the strong phase. If one wishes to generate signals "compatible" with a response spectrum, then the energy of the signal and its amplitude are determined by the datum of the target spectrum.

Two temporal modulation functions of the amplitude $q(t)$ are proposed in *Code_Aster*: the gamma function and the Jennings & Housner function [bib4]. The gamma function is written:

$q(t)={\alpha}_{1}{t}^{({\alpha}_{2}-1)}\exp(-{\alpha}_{3}t),t\in T$                                         (2)

The parameters ${\alpha}_{2}$ and ${\alpha}_{3}$ describe respectively the shape and the duration of the strong phase of the signal. The parameter ${\alpha}_{1}$ determines the energy of the signal and can be determined from the datum of the Arias intensity. The Jennings & Housner function [bib4] is written:

$q(t)=\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}{(t/{t}_{1})}^{2}& \mathrm{si}0\le t<{t}_{1}\\ 1& \mathrm{si}{t}_{1}\le t\le {t}_{2}\\ \exp(-\alpha {(t-{t}_{2})}^{\beta})& \mathrm{si}{t}_{2}<t\le T\end{array}$                                 (3)

The parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ determine the appearance of the slope after ${t}_{2}$ .

The parameters of these two functions are determined from the datum of the strong-phase duration ${T}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ and the instant of the beginning of the strong phase ${t}_{\mathrm{ini}}$ .

**Note:**

The parameters of the modulation functions are determined from mean values, as described below. The strong phases, maxima (PGA) and Arias intensities of the seismic signals generated with this modelling will consequently exhibit a certain variability about the means.

### 1.2 Parameterisation of the modulation functions

#### 1.2.1 Gamma modulation function

One identifies, by least squares, the parameters ${\alpha}_{2}$ and ${\alpha}_{3}$ such that the instant $\bar{t}{}_{1}$ corresponds to the beginning of the strong phase and $\bar{{t}_{2}}=\bar{t}{}_{\mathrm{ini}}+\bar{T}{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ to the end of the strong phase. The parameter ${\alpha}_{1}$ is a normalisation constant which is chosen such that equation (2) is satisfied if the Arias intensity is given as an input parameter.

In the case where a standard deviation or the median maximum are given, one normalises the Gamma function so that the energy during the strong phase corresponds to the energy of a constant unit modulation: .

${\int}_{\bar{{t}_{1}}}^{\bar{{t}_{2}}}q{(t)}^{2}\mathrm{dt}=\bar{T}{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ *.* For the generation of seismic signals compatible with a response spectrum (cf. §3) one chooses, in accordance with the definition of the peak factor, the equivalence of the total energy with an unmodulated signal of total duration $T{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ : $\intq{(t)}^{2}\mathrm{dt}=T{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ *.*

#### 1.2.2 Jennings & Housner modulation function

One determines the instant $\bar{t}{}_{\mathrm{ini}}$ as well as the two parameters, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ , of the Jennings & Housner modulation function so that the strong phase $\bar{T}{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ corresponds to that given. With the aid of the duration of the strong phase one then determines the instant $\bar{{t}_{2}}$ which corresponds to the end of the plateau.

The length of the plateau therefore always corresponds to the duration of the strong phase $\bar{T}{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ . The parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ of the model are parameters which determine the appearance of the modulation function beyond the strong phase. The function is then multiplied by a constant in order to respect the mean Arias intensity $\bar{I}{}_{a}$ , a standard deviation or the median maximum.

## 2 Evolutive Kanai-Tajimi PSD model

The Kanai-Tajimi PSD model [bib13], which expresses a filtered white noise and models the seismic acceleration in free field, is one of the most classical. The two parameters of the original Kanai-Tajimi model are therefore the natural angular frequency of the filter and the damping of the latter. This model has undergone developments since, in particular as regards the filtering of the low-frequency content [bib2] and the introduction of the evolution of the frequency content of the seismic signals ( *Ahmadi & Fan* [bib1] *)* . The filtering of the low frequencies makes it possible to obtain acceleration signals which can be integrated without drifts in displacement and in velocity (one must resort to the "*baseline correction*" if integration constants must be eliminated). Finally, a modulation function is applied in order to obtain the variation of the amplitude. This model is implemented in *Code_Aster* and available via the operator GENE_ACCE_SEISME, option DSP *.*

### 2.1 General formulation of the model

The modelling adopted is summarised in what follows:

- The process $Y$ is characterised by the Kanai-Tajimi (KT) PSD: this PSD models the absolute acceleration response of a mass subjected to an excitation by white noise (the seismic motion at bedrock). In other words, the Kanai-Tajimi PSD describes a white noise filtered by an oscillator of natural angular frequency ${\omega}_{0}$ and reduced damping ${\xi}_{0}$ .
- The fundamental angular frequency ${\omega}_{0}$ of the evolutive KT PSD is a function of time:

${S}_{\mathit{KT}}(\omega ,t)=\frac{({\omega}_{0}{(t)}^{4}+4{\xi}_{0}^{2}{\omega}_{0}{(t)}^{2}{\omega}^{2})}{({({\omega}_{0}{(t)}^{2}-{\omega}^{2})}^{2}+4{\xi}_{0}^{2}{\omega}_{0}{(t)}^{2}{\omega}^{2})}{S}_{0}$                             (10)

One assumes a linear evolution of the natural angular frequency with respect to time during the strong phase of the earthquake:

${\omega}_{0}(t)=\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}{\omega}_{1}& \mathrm{si}t<{t}_{1}\\ {\omega}_{1}-\omega '(t-{t}_{m})& \mathrm{si}{t}_{1}<t<{t}_{2}\end{array}$                             (11)

where ${t}_{1}$ and ${t}_{m}$ denote respectively the beginning and the mid-instant of the strong phase: ${t}_{m}=0.5({t}_{1}+{t}_{2})$ (cf. also §1.2 for a definition of these quantities). For $t<{t}_{1}$ the natural angular frequency is assumed constant and equal to ${\omega}_{1}={\omega}_{0}({t}_{1})$ .

For $t>{t}_{2}$ , the natural angular frequency is assumed constant and equal to ${\omega}_{2}={\omega}_{1}-\omega '({t}_{2}-{t}_{1})$ . The user must ensure that the natural angular frequency given by this relation remains positive over time.

- Filtering of the KT PSD by a filter of natural angular frequency ${\omega}_{f}$ and a reduced damping ${\xi}_{f}=1.0$ according to Clough & Penzien (CP). This filtering makes it possible to remove the very low-frequency content which leads to non-zero drifts (non-zero displacements and velocities at the end of the earthquake). In the *Code_Aster* implementation, one takes ${\omega}_{f}=0.05{\omega}_{0}$ by default, but the user may supply another value if he wishes. Seismologists call the frequency ${\omega}_{f}$  "Corner Frequency", namely the minimum frequency below which the spectrum must tend towards 0. This leads us to the corrected PSD ${S}_{\mathrm{CP}}(\omega )$ :

> ${S}_{\mathrm{CP}}(\omega ,t)=∣h(\omega )∣{}^{2}{S}_{\mathrm{KT}}(\omega ,t)$ , where the filter is written $h(\omega )=\frac{{\omega}^{2}}{({\omega}_{f}^{2}-{\omega}^{2}+\mathrm{2i}{\xi}_{f}{\omega}_{f}\omega )}$

- Two temporal modulation functions of the amplitude $q(t)$ are proposed: the gamma function and the Jennings & Housner function [bib4]. These functions as well as their parameterisation are described in Chapter 1.

The evolution of the fundamental angular frequency of the evolutive Kanai-Tajimi model is illustrated in **Figure 1a** . **Figure 2a**  shows the filtered Kanai-Tajimi PSD (Clough & Penzien PSD) for a given natural frequency. For regular PSD functions, the natural frequency of the filter is close to the fundamental frequency of the PSD. In Figure 1, one also visualises the bandwidth of the PSD, denoted $\delta$ . The bandwidth of the PSD is linked to the damping of the filter as explained in §3.1.

**Note:**

*A model very similar to that described above is proposed in the reference* (Rezaeian & Der Kiureghian, [bib9,bib10]) *. The difference between the two approaches lies mainly in the writing of the problem in the time domain by* Rezaeian & Der Kiureghian *and not in the frequency domain through the spectral density as proposed here. Now, the writing in the time domain has a certain number of disadvantages, such as the necessity of evaluating the convolution integral and of working with the Wiener process (white noise) which is not a second-order process. The formulation of the problem by a filtered white noise described by its evolutive PSD as proposed here is, by contrast, very straightforward and numerically much more efficient.*

### 2.2 Parameterisation of the modulation functions

The parameters which characterise the appearance of the modulation function are the strong-phase duration ${T}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ , the instant of the beginning of the strong phase ${t}_{\mathrm{ini}}$ . The amplitude of the signals to be generated is determined by the datum of the Arias intensity ${I}_{a}$ (energy), the standard deviation ${\sigma}_{X}$ over the duration of the strong phase or else the median maximum ${a}_{m}$ over the duration of the strong phase.

One assumes given the means of these parameters, in particular the mean strong-phase duration $\bar{T}{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ , the mean instant of the beginning of the strong phase $\bar{{t}_{\mathrm{ini}}}$ and the mean Arias intensity ${\bar{I}}_{a}$ .

In practice, the median maximum ${a}_{m}$ is associated with the datum of the PGA ( *Peak Ground Acceleration* ). Thus, if the PGA is given, then one considers that it corresponds to the median of the maxima ${a}_{m}=\mathrm{Médiane}({\max}_{t\in T}(∣X(t)∣))$ of the trajectories of the process (during the strong phase $T{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ ). This allows us to determine the standard deviation ${\sigma}_{X}$ corresponding to this maximum with the aid of the peak factor ${\eta}_{{T}_{\mathrm{SM}}}$ :

${a}_{m}\approx {\eta}_{{T}_{\mathrm{SM}}}{\sigma}_{X}$                                                 (8)

The peak factor is given by the formula

${\eta}_{{T}_{\mathrm{SM}},p}^{2}=2\ln({\mathrm{2N}}_{\eta}[1-\exp(-{\delta}^{1.2}\sqrt{\pi \ln({\mathrm{2N}}_{\eta})})])$                                 (9)

where ${N}_{\eta}=1,4427{T}_{\mathrm{SM}}{\nu}_{0}^{+}$ . The variables ${\nu}_{0}^{+}$ (central frequency) and $\delta$ (bandwidth) are computed from the moments of the PSD according to expression (17) of §3.1.

The mean Arias intensity ${\bar{I}}_{a}$ of a process $Y(t)\in ℝ$ modulated by a function $q(t)$ , described by equation (1), is expressed as:

$\bar{{I}_{a}}=E(\frac{\pi}{2g}{\int}_{0}^{\infty}{X}^{2}(t)\mathrm{dt})=\frac{\pi}{2g}{\int}_{0}^{\infty}{q}^{2}(t)E({Y}^{2}(t))\mathrm{dt}$                             (4)

knowing that $X(t)=q(t)Y(t)$ . The operator $E$ in the expression above denotes the mathematical expectation and $g=\mathrm{9.81m}/{s}^{2}$ . If one normalises the process $Y(t)$ so that ${\sigma}_{Y}(t)=1$ , one can write:

$\bar{{I}_{a}}=\frac{\pi}{2g}{\int}_{0}^{\infty}{q}^{2}(t)\mathrm{dt}$                                     (5)

In practice the integration is carried out up to the end instant $T$ of the seismic signals. The mean strong-phase duration $\bar{T}{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ is defined from the Arias intensity which expresses the energy contained in the seismic signal. Thus, $\bar{T}{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ is defined as the duration of time between the time instants ${t}_{0.05}$ and ${t}_{0.95}$ where respectively 5% and 95% of the Arias intensity are realised. The instant ${t}_{0.05}$ consequently denotes the beginning of the mean strong phase, $\bar{t}{}_{\mathrm{ini}}$ , such that:

$\bar{t}{}_{\mathrm{ini}}:\frac{\pi}{2g\bar{{I}_{a}}}{\int}_{0}^{{t}_{\mathrm{ini}}}{q}^{2}(t)\mathrm{dt}=0.05$                                 (6)

and $\bar{t}{}_{\mathrm{ini}}+\bar{T}{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ the end of the strong phase:

$\bar{t}{}_{\mathrm{ini}}+\bar{T}{}_{\mathrm{SM}}:\frac{\pi}{2g\bar{{I}_{a}}}{\int}_{0}^{\bar{t}{}_{\mathrm{ini}}+\bar{T}{}_{\mathrm{SM}}}{q}^{2}(t)\mathrm{dt}=0.95$                             (7)

Given the duration of the strong phase, the mean Arias intensity ${\bar{I}}_{a}$ makes it possible to determine the standard deviation of the process $X$ .

**Note:** *The parameters of the modulation functions are determined from the mean strong phase and the mean Arias intensity, as described above. The strong phases, maxima (PGA) and* *Arias intensities of the seismic signals generated with this modelling will consequently exhibit a certain variability about the means.*

### 2.3 Uncertainties and natural variability of the signals

The parameters of the Kanai-Tajimi model are:

- The mean duration of the strong phase $\bar{T}{}_{\mathrm{SM}}$ and the instant of the beginning of the mean strong phase $\bar{t}{}_{\mathrm{ini}}$ (modulation function),
- The natural angular frequency ${\omega}_{1}$ and the slope $\omega '$ of the Kanai-Tajimi PSD (or ${\omega}_{0}$ if one considers a constant value),
- The reduced damping ${\xi}_{0}$ of the Kanai-Tajimi PSD,
- The mean Arias intensity ${\bar{I}}_{a}$ (mean energy contained in the seismic signal), the PGA (median maximum ${a}_{m}$ ) or the standard deviation ${\sigma}_{X}$ .

In order to represent better the natural variability of the seismic signals and to take into account the uncertainty on the parameters of the model, one can model the latter by random variables. Each simulated seismic signal then corresponds to a particular draw of the parameters of the model. The Latin Hypercube sampling method constitutes an efficient method which makes it possible to sweep well the domain of definition of the parameters for a reduced number of draws.

The mean values as well as the distributions of these parameters can be estimated from natural seismic signals corresponding to the scenario sought. They are, for some of them, also available in the literature. One finds a very exhaustive treatment of them in the reference [bib9]. Means, minimum and maximum values and distributions of the parameters, identified from accelerograms recorded on medium to hard soil and $D>\mathrm{10km}$ (taken from the NGA earthquake database [bib14]), are presented in the appendix of this document. These results are taken from the report of Rezaeian & Der Kiureghian [bib9].

## 3 PSD model for generating signals "compatible" with a target SRO

In the case where the earthquake is modelled by a stationary Gaussian process during the strong phase ${T}_{\mathit{SM}}$ *,*  it is possible to establish an (approximate) relation between the spectral density ${S}_{Y}(\omega )$ of the process and its oscillator response spectrum (SRO), ${S}_{a}(\omega ,\xi )$ . The latter is indeed often given by seismologists or prescribed by codes and regulations.

### 3.1 Identification of a PSD compatible with a target spectrum

The first-passage problem makes it possible to link the SRO (for a given angular frequency ${\omega}_{n}$ and a given reduced damping ${\xi}_{0}$ ) to the standard deviation of the process via the peak factor $\eta$ :

${S}_{a}({\omega}_{n},{\xi}_{0})\approx {\omega}_{n}^{2}{\eta}_{{T}_{\mathrm{SM}},p}{\sigma}_{n}$                                 (12)

where ${\sigma}_{n}$ is the standard deviation of the response process

${\sigma}_{n}^{2}=\int∣{h}_{{\omega}_{n},{\xi}_{0}}(\omega )∣{}^{2}{S}_{Y}(\omega )d\omega$                                 (13)

and:

${h}_{{\omega}_{n},{\xi}_{0}}(\omega )=\frac{1}{{\omega}_{n}^{2}-{\omega}^{2}+\mathrm{2i}{\xi}_{0}{\omega}_{n}\omega }$                                 (14)

is the transfer function (filter) of natural angular frequency ${\omega}_{n}$ and of reduced damping ${\xi}_{0}$ .

The peak factor makes it possible to estimate the $p$ -fractiles of the distribution of the maxima of a Gaussian process from the standard deviation. The peak factor, due to Vanmarcke [bib16], is written:

${\eta}_{{T}_{\mathrm{SM}},p}^{2}=2\ln({\mathrm{2N}}_{\eta}[1-\exp(-{\delta}^{1.2}\sqrt{\pi \ln({\mathrm{2N}}_{\eta})})])$ .                    (15)

In this expression, $\delta$ is the bandwidth of the process (cf. Figure 2a) and ${N}_{\eta}$ is determined from

the central frequency ${\nu}_{0}^{+}$ as

${N}_{\eta}={T}_{\mathrm{SM}}{\nu}_{0}^{+}{(-\lnp)}^{-1}$                                     (16)

In this expression, $p$ denotes the fractile of the distribution of the maxima considered. In general, one takes $p=0.5$ which corresponds to the "median of the maxima". The central frequency ${\nu}_{0}^{+}$ denotes the frequency where the energy of the process is concentrated. The central frequency and the bandwidth can be expressed by the classical Rice formulae:

$\begin{array}{}{\nu}_{0}^{+}=\frac{1}{2\pi }\sqrt{\frac{{\lambda}_{2}}{{\lambda}_{0}}},\\ \delta =\sqrt{(1-\frac{{\lambda}_{1}^{2}}{{\lambda}_{0}{\lambda}_{2}})}\end{array}$                                             (17)

where the ${\lambda}_{i}$ are the spectral moments of the PSD of the response process:

${\lambda}_{i}={\int}_{-\infty }^{+\infty }∣\omega ∣{}^{i}∣{h}_{{\omega}_{n},{\xi}_{0}}(\omega )∣{}^{2}{S}_{Y}(\omega )d\omega$ ,                            (18)

with in particular $\sqrt{{\lambda}_{0}}$ the standard deviation of the process. In order to determine the PSD compatible with the SRO, one must invert expression (12). One can show that one has, as a first approximation, for an excitation by white noise (${S}_{Y}(\omega )=\mathrm{const}$ ) and for a filter with natural frequency ${\omega}_{n}$ :

${\lambda}_{0}={\int}_{-\infty }^{+\infty }∣{h}_{{\omega}_{n},{\xi}_{0}}(\omega )∣{}^{2}{S}_{Y}(\omega )d\omega \approx {S}_{Y}({\omega}_{n}){\int}_{-\infty }^{+\infty }∣{h}_{{\omega}_{n},{\xi}_{0}}(\omega )∣{}^{2}d\omega =\frac{\pi {S}_{Y}({\omega}_{n})}{2\xi {\omega}_{n}^{3}}$                 (19)

This formula was subsequently improved by Vanmarcke [bib15, bib16] in order to take into account the contribution of ${S}_{Y}(\omega )$ in the range of angular frequencies $[-{\omega}_{n},{\omega}_{n}]$ where ${h}_{{\omega}_{n}}(\omega )\approx 1/{\omega}_{n}^{2}$ :

${\lambda}_{0}\approx \frac{\pi {S}_{Y}({\omega}_{n})}{2\xi {\omega}_{n}^{3}}+\frac{2}{{\omega}_{n}^{4}}{\int}_{0}^{{\omega}_{n}}{S}_{Y}({\omega}_{n})d\omega -\frac{2}{{\omega}_{n}^{3}}{S}_{Y}({\omega}_{n})$             (20)

This leads us to the Vanmarcke formula for evaluating a PSD ${G}_{S}(\omega )$ from an SRO ${S}_{a}(\omega ,\xi )$ (for a given damping ${\xi}_{0}$ ):

${G}_{S}({\omega}_{n})=\frac{1}{{\omega}_{n}(\frac{\pi}{2{\xi}_{0}}-2)}\left[\frac{{S}_{a}^{2}({\omega}_{n},{\xi}_{0})}{{\eta}_{{T}_{\mathit{SM}},p}^{2}}-2{\int}_{0}^{{\omega}_{n}}{G}_{S}(\omega )d\omega \right],{\omega}_{n}>0$                  (21)

under the condition that these two functions are sufficiently smooth. The original Vanmarcke formula was then used in more or less similar forms by numerous authors [bib7,bib3]. It is implemented in its original version, namely formula (21), in GENE_ACCE_SEISME.

To determine a PSD compatible with an SRO by this formula, one must know the peak factor (15) which, itself, depends on the properties of the PSD to be identified (and which is therefore still unknown). To this end, one approximates the central frequency by the natural frequency of the oscillator so that ${N}_{\eta}={T}_{\mathrm{SM}}\frac{{\omega}_{n}}{2\pi }{(-\lnp)}^{-1}$ . Furthermore, one expresses the bandwidth as a function of the reduced damping $\xi$ , still for the response of an oscillator to a white noise, by the formula [bib15]:

$\delta =\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{1-{\xi}^{2}}{(1-\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan(\frac{2\xi \sqrt{1-{\xi}^{2}}}{1-2{\xi}^{2}}))}^{2}}$                              (22)

For low damping values $\xi <0.1$ , one can estimate the reduced damping by the following asymptotic relation for $\xi \to 0$ [bib15]:

$\delta \approx \sqrt{\frac{4\xi }{\pi}}$

A good approximation of the reduced damping can be obtained by taking $\xi \approx {\frac{\pi \delta }{4}}^{2/1.2}$ .

The identified PSD can then be used to generate trajectories of the stationary or quasi-stationary process (variation of the amplitude by multiplication by a modulation function q(t)). In order to match the target spectrum as well as possible, it is in general necessary to proceed to iterations in order to adjust the spectral content as well as possible [bib16].

In a first stage, one improves the adjustment of the PSD by calling upon the Rice formulae. The latter make it possible to link the PSD to the SRO, through the peak factor. One determines the moments of the PSD in order then to compute the peak factor (15) thanks to expressions (17). Thus, from the PSD at iteration i, ${G}_{S}^{i+1}({\omega}_{n})$ , one determines the corresponding SRO ${S}_{a}^{i}({\omega}_{n})$ by formula (12), and one evaluates the new estimate of the PSD, ${G}_{S}^{i+1}({\omega}_{n})$ , by computing:

${G}_{S}^{i+1}({\omega}_{n})={G}_{S}^{i}({\omega}_{n}){({S}_{a}({\omega}_{n})/{S}_{a}^{i}({\omega}_{n}))}^{2}$                                  (23)

In expression (23), ${S}_{a}({\omega}_{n})$ is the target response spectrum and ${S}_{a}^{i}({\omega}_{n})$ is the response spectrum, in the sense of the median of the maxima, determined by the Rice formulae for the PSD ${G}_{S}^{i}({\omega}_{n})$ .

In a second stage, one can adjust directly the spectral content of the time signal or of a set of signals. This is the approach generally adopted in the literature, for example [bib16]. In this approach, less rigorous from the point of view of signal processing and of stochastic processes, one generates a signal, one computes its response spectrum, and one iterates directly on its spectral content. One uses the same formula as before (expression (23)), which leads to:

${A}_{S}^{i+1}({\omega}_{n})={A}_{S}^{i}({\omega}_{n}){S}_{a}({\omega}_{n})/{S}_{a}^{i}({\omega}_{n})$ ,                             (24)

but where the spectrum ${S}_{a}^{i}({\omega}_{n})$ at iteration $i$ is the response spectrum determined from the accelerogram at iteration $i$ and ${A}_{S}^{i}({\omega}_{n})$ denotes the spectral content of the signal.

**Note: From a mathematical point of view, the spectral content of a realisation of the process (or of the accelerogram) corresponds to the realisation of the incremental process* $mathit{dZ}$ *defined in §4. It is the latter which is updated in the iterations in order to improve the fit to the target SRO.**

If one wishes to generate several signals compatible in median with a target SRO, one must iterate on the spectral content of this set of signals (the sample). Considering the case where one wishes to generate $L$ signals. The response spectrum to be computed at each iteration is the median of the SROs of the $L$ signals. It is then this median SRO which is used in formula (24) in place of ${S}_{a}^{i}({\omega}_{n})$ . This makes it possible in particular to obtain a set of independent (and uncorrelated) signals. This property is not guaranteed by generating and carrying out the iterations on distinct signals.

### 3.2 Non-stationary parametric model for the generation of signals compatible with a spectrum

The PSD compatible with the spectrum identified according to formula (21) does not make it possible to generate non-stationary signals with evolutive frequency content. For that, one must return to a parametric model in which the fundamental angular frequency ${\omega}_{0}$ is one of the parameters. Now, any physical (Gaussian) process can be described by a rational spectral density. One therefore works with a rational PSD which constitutes a generalisation of the Kanai-Tajimi model.

More precisely, the model adopted is the following:

${S}_{\mathit{FR}}(\omega ,t)=\frac{({R}_{0}^{2}+{R}_{2}^{2}{\omega}^{2})}{({({\omega}_{0}{(t)}^{2}-{\omega}^{2})}^{2}+4{\xi}_{0}^{2}{\omega}_{0}{(t)}^{2}{\omega}^{2})}$                             (25)

The parameters of this model, ${\omega}_{0}$ , ${\xi}_{0}$ and ${R}_{0}$ , ${R}_{1}$ , are identified by an optimisation algorithm so as to minimise the error between the PSD compatible with the spectrum, ${G}^{S}$ , (equation (21)) and the rational PSD, ${S}_{\mathrm{FR}}$ (equation (25)). The Simplex method is used by GENE_ACCE_SEISME. To initialise the algorithm, one chooses the parameters corresponding to the classical Kanai-Tajimi model. Furthermore, the Cough & Penzien filter described in §2 is applied.

**Note** : The formulation (25) has been retained because the *introduction of additional parameters, by increasing the order of the polynomials, does not lead to better results.*

The rational PSD makes it possible to generate signals whose mean spectrum is close to the target. In the general case, there remains a difference due to having chosen a parametric model. In order to match the target spectrum as well as possible, one postulates the equivalence of the energy of the stationary process over the duration of the strong phase and the non-stationary process:

${\int}_{0}^{{T}_{\mathrm{SM}}}{S}_{\mathrm{FRC}}(\omega ,t)\mathrm{dt}={G}^{S}(\omega ){\int}_{0}^{{T}_{\mathrm{SM}}}\mathrm{dt}$                             (26)

where ${S}_{\mathrm{FRC}}(\omega ,t)={S}_{0}(\omega ){S}_{\mathrm{RC}}(\omega ,t)$ is the rational PSD compatible with the spectrum. If one considers a non-evolutive rational PSD this amounts to applying a "corrective" term, ${S}_{0}(\omega )={G}^{S}(\omega )/{S}_{\mathrm{RC}}(\omega )$ , which expresses the difference between the PSD compatible with the SRO and the evolutive rational PSD model. It is this latter expression which is implemented by choosing the reference value at the instant ${t}_{m}$ (middle of the strong phase): ${S}_{\mathrm{RC}}(\omega ,{t}_{m})$ .

The compatible rational model is thus summarised:

- The process $Y$ is characterised by the rational-type PSD (FR) corrected so as to adjust as well as possible to the PSD compatible with the SRO.
- The central frequency ${\omega}_{0}$ of the evolutive $\mathit{KT}$ PSD is a function of time
- One applies a temporal modulation function of the amplitude $q(t)$ (cf. Section 1) in order to obtain the evolutive PSD (frequency content and amplitude) ${S}_{X}$ .

### 3.3 Introduction of the natural variability of the signals (record-to-record variability)

According to the attenuation laws, the spectral accelerations are assumed to follow a log-normal law. Consequently, the response spectra of the ground motion can be modelled by log-normal vectors. To facilitate the notations and the mathematical manipulations, one reduces oneself to Gaussian vectors by considering rather the log-spectra $l{S}_{a}$ . The components of this vector are the spectral accelerations at the frequencies $\omega ={\omega}_{1,}\mathrm{...},{\omega}_{N}$ . Thus, still according to the attenuation laws (for example [bib12]), one has $\%{S}_{a}({\omega}_{n})=f({\omega}_{n},x)+{ϵ}_{n}\sigma ({\omega}_{n})$ where x denotes the set of parameters characterising the seismic scenario (magnitude, distance etc.) and ${\epsilon}_{n}$ are standardised Gaussian random variables. This can also be written in the form: ${S}_{a}({\omega}_{n})=\bar{{S}_{a}}({\omega}_{n},x){\alpha}_{n}$ where $\bar{{S}_{a}}({\omega}_{n},x)={e}^{f({\omega}_{n},x)}$ are the median spectral accelerations and ${\alpha}_{n}={e}^{\sigma ({\omega}_{n}){\epsilon}_{n}}$ are log-normal random variables of unit median. This simple model neglects the correlations between the spectral accelerations. The correlation-coefficient model developed by Baker [bib3] from the American NGA databases [bib12, bib14] makes it possible to introduce this correlation.

Thus, for a given set of frequencies, the log-spectrum is a correlated Gaussian vector, of mean $\mu$ and of covariance matrix $\Sigma$ . The latter expresses the correlation between the different spectral accelerations. The mean vector $\mu$ contains the spectral log-accelerations given by the attenuation laws, $f({\omega}_{n},x)$ , whereas the covariance matrix can be constructed from the standard deviations $\sigma ({\omega}_{n})$ and the correlation coefficients ${\rho}_{ij}$ according to the relation:

${\Sigma}_{ij}={\rho}_{ij}\sigma ({\omega}_{i})\sigma ({\omega}_{j})$                                 (27),

This same log-normal distribution can be introduced into the PSD model in order to simulate accelerograms whose SROs respect the distribution of the target. In the manner of the spectral accelerations, one models the PSD by a random vector ${S}_{\mathit{FRC}}=({S}_{\mathit{FRC}}({\omega}_{1}),\mathrm{...},{S}_{\mathit{FRC}}({\omega}_{N}))$ of median ${\bar{S}}_{\mathit{FRC}}$ . The terms of this "random" PSD are written:

${S}_{\mathrm{FRC}}({\omega}_{n})={\alpha}_{n}^{2}{\bar{S}}_{\mathrm{FRC}}({\omega}_{n})$                                 (28)

where $\alpha =({\alpha}_{1,}\mathrm{...},{\alpha}_{N})$ is a correlated log-normal random vector, of unit median and of log-covariance matrix $\Sigma$ .

The choice of using, for the PSD, the same distribution (log-normal) as that observed for the SRO is justified by the fact that, for low dampings ${\xi}_{0}$ , one has the following approximation:

${S}_{a}^{2}({\omega}_{n},{\xi}_{0})\approx {\omega}_{n}^{4}{\eta}_{{T}_{\mathit{SM}},p=0,5}^{2}\frac{\pi {S}_{Y}({\omega}_{n})}{2{\xi}_{0}{\omega}_{n}^{3}}$

where ${S}_{a}({\omega}_{n},{\xi}_{0})$ is a target spectrum and ${S}_{Y}({\omega}_{n})$ the PSD compatible with the latter. This indicates a proportional relation between the PSD and the square of the SRO. This relation suggests that the PSD compatible with the spectra must follow, up to a factor, the same distribution as the target SRO. Whence relation (28).

**Note** : The procedure for the generation of accelerograms whose median spectrum as well as the fractiles are compatible with the distribution of the target SRO (given by the attenuation laws) is described in the reference [bib18].

### 3.4 Simulation of seismic signals compatible with an SRO: functionalities implemented in GENE_ACCE_SEISME

For the generation of seismic signals "compatible with a spectrum", the following three configurations can be considered:

1. Generation of a distinct accelerogram compatible with the target SRO: one carries out iterations on the spectral content of the accelerogram using formula (24).
2. Generation of accelerograms whose median spectrum is compatible with the target SRO: the median spectrum is considered for the iterations according to formula (24).
3. Generation of accelerograms compatible with the (log-normal) distribution of the SRO: one supplies the median spectrum and the spectrum at one sigma, namely the 84% fractile; this makes it possible to introduce the variability of the natural accelerograms (record-to-record variability) (§3.3).

GENE_ACCE_SEISME makes it possible to generate signals according to these three criteria. As for point 2, it is possible to determine the compatible PSD and then to generate seismic signals without iterations on the spectral content. If the sample of generated signals is sufficiently large and if the target SRO is a physical spectrum, then the median spectrum is close to the target. For non-physical response spectra, which have been broadened and have undergone other adjustment treatments, one can still proceed to iterations; the criterion then bears on the median spectrum. The same correction factor will be applied to all the accelerograms generated. A larger sample in general makes it possible to obtain a good fit to the SRO with fewer iterations.

When one integrates the generated signals in order to obtain the displacement, one can sometimes observe a divergent behaviour: the displacements do not vanish at the end of the earthquake. In order to avoid this type of problem, it is sufficient to filter the low frequencies of the acceleration signals (high-pass filter). This makes it possible to remove a possible drift in displacement. The choice of the filter frequency is made by FREQ_FILTRE.

### 3.5 Kanai-Tajimi PSD model in agreement with a target SRO

Alternatively, one can estimate the parameters of the Kanai-Tajimi PSD, namely the natural frequency ${\omega}_{0}$ and the reduced damping ${\xi}_{0}$ , from the moments of the PSD compatible with the spectrum: ${\lambda}_{i}={\int}_{-\infty }^{+\infty }∣\omega ∣{}^{i}{G}^{S}(\omega )d\omega$ . This makes it possible to construct a non-stationary earthquake model (non-stationary in amplitude and in frequency content) by introducing the evolution of the central frequency.

One must then identify the parameters ${\omega}_{0}$ and ${\xi}_{0}$ so that the moments of order 1 and 2 of the Kanai-Tajimi PSD and of the PSD ${G}^{S}(\omega )$ , compatible with the SRO, are close or coincide. In practice, it is appropriate to identify ${\omega}_{0}$ and ${\xi}_{0}$ such that the central frequency ${\nu}_{0}^{+}$ and the bandwidth $\delta$ of the PSDs, defined in Section 3.1, coincide. These two parameters are illustrated in Figure 1 and can be computed from the moments by the classical Rice formulae of expression (17). To determine these quantities, one must compute the moments, which can be carried out with *Code_Aster* with the aid of the operator POST_DYNA_ALEA. The identified natural angular frequency ${\omega}_{0}$ and reduced damping ${\xi}_{0}$ are then used to construct the earthquake model with GENE_ACCE_SEISME.

**Note** : *For the displacement response of an oscillator to a white noise, one knows analytical expressions of the parameters* ${\omega}_{0}$ *and* ${\xi}_{0}$ *as a function of the moments and therefore of the bandwidth and of the central frequency. These expressions also apply to the Kanai-Tajimi PSD, if the damping is low. The Kanai-Tajimi PSD corresponds indeed to the (absolute) acceleration response of an oscillator subjected to a white noise and not to the displacement response.*

Thus, if the damping is low, the natural frequency of the Kanai-Tajimi PSD can be taken equal to the central frequency. But the approximation is in general not very good. Studies have shown that a better approximation can be obtained by taking the frequency corresponding to the centre of gravity of the positive part of the PSD.

*Let us recall that the central frequency* ${\nu}_{0}^{+}$ *characterises* *the frequency where the energy of the process is concentrated. In the time domain, it* *corresponds to the rate of zero crossings with positive slope of the process. It can also be determined from an accelerogram by counting positive crossings through zero.*

## 4 Case of vector processes

### 4.1 2D and 3D seismic signals

Knowing the correlation coefficient r, it is possible to construct a spectral-density matrix ${S}_{X}(\omega ,t)\in {\mathit{Mat}}_{ℂ}(M,M)$ describing a 2D or 3D vector process from the scalar PSD ${S}_{X}(\omega ,t)\in ℝ$ . In the case of two horizontal components, $M=2$ , the PSD matrix is written:

${S}_{X}(\omega ,t)=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{S}_{X}(\omega ,t)& \rho \\ \rho & {S}_{X}(\omega ,t)\end{array}\right]$

For the earthquake, the vertical component is generally considered uncorrelated with the two horizontal components. GENE_ACCE_SEISME makes it possible to generate 2D signals with correlated horizontal components (defined by the correlation coefficient COEF_CORR, which may be zero) and a vertical component uncorrelated with the other two, but to which the horizontal/vertical ratio applies.

### 4.2 Seismic field variable in space

Within the framework of a time-history study with a seismic excitation variable in space, the seismic motion is described by a space-time field. After discretisation, the problem reduces to the simulation of a vector process, described by its matrix PSD as for the 2D problem.

In the literature, coherence functions $\gamma$ are proposed in order to define the spatial correlation of the seismic motion. Two types of coherence functions are available for the operator GENE_ACCE_SEISME: the exponential coherence function of Mita & Luco and the empirical function of Abrahamson. One finds more details on the coherence functions in the documentation of the operator DYNA_ISS_VARI, in particular R4.05.04. One constructs a spectral-density matrix ${S}_{X}(\omega ,t)\in {\mathit{Mat}}_{ℂ}(M,M)$ , where $M$ is the number of nodes for which the field must be evaluated. The components of this matrix are written:

${S}_{ij}(\omega ,t)=\gamma (\omega ,{d}_{ij}){S}_{X}(\omega ,t)$

where ${d}_{ij}$ is the horizontal distance between two nodes $i$ and $j$ of the mesh.

## 5 Simulation of trajectories of the process

The classical method for the simulation of a stationary, centred Gaussian stochastic process rests on the spectral representation of processes, cf. for example [bib11]. The spectral representation as well as the numerical simulation algorithm are recalled in what follows.

One treats the general case of a vector Gaussian process $Y(t)\in {ℝ}^{M},t\in [0,T]$ characterised by its matrix PSD ${S}_{Y}(\omega )\in {\mathrm{Mat}}_{ℂ}(M,M)$ . This method can be extended to the case of non-stationary processes with evolutive PSD ${S}_{X}(\omega ,t)\in {\mathit{Mat}}_{ℂ}(M,M)$ if the process is slowly varying.

### 5.1 Discretisation

The numerical simulation requires the discretisation of the time domain. One uses a time discretisation with constant step $\Delta t$ . The cut-off frequency is deduced directly from this value: ${\Omega}_{C}=\pi /(\Delta t).$ For a discretisation by N time steps, one denotes by ${t}_{j}=j\Delta t,j=0,\mathrm{...}N-1$ the time sampling points. Furthermore, one defines the frequency step by $\Delta \omega =2{\Omega}_{C}/N$ and the duration of the time signal is $T=\Delta t(N-1)$ .

The spectral densities are defined on the interval $F=[-{\Omega}_{C},+{\Omega}_{C}]$ and the frequency discretisation points are chosen as the mid-points of the bins:: ${\omega}_{j}=-{\Omega}_{C}+(0.5+j)\Delta \omega ,j=0,\mathrm{...}N-1$ .

### 5.2 Spectral representation of stationary processes

The spectral representation theorem (cf. [bib11]) states that there exists a vector stochastic process $dZ(\omega )\in {\mathrm{ℂ}}^{M}$ with orthogonal increments such that:

$Y(t)={\int}_{{\mathrm{ℝ}}^{M}}{\mathrm{e}}^{i\omega t}dZ(\omega )$                                     (29)

This process, called the spectral process associated with $Y$ , satisfies:

$E(dZ(\omega )dZ{(\omega ')}^{\ast })=\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}0& \mathit{si}\omega \ne \omega '\\ {S}_{Y}(\omega )d\omega & \mathit{si}\omega =\omega '\end{array}$ ,                    (30)

where $E$ is the mathematical-expectation operator and $dZ$ is a process with orthogonal increments.

The spectral-density matrix ${S}_{Y}(\omega )$ being, by construction, a positive Hermitian matrix for every $\omega \in F$ , there exists a matrix $L(\omega )\in {\mathrm{ℂ}}^{M}$ such that:

${S}_{Y}(\omega )=L(\omega )L{(\omega )}^{\mathrm{\ast }}$             (31)

The matrix $L(\omega )$ can be obtained by Cholesky decomposition if ${S}_{Y}(\omega )$ is positive definite. $L(\omega )$ is then a lower triangular matrix. In the more general case, the decomposition into eigenvectors can be used. If the process is scalar, the problem reduces to the computation of the square root of the PSD. One can then simulate trajectories of the process by the following formula:

$Y(t)=\sqrt{\Delta \omega }\mathrm{\Re }e{\sum}_{j=0}^{N-1}L({\omega}_{j}){\chi }_{j}{\mathrm{e}}^{i{\omega}_{j}t}$                      (32)

where the ${\chi }_{j}$ are complex Gaussian random vectors whose components are independent.

The use of the IFFT makes it possible to reduce considerably the numerical cost of the simulation. Indeed, one can write expression (26) in the form

$Y({t}_{k})=\sqrt{\Delta \omega }\mathrm{\Re }e({V}_{k}{\mathrm{e}}^{-i\pi k(1-1/N)})$                          (33)

where ${V}_{k}$ is computable by IFFT:

${V}_{k}={\sum}_{j=0}^{N-1}L({\omega}_{j}){\chi }_{j}{\mathrm{e}}^{2i\pi kj{N}^{-1}}$

This algorithm can be used for stationary Gaussian processes and for non-stationary ones with separable PSD. In these cases, one obtains the modulated seismic signals by applying the modulation function so that $X(t)=q(t)Y(t)$ . This is carried out by the operator GENE_ACCE_SEISME.

### 5.3 Spectral representation of non-stationary processes with evolutive PSD

The spectral representation still applies to non-stationary processes described by their non-separable evolutive PSD:

$Y(t)={\int}_{{\mathrm{ℝ}}^{M}}{\mathrm{e}}^{i\omega t}dZ(\omega ,t)$                          (34)

under the condition that the PSD evolves slowly with time [bib8,bib6]. There then exists a stochastic process $dZ(\omega ,t)\in {ℂ}^{M}$ with orthogonal increments such that

$E(dZ(\omega ,t)dZ{(\omega ',t)}^{\mathrm{\ast }})=\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}0& \mathit{si}\omega \mathrm{\ne }\omega '\\ {S}_{Y}(\omega ,t)d\omega & \mathit{si}\omega =\omega '\end{array}$                      (35)

where $E$ is the mathematical-expectation operator and $dZ$ is a process with orthogonal increments. One can then simulate trajectories of the process by the formula

$Y(t)=\sqrt{\Delta \omega }\mathrm{\Re }e{\sum}_{j=0}^{N-1}L({\omega}_{j},t){\chi }_{j}{\mathrm{e}}^{i{\omega}_{j}t}$                      (36)

where the ${\chi }_{j}$ are complex Gaussian random vectors whose components are independent. The matrix $L(\omega ,t)$ can still be obtained by Cholesky decomposition if the rank of ${S}_{Y}(\omega ,t)$ is maximal. By contrast, it is not possible to call upon the fast Fourier transform as in the stationary case (cf. equation (33)), which increases the computation times.

One obtains the modulated seismic signals by applying the modulation function so that $X(t)=q(t)Y(t)$ . This is carried out in *Code_Aster* by the operator GENE_ACCE_SEISME.
