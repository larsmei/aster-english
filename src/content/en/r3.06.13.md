# r3.06.13 Mixed interface finite elements for cohesive-zone models (xxx_INTERFACE and xxx_INTERFACE_S)

**Summary:**

This document presents a mixed interface finite element for cohesive-zone models. The corresponding keywords for the modellings in *Code_Aster* are PLAN_INTERFACE, AXIS_INTERFACE,3D_INTERFACE, PLAN_INTERFACE_S, AXIS_INTERFACE_S and 3D_INTERFACE_S.

## 1 Introduction

This cohesive-zone model accounts for the phenomena of crack initiation, of propagation (in a given direction) and of final failure. The mixed interface element is introduced in order to discretise the cracking path, whose degrees of freedom are the displacement of the two lips of the crack, as well as the density of the cohesive forces. The construction of the finite-element model and the integration of the constitutive law CZM_TAC_MIX are detailed. This document is largely inspired by the article of Lorentz 5; the interested reader may refer to it for more details.

## 2 Construction of the mixed interface finite element

### 2.1 Principle of energy minimisation in fracture mechanics

In their approach to fracture mechanics, Francfort and Marigo 5 describe the state of a structure $O$ by a displacement field $u$ which may admit discontinuities $\delta =\left[\left[u\right]\right]$ across the surfaces $\Gamma (u)$ . For a given loading the displacement is characterised by an optimality condition: it corresponds to the local minimum of the potential energy defined as the sum of the elastic strain energy, of the cohesive energy and of the work of the external forces:

$\begin{array}{}{E}_{\text{pot}}(u)={E}_{\text{el}}(u)+{E}_{\text{fr}}(\left[\left[u\right]\right])-{W}_{\text{ext}}(u)\\ {E}_{\text{el}}(u)=\underset{\Omega /\Gamma (u)}{\int}\Phi (\epsilon (u))d\Omega ;{E}_{\text{fr}}(\delta )=\underset{\Gamma (u)}{\int}\Pi (\delta )\text{dS}\end{array}$

where $\epsilon$ denotes the strain tensor, $\Phi$ the (volumetric) strain-energy density and $\Pi$ the (surface) cohesive-energy density. In this formulation, the fracture mechanisms are considered as reversible. Irreversibility will be introduced in the following part, from internal variables. The characteristics of the problem are deduced from the minimisation, namely:

- the prediction of the cracking path, since all the possible discontinuous displacements are taken into account;
- a stress criterion for crack initiation (see 5).
- the cohesive law relates the displacement discontinuity $\delta$ to the traction vector $t$ from the (generalised) derivative of $\Pi$ ;
- the contact conditions are handled via the cohesive energy, to which an indicator function forbidding interpenetration of the lips is added.

However, taking all the possible discontinuous displacements into account leads to numerical difficulties linked to the discretisation of the functional space $\text{BD}(O)$ . In particular, allowing discontinuities across each of the finite elements leads to a mesh dependence of the results. Alternative works are carried out, based on the regularisation of the discontinuities 5 or on mesh adaptation 5. The latter are however limited to surface energies of Griffith type (without cohesive forces) and lead to significant numerical difficulties. This is why a simplification is introduced in the model by considering that the potential displacement-discontinuity surfaces $\Gamma$ are postulated *a priori* and that they no longer depend on $u$ .

### 2.2 Search for a saddle point by a decomposition–coordination method

Despite the postulate on the direction of cracking, the minimisation of the energy is not obvious given the non-differentiability of the surface energy. One relies here on the decomposition–coordination technique introduced in 5, which condenses the non-differentiability to a local level (Gauss points).

#### 2.2.1 Augmented Lagrangian

The relation between the discontinuity field $\delta$ and the displacement field $u$ is introduced explicitly into the minimisation; the total energy E therefore depends explicitly on $u$ and on $\delta$ :

$E(u,\delta )=\underset{\Omega \setminus \Gamma }{\int}\Phi (\epsilon (u))d\Omega -{W}_{\mathrm{ext}}(u)+\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\Pi (\delta )\mathrm{d\Gamma }$

The minimisation of the potential energy is then equivalent to the following constrained minimisation problem (the displacement belonging to the set of kinematically admissible displacements):

$\underset{\begin{array}{}u,\delta \\ \left[\left[u\right]\right]=\delta \end{array}}{\min}E(u,\delta )$

The linear constraint $\left[\left[u\right]\right]=\delta$ is treated by dualisation: a solution $(u,\delta )$ of corresponds to a saddle point $(u,\delta ,\lambda )$ of the following Lagrangian, where $\lambda$ corresponds to the field of Lagrange multipliers:

$L(u,\delta ,\lambda )\underset{\mathrm{déf}}{=}E(u,\delta )+\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\lambda \cdot (\left[\left[u\right]\right]-\delta )\mathrm{d\Gamma }$

Finally, in order to gain coercivity with respect to $\delta$ , which will prove necessary later, a quadratic penalisation term is added, having no influence on the solution since it is equal to zero when the constraint is satisfied. The augmented Lagrangian ${L}_{r}$ , with the penalisation coefficient $r$ , is then written:

${L}_{r}(u,\delta ,\lambda )\underset{\mathrm{déf.}}{=}E(u,\delta )+\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\lambda \cdot (\left[\left[u\right]\right]-\delta )\mathrm{d\Gamma }+\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\frac{r}{2}{(\left[\left[u\right]\right]-\delta )}^{2}\mathrm{d\Gamma }$

#### 2.2.2 Characterisation of the saddle point

The first-order optimality conditions for the augmented Lagrangian are written in the following way:

$\forall \mathrm{\delta \delta }\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\left[t-\lambda -r(\left[\left[u\right]\right]-\delta )\right]\cdot \mathrm{\delta \delta }=0\text{avec}t\in \partial \Pi (\delta )$

$\forall \mathrm{\delta u}\underset{\Omega \setminus \Gamma }{\int}\sigma :\epsilon (\mathrm{\delta u})+\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\left[\lambda +r(\left[\left[u\right]\right]-\delta )\right]\cdot \left[\left[\mathrm{\delta u}\right]\right]={W}_{\text{ext}}(\mathrm{\delta u})\text{avec}\sigma =\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial \epsilon }(\epsilon )$

$\forall \mathrm{\delta \lambda }\underset{\Gamma}{\int}(\left[\left[u\right]\right]-\delta )\cdot \mathrm{\delta \lambda }=0$

The meaning of the subdifferential $\partial \Pi$ is given in part 3. At this stage, one contents oneself with saying that $t\in \partial \Pi (\delta )$ means that $t$ and $\delta$ are related by the cohesive law. The equation therefore gives an interpretation of the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ : apart from the penalisation term, these are the cohesive forces. The equation expresses the equilibrium of the forces in the volume and along the surface of the discontinuity $\Gamma$ . Finally, it imposes the constraint between the displacement field and its discontinuities.

### 2.3 Discretisation of the finite element

Since the trajectory of the crack $\Gamma$ is defined *a priori* and thanks to the small-perturbation assumption, the spatial discretisation of the system can rely on an interface finite element. Suppose that the subdomains ${O}_{-}$ and ${O}_{+}$ (parts of $O$ respectively below and above $\Gamma$ ) are discretised by tetrahedra or hexahedra so that the nodes on each side of $\Gamma$ coincide 1
. In this case, degenerate prisms or hexahedra can be used to discretise $\Gamma$ and to connect the two lips ${\Gamma}_{-}$ and ${\Gamma}_{+}$ of the potential cohesive crack (see Figure 2.3-a).

![Figure](/figures/r3.06.13/10000201000003630000022CB04B2E146FEF762E.png)

**Figure** 2.3-a **: Discretisation by an interface finite element.**

Let a mesh be characterised by the parameter $h$ (for example, the maximum size of the finite elements). In the domain $O$ a quadratic interpolation with classical Lagrange finite elements (continuous P2) is adopted. The discretised space of the **displacement fields** ${U}_{h}$ is written:

${U}_{h}=\left\lbrace u;\forall x\in \Omega u(x)=\left[N(x)\right]\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace \right\rbrace$

where $\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace$ denotes the vector of the nodal displacements and $\left[N\right]$ the matrix of the quadratic shape functions. The trace of the interpolated displacement on ${\Gamma}_{-}$ and ${\Gamma}_{+}$ is also piecewise quadratic and the displacement discontinuity is expressed in the following way:

$\forall x\in \Gamma {u}_{\mid {\Gamma}_{-}}(x)=\left[{N}_{-}(x)\right]\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace ;{u}_{\mid {\Gamma}_{+}}(x)=\left[{N}_{+}(x)\right]\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace$

$\forall x\in \Gamma \left[\left[u(x)\right]\right]=\left[D(x)\right]\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace \text{avec}\left[D(x)\right]\underset{\text{déf.}}{=}\left[{N}_{+}(x)\right]-\left[{N}_{-}(x)\right]$

where $\left[{N}_{-}\right]$ and $\left[{N}_{+}\right]$ correspond respectively to the trace of $\left[N\right]$ on ${\Gamma}_{-}$ and ${\Gamma}_{+}$ and where $\left[D\right]$ denotes the matrix of the quadratic shape functions which interpolates the displacement jump. Note that it may be useful to introduce a rotation into $\left[D\right]$ in order to obtain the components of $\left[\left[u\right]\right]$ in a local coordinate system and thus to distinguish the normal and tangential parts.

The **field of Lagrange multipliers** $\lambda$ is interpolated on $\Gamma$ by piecewise linear shape functions (continuous P1), leading to the following discretised space of Lagrange multipliers ${L}_{h}$ :

${L}_{h}=\left\lbrace \lambda ;\forall x\in \Gamma \lambda (x)=\left[L(x)\right]\left\lbrace \Lambda \right\rbrace \right\rbrace$

where $\left\lbrace \Lambda \right\rbrace$ corresponds to the nodal unknowns of the Lagrange multiplier and $\left[L\right]$ denotes the matrix of the linear shape functions on $\Gamma$ . In this way, the constraint $\left[\left[u\right]\right]=\delta$ imposed by is satisfied only in the weak sense.

Finally, the discretisation of the **discontinuity field** $\delta \in {D}_{h}$ is based on the collocation points on $\Gamma$ , of coordinates ${x}_{g}$ . These points correspond to the Gauss points, of weight ${\omega}_{g}$ , used to compute the integrals. A standard modelling, and a so-called under-integrated modelling, are distinguished here.

- In the standard modelling, the Gauss points number 3 (segments), 6 (triangles) or 9 (quadrilaterals) per element. This corresponds to a discontinuous P2 interpolation of $\delta$ . This choice has the advantage of limiting the risk of appearance of zero pivots in the tangent matrix, but it does not satisfy the LBB condition in the limit of an infinite penalisation parameter $r$ .
- In the under-integrated modelling, the Gauss points number 2 (segments), 3 (triangles) or 4 (quadrilaterals) per element. This corresponds to a discontinuous P1 interpolation of $\delta$ . This choice better satisfies the LBB condition in the limit $r\to \infty$ , but it is liable to give rise to zero pivots in the tangent matrix. These zero pivots appear when the stiffness of the elements in contact with the interface is not sufficient to ensure the coercivity of the formulation, in particular when the interface lies on a plane of symmetry, or between a volume and a structural element (bar, grid or membrane).

The spatial discretisation, the notations as well as the diagrams of the finite elements for the under-integrated modelling are summarised in Figure 2.3-b.

The field $\delta$ disappears from the global formulation thanks to static condensation. Indeed, with the discretisation adopted, the solution amounts to satisfying the cohesive law at each collocation point:

${t}_{g}={\lambda}_{g}+r(\left[\left[{u}_{g}\right]\right]-{\delta}_{g})\in \partial \Pi ({\delta}_{g})\text{avec}\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\left[\left[{u}_{g}\right]\right]=\left[{D}_{g}\right]\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace & ;\left[{D}_{g}\right]=\left[D({x}_{g})\right]\\ {\mu}_{g}=\left[{L}_{g}\right]\left\lbrace \Lambda \right\rbrace & ;\left[{L}_{g}\right]=\left[L({x}_{g})\right]\end{array}$

The integration of the constitutive relation (i.e. the solution of , cf. the following part) makes it possible to compute ${\delta}_{g}$ as a function of $\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace$ and $\left\lbrace \Lambda \right\rbrace$ which is denoted $\delta$ :

${t}_{g}={\lambda}_{g}+r(\left[\left[{u}_{g}\right]\right]-{\delta}_{g})\in \partial \Pi ({\delta}_{g})\iff {\delta}_{g}=\hat{\delta}(\left[\left[{u}_{g}\right]\right],{\lambda}_{g})=\delta (U,\Lambda )$

The penalisation parameter $r$ makes it possible to ensure the uniqueness of whatever $\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace$ and $\left\lbrace \Lambda \right\rbrace$ (cf. the following part). This constitutes a necessary condition for guaranteeing the robustness of the model.

One then has the non-linear system whose unknowns are the nodal displacements $\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace$ and the nodal Lagrange multipliers $\left\lbrace \Lambda \right\rbrace$ :

$\underset{\Omega \setminus \Gamma }{\int}{\left[\nabla N\right]}^{T}\text{:σ}(U)+\sum_{g}{\omega}_{g}{\left[{D}_{g}\right]}^{T}\cdot (\left[{L}_{g}\right]\left\lbrace \Lambda \right\rbrace +r\left[{D}_{g}\right]\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace -r\delta (U,\Lambda ))=\left\lbrace {F}_{\text{ext}}\right\rbrace$

$\sum_{g}{\omega}_{g}{\left[{L}_{g}\right]}^{T}\cdot (\left[{D}_{g}\right]\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace -\delta (U,\Lambda ))=0$

The volume integral and the nodal vector of the external forces are computed in the usual way. Finally, the system is solved simultaneously with respect to $\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace$ and $\left\lbrace \Lambda \right\rbrace$ by means of a (generalised) Newton method, where the tangent operator is symmetric since it corresponds to a search for a saddle point.

This poses a priori no problem for the meshing algorithms, at least for cracks having a simple shape, for example plane.

## 3 Integration of the cohesive law CZM_TAC_MIX

The cohesive behaviour is determined by the cohesive-energy density $\Pi (\delta )$ . Although the variational formulation presented in the previous part is independent of the choice of the cohesive energy, a particular cohesive model is now considered, whose specific features are detailed. Its main characteristics are:

- contact conditions (non-interpenetration of the lips);

- perfect initial adhesion (no regularisation of the surface energy);

- coupling between the failure modes (tension and shear);

- total failure (zero cohesive forces beyond a certain damage threshold);

- irreversibility of the failure.

This means in particular that there is no final friction, nor any distinction between the failure mechanisms in tension and in shear.

**Note:**

*The Code_Aster keyword corresponding to this cohesive law is* *CZM_TAC_MIX. For more details on this law (internal variables, input parameters, consistent tangent matrix) and on other cohesive laws, one may refer to* 5 *(R7.02.11).*

### 3.1 Preliminary notations

Because of the constraint of non-interpenetration of the lips of the crack, the normal direction $n$ for the potential discontinuity surface $\Gamma$ (opening/compression) must be distinguished from the in-plane directions (sliding, shear). To this end, the following notations are introduced, where $v$ represents any vector quantity:

${v}_{n}=v\cdot n;{v}_{\text{//}}=v-{v}_{n}n;{\langle v\rangle }_{+}=\langle {v}_{n}\rangle n+{v}_{\text{//}};{\parallel v\parallel }_{+}={({\langle v\rangle }_{+}\cdot {\langle v\rangle }_{+})}^{1/2}$

and where $\langle .\rangle$ denotes the positive part of a scalar.

### 3.2 Definition of the cohesive law

The cohesive law proposed by Talon and Curnier 5 meets the five points mentioned previously and falls within the framework of the energetic formulation. The responses of the model subjected to pure tension and to pure shear, as well as the stress initiation criterion, are detailed in Figure 3.2-a.

![Figure](/figures/r3.06.13/10000201000002FA000002D6F05F7936ED09CADB.png)

**Figure** 3.2-a **: Responses of the Talon–Curnier law** CZM_TAC_MIX **in pure tension–compression (a), pure shear (b) and stress initiation criterion (c)**

The corresponding cohesive energy is defined as follows:

First, the displacement discontinuities in opening and in shear are gathered into a single scalar ${\delta}_{\text{eq}}$ which measures the amplitude of the discontinuity, thus meeting point 3:

${\delta}_{\text{eq}}=N(\delta )=\sqrt{\delta \cdot \delta }$

Next, irreversibility 3 is taken into account via a new scalar internal variable $\kappa$ which measures the current maximum loading level:

$\kappa (t)=\underset{{t}^{'}<t}{\max}{\delta}_{\text{eq}}({t}^{'})$

The cohesive energy depends on the internal variable $\kappa$ and on the equivalent displacement jump ${\delta}_{\text{eq}}$ . The contact conditions 3 are handled by an indicator function which imposes a positive normal discontinuity ${\delta}_{n}\ge 0$ :

$\Pi (\delta ,\kappa )={I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}({\delta}_{n})+\psi (\max({\delta}_{\text{eq}},\kappa ))$

The function $\psi$ characterises in particular the reaction to a loading in pure mode I. According to 5, $\psi$ must be an increasing and differentiable function, where ${\sigma}^{c}={\psi}^{'}(0)$ defines the critical stress, a parameter of the initiation criterion 3 represented in Figure 3.2-a. Moreover, the stability of the fracture process requires that $\psi$ be concave 5. Finally, the final failure 3 occurs as soon as $\psi$ reaches its upper limit ${G}^{c}$ for a finite value ${\delta}_{\text{eq}}={\delta}^{c}$ , where ${G}^{c}$ is the fracture energy and ${\delta}^{c}$ the critical opening beyond which the cohesive forces vanish.

Thus, the following function $\psi$ is chosen, which corresponds to the responses represented in Figure 3.2-a:

$\psi ({\delta}_{\text{eq}})=\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}{G}^{c}\frac{{\delta}_{\text{eq}}}{{\delta}^{c}}(2-\frac{{\delta}_{\text{eq}}}{{\delta}^{c}})& \text{if}{\delta}_{\text{eq}}\le {\delta}^{c}\\ {G}^{c}& \text{if}{\delta}_{\text{eq}}\ge {\delta}^{c}\end{array}$

with the relation between the material parameters;

${G}^{c}=\frac{1}{2}{\sigma}^{c}{\delta}^{c}$

At this stage, the energy and the cohesive law which derives from it are entirely defined. However, one can explicitly express the relation between the displacement discontinuity $\delta$ and the stress vector $t$ , in condensed manner in the following differential inclusion, where $\partial \Pi$ is the subdifferential of $\Pi$ (see Clarke 5):

$t\in \partial \Pi (\delta )$

For a given value of $\kappa$ , the subdifferential $\partial \Pi (\delta )$ can be interpreted as the cone formed by the set of the slopes of the directional derivatives of $\Pi$ at $\delta$ for all the admissible directions. Mathematically, this is formulated in the following way:

$\partial \Pi (\delta )=\left\lbrace t\in {R}^{3};\forall \upsilon \in {R}^{3}t\cdot \upsilon \le {\Pi}^{°}(\delta ,\upsilon )\right\rbrace$

where ${\Pi}^{°}(\delta ,\upsilon )$ is the directional derivative of $\Pi$ with respect to $\delta$ in the direction $\upsilon$ :

${\Pi}^{°}(\delta ,\upsilon )=\underset{\begin{array}{}\rho \to {0}^{+}\\ d\to \delta \end{array}}{\text{limsup}}\frac{\Pi (d+\rho \upsilon )-\Pi (d)}{\rho}$

This definition coincides with the gradient of $\Pi$ everywhere that the latter is differentiable. According to the definition, the points deserving particular attention are $\delta =0$ , ${\delta}_{n}=0$ and ${\delta}_{\text{eq}}=\kappa$ . One therefore deduces from them the following four cases (illustrated in colour in Figure 3.2-a):

1. Point ($\kappa =0$ and $\delta =0$ ): perfect adhesion, i.e. initiation criterion (pink branch)

$\partial \Pi (\delta )=\left\lbrace t\in {R}^{3};{\parallel t\parallel }_{+}\le {\sigma}^{c}\right\rbrace$

1. Domain where ${\delta}_{\text{eq}}<\kappa$ : return to zero at zero stress (green branch)

$\partial \Pi (\delta )=\left\lbrace {t}_{n}n;{t}_{n}\le 0\text{et}{\delta}_{n}\ge 0\text{et}{t}_{n}{\delta}_{n}=0\right\rbrace$

1. Hypercone ${\delta}_{\text{eq}}=\kappa >0$ : vertical unloading of the stress (orange branch)

$\partial \Pi (\delta )=\left\lbrace {t}_{n}n+\rho \delta ;0\le \rho \le \frac{{\psi}^{'}(\kappa )}{\kappa}\text{et}{t}_{n}\le 0\text{et}{\delta}_{n}\ge 0\text{et}{t}_{n}{\delta}_{n}=0\right\rbrace$

1. Domain where ${\delta}_{\text{eq}}>\kappa$ : damage (blue branch)

> $\partial \Pi (\delta )=\left\lbrace {t}_{n}n+{\psi}^{'}({\delta}_{\text{eq}})\frac{\delta}{{\delta}_{\text{eq}}};{t}_{n}\le 0\text{et}{\delta}_{n}\ge 0\text{et}{t}_{n}{\delta}_{n}=0\right\rbrace$

**Notes:**

- *There exists a domain for the cohesive force, where the discontinuity is zero: this is the initiation criterion represented in* Figure 3.2-a *linked to the non-differentiability of* $\Pi$ *at* $\delta =0$ *. The shape of the domain depends on the expression of* ${\delta}_{\text{eq}}$ *.*
- *The Kuhn–Tucker condition, which appears in , makes it possible to describe the contact conditions (red branch* Figure 3.2-a *). In this case,* ${t}_{n}$ *is negative, and corresponds to a compression whose value is not defined by the cohesive law.*

### 3.3 Numerical integration

According to , the numerical integration of the cohesive law amounts to computing ${\delta}_{g}$ for given values of $\left[\left[{u}_{g}\right]\right]$ and of ${\lambda}_{g}$ , and this for each Gauss point (the index $g$ is now omitted). In fact, it is a characterisation of the following minimum:

$\underset{\delta \in {ℝ}^{3}}{\min}\left[\lambda \cdot (\left[\left[u\right]\right]-\delta )+\frac{r}{2}{(\left[\left[u\right]\right]-\delta )}^{2}+\Pi (\delta ,\kappa )\right]\iff \lambda +r\left[\left[u\right]\right]-r\delta \in \partial \Pi (\delta ,\kappa )$

Moreover, the evolution of the internal variable $\kappa$ (see ) must be taken into account. A time discretisation is then necessary; consider a series of instants ${t}^{0}<{t}^{1}<\dots <{t}^{n}$ and the corresponding quantities ${\lambda}^{n}$ , $\left[\left[{u}^{n}\right]\right]$ , ${\delta}^{n}$ and ${\kappa}^{n}$ . During the solution, the iterative process is divided into two parts:

${\delta}^{n}=\underset{\delta \in {ℝ}^{3}}{\text{argmin}}\left[{\lambda}^{n}\cdot (\left[\left[{u}^{n}\right]\right]-\delta )+\frac{r}{2}{(\left[\left[{u}^{n}\right]\right]-\delta )}^{2}+\Pi (\delta ,{\kappa}^{n-1})\right]$

${\kappa}^{n}=\max({\kappa}^{n-1},{\delta}_{\text{eq}}^{n})$

The second part is trivial; the minimisation therefore amounts to solving the first with $\kappa ={\kappa}^{n-1}$ a known parameter. A graphical interpretation of the differential inclusion is supplied in Figure 3.3-a (pure mode I without unloading): the solution corresponds to the intersection of the linear function $\delta \to \lambda +r\left[\left[u\right]\right]-r\delta$ (with a negative slope given by the penalty coefficient $r$ ) with the graph $\partial \Pi (\delta ,{\kappa}^{n-1})$ .

$r>\underset{x\ge 0}{\max}\mid {\psi}^{''}(x)\mid =\frac{{\sigma}^{c}}{{\delta}^{c}}={H}^{c}$

## 4 Conclusion

An interface finite-element model is proposed in order to model the evolution of cohesive cracks along a predefined path. The latter is compatible with the use of classical volume finite elements. Its unknowns are the nodal displacements on the lips of the cohesive crack as well as the nodal Lagrange multipliers, corresponding to the surface density of the cohesive forces. The Lagrangian of the problem is augmented; this makes it possible to ensure a convexity condition (via the penalisation parameter) which guarantees the uniqueness of the solution during the local integration of the law.

This approach nevertheless presents the following limits and drawbacks:

- The potential trajectories of the crack must be postulated a priori.
- Additional degrees of freedom, corresponding to the cohesive forces, are introduced. However their number remains small since they are limited to the potential trajectories of the crack.
- The introduction of Lagrange multipliers leads to a mixed formulation: the solution of the problem therefore amounts to a search for a saddle point and no longer to that of a minimum as was the case in the initial energetic formulation 5.
- It is necessary to augment the Lagrangian in order to have a local convexity property. This implies the introduction of a penalisation parameter, with no influence on the continuous problem, but which could affect the results of the discretised problem. However, the numerical examples in 5 show that this sensitivity remains weak and disappears with mesh refinement.
- The local integration of the cohesive law relies on the computation of the displacement discontinuities from the cohesive forces. An inverse approach is generally adopted in the literature for interface elements.

However a certain number of interesting properties emerge:

- No regularisation of the cohesive law is necessary, in particular as regards the initial adhesion or the contact condition.
- The choice of a quadratic discretisation for the displacements and a linear one for the Lagrange multipliers makes it possible to satisfy the LBB condition. The latter makes it possible to ensure the convergence of the solution with mesh refinement in terms of displacements and of cohesive forces (see the numerical example in 5).
- The rate of convergence which would be obtained without an interface is not disturbed by the presence of the interface elements (see 5).
- The search for a saddle point leads to a symmetric tangent matrix.
- This interface model is compatible with the usual solution algorithms of *Code_Aster* such as the Newton method, the line search or the control of the loading. This is illustrated by 2D and 3D analyses in 5, thus demonstrating the robustness and the reliability of such a model.
