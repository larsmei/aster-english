# r5.06.01 Model reduction in linear and non-linear dynamics: RITZ method

**Summary:**

This document presents the principle of model reduction by projection onto a reduced basis (Ritz method).

The basis most commonly used is the modal basis.

The truncation problems due to the use of a reduced basis are mentioned. Truncation corrections are proposed.

The description and the properties of the algorithms for solving the system of second-order differential equations obtained in transient analysis are presented in the document [R5.06.04](doc:r5.06.04).

## 1 Introduction

Starting from a description of the geometry and of the materials of the structures, the finite-element method makes it possible to create an accurate and reliable model, but of large dimensions. In the case of a dynamics problem, one wishes to compute the response of a system for different time instants (transient analysis) or for different frequencies (harmonic analysis). The size of the finite-element model obtained is often irreconcilable with the number of analyses required in order to obtain all the desired results.

For a restricted set of dynamic loadings, there generally exists a subspace of small dimension making it possible to describe the dynamic behaviour of the structure under specific loadings.

The projection of the model onto a restricted basis is called the Ritz method or the Rayleigh-Ritz method.

This document contains the following points:

- a presentation of the Ritz methods, their use in the linear case,
- a detail of the possible truncation corrections,
- the generalisation to the non-linear case of the Ritz methods,
- two simple illustrative examples.

## 2 Linear Ritz reduction methods

### 2.1 General description

#### 2.1.1 Continuous formulation

The Ritz method consists in projecting the displacement onto a restricted basis of functions satisfying the kinematic conditions of the problem:

$\tilde{u}(M,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\eta}_{i}(t){\psi}_{i}(M)$                             eq 2.1.1-1

The displacement is described by a series of independent shapes $\left\lbrace {\psi}_{i}(M);i=1\dots n\right\rbrace$ multiplied by amplitudes that are functions of time $\left\lbrace {\eta}_{i}(t);i=1\dots n\right\rbrace$ .

The difficulty consists in defining this family of shapes $\left\lbrace {\psi}_{i}(M);i=1\dots n\right\rbrace$ which, unlike the shape functions of the finite-element method, are non-zero over the greater part of the structure.

The quality of the approximation is linked to the fact that the displacements obtained have a good approximation in the subspace generated by $\text{Vect}\left\lbrace {\psi}_{i}(M),i=1\dots n\right\rbrace$ *.*

**Projection onto a modal basis**

It is known that the eigenmodes $\left\lbrace {\varphi}_{i}(M);i=1\dots \infty \right\rbrace$ generate the space of the kinematically admissible fields. The displacement is decomposed as:

$u(M,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}{\eta}_{i}(t){\varphi}_{i}(M)$                             eq 2.1.1-2

The option most commonly used for the Ritz method then consists in taking as projection basis the first $n$ modes:

$\tilde{u}(M,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\tilde{\eta}}_{i}(t){\varphi}_{i}(M)$                             eq 2.1.1-3

The displacement obtained is an approximation of the real displacement.

It may be of interest to add to the first $n$ modes other shapes (see [§2.6.2]).

#### 2.1.2 Finite-element approximation

In the case of an approximation of the displacement by finite elements, the displacement is already approximated in the space of the shape functions:

${u}^{h}(M,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\text{Nh}}{q}_{i}(t){N}_{i}(M)$                                 eq 2.1.2-1

$U$ denotes the vector of the degrees of freedom of the displacement: $U(t)=\left[{q}_{1}(t),{q}_{2}(t),\dots {q}_{\text{Nh}}(t)\right]$ ;

**Ritz method in finite dimension**

If $n<\text{Nh}$ , the Ritz method applied to the field $u(M,t)$ then comes as a second approximation:

$\tilde{U}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\eta}_{i}(t){\psi}_{i}$                                     eq 2.1.2-2

with $\left\lbrace {\psi}_{i,i=1\dots n}\right\rbrace$ the basis of the $n$ independent and kinematically admissible vectors. One sets

$\Psi =\left[{\psi}_{1},{\psi}_{2},{\psi}_{3,\dots },{\psi}_{n}\right]$ . Whence the matrix writing: $U=\Psi \eta$                     eq 2.1.2-3

### 2.2 Projection onto a reduced basis

Consider the following differential system obtained by a finite-element method:

$\mathrm{M}\ddot{U}+\mathrm{C}\dot{U}+\mathrm{K}U=\mathrm{F}\text{}U\in {R}^{\text{Nh}}$                         eq 2.2-1

The solution sought is in the form [eq 2.1.2-3]. Considering the same form for the virtual displacement, one obtains:

${\Psi}^{T}M\psi \ddot{\eta}+{\Psi}^{T}C\Psi \dot{\eta}+{\Psi}^{T}K\Psi \eta ={\Psi}^{T}F\text{}\eta \in {R}^{n}$                     eq 2.2-2

where: $\eta$ is the vector of the generalised displacements, $\stackrel{ˉ}{K}={\Psi}^{T}K\Psi \text{et}\stackrel{ˉ}{M}={\Psi}^{T}M\Psi$ are called respectively generalised stiffness and mass matrices.

The system [eq 2.2-2] is generally a coupled differential system; the generalised matrices of which it is composed are in the general case full, even if initially the matrices $M$ and $K$ were sparse. The particular structure is therefore lost, in favour of a much more reduced problem size $n\times n$ .

In the general case, the system [eq 2.2-2] supplies only an approximate solution of the system [eq 2.2-1]. The error that is committed is called the truncation error.

One has no information on the value of this error. It can be very large if the projection subspace is poorly chosen. One only knows that this error decreases when the size of the projection basis increases.

If one has a priori information on the form of the solution, the projection basis can be chosen efficiently so as to minimise this error. For example, if one knows that the solution consists only of rigid-body motions, the dimension of the space can be restricted to 6.

In what follows, the eigenmode basis is chosen as the projection basis.

### 2.3 Projection onto a modal basis

**Eigenmodes**

The modes are defined as the pairs $\left\lbrace {({\omega}^{{h}_{i}},{\Phi}^{{h}_{i}})}_{i=1\dots \text{Nh}}\right\rbrace$ solutions of the equation:

$(K-{\omega}^{2}M)\Phi =0$                                 eq 2.3-1

**Note:**

*It is advisable to check that the modes computed by finite-element approximation are sufficiently representative:* $({\omega}^{{h}_{i}},{\Phi}^{{h}_{i}})\approx ({\omega}_{i},{\phi }_{i})$ *.* *One may consider that the finite-element approximation is correct when the modal deformed shapes have a wavelength greater than the size of the mesh cells of the mesh (the notion of wavelength is a generalisation of the notion defined on the wave equation; it can be defined as twice the length between two nodes of the modal deformed shape).*

*In what follows, the index* $h$ *corresponding to the finite-element approximation is omitted.*

**Rayleigh quotient**: energy interpretation

The angular frequencies and eigenforms can be defined as the solutions of the following minimisation problem *:*

$\forall i\in \left[1,\text{Nh}\right]:{\Phi}_{i}$ minimises, in the subspace ${R}^{\text{Nh}}-\text{Vect}\left\lbrace {\Phi}_{j},j\in \left[0,i-1\right]\right\rbrace$ , the functional:

$R(X)=\frac{{X}^{t}\text{KX}}{{X}^{t}\text{MX}}\text{on}\text{pose}:$ ${\omega}_{i}^{2}=\frac{{\Phi}_{{i}^{t}}K{\Phi}_{i}}{{\Phi}_{{i}^{t}}M{\Phi}_{i}}=R({\Phi}_{i})$                 eq 2.3-2

**Reduction method**

A reduction method very widely employed for linear problems is the modal recombination method. It consists in choosing as projection basis the first $n$ eigenmodes of the structure $\left\lbrace {\Phi}_{i,i=1\dots n}\right\rbrace$ *.*

$\tilde{U}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\eta}_{i}(t){\Phi}_{i}$                                     eq 2.3-3

Consider again the following differential system:

$M\ddot{U}+C\dot{U}+KU=F\text{}U\in {R}^{\text{Nh}}$                             eq 2.3-4

The eigenmodes $\left\lbrace {\Phi}_{i,i=1\dots \text{Nh}}\right\rbrace$ have the property of being $M$- and $K$-orthogonal, that is to say that one has the following relations:

$\begin{array}{}{\Phi}_{i}^{T}M{\Phi}_{j}={m}_{i}{\delta}_{ij}\\ {\Phi}_{i}^{T}K{\Phi}_{j}={k}_{i}{\delta}_{ij}\end{array}$

$\delta$ is the KRONECKER symbol

${m}_{i}$ is called the modal mass or generalised mass of mode $i$

${k}_{i}$ is called the modal stiffness or generalised stiffness of mode $i$

The matrices of $M$ and $K$ projected onto the eigenmode basis are therefore diagonal; this is one of the advantages that motivated the use of the modal basis as a projection basis. The system [eq 2.3‑4] projected onto the basis of the first eigenmodes of the system is written:

$(\begin{array}{ccc}\setminus & 0& 0\\ 0& {m}_{i}& 0\\ 0& 0& \setminus \end{array})\ddot{\eta}+{\Phi}^{T}C\Phi \dot{\eta}+(\begin{array}{ccc}\setminus & 0& 0\\ 0& {k}_{i}& 0\\ 0& 0& \setminus \end{array})\eta ={\Phi}^{T}{F}_{\text{ext}}$                 eq 2.3-5

The projection of the matrix $C$ has no reason, in all generality, to be diagonal as well. If the system is strongly damped (presence of dampers on the structure), this matrix will not be diagonal.

**Note:**

*Unlike what many software packages do, Code_Aster makes it possible in this case to integrate the system of coupled modal equations without diagonalisation of the generalised damping matrix. The integration method is in this case an implicit NEWMARK method or an explicit EULER method.*

On the other hand, if the only damping coming into play is a structural damping (internal dissipation of the material for a homogeneous structure), it is then legitimate to make the assumption of proportional damping, also called the BASILE assumption; in this case $C$ is expressed as a linear combination of $M$ and $K$ (RAYLEIGH damping), and its projection onto the eigenmodes is diagonal (cf. doc [R5.05.04](doc:r5.05.04) on the modelling of damping).

In this case, the system [eq 2.3-4] splits into p decoupled linear differential equations of the second order. The response of the system is then the recombination of the response of p simple oscillators associated with the eigenmodes, whence the expression "modal superposition" commonly used.

Each differential equation is written ${m}_{i}$ :

${m}_{i}{\ddot{\eta}}_{i}+{c}_{i}{\dot{\eta}}_{i}+{k}_{i}{\eta}_{i}={f}_{i}$                                 eq 2.3-6

or again, dividing by the modal mass:

${\ddot{\eta}}_{i}+2{\xi}_{i}{\omega}_{i}{\dot{\eta}}_{i}+{\omega}_{i}^{2}{\eta}_{i}=\frac{{f}_{i}}{{m}_{i}}$                             eq 2.3-7

with:

${\xi}_{i}\text{amortissement modal réduit}=\frac{{c}_{i}}{{c}_{\text{critique}}}=\frac{{c}_{i}}{2{m}_{i}.{\omega}_{i}}$

This equation can be solved very simply in the frequency domain:

${\stackrel{ˆ}{\eta}}_{i}=\frac{{\stackrel{ˆ}{\mathrm{f}}}_{i}(\omega )}{{m}_{i}.(-{\omega}^{2}+2.{\xi}_{i}{\omega}_{i}\omega +{\omega}_{i}^{2})}$                             eq 2.3-8

where $\stackrel{ˆ}{}$ represents the FOURIER transform and $\omega$ the excitation frequency.

Particular numerical methods such as the DUHAMEL integral make it possible to take this expression from the frequency domain to the time domain. (see for example doc [R5.05.01](doc:r5.05.01) on a time-integration method).

### 2.4 Modal truncation error

In the case of modal recombination with proportional damping, the truncation error committed by projecting onto the basis of the first eigenmodes of the system can be brought out. Indeed, if one considers the complete basis of the n eigenmodes of the discretised problem, there is equivalence between the initial problem and the projected problem. Therefore the exact solution of the problem discretised by finite elements is written:

$U=\sum_{i}^{\text{Nh}}{\eta}_{i}{\Phi}_{i}$

where the generalised coordinates are the solution of:

${\ddot{\eta}}_{i}+2{\xi}_{i}{\omega}_{i}{\dot{\eta}}_{i}+{\omega}_{i}^{2}{\eta}_{i}=\frac{{f}_{i}}{{m}_{i}}$

the summation extending over **all the eigenmodes** of the system (of finite size).

Solving the problem with a reduced number of eigenmodes, $n<\text{Nh}$ . The solution obtained is the following:

$\tilde{U}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\eta}_{i}{\Phi}_{i}$

The error committed by truncating the basis of representation of the solution is therefore:

$E=U-\tilde{U}=\sum_{i=n+1}^{\text{Nh}}{\eta}_{i}{\Phi}_{i}$                                     eq 2.4-1

In the frequency domain the expression of the error is:

${\stackrel{ˆ}{E}}_{(\omega )}=\stackrel{ˆ}{U}-\stackrel{ˆ}{\tilde{U}}=\sum_{i=n+1}^{\text{Nh}}\frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{t}{\stackrel{ˆ}{F}}_{(\omega )}}{{m}_{i}}.\frac{1}{{\omega}_{i}^{2}-{\omega}^{2}+\mathrm{2j}{\xi}_{i}\omega {\omega}_{i}}.{\Phi}_{i}$             eq 2.4-2

the summation is carried out over all the neglected modes of the system.

Let us study the relative response $\eta /{\eta}_{\text{statique}}$ of an oscillator to a purely sinusoidal excitation of variable frequency (diagram below), with ${\eta}_{\text{statique}}$ the coefficients of the static response corresponding to a static force. Three intervals can be distinguished in the spectrum, in which the oscillator has a different behaviour. At low frequency $(\omega \text{<<}{\omega}_{0})$ the oscillator has a static response. Around ${\omega}_{0}$ the oscillator has a dynamic response (amplification of the mode), and at high frequency the oscillator responds in an inertial manner ($\frac{1}{{\omega}^{2}}$ predominant term).

![Figure](/figures/r5.06.01/100009940000300F00001AC5679736D26C6FF589.svg)

Suppose that the excitation of the system, defined by the vector $F(\omega )$ , is narrow-band, in particular that it is zero for frequencies higher than a given ${\omega}_{\max}$ .

In this case, in order to represent correctly the response of the linear system, all the modes having an angular frequency lower than ${\omega}_{\max}$ must assuredly be taken into account, because these will respond dynamically to the excitation.

On the other hand, the modes such that ${\omega}_{i}\text{>>}{\omega}_{\max}$ nevertheless have a static contribution to the response of the system. It is often these modes that are not taken into account.

By making a limited expansion in $\omega$ in the neighbourhood of $0$ . One obtains the principal part of the error, which is:

${\stackrel{ˆ}{E}}_{(\omega )}=\stackrel{ˆ}{U}-\stackrel{ˆ}{\tilde{U}}=\sum_{i=p+1}^{n}\frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}.{\stackrel{ˆ}{F}}_{(\omega )}}{{k}_{i}}.(1-2j{\xi}_{i}\frac{\omega}{{\omega}_{i}}+0(\frac{\omega}{{\omega}_{i}})).{\Phi}_{i}$             eq 2.4-3

The error is all the smaller as the generalised stiffnesses of the neglected modes are large. In principle, therefore, all the most flexible modes will have to be taken until the residual flexibility of an additional mode is negligible in relative value with respect to the sum of the flexibilities already taken into account:

$\frac{1}{{k}_{n+1}}\text{<<}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{{k}_{i}}$

However, it is observed that by neglecting the high-frequency modes a systematic error is committed on the response of the system (even at low frequency). There exist different possibilities, which we shall now detail, for correcting the response in the range $\left[0,{\omega}_{\max}\right]$ in which the modes have been chosen.

### 2.5 Corrections of the modal truncation

In order to remedy the truncation problem due to the neglected modes, one must try to estimate their effect in the frequency domain $\left[0,{\omega}_{\max}\right]$ of interest. We have seen that the neglected modes having an eigen angular frequency such that ${\omega}_{i}\text{>>}{\omega}_{\max}$ have a so-called static contribution to the response of the system in the domain $\left[0,{\omega}_{\max}\right]$ . The correction techniques consist in computing this static contribution.

#### 2.5.1 A posteriori static correction

The truncation error, considering only the static response of the neglected modes (inverse transform of the principal part of the error), is:

${E}_{(t)}=U-\tilde{U}\approx \sum_{i=n+1}^{\text{Nh}}\frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}.{F}_{(t)}}{{k}_{i}}.{\Phi}_{i}$                                 eq 2.5.1-1

But a priori the neglected modes as well as their generalised stiffnesses are unknown. On the other hand, one knows how to determine the complete static response of the system to a loading $F$ ; the latter is:

$U={K}^{-1}.{F}_{(t)}\approx \sum_{i=1}^{\text{Nh}}\frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}.{F}_{(t)}}{{k}_{i}}.{\Phi}_{i}$

The correction that must be applied is therefore:

$\sum_{i=n+1}^{\text{Nh}}\frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}.{F}_{(t)}}{{k}_{i}}.{\Phi}_{i}\approx {K}^{-1}.{F}_{(t)}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}.{F}_{(t)}}{{k}_{i}}.{\Phi}_{i}$

The corrected solution of the response of the system is therefore:

$U=\tilde{U}+E\approx \tilde{U}+{K}^{-1}.{F}_{(t)}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}.{F}_{(t)}}{{k}_{i}}.{\Phi}_{i}$                     eq 2.5.1-2

This correction is called a posteriori, because it does not intervene in the dynamic solution of the linear system and may be computed only much later. If ${F}_{(t)}$ decomposes into $k$ products of functions of time by functions of the space coordinates, this correction requires a factorisation of $K$ and $k$ solutions.

This method has the advantage of not increasing the number of vectors taken into account in the basis. This method is applicable in the case of a narrow-band excitation, or at least one having a known cut-off frequency. The correction is exact in the low-frequency domain but may distort the response of the system at high frequency [§Annexe1].

#### 2.5.2 Addition of static modes to the basis

Suppose that the loading $F(t)$ is written:

$\mathrm{F}(t)=\sum_{j}{\alpha}_{j}(t).{\mathrm{F}}_{j}$

The second way of correcting the truncation error consists in adding to the basis of the initial eigenmodes **static modes** ${\Psi}_{j}$ defined as the deformed shape under each given force ${F}_{j}$ :

${\Psi}_{j}={K}^{-1}.{F}_{j}$                                     eq 2.5.2-1

The new projection basis to be considered is the following:

$\stackrel{ˆ}{\Phi}=[{\Phi}_{1},{\Phi}_{2},...,{\Phi}_{p},{\Psi}_{1},{\Psi}_{2},...,{\Psi}_{m}]=[\Phi \Psi ]$                 eq 2.5.2-2

The generalised components to be used are the following:

$\stackrel{ˆ}{\eta}=[{\eta}_{1},{\eta}_{2},...,{\eta}_{p},{\mu}_{1},{\mu}_{2},...,{\mu}_{m}]=[\eta ,\mu ]$                     eq 2.5.2-3

The problem projected onto the completed basis is:

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}(\begin{array}{ccc}\setminus & 0& 0\\ 0& {m}_{i}& 0\\ 0& 0& \setminus \end{array})& {\Phi}^{T}.M.\Psi \\ {\Psi}^{T}.M.\Phi & {\Psi}^{T}.M.\Psi \end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\ddot{\eta}\\ \ddot{\mu}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{cc}(\begin{array}{ccc}\setminus & 0& 0\\ 0& {k}_{i}& 0\\ 0& 0& \setminus \end{array})& {\Phi}^{T}.K.\Psi \\ {\Psi}^{T}.K.\Phi & {\Psi}^{T}.K.\Psi \end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\eta \\ \mu \end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\Phi}^{T}.F.\alpha \\ {\Psi}^{T}.F.\alpha \end{array}\right\rbrace$         eq 2.5.2-4

It is observed that the diagonal character of the generalised matrices has been lost, but the advantage obtained is that the basis completed with static modes makes it possible to represent correctly the low-frequency behaviour of the initial system.

For example it is simple to show that at zero frequency the solution of this system is:

$\eta =0$ and $\mu =\alpha$ which is the exact solution of the initial static problem.

Appendix 1 presents the comparison, on a discrete system with 3 degrees of freedom, between the exact solution, the solution projected onto 1 mode, the one projected onto one mode with a static correction, and the solution constituted by 1 eigenmode and 1 static mode.

One notices that the addition of static modes makes it possible to extend beyond the interval $\left[0,{\omega}_{\max}=\max({\omega}_{j})\right]$ the good dynamic representation of the system. This technique therefore seems very interesting; it has the merit of carrying out the correction immediately, which will be of interest for the non-linear methods, in which one needs knowledge of the physical displacements at each time step.

The practical implementation of this technique is specified in [U2.06.04](doc:u2.06.04).

## 3 Extension of the Ritz reduction methods to the non‑linear case

### 3.1 General problem

The discretised non-linear dynamics problem without damping can most often be put in the following form:

$M.\ddot{X}+G(X)=F(t)\text{}X\in {R}^{n}$                         eq 3.1-1

$G(X)$ is a non-linear function of $X$ which represents the internal forces of the system as well as all the other forces that depend on the displacement, $F$ the vector of the external forces and $M$ the mass matrix of the system.

**The tangent stiffness matrix** of the system is by definition:

${K}_{(x)}^{\text{tg}}=\frac{\partial G}{\partial X}(x)$                                     eq 3.1-2

It makes it possible to define at each time instant a modal basis by:

$(-{\omega}_{i(X)}^{{\text{tg}}^{2}}M+{K}^{\text{tg}}).{\Phi}_{i(X)}^{\text{tg}}=0$                             eq 3.1-3

The modes thus defined depend on $X$ , hence on the time instant $t$ .

Knowing that the computation of the modal basis is very costly in computation time, the idea of wanting to project the model onto a modal basis at each time step, and then to solve, is of no interest compared with a direct solution.

The method most commonly used consists in defining a projection basis by adding to the modes computed on an initial configuration shapes making it possible to project the non-linearity.

Example: in the case where the non-linearity comes from a pointwise impact, it is proposed to enrich the modal basis with static modes making it possible to project the force undergone by the structure during the impact [R5.06.04](doc:r5.06.04).

The Ritz method always remains relevant in non-linear analyses, if the basis chosen makes it possible to project the displacements and the forces correctly.

The non-linear problem projected onto an arbitrary basis $\Psi$ is written:

${\Psi}^{T}M\Psi .\ddot{\eta}+{\Psi}^{T}G(X)={\Psi}^{T}F\text{}\eta \in {R}^{n}$                         eq 3.1-4

Two possibilities are then possible:

- the non-linearities are localised and the non-linearity can be evaluated on the projection basis: $G(X)=G(\Psi \eta )$ .
- The problem to be solved is a non-linear differential system in $\eta$ of smaller size. Different strategies are possible for solving this problem, depending essentially on the integration technique that one wishes to use.
- the non-linearities are global, and one must return to the space of the physical degrees of freedom in order to compute the internal forces: $G(X)$ .

This second method is more costly; it is much less common.

Appendix 2 presents the comparison, on a system with 3 degrees of freedom with a non-linearity in ${x}^{3}$, between the exact solution and the solution obtained by the method above with 1 and then 2 modes. One notices that it is necessary to take more modes into account than for the linear problem. On the other hand, on this example 2 modes are quite sufficient to describe the system.

### 3.2 Indication of the projection error

For non-linear problems the physical meaning of the number of modes to be taken into account is completely lost, and if the reduction methods always give a solution one must know the degree of confidence that can be granted to them. One way of proceeding, which is a little costly but indispensable, is to compute the residual of the initial system at each time step. It is defined by:

$R=M.\ddot{X}+G(X)-F(t)$

This residual vector is unfortunately not zero; it is only its projection onto the basis used that is.

A norm can then be computed for this residual; the smaller the norm of the residual, the more confidence one will be able to grant to the solution.

In order to use a relative value, it is of interest to compute the following fraction:

$r=\frac{\parallel R\parallel }{\max(\parallel F(t)\parallel ,\parallel G(X)\parallel ,\parallel M.\ddot{X}\parallel )}$                         eq 3.2-1

**Note:**

*This indicator is not currently implemented in Code_Aster.*

## 4 Use in Code_Aster

In *Code_Aster,* the Ritz methods are usable in transient analysis essentially through the operator DYNA_VIBRA [U4.53.03](doc:u4.53.03).

A phase of projection of the stiffness and mass matrices onto a basis of vectors is carried out by the operators PROJ_MATR_BASE [U4.63.12](doc:u4.63.12) and PROJ_VECT_BASE [U4.63.13](doc:u4.63.13).

The generalised dynamic problem is then solved in the operator DYNA_VIBRA by an explicit integration scheme (EULER or DEVOGELEARE) or an implicit one (NEWMARK). The characteristics and properties of the integration schemes are presented in the note [R5.06.04](doc:r5.06.04). For the structures for which the BASILE assumption does not apply (non-proportional damping) the damping matrix, which does not become diagonal, will also be projected. The integration of the coupled system is then necessarily done with the implicit scheme (NEWMARK) or the explicit scheme (EULER).

The localised non-linearities are specified directly in the operator DYNA_VIBRA. Localised non-linearities of impact and friction type can be introduced (see [R5.06.03](doc:r5.06.03) Modelling of impacts and friction), as well as modal forces that are functions of the displacement or of the velocity.

The a posteriori static truncation corrections are available in the case of a single excitation (see [R4.05.01](doc:r4.05.01) Seismic response).

The addition of static modes can be done by using beforehand the operators MODE_STATIQUE [U4.52.14](doc:u4.52.14) and DEFI_BASE_MODALE [U4.64.02](doc:u4.64.02). When the problem includes non-linearities, only the explicit schemes can be used.

For the global non-linearities [eq 3.1-4], it is possible to use the command DYNA_NON_LINE [U4.53.01](doc:u4.53.01) with the operand PROJ_MODAL, which makes it possible to compute at each time step the internal forces as a function of the physical degrees of freedom, and then to project the problem onto a modal basis.

An operation of return to the physical basis is then necessary in order to obtain the physical quantities (grandeur) such as displacement, velocity or acceleration on the structure. This operation is carried out by the operator REST_GENE_PHYS [U4.63.31](doc:u4.63.31) for the whole of the structure, or RECU_FONCTION, (factor keyword RESU_GENE) [U4.32.01](doc:u4.32.01) for the monitoring of a quantity (grandeur) at a node. In the case of global non-linearities (analysis with DYNA_NON_LINE) the operator REST_COND_TRAN [U4.63.33](doc:u4.63.33) is used.

More generally, the Ritz approach can be used in harmonic analysis by the command DYNA_VIBRA [U4.53.03](doc:u4.53.03) and in power spectral density by the command DYNA_ALEA_MODAL [U4.53.23](doc:u4.53.23).

Finally, dynamic substructuring can be regarded as a specific Ritz method [R4.06.02](doc:r4.06.02).

Consider the following discrete system with three masses:

![Figure](/figures/r5.06.01/1000057000001DAA000005FF7147BD49AF5E9E24.svg)

The stiffness and mass matrices are:

$M=(\begin{array}{ccc}m& 0& 0\\ 0& m& 0\\ 0& 0& m\end{array})\text{}K=(\begin{array}{ccc}k& -k& 0\\ -k& \mathrm{2k}& -k\\ 0& -k& \mathrm{2k}\end{array})$

That is: ${\omega}_{0}^{2}=\frac{k}{m}$

The eigenmodes and their angular frequency are:

Let us compare the responses of the system modelled by a single eigenmode with or without static correction:

![Figure](/figures/r5.06.01/1000082600002711000017C52A0F1B6DBD98C38F.svg)

It is observed that the static correction makes it possible to correct the response at low frequency; the model with 1 mode plus correction matches the exact solution perfectly at low frequency. On the other hand, at high frequency (beyond the first mode), this correction leads to overestimating the response enormously. The use of the static correction will therefore have to be used with caution and within the framework of a narrow-band excitation.

Let us look at what the method of addition of a static mode gives.

If a unit force is applied at point 1, the static deformed shape is:

${\Psi}_{s}=\frac{1}{k}(\begin{array}{c}3\\ 2\\ 1\end{array})$

The projected mass and stiffness matrices that one obtains are the following:

$\stackrel{ˆ}{M}=(\begin{array}{c}1,841m\frac{5,049}{{\omega}_{0}^{2}}\\ \frac{5,049}{{\omega}_{0}^{2}}14\text{}\frac{m}{{k}^{2}}\end{array}\text{})\text{}\text{et}\text{}\stackrel{ˆ}{K}=(\begin{array}{c}0,365k\text{}1\\ \text{}1\text{}\frac{3}{k}\text{}\end{array})$

this system has as eigenfrequencies:

${\omega}_{1}^{2}=0,198{\omega}_{0}^{2}$ and ${\omega}_{1}^{2}=1,667{\omega}_{0}^{2}$

Consider the following discrete system with three masses:

![Figure](/figures/r5.06.01/1000061600001D8F00000634866885CE8A2DA923.svg)

The stiffness and mass matrices are:

$M=(\begin{array}{ccc}m& 0& 0\\ 0& m& 0\\ 0& 0& m\end{array})\text{}K=(\begin{array}{ccc}k& -k& 0\\ -k& \mathrm{2k}& -k\\ 0& -k& \mathrm{2k}\end{array})$

Let us make this system non-linear by adding a cubic internal-force term between $\mathrm{x1}$ and $\mathrm{x2}$ :

$F=k.{(\mathrm{x1}-\mathrm{x2})}^{3}$

Let us seek to evaluate the response of this system to a forced excitation of frequency close to the first eigenfrequency of the linear system (one has chosen $\omega =0,18{\omega}_{0}$ ), with a large amplitude ${F}_{m}=3.k$ .

In this configuration, the response of the system cannot be evaluated by the response of the linear system (the cubic term is far too large); a non-linear analysis with pseudo-forces must be implemented, as was shown in [§3.2].

The curves of the transient results of this method can be seen in the report [bib8], taking into account one or 2 eigenmodes of the initial linear system.

With a single eigenmode, one notices that a relatively large error is committed (sometimes reaching 50%); on the other hand it is satisfactory to observe that the extrema of the vibrations are predicted fairly well. One might have hoped that, by exciting below the first eigenfrequency, a single eigenmode would have sufficed to model the response of the system; one sees here that this is not the case. As is often observed for non-linear systems, the system also responds with superharmonics of the excitation frequency.

On the other hand, by taking 2 eigenmodes to model the response of this structure with 3 degrees of freedom, a very satisfactory result is obtained (a few % error on the amplitude); to the eye one has difficulty distinguishing the difference. This shows that, by choosing a sufficiently rich projection basis, a complex dynamic system with non-linearities can be modelled very well thanks to a pseudo-force method.
