# r7.01.12 Modelling of the thermo-hydration, drying and shrinkage of concrete

**Summary**

Two types of phenomena, occurring at distinct periods in the life of a concrete, are described here:

- on the one hand a thermo-hydration reaction generating a so-called endogenous shrinkage, appearing at the early age of the concrete (the first 100 days),
- on the other hand an evaporation of part of the water not used in the hydration process, a phenomenon called drying and leading to a drying shrinkage. This phenomenon can last, depending on the dimensions of the concrete structure as built, from a few months to several years.

These phenomena are modelled in the form of diffusion equations whose solution is represented by new variables making it possible to compute directly the strains of the endogenous shrinkage (due to hydration) and of the drying shrinkage (due to drying).

## 1 Introduction

The behaviour of concrete, a material that is brittle in tension, heterogeneous and porous, is governed by numerous and complex physico-chemical phenomena. The losses of prestress induced by the delayed behaviour of concrete (shrinkage and creep) reduce over time the loading domain that the structure can support. These delayed strains, which appear in the concrete during its life, are composed of the early-age shrinkage (endogenous shrinkage specific to hydration and thermal shrinkage), of the drying shrinkage with the modelling of drying, and, as soon as it is subjected to stresses, of basic creep and drying creep.

In the design rules, the delayed strains of concrete are generally based on empirical rules calibrated on a large number of results from the literature, taking into account the principal parameters, such as temperature, humidity, aggregate content and the water/cement proportion. The kinetics of the phenomena use equivalent times computed by means of an Arrhenius law in order to take account of ageing and temperature.

A detailed analysis of the physico-chemical phenomena which are at the origin of the various delayed strains of concrete makes it possible to propose a modelling on the basis of an equivalent-continuum model  is proposed (basic creep and drying creep are not treated here).

### 1.1 Phenomenological aspects of the behaviour of concrete at early age: thermo-hydration

Early age is defined as the first 100 days of the life of the concrete. Endogenous shrinkage, or hydration shrinkage, and thermal shrinkage intervene from the first instants of setting (at early age), for a duration ranging from a few hours to a few days for thermal shrinkage, and from a few months to one year for hydration shrinkage, generally completed at the time of prestressing. Phenomena of restrained shrinkage or of differential shrinkage, in the formwork, can be at the origin of stresses or of cracks which must be assessed.

In the liquid phase, concrete is a viscous fluid in which the solid grains are in suspension in the hydraulic binder containing solid particles (cements...). Following the formation of the first hydrates, the setting of the concrete occurs, some ten hours after its manufacture, which corresponds to the establishment of connected hydrate bridges between the cement grains throughout the material. At the very beginning, the grains are relatively dispersed in the mixing water. Over time, the hydration of the cement grains is accompanied by a consumption of this mixing water. Experimentally, one observes that the volumetric balance of the reaction is negative; this is the Le Chatelier contraction. Put simply, the total volume of the hydrates is nearly 10% smaller than the total volume of these constituents. Mechanically, at the scale of the cement grains, the phenomenon stops when the hydrate bridges formed between the grains are rigid enough to prevent a possible relative approach of the grains. The macroscopic consequences for the structures are practically non-existent, since throughout the duration of this phase the concrete is still deformable, and any contraction is compensated by a granular readjustment of the material against the walls of the formwork. Although of relatively small extent, and of insufficient mechanical effect to generate real cracking of the concrete, the stresses generated at the interface of two consecutive lifts can consume 50% of the tensile-strength margin of the material.

The setting of the concrete, accompanied by the hydration of the cement, leads to an exothermic reaction. In massive structures the temperature can then rise to more than $50°C$ . Hydration is a thermally activated reaction, that is to say that the hydration rate increases with temperature. When the hydration rate decreases, the temperature falls, leading to a thermal shrinkage. In addition, the mechanical properties of the concrete vary as a function of its degree of hydration, and finally the consumption of water occurring during hydration leads to a capillary shrinkage. The various shrinkages can produce stresses much higher than the (low) tensile strength of the concrete and lead to cracking of the material.

The computation of the temperature and degree-of-hydration fields is available with the command [THER_NON_LINE](doc:u4.54.02). The computation of the mechanical fields taking endogenous shrinkage into account is carried out with the command [STAT_NON_LINE](doc:u4.51.03).

### 1.2 Drying and drying shrinkage

Modelling drying is important because the physico-chemical and mechanical properties of the material depend strongly on the humidity inside it. The objective is to propose a macroscopic modelling of the drying of concrete from a restricted number of parameters, easily measurable experimentally, from a non-linear transient diffusion law for humidity, chained to the temperature, dispensing with the complex mechanical, physical and chemical couplings at the scale of the material.

On removal of the formwork, the concrete is immersed in an external environment which generally presents a degree of humidity of the order of 60 to 80% RH (relative humidity = ratio of the vapour pressure to the saturated vapour pressure for a given temperature). It then undergoes a genuine hydric shock (by analogy with a thermal shock). The concrete is then in thermodynamic disequilibrium with the atmosphere. Drying will allow it to recover a hydric equilibrium with the external medium.

Physically, drying involves complex phenomena intimately coupled to one another, depending on the heterogeneous and granular structure of the concrete. At the macroscopic scale, it is possible to model drying as a non-linear diffusion phenomenon, with Darcy-type diffusion in the liquid phase, as long as there is continuity of the liquid phase, and with Fick-type diffusion in the gas phase, for the water vapour.

Drying shrinkage is the primary macroscopic consequence of the drying of concrete. It is the direct continuation of the capillary-tension phenomena which are at the origin of endogenous shrinkage. By its intensity, the strains being of the order of $400 \times 10^{-6}$ to $800 \times 10^{-6}$ for 50% hygrometry and for ordinary concretes, it is one to three times larger than the elastic strain for a loading in the neighbourhood of $10 \text{MPa}$ .

The modelling of thermo-hydration in the non-linear thermal operator is presented first, then the modelling of drying, and finally the introduction of endogenous shrinkage and of drying shrinkage into the non-linear mechanical operator.

## 2 Formulation of thermo-hydration

### 2.1 Thermo-hydration equation

As mentioned in the introduction, the hydration of concrete is a strongly exothermic reaction. Taking it into account in the heat equation as a **source term** is therefore necessary (see [R5.02.02]). The right-hand side, which contains the internal heat sources, can then be enriched in the following way

$$
\left\lbrace
    \begin{array}{c}
    \rho\therCp\frac{d \temp }{dt} + \divVector{\therFlux} = Q \frac{d\xi} {dt} + \therSource \\
    \therFlux = -\therCondTher \gradScal{\temp}
    \end{array}
\right .
$$

where:

- $\therFlux$ is the heat flux,
- $\therSource$ is an internal heat source (in $J s^{-1} {m}^{-3}$ ),
- $\rho \therCp$ is the volumetric heat capacity at constant pressure (in $J {m}^{-3} K^{-1}$ ),
- $\therCondTher$ is the thermal conductivity ($W {m}^{-2} K^{-1}$ ),

and, specifically to hydration:

- $\xi$ is the degree of hydration, by definition $\xi \in \left[ 0;1 \right]$ ;
- $Q$ is the heat of hydration (in $J {m}^{-3}$), that is the heat produced by the hydration of a unit volume of concrete.

The evolution of hydration depends on the composition of the concrete and on the temperature; a high temperature accelerates the hydration reaction.

Equation  can be solved if the function $\xi (t,\temp)$, and therefore $\frac{d\xi }{dt}(t,\temp)$, is known.

But the parameter $\frac{d \xi}{d t}$ is preferred to the hydration itself, and the time $t$ is thus eliminated. The corresponding function is called the **affinity**

$$
\frac{d\xi}{dt}
=
f^{\text{aff}} (\xi,\temp)
$$

The heat of hydration $Q$ and the function $f^{\text{aff}}(\xi ,\temp)$ must be supplied by the user under the keyword THER_HYDR of [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01). Their experimental determination is carried out by means of an adiabatic test.

### 2.2 Use of the adiabatic test for the determination of the affinity function and of the heat of hydration.

In an adiabatic test, a sample of fresh and thermally insulated concrete is immersed in a calorimeter and the evolution of the temperature ${\temp}^{\text{ad}}(t)$ is measured over time until hardening.

This test can be used to determine the heat of hydration as well as the affinity function. Indeed, under adiabatic conditions the equation simplifies because $\divVector{\therFlux}=0$ . In addition, $\therSource=0$ because one considers that the only heat source is the hydration of the concrete. Integration of  from the beginning ( $\xi =0$ ) to the degree of hydration $\xi$ then gives the expression:

$$
Q\xi ({\temp}^{\text{ad}}(t))
=\
\rho \therCp({\temp}^{\text{ad}}(t)-{\temp}_{0})
$$

If one assumes that $\xi=1$ (end of the test), one obtains the heat of hydration:

$$
Q
=
\rho \therCp \left( {\temp}_{\infty}^{\text{ad}}-{\temp}_{0} \right)
$$

where $\temp_0$ and ${\temp}_{\infty}^{\text{ad}}$ are the temperatures measured at the beginning and at the end of the adiabatic test.

Using this expression for the heat of hydration, one obtains the evolution of the hydration (and therefore of its derivative $\frac{d \xi}{dt}$ ), in the form:

$$
\xi({\temp}^{\text{ad}}(t))
=
\frac{{\temp}^{\text{ad}}(t)-{\temp}_{0}}
{{\temp}_{\infty}^{\text{ad}}-{\temp}_{0}}
$$

The adiabatic test therefore supplies the functions $\xi({\temp}^{\text{ad}}(t))$ and :math:{temp}^{text{ad}}(t)` . The parameters of the affinity function $f^{\text{aff}}(\temp,\xi )$ can be identified from the measurement points obtained during the adiabatic test, $f^{\text{aff}}({\temp}^{\text{ad}},{\xi}^{\text{ad}})$ .

An expression for $f^{\text{aff}}(\temp,\xi)$ has been suggested by  in the form of an exponential of the temperature:

$$
\frac{d\xi }{dt}
=
f^{\text{aff}}(\xi,\temp )
=
A(\xi) \, \exp\left(-\frac{{E}_{a}}{R \, \temp} \right)
$$

where $\frac{{E}_{a}}{R}$ is the Arrhenius constant (a rather empirical parameter varying between $4000°K$ and $7000°K$ , and considered to be equal to $4000°K$ in the absence of additional information). For the adiabatic test one then has:

$$
A({\temp}^{\text{ad}}(\xi ))
=
\frac{1}{{\temp}_{\infty}^{\text{ad}}-{\temp}_{0}}
\,
\frac{{d\temp}^{\text{ad}}}{dt}({\temp}^{\text{ad}}(\xi))
\,
\exp{\left( \frac{{E}_{a}}{R \, \temp^{\text{ad}}(\xi) }\right) }
$$

where the inverse functions ${\xi}^{-1}$, ${({\temp}^{\text{ad}})}^{-1}$ have been used to eliminate the time parameter $t$ .

can also be written in the following way:

$$
\xi(t)
=
\frac{{\temp}^{\text{ad}}(t)-{\temp}_{0}}{{\temp}_{\infty}^{\text{ad}}-{\temp}_{0}}
=
\frac{Q(\temp(t))}{Q({\temp}_{\infty})}
$$

In fact, one can define in a general way the degree of hydration at each time instant $t$ as being the ratio of the quantity of heat released up to the time instant $t$ to the total quantity of heat released at the end of the hydration process.

**Note**

Within the framework of non-linear thermal analysis [R5.02.02], the first of the equations is often written in the equivalent form $\frac{d\beta (\temp)}{dt}+\divVector{\therFlux}=Q\frac{d\xi (\temp)}{dt}+\therSource$, $\beta$ being the volumetric enthalpy. Indeed, in the command file, it is necessary to supply the enthalpy and not the volumetric heat capacity; see [R5.02.02].

## 3 Discretisation of the thermo-hydration problem

### 3.1 Choice of the solution method

The method chosen consists in solving globally the non-linear equation  by making use of the non-linear thermal algorithm, and in solving locally the equation  which represents the evolution law of a kind of internal variable representing the degree of hydration, this law being expressed by a function of the thermal state of the system. Indeed, there is no spatial differential operator for the variable $\xi$ in the equations and therefore no need for a finite element. The relation  represents a local law as in plasticity. One then keeps the same number of degrees of freedom as for classical thermal analysis. Such a decoupled process nevertheless leads to the computation of the same quantities several times. Indeed, suppose that $\xi$ is discretised at the nodes of the elements. Consider the example sketched as follows:

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.12/100000000000037500000125E0A9AF2C56FBBF9C.png)

On node **1** , the evolution equation will be integrated four times. A possible solution would have been for the local computations to be carried out on nodal fields (Aster concept CHAM_NO) and not on fields of nodes by element, which is currently impossible.

The solution which was finally adopted consists in computing $\xi$ at the Gauss points of the element, which is all the more natural in that, for the mechanical computation, Young’s modulus depends explicitly on $\xi$ . This nevertheless generates many local computations unless the finite element is strongly under-integrated. For example, if one considers a mesh comprising $N$ 20-node hexahedral elements, there are about ${4 \times N}$ nodes and ${27 \times N}$ Gauss points.

### 3.2 Solution algorithm

The weak formulation of equation  is written in the following way:

$$
\int_{\domain} {\dot{\beta}(\temp) \, \tempVirt \measDomain}
+
\int_{\domain} {\therCondTher (\temp) \gradScal{\temp} \cdot \gradScal{\tempVirt} \measDomain }
=
\int_{\domain} {\therSource \, \tempVirt \measDomain}
+
\int_{\domain} { Q \, A(\xi) \exp{\left(-\frac{{E}_{a}}{R \, \temp} \right)} \, \tempVirt \measDomain }
+
\int_{\bound} { \Phi \, \tempVirt \measBound }
$$

for any virtual temperature $\tempVirt$.
The development of thermo-hydration within the general algorithm of non-linear thermal analysis therefore consists in discretising explicitly, in the right-hand side, the term $\int_{\domain} { Q \, A(\xi) \exp{\left(-\frac{{E}_{a}}{R \, \temp} \right)} \, \tempVirt \measDomain }$ .

Denoting respectively by ${\xi}^{-}$, ${\temp}^{-}$, ${\xi}^{+}$ and ${\temp}^{+}$ the hydration and temperature variables at the beginning and at the end of the time step, one computes at each Gauss point the quantity:

$$
Q \, A(\xi^{-}) \exp{\left(-\frac{{E}_{a}}{R \, \temp} \right)}
$$

which is integrated directly into the right-hand side. After each solution of the current step, the variables are updated (${\xi}^{+}={\xi}^{-}$ and ${\temp}^{+}={\temp}^{-}$ ). The convergence test is active only on the temperature; the variable $\xi$ does not enter the iterative Newton process used in non-linear thermal analysis. Taking hydration into account is in fact only taking into account a heat source known at the beginning of the time step. This purely explicit discretisation therefore requires the use of sufficiently small time steps.

## 4 Formulation of drying

This part refers to the specification document for the development of drying  , as well as to the thesis of L. Granger  .

### 4.1 Modelling and equations of drying

The modellings of thermal analysis or of thermo-hydration and of drying are decoupled at the solution stage. Drying is then presented as an operation chained to the thermal analysis. Since the equations making it possible to solve drying and non-linear thermal analysis are similar up to the coefficients, this decoupling makes it possible to integrate the solution of the drying analysis by using directly the solution module of non-linear thermal analysis, without adding new phenomena, new element types, or new computation options.

The concentration or water content $C$, the computational variable in the modelling of drying, is assimilated, in terms of variable type, to a temperature. The transient temperature field, appearing in the drying equation, is only an auxiliary parameter on which the diffusion coefficient may depend.

The phenomena of thermal analysis and of drying, within the framework of a modelling decoupled between thermal analysis and drying, are governed by the following equations:

- equation of “classical” thermal analysis:

$$
\left\lbrace
    \begin{array}{c}
    \rho\therCp\frac{d \temp }{dt} + \divVector{\therFlux} = Q \frac{d\xi (\temp)} {dt} + \therSource(\temp) \\
    \therFlux = -\therCondTher(\temp) \gradScal{\temp}
    \end{array}
\right .
$$

  with $\rho\therCp$ the volumetric heat capacity at constant pressure, $\therCondTher$ the thermal conductivity, $Q$ the heat of hydration and $\therSource(\temp)$ the internal source.

- equation characterising drying:

$$
\frac{\partial C}{\partial t}
-
\divVector\left( D(C,\temp) \gradScal{C} \right)
=
0
$$

  where $C$ (${m}^{3}v / {m}^{3}$ or $l/{m}^{3}$ ) is the computational variable (volumetric concentration or water content), $\temp$ is the input variable of the analysis (the temperature), an auxiliary variable of the drying solution, $D$ (${m}^{2}/s$ ) is a diffusion coefficient, characterising the non-linearity of the equation, and depending both on the computational variable, $C$ , and on the auxiliary variable, $\temp$. This diffusion law is given in various forms, according to the model retained (Bazant law, Granger law, Mensi law, Richards-Fick law, cf. r7.01.12-diffusion-usuelles and ).

Equations  and  correspond to a chained thermal/drying analysis. One can therefore compute $\temp$ without knowing the water concentration, then compute the latter, for which $\temp$ is then a parameter (assuming that the thermal conductivity $\therCondTher$ does not depend on the water concentration $C$ ). Note also that the drying phenomenon is decoupled from the mechanical evolutions of the concrete.

### 4.2 Diffusion coefficient

The material is described by the diffusion coefficient $D$, characteristic of the material, depending both on the temperature $\temp$ and on the water concentration $C$. The equation of moisture migration in concrete comes from those of the mechanics of porous media. One should refer to  for more detail. Classically, a diffusion law expresses a flux as the product of a quantity characteristic of the material by the gradient of an intensive quantity. The various quantities considered are defined by an average over the representative elementary volume, insofar as one can define this average for the material considered, so that the differentiation operators have a meaning. One therefore generally makes the assumption which consists in supposing that the liquid and gas phases are connected:

- for the diffusion of vapour, one starts from the positivity of the dissipation associated with transport of the gas phase, distinguishing two phenomena, a permeation-type phenomenon (Darcy), related to pressure gradients, and a diffusion-type phenomenon (Fick), related to concentration gradients,
- for the diffusion of liquid water, the positivity of the dissipation associated with transport of the liquid water, and Darcy’s law, make it possible to express the liquid flux as a function of the liquid pressure. Kelvin’s law, describing the coexistence of the two liquid and gas phases by writing the equality of the specific free enthalpies, leads to the expression of the flux as a function of the gradient of the degree of humidity.

From the two preceding results, one obtains the expression of the total flux as a function of the gradient of the degree of water concentration. The classical experimental methods in drying problems generally give access to the water concentration, and very rarely to the relative humidity. It is therefore preferable to express the flux as a function of the water content, classically using the desorption isotherm of the concrete, which relates the water content, $C$ , and the relative humidity, $h$ . The relative humidity is the ratio between vapour pressure and saturated vapour pressure.

The postulate of the local state stipulates that the current state of a homogeneous system in any evolution can be characterised by the same variables as at equilibrium, and that it is independent of the rates of evolution. In other words, the water content $C$ and the relative humidity $h$ are indeed related by the same relation as at equilibrium. This leads to the classical diffusion equation .

This equation highlights the non-linear character of moisture diffusion in concrete. In industrial cases, the temperature is in general not uniform in the structure. It is therefore necessary to take into account a moisture diffusion coefficient which depends on the temperature. In practice, in the literature, the best-known authors (Bazant cf ) propose an expression for the diffusion coefficient of the type:

$$
D(C,\temp)
=
D(C,{\temp}_{0})(\frac{\temp}{{\temp}_{0}})\exp{(\frac{-{Q}_{s}}{R}(\frac{1}{\temp}-\frac{1}{{\temp}_{0}}))}
$$

with ${Q}_{s}/R=4700{K}^{-1}$ and $\temp$ in $K$.

**Note**

From the way things are presented, it would seem that one has not used the fact that drying is a phenomenon coupled with mechanics (that is to say that it is the cause of a drying shrinkage). In reality, we have made the assumption of a decoupling of the phenomena, when we used the sorption/desorption curve. In fact, during the measurement of the weight loss at equilibrium as a function of $h$, the specimen undergoes a shrinkage. At the microscopic level, everything happens as if the shrinkage, by modifying the porosity, were going to interact with the relative hygrometry inside the specimen, since the vapour pressure and $h$ increase. This drying shrinkage being very small, it is usual to neglect it in the computations of the water content. There is therefore only a chaining between the computation of the water content and the mechanical computation of drying shrinkage.

### 4.3 Usual diffusion laws

The diffusion law, a function of the two parameters $C$ and $\temp$ , can be defined freely by the user in the form of a nappe. However, several models found in the literature are also available. These models are presented below.

#### 4.3.1 Law proposed by Granger

$$
D\left(C,\temp\right)
=
A \, \exp{\left(B \, C\right)} \left(\frac{\temp}{{\temp}_{0}}\right)\exp{\left(\frac{-{Q}_{s}}{R}\left(\frac{1}{\temp}-\frac{1}{{\temp}_{0}}\right)\right)}
$$

$A$ (homogeneous to an area per unit time), $B$, ${\temp}_{0}$, ${Q}_{s}$ and $R$ ($\frac{Q_s}{R}$ in $K$ ) are coefficients chosen by the user. $D$ is a function of the temperature and of the water concentration.

#### 4.3.2 Mensi law

$$
D(C)=A\ \exp{(B \, C)}
$$

$A$ and $B$ are coefficients chosen by the user. $D$ is a function only of the water concentration.

#### 4.3.3 Bazant law

The Bazant law is expressed from the degree of humidity $h$ , which is related to the water concentration by the sorption/desorption curve. The form of this law is the following:

$$
D(h) = D_1
\left(\alpha+
    \frac{1-\alpha}
         {1+
            \left( \frac{1-h(C)}{1-0.75}\right)^n}
\right)
$$

Usually ${D}_{1}={3.10}^{-10}{m}^{2}/s$, $\alpha$ lies between $0.025$ and $0.1$, $n$ is of the order of $6$. $h(C)$ is the degree of humidity, which is expressed as a function of the water concentration by means of the sorption/desorption curve.

The sorption/desorption curve can be introduced in the form of a standard tabulated function, knowing that in reality this curve presents a hysteresis, but can be considered as invertible if one takes account of only one direction of travel.

#### 4.3.4 SECH_RFT law

The SECH_RFT diffusion law is taken from the literature model RICHARDS_FICK_TEMPERATURE .

This law models the permeation of water in liquid form and the diffusion of vapour in the gas mixture on the basis of the transport properties measurable in the laboratory (porosity, permeability, desorption isotherm, vapour diffusion), whereas the Granger law models only the permeation of water in liquid form by an empirical expression.
It may be noted that in this law, unlike the others, the transport equation is written as a function of the degree of water saturation and not of the water concentration.

The degree of water saturation $S_r$ is related to the water concentration $C$ (variable SECH) according to the following expression:

$$
S_r = \frac{C}{1000 \times \phi}
$$

With $\phi$ corresponding to the porosity of the material.

As for the other laws, it assumes that drying takes place at a gas pressure that is constant and equal to atmospheric pressure. The effects of temperature are better represented in the RFT model (especially for temperatures higher than 50°C).

The hydric transport equation described by this model is a non-linear diffusion equation expressed as a
function of the degree of water saturation $S_r$ and which is written in the form:

$$
\frac{\partial S_r}{\partial t}
-
\divVector{D_{lv} \, \gradScal{S_r}}
=
0
$$

With $D_{lv}= D_l + D_v$. The coefficients $D_l$ and $D_v$ are, respectively, the diffusion coefficients of liquid water and of water vapour.

The coefficient $D_l$, describing the transport of liquid water, is given by Darcy’s law:

$$
D_l(S_r,\temp)
=
\frac{ K_l(\temp) \, K_{rl}(S_r)}
     {\phi\eta_l(\temp)}
\,
\frac{\partial P_c}
     {\partial S_r}
(S_r,\temp)
$$

The coefficient $D_v(S_r, \temp)$ describing the diffusion of water vapour in the gas phase is given by Fick’s
law:

$$
D_v(S_r,\temp)
=
\frac{D_{v0}(S_r,\temp)\, P_v(\temp)}
    {\phi }
\left( \frac{M}{\rho_l R \temp}  \right)^2
\frac{\partial P_c}
     {\partial  S_r}
(S_r,\temp)
$$

With

- $P_c(S_r,\temp)$, capillary pressure determined by the desorption isotherm of water as a function of saturation, calibrated by the Leverett model. Its expression is given in [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01) (BETON_DESORP).
- $K_l(\temp)$, the intrinsic permeability to the liquid (homogeneous to an area), which is defined by the following relation:

$$
K_l(\temp)
=
K_0 \exp{\left[ \exp \left( \frac{\temp-\temp_0}{\temp_c} \right) -1 \right]}
$$

- $K_{rl}(S_r)$ the relative permeability to water, which can be determined by a Muallem-Van Genuchten type law:

$$
K_{rl}(S_r)
=
\left(S_r\right)^p \left(1-\left(1-S_r^{1/\beta}\right)^\beta\right)^2
$$

- $D_{v0}(S_r, \temp)$, the effective vapour diffusion coefficient, which is written

$$
D_{v0}(S_r, \temp) = D_0(\temp) R_m(\phi, S_r)
$$

- $D_0$, the free-vapour diffusion coefficient (homogeneous to an area per unit time):

$$
D_0(\temp) = 0.217\cdot10^{-4} \left(\frac{\temp+273.15}{273.15}\right)^{1.88}
$$

It is specified that this expression is associated with the unit $m^2/s$. It is however possible
to use this law for another system of units thanks to the keywords UNITE_LONGUEUR and UNITE_TEMPS (see [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01)).

- $R_m(S_r, \phi)$, the Millington resistance factor:

$$
R_m(S_r, \phi) = \phi^a D_{rv}(S_r)
$$

- $D_{rv}(S_r)$, the relative vapour diffusion coefficient:

$$
D_{rv}(S_r) =  \left(1-S_r\right)^b
$$

- $P_v(S_r,\temp)$, the vapour pressure:

$$
P_v(\temp) = h(S_r, \temp)\cdot P_{vs}(\temp)
$$

- $h(S_r, \temp)$, the relative humidity determined by the desorption isotherm of water as a function of saturation:

$$
h(S_r, \temp)=  \exp{\left(Pc(S_r, \temp) \frac{M}{\rho_l(\temp) R \temp} \right)}
$$

All these expressions depend on several parameters.

**Fixed parameters**

- $R$ the perfect-gas constant ($R = 8.3144 J/mol/K$)
- $M$ the molar mass of water ($M = 18 · 10^{-3} kg/mol)$

**User parameters of the material SECH_RFT**

- $K_0$, the intrinsic permeability of the liquid at the reference temperature (homogeneous to an area), which corresponds to the parameter **PERM_IN**.
- $\temp_c [K]$ the thermal activation temperature, which corresponds to the parameter **QSR_K**.
- $a$, first Millington coefficient, which corresponds to the parameter **A_MIL**.
- $b$, first Millington coefficient, which corresponds to the parameter **B_MIL**.
- $p$, pore interaction factor, which corresponds to the parameter **VG_M_P**.

**User parameters of the material BETON_DESORP**

- $\temp_0 [K]$ the reference temperature. The corresponding material parameter is **TEMP_0_C** [°C] (which is converted into degrees kelvin).
- $\phi$, the mean porosity, which corresponds to the parameter **PORO**.
- $\beta$, Van Genuchten parameter, which corresponds to the parameter **VG_N**.

**Non-parameterisable quantities depending on the temperature**

- $\eta_l(\temp)  [Pa.s]$, the dynamic viscosity of liquid water, of expression: $0.6612(\temp-229)^{-1.562}$
- $\rho_l(\temp) [kg/m^3]$, the density of liquid water, of expression: $314.4+685.6 \Bigl(1. -\bigl( \frac{\temp-273.15}{374.14}\bigr)^{\frac{1}{0.55}}\Bigr)^{0.55}$
- $Pvs(\temp) [Pa]$, the saturated vapour pressure of Rankine, of expression: $Pvs(\temp)=101325\,\exp\left(13.7-\left(5120/\left(\temp+273.15\right)\right)\right)$

These expressions, just like the values of $R$ and $M$, are given for information together with their units. They are globally involved in
the formulae in the form of ratios and therefore do not interfere with the units of the analysis.
Only $\eta_l(\temp)$ is adapted to the system of units of the analysis according to the value of the material parameter UNITE_TEMPS.

### 4.4 Modelling of the boundary conditions

The boundary conditions are in general expressed by a non-linear relation between the water-concentration flux ${w}^{\text{fl}}$ (of dimension $l/{m}^{3} \times m \, s^{-1}$ ) and the water concentration. These conditions are therefore analogous to the so-called exchange conditions in thermal analysis. One may for example use the formula proposed by L. Granger  page 181:

Its expression is the following:

$$
{w}^{\text{fl}}
=

## 5 Integration of drying in Code_Aster

These developments concern the 2D elements, the axisymmetric elements and the 3D isoparametric elements, with any number of nodes, linear and quadratic.

### 5.1 Introduction of the notion of behaviour into the non-linear thermal operator

The operator [THER_NON_LINE](doc:u4.54.02) is used for the solution of non-linear thermal problems. But the same solution module is used to solve drying and hydration problems, because of the analogy of the equations.

The notion of behaviour has been added in the non-linear thermal operator, with a nomenclature and a syntax analogous to those of the non-linear mechanical operator. For drying it implies a notion of topological entity, on which this behaviour is applied. This can be useful when there are several types of possible diffusion laws, or when one wants to carry out a purely thermal analysis on one part of the mesh, while on another part one carries out a thermo-hydration analysis (on the other hand, the simultaneous use on the same mesh of drying-type behaviours and of thermal or hydration-type behaviours would not make sense).

A ‘drying’ behaviour is associated with each of the diffusion laws, as they can be found in the literature, just as a specific material is associated with each of the diffusion laws, in order to define their characteristic coefficients. The solution of drying is identical, up to the coefficients, to that of non-linear thermal analysis, and no modification has been made to the solution algorithm.

For drying, five distinct behaviours are defined under the keywords SECH_GRANGER, SECH_BAZANT, SECH_MENSI, SECH_RFT or SECH_NAPPE, in order to characterise each of the possible diffusion laws. They can be assigned to complementary parts of the mesh, during the same analysis. The simultaneous definition of several ‘drying’ behaviours associated with different topological entities requires several occurrences of the keyword COMPORTEMENT. The topological entity must then be identified by supplying the keyword GROUP_MA.

In parallel with the ‘drying’ behaviours, in the operator [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01), materials make it possible in a first stage to define the values of the coefficients of the diffusion laws, non-linear functions of the water content and of the temperature. The user can choose the law (or laws) of his choice, and defines the value he wishes for each of these coefficients.

The keyword SECH_GRANGER makes it possible to define the diffusion law of liquid and gaseous water in its most classical form among the expressions in the literature. Four coefficients as well as a reference temperature ${\temp}_{0}$ characterise this law.

The keywords SECH_MENSI and SECH_BAZANT make it possible to define the laws of Mensi and of Bazant, by means of the appropriate coefficients. The Bazant law, being expressed from the degree of humidity, requires the definition of a desorption curve making it possible to convert the water content into a degree of humidity within the framework of this modelling.

The keyword SECH_RFT makes it possible to define a law of type RICHARDS_FICK_TEMPERATURE. It is described in section r7.01.12-sech_rft.

Finally, the keyword SECH_NAPPE makes it possible to use a diffusion law, from a tabulated function of two variables, which will be interpolated in the analyses from the values of the water concentration and of the temperature. This last possibility has the drawback of not removing the ambiguity between these two variables associated with an identical type, 'TEMP'.

It is necessary, for drying, to introduce as input to the analysis a concept of type [evol_ther], representing the evolution of the temperature field of the concrete structure, within the framework of a chained thermal/drying analysis. Indeed, the drying analysis requires the preliminary computation of the temperature and possibly of the hydration, because the diffusion coefficient $D(C,\temp)$ depends on the temperature.

### 5.2 Implementation of the boundary conditions for drying

#### 5.2.1 Expression of the boundary conditions

The boundary conditions are expressed in the form of a humidity flux on the surfaces in contact with the external medium according to the expression .

#### 5.2.2 Delimitation of the drying analysis by means of the boundary conditions

The drying analysis is defined on the whole of the mesh where finite elements are assigned. In order to make the drying analysis effective only on a portion of the mesh (this with the aim of keeping the same model for the drying analyses and for the mechanical analyses and of facilitating the restarts of the analysis), the boundary conditions will be used. Indeed, drying takes place only if there is exchange with the outside. It is therefore the assignment of the boundary conditions which makes it possible to “localise” the analysis. The absence of drying on a portion of the structure will be expressed by the absence of boundary conditions on the exchange surfaces concerned.

#### 5.2.3 Implementation in Aster

The boundary conditions can be defined, as in thermal analysis, in the form of a non-linear normal flux formulated from a tabulated function of the computational variable, and interpolated during the analyses. This makes it possible to avoid creating new computation options, analogous to the non-linear thermal options which compute the left-hand side and the right-hand side, and which can be used directly for drying. It is then sufficient to choose a tabulated function corresponding to the expression of the flux, given by the equation .

By means of a predefined function ([FORMULE](doc:u4.31.05)), the expression of the flux, given in polynomial form and as a function of the computational variable, is transformed into a tabulated curve, by means of [CALC_FONC_INTERP](doc:u4.32.01). One therefore does not create a new computation option for the treatment of the boundary conditions.

### 5.3 Numerical integration of drying

The heat equation  leads, in the case of a normal-flux boundary condition on the boundary $\Gamma$, to the variational formulation:

$$
\int_{\domain} {\dot{\beta}(\temp) \, \tempVirt \measDomain}
+
\int_{\domain} {\therCondTher (\temp) \gradScal{\temp} \cdot \gradScal{\tempVirt} \measDomain }
=
\int_{\domain} {\therSource(\temp) \, \tempVirt \measDomain}
+
\int_{\bound}  {\therCondTher (\temp) \, \frac{\partial \temp}{\partial n} \, \tempVirt \measBound }
$$

In an analogous way, the equation governing drying  leads, in the case of a normal-flux boundary condition on the boundary $\Gamma$, to the variational formulation:

$$
\int_{\domain} {\dot{C}(\temp) \, \tempVirt \measDomain}
+
\int_{\domain} { D(C,\temp) \gradScal{C} \cdot \gradScal{\tempVirt} \measDomain }
=
0
+
\int_{\bound}  { D(C,\temp) \, \frac{\partial C}{\partial n} \, \tempVirt \measBound }
$$

The solution of drying is integrated into the operator [THER_NON_LINE](doc:u4.54.02), by replacing $\rho \therCp$ by the constant function equal to the identity, and the conductivity by the diffusion $D(C,\temp)$ , the temperature appearing as a constant in the computations (auxiliary variable). Depending on the diffusion law chosen, one must compute the value of the diffusion coefficient as well as its derivatives, according to the temperature and the water concentration at the current time instant, at the current point.

One should refer to the documentation of the non-linear thermal operator [R5.02.02](doc:r5.02.02) for further details on the numerical integration of non-linear thermal analysis.

Within the framework of drying, the boundary conditions are given in terms of normal flux, and lead, as in thermal analysis, to a term in the left-hand side and to a term in the right-hand side.

## 6 Formulation of endogenous and drying shrinkage

### 6.1 Endogenous shrinkage

Within the framework of a formulation of shrinkage in terms of strain, the total strain increment can be decomposed into the sum of a thermal component, of a component representing the endogenous shrinkage, and of a component representing the drying shrinkage, added to the mechanical component (elasticity, creep, ...).

The simplest way of representing the drying shrinkage $\strain_{\text{dess}}$ is to put it in the form:



$$
\begin{align}\begin{aligned}\Delta \strain_{\text{dess}}
=
\left(
    -\kappa \left( {C}_{0}-{C}^{+} \right )
    +\kappa \left( {C}_{0}-{C}^{-} \right )\\\right)
\,
\tensTwoUnit
=
- \kappa \, \Delta C \, \tensTwoUnit\end{aligned}\end{align}
$$

$\Delta {\epsilon}_{\text{dessiccation}}=\left[-\kappa \left({C}_{0}-{C}^{+}\right)+\kappa \left({C}_{0}-{C}^{-}\right)\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}\right].\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}{I}^{d}=-\kappa \Delta C.\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}{I}^{d}$                                 eq 6.1-1

where $C$ is the water concentration, ${C}_{0}$ the initial water concentration and $\kappa$ a coefficient characterising the shrinkage, assumed constant.

One can model the endogenous shrinkage in the form:

$$
\Delta \strain_{\text{endo}}
=
-\beta \, \xi \, \tensTwoUnit
$$

where $\xi$ is the hydration and $\beta$ a coefficient characteristic of the material whose dependences are poorly known.

Drying and endogenous shrinkages can therefore enter a constitutive law by replacing in it the usual terms $\Delta \strain -\Delta \strain_{\text{ther}}$ by $\Delta \strain -\Delta \strain_{\text{ther}}-\Delta \strain_{\text{dess}}-\Delta \strain_{\text{endo}}$ . These terms are taken into account for the elastic and elastoplastic behaviours of Von Mises type and for several concrete models
(BETON_UMLV, BETON_BURGER, BETON_DOUBLE_DP, ENDO_ISOT_BETON, MAZARS). One then has, for example, in 1D elasticity:

$$
\delta \strainCmp
=
\frac{1}{\youngModulus(\xi)} \, \Delta \stressCmp
=
\Delta \strainCmp_{\text{ther}}
+
\Delta \strainCmp_{\text{endo}}
+
\Delta \strainCmp_{\text{dess}}
$$

The mechanical parameter $\youngModulus$ (Young’s modulus) depends principally on the hydration variable $\xi$ .

This formulation of drying shrinkage and of endogenous shrinkage has the advantage of using directly the water content $C$ , which one can relate to the weight loss by simple integration over the volume. If one used the relative humidity $h$ , it would have to be translated back into terms of water content by means of the desorption isotherm of each of the various concretes.

These parameters can be defined in a relatively general framework, as functions of the various computational variables and auxiliary variables (temperature, hydration, water concentration, or constants) in order to leave the user the choice of defining freely the dependences of the parameters. It remains the user’s responsibility to use the functions in order to reproduce the expression of Young’s modulus.

For more detail on these formulations, and on the means of computing the coefficients $\kappa$ and $\beta$ , one should refer to the thesis of L. Granger, , pages 99 onwards, and pages 210 onwards.

For the mechanical analysis the variables $\xi$ (the hydration) and $C$ (water concentration) are data, as is the temperature in a thermomechanical analysis.

### 6.2 Integration of shrinkage into the mechanical constitutive laws

Thermal analysis and drying are decoupled from the mechanical solution, just as drying is an operation chained to thermal analysis and to hydration. This decoupling makes it possible to integrate shrinkage into the solution operator of non-linear mechanics, without adding new phenomena, behaviours, element types and computation options. In addition, it makes it possible to introduce shrinkage in a simple way into all the non-linear constitutive laws. The syntax of [STAT_NON_LINE](doc:u4.51.03) is not modified.

In the current version of the non-linear mechanical operator, shrinkage has been integrated into the elastic behaviour (ELAS), into the Von Mises elastoplastic behaviour (VMIS_ISOT_* ) and into the models specific to concretes: MAZARS, ENDO_ISOT_BETON, BETON_DOUBLE_DP, GRANGER* , BETON_UMLV_FP. It consists in removing the shrinkage terms from the total strain, before the solution of the equilibrium equations at the Gauss points, in the same way as thermal expansion is taken into account.

The coefficients $\kappa$ and $\beta$ characterising endogenous and drying shrinkages are defined under the keyword 'ELAS_FO' in [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01) (operands K_DESSIC and B_ENDOGE respectively), as constants. The other mechanical characteristics, Poisson’s ratio and Young’s modulus, can also be defined as functions of the new variables HYDR and SECH, which have been added to the catalogues of the two operators [DEFI_FONCTION](doc:u4.31.02) and [DEFI_NAPPE](doc:u4.31.03).

The results, of type [evol_ther], coming from a non-linear thermal analysis, or from a thermo-hydration analysis, and from a drying analysis, and corresponding respectively to the thermo-hydric fields of type 'TEMP/HYDR', or to the drying field of type 'TEMP', are transmitted to the mechanical analysis by means of the operator [AFFE_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.03) (keyword AFFE_VARC). The mechanical analyses associating these fields make it possible:

- to compute the endogenous and drying shrinkages, in the case where the associated material characteristics will have been defined beforehand in [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01),
- to interpolate Young’s modulus and Poisson’s ratio when these are functions of the hydration or drying variables.

- In the presence of a drying field, it is necessary to supply the keyword VALE_REF in the command [AFFE_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.03). This value defines the drying value for which the drying shrinkage is zero.
- One must therefore take care to be consistent with the drying values used (in particular at the initial time instant!)
- Drying and hydration are not taken into account for the structural elements. For this type of element, it will be necessary to define zero coefficients $\kappa$ and $\beta$, otherwise the analysis will stop in fatal error
- The behaviours FLUA_PORO_BETON, ENDO_PORO_BETON and BETON_RAG take these effects into account but differently

**Notes**

### 6.3 Tangent matrix

The computation of the tangent matrices of the various non-linear constitutive laws is not affected by the addition of endogenous shrinkage and of drying shrinkage, because one neglects the derivatives, with respect to the hydration and drying variables, of the terms of the equilibrium equations, just as the derivatives of these same terms with respect to temperature are usually neglected. These derivatives appear at second order.
