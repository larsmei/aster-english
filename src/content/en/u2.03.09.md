# u2.03.09 Overview of the constitutive models for soils and rocks, and for joints

> **Summary.**

The aim of this document is to provide an overall view of the modelling possibilities open to the user for the constitutive behaviour of soils and rocks, to which the constitutive behaviour of joints has also been added in order to describe the interfaces between geomaterial media. Different choices are always possible, and the aim of this document is not to take the place of the engineer's analysis, but rather to make it easier to choose the modelling options, according to the needs and the tools available, and to direct the user towards more specific documents.

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 General points on the constitutive behaviour of soils and rocks, and of joints

The modelling of the constitutive behaviour of the soils and rocks which make up many geotechnical structures seeks to represent complex phenomenologies, in particular non-linear ones, according to the objectives of the study being carried out. One may, for example, be concerned with assessing the bearing capacity of a foundation, the rise in water pressures, seismically induced settlements, the evolution of a slip zone, the margin available before the onset of liquefaction, the long-term stability of an underground structure, the amplification of waves in a sedimentary basin...

The mechanical constitutive behaviour of geomaterials is characterised by:

- an asymmetry between tensile states and compressive states;
- irreversible evolutions, to be described by internal history variables;
- a *consolidation*, for a spherical stress path, which exhibits a strong non-linearity;
- a “critical state” curve, which is a material datum, in the ${\log}_{10}p'–e$ plane (*confining pressure*, also called *mean effective stress* $p'=\frac{1}{3}\text{tr}(\sigma ')$ – *void ratio* $e$, which is linked directly to the *density index* ${D}_{r}$) where the soil, when it undergoes pure shear, no longer shows any evolution of the volumetric strains (one has $\dot{{\varepsilon}_{p}^{v}}=0$); and a strength plateau is reached in the confining pressure – deviatoric stresses plane $p'–q$. For a cohesionless soil, the “critical state” curve is rather straight in the ${\log}_{10}p'–e$ plane, whereas it is curved for a clayey soil. At the initial state of the soil, in the ${\log}_{10}p'–e$ plane, the domain to the left of the “critical state” curve characterises dilatant situations, whereas to the right one has contractancy;
- a *dilatancy*, that is, a coupling between volumetric behaviour and deviatoric behaviour, which varies during the evolution of the degradation of the soil and also with the evolution of the pore pressures, which act on $p'$. The name of Roscoe is associated with this phenomenology. The dilatancy phase generally follows a prior phase of *contractancy*; these two phases are separated by a “characteristic state” curve in the $p'–q$ plane (where one has $\dot{{\varepsilon}_{p}^{v}}=0$), dependent on the consolidation state, cf. for example [bib28]_. The *dilatancy angle* $\psi$ characterises the slope of this curve, which always lies below the “critical state” curve, or is superimposed on it;
- a *strength* depending on the loading history, increasing or decreasing (positive or negative hardening), associated with residual strains. A dilatant soil may see its strength decrease (*softening*) after a stage of positive hardening, whereas a contracting soil may undergo positive hardening up to failure, described by the “critical state” curve drawn in the confining pressure – deviatoric stresses plane $p'–q$. The internal friction angle ${\phi }_{\mathrm{pp}}$ characterises the slope of this “critical state” curve in the $p'–q$ plane. At the critical state, an evolution of the distortion occurs without any increase in the volumetric strain. In the case of a clay, these situations are associated with the value of the degree of *consolidation* of the soil (called *OCR* for “Over-Consolidation Ratio”, equal to the ratio between the greatest vertical effective stress reached during the loading process and the in-situ vertical effective stress), whereas for a sand, the initial state in the ${\log}_{10}p'–e$ plane defines the softening and dilatant character, or the contractant and non-softening character, of the constitutive behaviour;
- an initial *cohesion* (that is, a strength under deviatoric loading) for clays, almost zero for dry sands, even though the latter, once saturated, exhibit a certain cohesion; the cohesion of rocks is generally much higher;
- this phenomenology therefore reflects a common characteristic: the dependence of the constitutive behaviour on the state of the material in the ${\log}_{10}p'–e$ plane;
- the *elastic characteristics* also depend on the initial void ratio ${e}_{0}$ and on the confining pressure: this characterises a non-linear elasticity law. The elasticity modulus is therefore higher at depth than at the surface. In practice, even if the elasticity domain is very small, it is useful to represent this reduction of the elasticity modulus which occurs from the very beginning of the loading process. Poisson's ratio (under the isotropic assumption) is in general evaluated by means of the compressibility coefficient, which is more readily available. One sometimes needs to take into account a certain anisotropy induced by the stratification;
- *hysteresis* loops under cyclic loading, whose shape also depends on the confining pressure and on the *plasticity index* ${I}_{p}$, which is measured in the laboratory;
- in particular, a cohesionless material (sand...) under strain-controlled deviatoric cyclic loading densifies progressively (contractancy), especially if it is *loose* in the initial state (low value of ${D}_{r}$); on the other hand, if the material is *dense* (*overconsolidated*) it undergoes a damage phase (dilatancy) after the peak has been reached, followed by failure (critical state). If the amplitude of these cycles is large, one may observe the absence of a stabilisation of the cyclic response: one no longer has shakedown but the onset of ratcheting;
- tangent and secant stiffnesses depending on the loading history;
- an influence of the loading rate: an equivalent *viscosity* may make it possible to represent this effect, even if it is difficult to quantify for sands; this characteristic is important for clays and rocks; all the behavioural properties cited above may depend on the loading rate;
- and finally a strong *spatial variability of the mechanical properties*, characteristic of a natural material.

To these common characteristics, the principal differences between a soil and a rock should be defined:

- a *soil* is characterised by a generally porous medium having an *unconsolidated* behaviour, that is, deformable enough that it can be “worked”; the constitutive law of a soil will be concerned mainly with characterising its *plastic flow* and its strength under *cyclic* loading; a soil may be described by a granular-type porous continuous medium: the plastic flow and the long-term behaviour are intimately linked to the question of the variations in pore pressures of the fluid present in the porosity;
- a *rock* is characterised by a cohesion much higher than that of a soil, and a stiff behaviour exhibiting a certain hardness; the constitutive law of a rock will be concerned mainly with characterising instantaneous and delayed *cracking*, as well as *fatigue* failure. The *damage* state of the rock, at the scale of a rock mass, is quantified by the RQD factor (Rock Quality Designation). It is important to take into account the discontinuous character of a rock mass at the scale of the structure, that is, to incorporate into the modelling the possible fractures which exceed the limits of what the constitutive law can represent. A *joint* model may prove relevant. The porosity of a rock is generally much lower than that of a soil, so hydromechanical coupling in rocks plays a role mainly in the long term.

The *interfaces* of media made up of geomaterials are in general also characterised by complex behaviours: one cannot always be satisfied with a perfect modelling by simple continuity of the displacements, equivalent to continuity of the stress vector at the interface. A *joint* model is therefore defined. One finds the phenomena of cohesion and of dilatancy as in soils and, in addition, the unilateral character of failure. Hydromechanical coupling plays an essential role in certain types of application.

The various constitutive models attempt to represent all or part of these characteristics.

The parameters of the constitutive models are identified from laboratory tests:

- simple shear, triaxial (consolidation at various confinement values, in a drained or undrained situation), torsion, isotropic compression, oedometer (drained cylindrical specimen subjected to a monotonic uniaxial compression, and to prevented lateral displacements: obtaining a consolidation curve);
- these tests may be monotonic or cyclic, drained or undrained, strain-controlled or stress-controlled;
- resonant column: obtaining the elastic moduli;
- creep, failure, and relaxation for rocks;

whose results must sometimes be corrected in order to reflect the real stress path in place, and from in-situ tests, at various depths in the soil. These tests are supplemented by geological analyses and site investigations:

- SPT (*standard penetration test*), a test carried out by driving a tube, which does not provide a direct measurement of a physical parameter;
- CPT (penetrometers), a monotonic static cone test driven at constant speed, which does not provide a direct measurement of a physical parameter;
- pressuremeter: obtaining the elastic moduli, the strength and the creep moduli; one thus considers, for example, the “Ménard” pressuremeter test carried out by applying an increasing controlled pressure in a vertical cylindrical borehole.
- CPTU (with measurement of the pore pressure);
- shear-wave velocity ${V}_{s}$ at low strain level (*cross-hole*, SASW);
- flat-jack test in order to assess the initial stress state in a rock mass.

These tests produce relations between the number of cycles and the CSR (cyclic stress ratio equal to $\tau /\sigma {'}_{v}$), the overconsolidation state (OCR, linked to the plasticity index ${I}_{p}$), the CRR (cyclic resistance ratio), and the cyclic degradation curves $G-\gamma$. The elastic behaviour domain of soils is limited to very small strains: $\varepsilon <{10}^{-5}$.

In *Code_Aster*, the convention of structural mechanics has been adopted: stresses and strains are positive in tension, and compressive stresses have negative values.

### 1.2 Objectives

The modeller will have to find the best compromise between complexity (of the phenomena, of the calibration of the parameters...), the cost of the study, the robustness of the integration, and the accuracy or the representativeness of the results sought, according to the data available (parameters and laboratory tests) and the physical validation cases available in the loading domain aimed at by the study, by choosing correctly the type of constitutive models, bearing in mind that a wide choice of constitutive models is available in *Code_Aster*, but also in the literature...

This document is designed to help with this task. It brings together items already available elsewhere in the *Code_Aster* documentation, and also provides the experience feedback of the authors who contributed to these constitutive models. Various angles of comparative analysis of these models are thus set out in a generic manner: target materials, phenomenology, characteristics and type of formulation of the law, modellings usable with *Code_Aster*, number and type of parameters, identification procedure from the available tests, number and type of internal variables (in the strict sense: corresponding to the mathematical formulation of the law, but also local variables useful to the analyst: quantities of interest to the engineer), verification and validation test cases, references, publications, general opinion (robustness, developability...), prospects.

### 1.3 Geotechnical structures made of geomaterials as porous media

The geotechnical structure is made up of a soil, or of a rock, generally porous (the solid phase is called the soil skeleton), and this porosity is occupied by fluids: water, air, gas, occluded air, with a variable saturation. These fluids flow within the porous medium and contribute to the mechanical response; various diffusion laws govern these flows, see [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11). These laws correspond to dissipative phenomena, for example Darcy's law, which describes the flow of the fluid phase or phases within the porous matrix, and is parameterised by the permeability or permeabilities. Depending on the boundary conditions of the medium imposed on the fluid or fluids, one is in a *drained* situation or in an *undrained* situation. One should consult the documentation for the use of porous-medium models [U2.04.05](doc:u2.04.05).

The notion of “*effective stresses*” makes it possible to describe the contribution of the soil skeleton to the overall mechanical response of the porous medium, associated with its kinematics, described by the strain tensor $\varepsilon$. This is Terzaghi's principle, which decomposes the total-stress tensor into effective stresses and into the contribution due to the fluid or fluids: $\sigma =\sigma '-{\mathrm{b.p}}_{\mathrm{lq}}\mathrm{Id}$, where ${p}_{\mathrm{lq}}$ denotes the pore pressure (assumed positive in compression in *Code_Aster*) in the single-phase case (*saturated* case), $\sigma '$ the “effective stress” tensor and $b$ the coefficient of the isotropic BIOT tensor; under Terzaghi's assumption, one has: $b\to 1$.

In the case where a gas phase is also present, the liquid saturation ${S}_{\mathrm{lq}}$ is introduced into the following total differential expression: $d\sigma =d\sigma '-\mathrm{b.}(d{p}_{\mathrm{gz}}\mathrm{Id}-{S}_{\mathrm{lq}}.d{p}_{c}\mathrm{Id})$, where ${p}_{\mathrm{gz}}$ denotes the gas pressure (assumed positive in compression in *Code_Aster*) and ${p}_{c}={p}_{\mathrm{gz}}-{p}_{\mathrm{lq}}$ denotes the *capillary pressure*, also called *suction* (positive in the unsaturated state). The notion of “*net stresses*” is then defined: $d\tilde{\sigma}=d\sigma +\mathrm{b.}(d{p}_{\mathrm{gz}}\mathrm{Id})$.

In the absence of a water phase in the soil, in particular above the level of the water table, one may treat the equilibrium problem of the structure in a “purely mechanical” situation. Otherwise, one will treat the equilibrium problem with coupled (thermo-)hydro-mechanical modelling, with the corresponding finite elements.

Whereas the presence of a water phase in the soil has little effect on the shear-wave velocity, on the contrary it does affect the pressure-wave velocity. For a clayey soil above the level of the water table, partial saturation also affects the value of the pressure-wave velocity.

Some models are designed to provide a mechanical response in “total stresses”, which sum the contribution of the skeleton and that of the “water” phase and drive the expression of the criteria.

A mechanical constitutive model defined in effective stresses is designed to describe drained and undrained situations; its identification is preferably carried out according to the type of application envisaged, because the constitutive models do not completely model the interaction between the skeleton and the fluid phase.

The transition from the saturated case to the unsaturated case is delicate, because of the handling of the appearance or disappearance of a phase; Code_Aster treats this transition transparently by means of a modelling of *HH2* type [U2.04.05](doc:u2.04.05). Furthermore, a solution proposed in the literature for treating undrained situations in the presence of occluded air, in the vicinity of full saturation, consists in adopting an equivalent compressibility of the fluid in order to take the occluded air into account, and a corrected cohesion. This equivalent compressibility may be evaluated, for example, with the Boutonnier model, [bib26]_, and chosen as an input parameter of the *code_aster* models.

Joints are characterised by a hydraulic behaviour which is very different in the direction normal to the joint and in the tangential directions, for which the opening and the tortuosity of the joint play an essential and non-linear role. In the reverse sense, the hydraulic flow also affects the failure characteristics of the joint.

## 2 Modelling of the constitutive behaviour according to the type of analysis to be carried out

Soils are first of all porous media: their mechanical response results from the behaviour of the solid skeleton combined with that of the liquid phase, or indeed the gas phase, present in the porosities of the soil, whose flow is characterised by the permeability.

The mechanical models for geomaterials (soils, rocks) may for the most part be used in purely mechanical modellings or in THM modellings, via the keywords KIT_HM, KIT_HHM, KIT_THM, KIT_THHM, within the framework of the *effective-stress* hypothesis, that is, a hypothesis which governs the contribution of the pore pressures of the fluid or fluids present in the porosities to the equilibrium of the porous medium, if one is in a quasi-saturated situation. In the partially saturated case, some models are formulated in *total stresses*, or net stresses.

One area of verification study of a geotechnical structure consists in checking its *bearing capacity*, in particular under the action of monotonic loadings, with respect to various failure modes, in particular by shear and sliding along *failure surfaces*, or else *diffuse failure modes* in the volume of soil...

Another area of verification study of a geotechnical structure consists in the analysis of *settlements* under the action of monotonic or cyclic loadings.

For higher cyclic loadings, for example induced by an earthquake, one must analyse the risk of *liquefaction*, by:

- *loss of strength*, concerning cohesionless soils more than fine clayey soils. Failure by liquefaction results in an increasing *excess pore pressure*, which then causes a deconsolidation of the soil (the effective consolidation pressure of the soil approaches zero), which suddenly loses its shear strength and therefore its bearing capacity, through the vanishing of the effective stresses. This may occur under cyclic or monotonic loading conditions, under undrained conditions, in particular for loose sands. A distinction is made from
- *cyclic mobility*, for fine clayey or dilatant soils, arising from softening and from the evolution of the pore pressures, following the crossing of the critical-state curve. *Failure by cyclic mobility* corresponds to a path on which the mean effective stress decreases rapidly, dilatancy appears, and the cyclic deviatoric strain grows and accumulates until failure.

A local criterion proposed is the excess pore-pressure ratio ${r}_{u}$. This criterion has several variants: an “engineering” variant in which the strength of the soil is expressed with the level of vertical stress estimated in the structure, and another in which it is expressed directly by means of the updated strength calculated by the constitutive law. Both expressions are available as an instantaneous “differential” version with respect to an initial state, or as an absolute instantaneous version. When the value of ${r}_{u}$ tends towards unity, one has “fluidisation” of the soil.

One may use the operator POST_LIQUEFACTION [U4.84.41](doc:u4.84.41), in post-processing of a non-linear equilibrium analysis.

It is possible to use, in order to discretise the kinematics of the medium, 3D volume finite elements [R3.01.00](doc:r3.01.00) or 2D surface finite elements, when this is possible, by resorting to simplifying assumptions such as plane strain, plane stress or axisymmetry, with or without taking hydromechanical coupling into account.

Furthermore, in order to reduce the difficulties associated with constitutive laws in the dilatant phase, which produce a loss of uniqueness of the solutions, *code_aster* proposes regularisation methods, in particular by the first gradient of volumetric dilatation, cf. [R5.04.03](doc:r5.04.03) and the associated finite elements.

> **Note 1.**

A distinction will be made between verification and validation: verification is understood in the sense of the equations modelled and expressed in the software, whereas validation denotes the comparison of the results of the model in the software against reference experimental measurements, in situations representative of the intended applications.

> **Note 2.**

The constitutive laws below are formulated in small transformations (HPP), but it is possible with code_aster to use them within a framework of large transformations (GDEF_LOG models for large plastic strains and GROT_GDEP while remaining in small strains) for studies in pure mechanics.

### 2.2 Identification of the constitutive parameters of soils

One uses tests on soil specimens: triaxial tests (shear, domain of medium and high strains), oedometer tests (measurement of the compressibility and of the consolidation), isotropic compression tests (even though they are more difficult to carry out), cyclic tests, alternated and half-alternated, resonant-column tests (domain of small strains $\le 5.{10}^{-4}$). The link should be made between the parameters of the constitutive models resulting from the experimental measurements and the parameters of interest measured in situ for the engineer, such as “the plasticity index”, “the void ratio”, the cyclic resistance to liquefaction (CSR), the cone resistance (CPT)...

The tests must be carried out with various density indices ${D}_{r}$ (associated with the void ratio ${e}_{0}$) and various confining pressures (in particular in order to identify the critical state properly) and under drained and undrained conditions.

#### 2.2.1 General physical parameters

A first class of general physical parameters characterises the material:

- dry soil density ${\rho}_{s}$,
- saturated soil density,
- initial porosity ${\varphi}_{0}$,
- void ratio ${e}_{0}=\frac{{\varphi}_{0}}{1-{\varphi}_{0}}$ or density index ${D}_{r}$,
- particle-size curve,
- initial degree of saturation ${S}_{0}$,
- plasticity index ${I}_{p}$, linked to the internal friction angle ${\phi }_{\mathrm{pp}}$ and to the Atterberg limits,
- permeability to the liquid and/or to the gas.

#### 2.2.2 Elastic parameters

The second step consists in the identification of the elastic parameters supplied to *code_aster* (drained bulk modulus ${K}_{0}$, shear modulus ${G}_{0}$ in the drained situation), which are highly dependent on the confining pressure ${P}_{\mathrm{réf}}$ or mean stress (via the void ratio). The datum of the coefficient of earth pressure at rest ${k}_{0}$ helps to establish the initial elasticity of the material in place.

One recalls that Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio are expressed by:

${E}_{0}=\frac{9{K}_{0}{G}_{0}}{3{K}_{0}+{G}_{0}}$     and         ${\nu}_{0}=\frac{3{K}_{0}-2{G}_{0}}{6{K}_{0}+2{G}_{0}}$

The expressions in the drained situation involve the Biot coefficient and distinguish the drained compressibility of the porous medium from the compressibility of the solid grains ${K}_{s}$:

$\frac{1}{{K}_{s}}=\frac{1-b}{{K}_{0}}$

For a soil made up of grains (sands, gravels), it may be necessary to supply a parameter describing the non-linear dependence between the stiffness and the applied stress.

*Remarks:*

- comparing numerical predictions with a measurement carried out in situ on the structure, for low-level loadings (in geophysics: use of ambient noise, for example), constitutes a relevant approach for an analysis of an existing structure.
- it is, however, difficult to assess the initial anisotropic characteristics, linked to the geological formation of the soils in place.

The tests carried out in situ are performed, for example, by means of a pressuremeter (radial expansion) or of dynamic penetrometers.

#### 2.2.3 Non-linear constitutive parameters

The following steps consist in the identification of the parameters associated with the description of the cohesion, of the strength (failure criterion) and of the hardening, and of the contractancy and dilatancy domains, which depend on the consolidation (“normally consolidated” case, “overconsolidated” case). In particular, one recalls that the dilatancy angle $\psi$ is less than or equal to the internal friction angle ${\phi }_{\mathrm{pp}}$. It will be noted that the natural equilibrium of a slope made up of a non-cohesive material, whether saturated or dry, requires that the friction angle ${\phi }_{\mathrm{pp}}$ be greater than that of the slope.

Under cyclic conditions, one seeks to identify the *degradation* curves $G-\gamma$, $D-\gamma$ and the CSR.

*code_aster* proposes a specific command to assist with the identification of the constitutive parameters of soils: CALC_ESSAI_GEOMECA, cf. [U4.90.21](doc:u4.90.21). It makes it possible to simulate, for a material point, various loading paths characteristic of the usual geomechanical laboratory tests, monotonic/cyclic, drained/undrained, and to post-process the results obtained:

- monotonic drained triaxial test, strain-controlled;
- monotonic undrained triaxial test, strain-controlled;
- drained cyclic shear test, strain-controlled;
- undrained cyclic triaxial test, force-controlled;
- alternated drained cyclic triaxial test, strain-controlled;
- undrained cyclic triaxial test, strain-controlled;
- drained cyclic oedometer test, force-controlled;
- drained cyclic isotropic compression test, force-controlled.

For its use, one should choose the behaviour, the necessary material parameters, and the loading data of the test considered. For the strain-controlled tests, it is advisable to supply strains of logarithmic type (GDEF_LOG) or Hencky strains, because in many tests the strains leave the framework of small transformations. This large-strain formulation allows the direct extension of the use of the elastoplastic constitutive relations developed within the framework of small transformations.

### 2.4 Identification of the constitutive parameters of rocks

One uses tests on rock specimens: triaxial tests (shear, domain of medium strains, carried out on an unconfined rock sample), creep and relaxation tests, isotropic compression tests. These tests are carried out at different temperatures. Other tests on rock samples are practised: cone indentation tests, for example, in order to assess the elastic characteristics, continued up to failure, extension tests, Brazilian tests. Finally, certain tests are carried out in situ, for example the assessment of the *RQD*.

A first class of parameters characterises the material:

- density of the dry rock,
- density of the saturated rock,
- porosity,
- degree of saturation,
- permeability,
- *RQD* (Rock Quality Designation index), which assesses the discontinuities present in the rock (weathering).

A second class of parameters characterises the material mechanically:

- elastic parameters (static, dynamic),
- creep parameters, cyclic behaviour parameters,
- unconfined uniaxial compressive strength,
- Hoek-Brown strength parameter,
- P- and S-wave velocities.

*code_aster* proposes a specific command to assist with the identification of the constitutive parameters of rocks for the 'DRUCK_PRAGER' models: CALC_ESSAI_GEOMECA, cf. [U4.90.21](doc:u4.90.21). It makes it possible to simulate, for a material point, various loading paths characteristic of the usual geomechanical laboratory tests, monotonic/cyclic, drained/undrained, and to post-process the results obtained.

For its use, one should choose the behaviour, the necessary material parameters, and the loading data of the test considered.

### 2.5 Modellings of the constitutive behaviour of joints

The *interfaces* of media made up of geomaterials must also be modelled. One cannot always be satisfied with a perfect modelling by simple continuity of the displacements, equivalent to continuity of the stress vector at the interface, supplemented, for a porous medium, by the continuity of the pore pressures.

The joint appears as a discontinuity, rough, possibly reinforced by a filling material.

*code_aster* has particular *joint* finite elements for studies in pure mechanics in 2D and in 3D (XXX_JOINT).

Particular *joint* finite elements for coupled hydromechanical studies (XXX_JOINT_HYME in 2D and in 3D, and XXX_JHMS only in 2D) are also available: see [R3.06.09](doc:r3.06.09) and [R7.02.15](doc:r7.02.15): they represent the displacement and the fluid pressure in the joint, in particular a fluid flow inside it (cubic law, see [R7.01.25](doc:r7.01.25)). The introduction of the fluid into the joint modifies the normal mechanical stress.

The *joint* (or crack) finite elements for coupled hydromechanical studies (saturated medium) in 2D, with a 1D support, see [R7.02.15](doc:r7.02.15), (PLAN_JHMS, AXIS_JHMS), aim to model the displacement discontinuity across the joint and the Darcy flow along the joint. The pressure field is considered to be continuous across the interface, whereas flux discontinuities are allowed across the interface (unlike the XXX_JOINT_HYME finite elements), and a cubic law describes the volume flux as a function of the pressure gradient along the interface: the intrinsic permeability is therefore cubic as a function of the joint opening (discontinuity of the normal displacement).

The parameters for modelling the hydraulic behaviour of the joint (permeability, porosity, Biot modulus, fluid viscosity...) are those of porous media, cf. [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11).

One may thus take into account the propagation of uplift pressures at a concrete–rock interface, and a fluid flow within a crack in a porous medium. ### 2.6 Identification of the constitutive parameters of joints

It is generally accepted that the adhesion limit at the interface is less than or equal to the value of the cohesion of the least resistant material in contact at the interface.

## 3 What Code_Aster cannot (yet) do

No model is available directly in *code_aster* to represent:

- the dependence of the permeability on the degradation state of the geomaterial;
- the dependence of the cohesion on the saturation state.
