# r7.02.05 Computation of the stress intensity factors in linear thermoelasticity

**Summary:**

One presents the method for computing the stress intensity factors ${K}_{I}$ , ${K}_{\mathrm{II}}$ and ${K}_{\mathrm{III}}$ in linear thermoelasticity. The formulation considers the energy release rate as a symmetric bilinear form of the displacement field $u$ and uses the explicit expressions of the singular displacement fields known in plane linear elasticity.

This method can be used by means of the option CALC_K_G of the command CALC_G, both for a meshed crack (classical finite elements) and for a non-meshed crack (enriched finite elements: X-FEM method).

This method can also be used to compute the stress intensity factors associated with the vibration eigenmodes of a structure.

## 1 Expressions of the stress intensity factors in linear thermoelasticity

### 1.1 Presentation in plane linear thermoelasticity

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.05/10000000000002330000014D87ABF7EBE5F9EADC.png)

Let the Cartesian coordinate axes be $O{x}_{1}$ in the prolongation of the crack and $O{x}_{2}$ perpendicular to the crack. The problem is plane. We shall express the Cartesian components of the displacements and of the stresses as a function of the polar coordinates $r$ and $\theta$ .

In linear elasticity, the system of the equilibrium equations, without body force, and the homogeneous boundary conditions on the crack, the stresses zero at infinity, admit a non-trivial solution of the form ${u}_{i}=\sqrt{(r)}{g}_{i}(\theta )$ . The stresses are infinite at the crack tip like ${r}^{-1/2}$ [bib3].

For an arbitrary problem in plane linear elasticity (plane strains or plane stresses), the displacement field $u$ can be decomposed into a singular part and a regular part. The singular part, also called the singularity, is the one made explicit above. It is associated with the stress intensity factors $K$ . In linear elasticity, the fracture modes $I$ (opening) and $\mathrm{II}$ (in-plane sliding) are separated:

$u={u}_{R}+{K}_{I}{u}_{S}^{I}+{K}_{\mathit{II}}{u}_{S}^{\mathit{II}}$

with:

$\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{u}_{\mathit{S1}}^{I}=\frac{1+\nu }{E}{(\frac{r}{2\pi })}^{1/2}\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})(k-\cos\theta )\\ {u}_{\mathit{S2}}^{I}=\frac{1+\nu }{E}{(\frac{r}{2\pi })}^{1/2}\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})(k-\cos\theta )\end{array}$

$\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{u}_{\mathit{S1}}^{\mathit{II}}=\frac{1+\nu }{E}{(\frac{r}{2\pi })}^{1/2}\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})(k+\cos\theta -2)\\ {u}_{\mathit{S2}}^{\mathit{II}}=-\frac{1+\nu }{E}{(\frac{r}{2\pi })}^{1/2}\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})(k+\cos\theta -2)\end{array}$

where:

| $k=3-4\nu$ | in plane strains D_PLAN |
| --- | --- |
| $k=(3-\nu )/(1+\nu )$ | in plane stresses C_PLAN |

and:

$E$ YOUNG's modulus

$\nu$ POISSON's ratio

The distribution of the singular stresses in the vicinity of the crack is given by the formulae:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{11}^{S}={K}_{I}{\sigma}_{11}^{I}+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}{\sigma}_{11}^{\mathrm{II}}\\ {\sigma}_{12}^{S}={K}_{I}{\sigma}_{12}^{I}+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}{\sigma}_{12}^{\mathrm{II}}\\ {\sigma}_{22}^{S}={K}_{I}{\sigma}_{22}^{I}+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}{\sigma}_{22}^{\mathrm{II}}\end{array}$

with:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{11}^{I}=\frac{1}{{(2\pi r)}^{1/2}}\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})(1-\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})\sin(\frac{3\theta }{2}))\\ {\sigma}_{12}^{I}=\frac{1}{{(2\pi r)}^{1/2}}\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})\cos(\frac{3\theta }{2})\\ {\sigma}_{22}^{I}=\frac{1}{{(2\pi r)}^{1/2}}\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})(1+\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})\sin(\frac{3\theta }{2}))\end{array}$

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{11}^{\mathrm{II}}=-\frac{1}{{(2\pi r)}^{1/2}}\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})(2+\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})\cos(\frac{3\theta }{2}))\\ {\sigma}_{12}^{\mathrm{II}}=\frac{1}{{(2\pi r)}^{1/2}}\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})(1-\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})\sin(\frac{3\theta }{2}))\\ {\sigma}_{22}^{\mathrm{II}}=\frac{1}{{(2\pi r)}^{1/2}}\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})\cos(\frac{3\theta }{2})\end{array}$

**Note:** the expression of the singular displacement and stress fields established in 2D – plane strains can be extended to the 2D – axisymmetric case [bib12]. The singular functions are however valid only asymptotically, in other words the distance *r*  to the crack tip must remain small compared with the radius of the crack tip.

### 1.2 Extension to the 3D case

Besides the plane modes of opening and of in-plane sliding, a third mode of antiplane sliding (characterised by ${K}_{\mathrm{III}}$ ) can be defined. The singular fields associated with mode $\mathrm{III}$ are identified by solving the equilibrium equations of an infinite cracked plane medium for displacements only along the axis ${x}_{3}$ :

> $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{u}_{\mathrm{S1}}^{m}=0\\ {u}_{\mathrm{S2}}^{m}=0\\ {u}_{\mathrm{S3}}^{m}=\frac{4(1+\nu )}{E}{(\frac{r}{2\pi })}^{1/2}\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})\end{array}$     and     $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{31}^{m}=-\frac{1}{{(2\pi r)}^{1/2}}\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})\\ {\sigma}_{32}^{m}=\frac{1}{{(2\pi r)}^{1/2}}\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})\end{array}$

In the three-dimensional case, one can show that the asymptotic behaviour of the displacements and of the stresses is the sum of the solutions corresponding to the modes $I$ and $\mathrm{II}$ (in plane strains) and to mode $\mathrm{III}$ (antiplane), and of four other particular solutions, but which are more regular than the preceding ones [bib9]. The principal term of the singular fields therefore remains unchanged, and the displacement field in 3D is then expressed in the following manner:

$u(s)={u}_{R}+{K}_{I}(s){u}_{S}^{I}+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}(s){u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}}+{K}_{\mathrm{III}}(s){u}_{S}^{\mathrm{III}}$

where $s$ is the curvilinear abscissa along the crack front and ${u}_{S}^{I}$ , ${u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}}$ and ${u}_{S}^{\mathrm{III}}$ are the singular displacements defined in the preceding paragraph (expressed, for each point of the crack front, in a suitable local frame).

### 1.3 IRWIN's formula and energy release rate G

In linear elasticity, the stress intensity factors are related to the energy release rate $G$ by IRWIN's formula:

$G=\frac{1-{\nu}^{2}}{E}({K}_{I}^{2}+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}^{2})$ in plane strains

$G=\frac{1}{E}({K}_{I}^{2}+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}^{2})$ in plane stresses

$G(s)=\frac{1-{\nu}^{2}}{E}({K}_{I}^{2}(s)+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}^{2}(s))+\frac{{K}_{\mathrm{III}}^{2}(s)}{2\mu }$ , with $\mu =\frac{E}{2(1+\nu )}$ , in 3D

One can show that this relation remains valid in the presence of initial stresses (hence in affine elasticity).

The energy release rate $G$ is defined by the opposite of the derivative of the potential energy with respect to the propagation of the crack tip. In *Code_Aster* , $G$ is computed by the theta method [bib5 and documentation R7.02.01] which is a Lagrangian method of derivation of the potential energy.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.05/10000A8400001639000011C70A99DF73D9087756.svg)

Thus, one considers transformations $M\to M+\eta \theta (M)$ of the reference domain ${\Omega}_{0}$ into a domain ${\Omega}_{\eta}$ which correspond to propagations of the crack. To these families of reference configuration thus defined ${\Omega}_{\eta}$ there correspond families of deformed configurations whose crack has propagated. The potential energy defined on ${\Omega}_{\eta}$ is brought back onto ${\Omega}_{0}$ .

One considers the surface forces $F$ and the body forces $f$  applied respectively to ${\Gamma}_{F}$ and ${\Omega}_{0}$ . One denotes by $\Psi (\varepsilon (u))$ the free-energy density, $u$ the displacement field, $T$ the temperature field and $\theta$  the vector field describing the direction of the transport at $\eta =0$ , $\Lambda$ the elasticity tensor

and ${\sigma}^{0}$ the initial-stress field, then the general expression of the energy release rate $G$ [bib5] is:

$\begin{array}{ccc}G=& {\int}_{\Omega}\left[\sigma (u):(\nabla u.\nabla \theta )-\Psi (\epsilon (u))div\theta \right]d\Omega & \leftarrow \mathit{terme}\mathit{classique}\\ & -{\int}_{\Omega}\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial T}(\nabla T.\theta )d\Omega & \leftarrow \mathit{terme}\mathit{dû}à\mathit{la}\mathit{thermique}\\ & +{\int}_{\Omega}\left[(\nabla f.\theta )u+\mathit{f.}udiv\theta \right]d\Omega & \leftarrow \mathit{terme}\mathit{dû}\mathit{aux}\mathit{forces}\mathit{volumiques}f\mathit{sur}\Omega \\ & -{\int}_{\Omega}\left[({\Lambda}^{-1}:\sigma (u)):(\nabla {\sigma}^{0}.\theta )\right]d\Omega & \leftarrow \mathit{terme}\mathit{dû}à\mathit{la}\mathit{contrainte}\mathit{initiale}\\ & +{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{F}}\left[(\nabla F.\theta )u+F.u(div\theta -n.\frac{\partial \theta }{\partial n})\right]d\Gamma & \leftarrow \mathit{terme}\mathit{dû}\mathit{aux}\mathit{forces}\mathit{surfaciques}F\mathit{sur}{\Gamma}_{F}\\ & -{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{u}}\left[(\sigma .n).(\nabla u.\theta )\right]d\Gamma & \leftarrow \mathit{terme}\mathit{dû}\mathit{aux}\mathit{déplacements}\mathit{imposés}\mathit{sur}{\Gamma}_{u}\end{array}$

In linear elasticity, $G$ can be considered as a symmetric bilinear form of the displacement field $u$ . The elastic energy density $\Psi (\varepsilon (u))$ is written:

$\Psi (\epsilon (u))=\frac{1}{2}\epsilon (u):\Lambda :\epsilon (u)=\frac{1}{2}B(u,u)$

denoting:

$B$ the symmetric bilinear form defined by: $B(u,v)=\varepsilon (u):\Lambda :\varepsilon (u)$

and the bilinear form ${g}_{\mathit{cla}}(.,.)$ associated with the classical term of $G$ is defined by:

$\begin{array}{cc}{g}_{\mathit{cla}}(u,v)=& \frac{1}{2}{\int}_{\Omega}[\frac{\partial B}{\partial \nabla u}.(\nabla v.\nabla \theta )+\frac{\partial B}{\partial \nabla v}.(\nabla u.\nabla \theta )-B(u,v)div\theta ]d\Omega \\ & \end{array}$

The construction of the other terms (body and surface forces, thermal terms, initial stresses and imposed displacements) is defined in each sub-part.

One has $G=g(u,u)$ if $u$ is the solution of the elastic problem.

### 1.4 Decoupling of the fracture modes

To decouple the three fracture modes and compute the three associated stress intensity factors, one uses the symmetric bilinear form $g(.,.)$ and the decomposition of the displacement field $u$ into regular and singular parts [bib7]. To simplify the presentation, one first places oneself in the plane case, but the properties generalise without difficulty to the 3D case.

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}g(u,{u}_{S}^{I})=g({u}_{R}+{K}_{I}{u}_{S}^{I}+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}},{u}_{S}^{I})=g({u}_{R},{u}_{S}^{I})+{K}_{I}g({u}_{S}^{I},{u}_{S}^{I})+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}g({u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}},{u}_{S}^{I})\\ g(u,{u}_{S}^{I})=g({u}_{R}+{K}_{I}{u}_{S}^{I}+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}},{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})=g({u}_{R},{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})+{K}_{I}g({u}_{S}^{I},{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}g({u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}},{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})\end{array}$

One shows in plane linear thermoelasticity that ${u}_{S}^{I}$ and ${u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}}$ are orthogonal for the scalar product defined by the bilinear form $g(.,.)$ and that the terms involving the regular part vanish:

$\begin{array}{ccc}g({u}_{S}^{I},{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})=& g({u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}},{u}_{S}^{I})=& 0\\ g({u}^{R},{u}_{S}^{I})=& g({u}^{R},{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})=& 0\end{array}$

One therefore finally has:

$\begin{array}{ccc}g(u,{u}_{S}^{I})& =& {K}_{I}g({u}_{S}^{I},{u}_{S}^{I})\\ g({u}^{R},{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})& =& {K}_{\mathrm{II}}g({u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}},{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})\end{array}$

Moreover, by writing the energy release rate in the form:

$G=g(u,u)=g({u}_{R}+{K}_{I}{u}_{S}^{I}+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}},{u}_{R}+{K}_{I}{u}_{S}^{I}+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})$

one recovers IRWIN's formula by reusing the orthogonality properties:

$g(u,u)={K}_{I}^{2}g({u}_{S}^{I},{u}_{S}^{I})+{K}_{\mathrm{II}}^{2}g({u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}},{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})$

with:

$\begin{array}{ccccc}g({u}_{S}^{I},{u}_{S}^{I})& =& g({u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}},{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})& =& \frac{1-{\nu}^{2}}{E}\end{array}$ in C_PLAN

$\begin{array}{ccccc}g({u}_{S}^{I},{u}_{S}^{I})& =& g({u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}},{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})& =& \frac{1}{E}\end{array}$ in D_PLAN

Finally, in a general manner:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{K}_{I}=Eg(u,{u}_{S}^{I})\\ {K}_{\mathrm{II}}=Eg(u,{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})\end{array}$         in C_PLAN

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{K}_{I}=\frac{E}{1-{\nu}^{2}}g(u,{u}_{S}^{I})\\ {K}_{\mathrm{II}}=\frac{E}{1-{\nu}^{2}}g(u,{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{II}})\end{array}$     in D_PLAN and in 3D

and ${K}_{\mathrm{III}}=2\mu .g(u,{u}_{S}^{\mathrm{III}})$     in 3D

The implementation of the computation of the stress intensity factors in linear thermoelasticity in *Code_Aster*  is carried out from the expression of the energy release rate

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.05/Object_76.svg)

in linear elasticity, written in symmetric bilinear form, by introducing the known expressions of the singular displacements, and by using the theta method.

#### 2.3.3 Body-force term

$\mathrm{TFOR}=(\nabla f.\theta )u+f.udiv\theta$

Rigorously, this term is linear and not quadratic in displacement. The construction of the associated symmetric bilinear form is therefore not trivial. One then considers that there exists a dependence of the force on the displacement field; one denotes by ${f}_{u}$ the body forces associated with the displacement field $u$ for the elastic problem and ${f}_{v}$ the body forces associated with the fictitious admissible field $v$ . One can then construct a symmetric bilinear expression of $\mathit{TFOR}$ $(u,v)$ as follows:

$\mathrm{TFOR}(u,v)=\frac{1}{2}[(\nabla {f}_{u}.\theta )v+(\nabla {f}_{v}.\theta )u+({f}_{u}.v+{f}_{v}.u)div\theta ]$

One will note well that $\mathit{TFOR}(u,u)=\mathit{TFOR}$ .

As the expressions which we are led to compute are of the type $\mathrm{TFOR}(u,u)$ and $\mathit{TFOR}(u,{u}_{S})$ , where $u$ and ${u}_{S}$ are respectively the displacement field and the singular field, and that:

${f}_{{u}^{S}}=div(\sigma ({u}_{S}))=0$ on $\Omega$

One restricts oneself to writing:

$\mathrm{TFOR}(u,v)=\mathrm{CS}[(\nabla {F}_{u}.\theta )v+{F}_{u}.vdiv\theta ]$ with $\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\mathit{CS}=0.5& v={u}_{S}\\ \mathit{CS}=1& v=u\end{array}$

The same note is valid for the classical thermal term, the supplementary term due to the thermal part and the terms due to the surface forces.

The body-force terms and the initial-stress term generate terms that are linear and not quadratic in $u$ and whose associated bilinear form is therefore more delicate to construct,

To do this, let us consider that the initial stresses and the body forces also depend on the displacement field; one then introduces

#### 2.3.4 Initial-stress term

${\mathit{TINI}}_{1}=-{\int}_{\Omega}\left[({\Lambda}^{-1}:\sigma (u)):(\nabla {\sigma}^{0}.\theta )\right]d\Omega$

Rigorously, this term is linear and not quadratic in displacement. The construction of the associated symmetric bilinear form is therefore not trivial. One carries out the same reasoning as for the body-force term, considering that there exists a dependence of the force on the displacement field; one denotes by ${\sigma}_{u}^{0}$ the body forces associated with the displacement field $u$ for the elastic problem and ${\sigma}_{v}^{0}$ the body forces associated with the fictitious admissible field $v$ . One can then construct a symmetric bilinear expression of ${\mathit{TINI}}_{1}$ as follows:

${\mathit{TINI}}_{1}\left(u,v\right)=-\frac{1}{2}{\int}_{\Omega}\left[({\Lambda}^{-1}:{\sigma}_{u}):(\nabla {\sigma}_{v}^{0}.\theta )+({\Lambda}^{-1}:{\sigma}_{v}):(\nabla {\sigma}_{u}^{0}.\theta )\right]d\Omega$

with ${\sigma}_{u}=\Lambda :\mathit{sym}(\nabla u)+{\sigma}_{u}^{0}$ and ${\sigma}_{v}=\Lambda :\mathit{sym}(\nabla v)+{\sigma}_{v}^{0}$

In the presence of initial stresses, the formulation of the free energy and the stress/strain relation are also modified.

$\Psi (\epsilon (u),{\sigma}^{0})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\epsilon (u)+{\Lambda}^{-1}:{\sigma}^{0}\right):\Lambda :\left(\epsilon (u)+{\Lambda}^{-1}:{\sigma}^{0}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\epsilon (u)-{\epsilon}_{\mathit{ref}}\right):\Lambda :\left(\epsilon (u)-{\epsilon}_{\mathit{ref}}\right)$ $\sigma =\Lambda :\epsilon (u)+{\sigma}^{0}$

with ${\epsilon}_{\mathit{ref}}=-{\Lambda}^{-1}:{\sigma}^{0}$

This adds two terms linked to the classical term $\sigma (u):(\nabla u\nabla \theta )-\Psi (\epsilon (u))div\theta$ :

- a term linked to the addition of the initial stress in the stress: ${\mathit{TINI}}_{2}={\sigma}^{0}:(\nabla u\nabla \theta )$
- a term linked to the modification of the free energy: ${\mathit{TINI}}_{3}=-\frac{1}{2}\left(2\epsilon (u)-{\epsilon}_{\mathit{ref}}\right):{\sigma}^{0}div\theta$

One carries out the same reasoning and one constructs the terms of the associated bilinear form:

- ${\mathit{TINI}}_{2}\left(u,v\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left({\sigma}_{v}^{0}:\nabla u+{\sigma}_{u}^{0}:\nabla v\right).\nabla \theta$
- ${\mathit{TINI}}_{3}\left(u,v\right)=-\frac{1}{4}\left(\left(2\epsilon (u)\right):{\sigma}_{v}^{0}+\left(2\epsilon (v)\right):{\sigma}_{u}^{0}+2{\sigma}_{u}^{0}:{\Lambda}^{-1}:{\sigma}_{v}^{0}\right)div\theta$

Two situations arise: either $v=u$ (determination of G), or $v={u}_{S}$ (determination of the stress intensity factors). In this second case, one must remember that the initial-stress field is determined before the appearance of the crack, and therefore is a priori not compatible with a singular field. Its contribution to the displacements in fact intervenes through the total stress in equilibrium with the displacement field.

One therefore writes:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}{\sigma}_{v}^{0}={\sigma}^{0}& \mathit{si}v=u\\ {\sigma}_{v}^{0}=0& \mathit{si}v={u}_{S}\end{array}$

Finally, for the computation of the energy release rate:

- ${\mathit{TINI}}_{1}\left(u,u\right)=-{\int}_{\Omega}\left[(\epsilon \left(u\right)+{\Lambda}^{-1}:{\sigma}^{0}):(\nabla {\sigma}^{0}.\theta )\right]d\Omega$
- ${\mathit{TINI}}_{2}\left(u,u\right)={\int}_{\Omega}\left({\sigma}^{0}:\nabla u\right).\nabla \theta d\Omega$
- ${\mathit{TINI}}_{3}\left(u,u\right)=-\frac{1}{2}{\int}_{\Omega}\left(\left(2\epsilon (u)\right):{\sigma}^{0}+{\sigma}^{0}:{\Lambda}^{-1}:{\sigma}^{0}\right)div\theta d\Omega$

And for the stress intensity factors:

- ${\mathit{TINI}}_{1}\left(u,{u}_{S}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}{\int}_{\Omega}\left[(\epsilon \left({u}_{S}\right)):(\nabla {\sigma}^{0}.\theta )\right]d\Omega$
- ${\mathit{TINI}}_{2}\left(u,{u}_{S}\right)=\frac{1}{2}{\int}_{\Omega}\left({\sigma}^{0}:\nabla {u}_{S}\right).\nabla \theta d\Omega$
- ${\mathit{TINI}}_{3}\left(u,{u}_{S}\right)=-\frac{1}{2}{\int}_{\Omega}\left(\left(\epsilon ({u}_{S})\right):{\sigma}^{0}\right)div\theta d\Omega$

#### 2.3.5 Surface-force term

The surface-force term is written:

$\mathrm{TSUR}=(\nabla F.\theta )u+\mathrm{F.}u(div\theta -\mathrm{n.}\frac{\partial \theta }{\partial n})$

The term in $\mathrm{n.}\frac{\partial \theta }{\partial n}$ is zero because the gradient of $\theta$ is orthogonal to $n$ . As for the body-force term, the bilinear expression

#### 2.3.6 Modal-dynamics term

In the case of dynamic problems, a supplementary term

appears in the decomposition of the energy release rate expressed in paragraph  1.3 :

$\mathit{TDYN}=-{\int}_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\rho \dot{u}\ddot{u}.div\sigma .d\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\rho \dot{u}\nabla \dot{u}.\theta .d\Omega +{\int}_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2}\rho \ddot{u}\nabla u.\theta .d\Omega$

where $\dot{u}$ and $\ddot{u}$ are respectively the first and second derivatives of the displacement field

$\mathit{TDYN}(u,v)=-\frac{1}{2}(\rho {\omega}^{2}{u}_{k}{v}_{k,j}{\theta}_{j}+\rho {\omega}^{2}{v}_{k}{u}_{k,j}{\theta}_{j})$ .

### 2.4 Singular displacement fields and their derivatives

The singular fields ${u}_{S}^{I}$ , ${u}_{S}^{\mathit{II}}$ and ${u}_{S}^{\mathit{III}}$ , associated respectively with the modes $I$ , $\mathit{II}$ and $\mathit{III}$ , are known explicitly as well as their derivatives. They are written as a function of the polar coordinates in the frame attached to the crack.

The successive introduction of these singular fields makes it possible, as indicated in §1, to compute elementarily the stress intensity factors ${K}_{I}$ , ${K}_{\mathit{II}}$ and ${K}_{\mathit{III}}$ .

### 2.5 Post-processing of the results of KI and KII

#### 2.5.1 For 2D problems

${\Omega}_{m}^{0}\equiv \Omega$

Let ${K}_{I}(\eta ,m)$ , ${K}_{\mathrm{II}}(\eta ,m)$  , $G(\eta ,m)$ be the stress intensity factors and the energy release rate of ${\Omega}_{m}^{\eta}$ subjected to the same loading as $\Omega$ .

#### 2.5.2 For 3D problems

The propagation direction of a crack in 3D can be determined by the principle of the *Maximum Hoop Stress Criterion* (maximisation of the circumferential stress) [bib11]. The propagation angle is then expressed in the following manner:

$\beta =2\arctan[\frac{1}{4}.(\frac{{K}_{I}}{{K}_{\mathit{II}}}-\mathit{sign}({K}_{\mathit{II}}).\sqrt{{(\frac{{K}_{I}}{{K}_{\mathit{II}}})}^{2}+8})]$

L’angle $\beta$ , computed systematically, is indicated in the result table produced by the command CALC_G (column BETA).
