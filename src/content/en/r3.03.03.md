# r3.03.03 3D–beam, 2D–beam, shell–3D, 3D–3D and 2D–2D connections

**Summary:**

This document explains the principle adopted for connecting:

- a 3D or 2D continuum modelling and a beam modelling,
- a 3D continuum modelling and a shell or plate modelling.
- a continuum modelling and a continuum modelling of the same dimension.

For the 3D-beam or 2D-beam modelling, this connection takes the form of linear relations linking the displacements of the nodes of the structure:

- In 3D, six linear relations linking the displacements of all the 3D nodes (3 degrees of freedom per node) to the beam node, which has 6 degrees of freedom.
- In 2D, three linear relations linking the displacements of all the 2D nodes (2 degrees of freedom per node) to the beam node, which has 3 degrees of freedom.

For the 3D-shell modelling, the connection also ensures compatibility between the displacements and rotations of the shell nodes (5 degrees of freedom) and the 3 degrees of freedom of the solid part.

## 1 Presentation

The command used to handle the connections between beam elements and 2D or 3D elements is [AFFE_CHAR_MECA](doc:u4.44.01), via the keyword `LIAISON_ELEM` with the option `OPTION='3D_POU'` or `OPTION='2D_POU'`.
The connection between solid elements and shell elements also uses this command, with the option `OPTION='COQ_3D'`.
The reader may refer to [:ref:`R3.03.06 <R3.03.06>`] for further details on the link between beam elements and shell elements.

## 2 The 3D-shell connection

### 2.1 Objective

The main aim is to have a connection model between a shell structure and a three-dimensional 3D structure, using a link formulation by a weak variational method.
This more rigorous approach makes it possible to ensure a consistent and accurate transmission of the mechanical forces.

![Figure](/figures/r3.03.03/shell_solid.png)

*Connection between a shell and a 3D model*

### 2.2 The framework of the connection

The coupling between the two domains (solid and shell) is formulated using a weak variational approach, leading to a mixed system of Lagrangian type.
The global system is then written in the following form:

where:

- $\tensTwo{K}$ is the global stiffness matrix arising from the assembly of the domains,
- $\disp = (\disp_v, \disp_s) \in \mathbf{V}_v \times \mathbf{V}_s$ gathers the displacement and rotation unknowns of the two subdomains,
- $\lambda_l$ and $\lambda_c$ are the Lagrange multipliers associated respectively with the shell-solid link conditions and with any boundary conditions,
- $\tensTwo{B}_l$ and $\tensTwo{B}_c$ represent the link and boundary-condition operators, respectively.

The variational equation associated with the kinematic link constraint is written:

$$
\int_{\Gamma } \lambda_l \, \gamma_{\Gamma}(\disp_v - \disp_s) \, d\mu = 0
\quad \forall \lambda_l \in W_{\lambda}
$$

which amounts to imposing:

$$
\mathbb{P}_{W_{\lambda}} \left( \gamma_{\Gamma}(\disp_v - \disp_s) \right) = \tensTwo{B}_l \disp = 0
$$

where:

- $W_{\lambda}$ is the space of the Lagrange multipliers
- $\mathbb{P}_{W_{\lambda}}$ denotes the orthogonal projection operator onto the space $W_{\lambda}$ in the sense of the $L^2(\Gamma)$ scalar product.

This guarantees the weak continuity of the displacements at the interface $\Gamma$ between the two models, thus ensuring a mechanically consistent coupling.

### 2.3 Invertibility of the system: discrete case

Let $h$ be a parameter characterising the fineness of the mesh. Independence of the constraints is assumed, that is:

$$
\ker \tensTwo{B}_l + \ker \tensTwo{B}_c = \mathbf{V}^h_v \times \mathbf{V}^h_s
$$

In this case, a necessary condition for invertibility is the **surjectivity** of $\tensTwo{B}_l$. This amounts to checking the following inequality:

$$
\sup_{\disp \in \mathbf{V}^h_v \times \mathbf{V}^h_s}
\frac{ \langle \lambda_l, \tensTwo{B}_l \disp \rangle }
     { \lVert \disp \rVert_{\mathbf{V}^h_v \times \mathbf{V}^h_s} }
\geq c(h) \lVert \lambda_l \rVert
\quad \text{avec } c(h) > 0
$$

where $\langle \cdot , \cdot \rangle$ denotes the scalar product in $L^2(\Gamma)$.

#### 2.3.1 Space of the Lagrange multipliers

If a Lagrange-multiplier space is chosen such that:

$$
W_{\lambda}^h = \gamma_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{V}^h_s)
$$

that is, if functions of the following form are considered:

$$
\lambda_l(s, t) = \lambda_1(s) + \frac{t}{2} \lambda_2(s)
$$

The functions $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ belong to the same space as that used for the shell kinematics induced by the trace operator.
In this case, the previous stability condition is satisfied, but the stability constant nevertheless remains dependent on the mesh.

Note in passing that the **quality of the link** also depends on the following **level of inclusion**:

$$
\mathbb{P}_{W_{\lambda}^h} \left( \gamma_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{V}^h_v) \right)
\subset \gamma_{\Gamma}(\mathbf{V}^h_s)
$$

When this inclusion is an equality, this means that the discretisation used for the solid part does reproduce the shell kinematics at the interface.

## 3 The 3D-beam connection

### 3.1 Objectives and excluded solutions

![Figure](/figures/r3.03.03/raccord3DPoutre.svg)

*Connections between beams and a 3D model*

When one wishes to analyse in detail a part of a complex slender structure [r3.03.03-fig-3D-poutre], one may, in order to minimise the size of the mesh to be handled, wish to represent the structure by a beam "far" from the part to be analysed. The purpose of the beam idealisation is to bring realistic boundary conditions to the edges of the part modelled and meshed as a 3D continuum. The 3D‑beam connection must therefore satisfy the following requirements:

- **P1**: be able to transmit the beam forces (torsor) to the 3D mesh
- **P2**: not generate 'spurious' stresses (or even stress concentrations), because the connection would then have to be placed far enough from the zone to be analysed for these disturbances to be attenuated in the zone
- **P3**: not favour the kinematic connection conditions or the static connection conditions one with respect to the other. It must be equivalent to bring a force torsor or a displacement torsor back to the boundaries of the 3D domain.
- **P4**: admit arbitrary constitutive behaviours on either side of the connection (elasticity, plasticity…) and also allow a dynamic analysis.

If these objectives are met, the connection rules can also be used to treat the problem of the clamping of a beam into a 3D solid. However, the stress distribution in the solid around the clamping will remain rather coarse and must be used with caution. It is preferable to mesh the connection in 3D and then to extend the start of the 3D mesh of the beam cross-section by one of the beam elements with a 3D/beam connection [r3.03.03-fig-3D-poutreInside].

![Figure](/figures/r3.03.03/raccord3DPoutreInside.svg)

*Connection between a beam and a 3D model*

In view of objectives 1 to 4, two common connection techniques can already be ruled out:

1. the first, which reduces the whole connection to the treatment of link conditions between the facing points at the intersection of the neutral axis of the beam and the 3D solid. Apart from the difficulty of correctly defining the "pointwise" rotation of the material point belonging to the 3D solid, the forces are concentrated (concentrated reaction, torque) at this point and the kinematic/static symmetry is broken by favouring a particular kinematics.
2. the second solution, which fully imposes a beam displacement (Navier-Bernoulli) on the points of the 3D solid lying at the intersection of the 3D solid and the beam cross-section. In elasticity, it is known that the assumption of undeformability of the cross-sections in their plane is only an approximation. Correct from the energy point of view for the beam, it leads to stress concentrations in the neighbourhood of the boundaries of the junction cross-section for the 3D solid.

**Note**

It goes without saying that everything presented here is valid only under the small-perturbation assumption (small displacements).

### 3.2 Orientation

We shall start from the mechanical elements of the connection for the three-dimensional solid:

- the stress vector field $\stress \, \normal$ defined on the trace of the beam cross-section $S$ on the 3D solid, $\normal$ being the normal to the plane of $S$ ,
- the displacement field ${\disp}^{\text{3D}}$ defined on this same domain,

and for the beam:

- the torsor $\mathcal{T}$ of the beam forces at the geometric centre of inertia $G$ of $S$ ,
- the torsor $\mathcal{D}$ of the beam displacements at this same point,

These mechanical quantities are related by:

- the kinematic continuity conditions,
- the equilibrium conditions of the connection.

The first conditions are the link conditions to be imposed in a "displacement" approach; the second are deduced from the weak formulation of equilibrium via the virtual work of the contact actions between beam and solid (which is nothing other than the expression of the action-and-reaction principle written for the interface $S$ ). On the surface $S$ , one indeed has, for every admissible virtual displacement $(\vector{v}, \vector{T}, \vector{\Omega} )$:

$$
{\int} \stress \, \normal \cdot \vector{v} \, \, dS
=
\vector{F} \cdot \vector{T}
+
\vector{M} \cdot \vector{\Omega}
$$

where

- $\vector{T}$ and $\vector{\Omega}$ are respectively the translation and the infinitesimal rotation of the beam: $\mathcal{D}=(\vector{T}, \vector{\Omega} )$
- $\vector{F}$ and $\vector{M}$ are respectively the resultant and the moment in the beam at the connection point: $\mathcal{T}=(\vector{F}, \vector{M})$

The left-hand side of this equality will supply the scalar product by means of which the "beam component" of a 3D displacement field defined on $S$ will be defined. By using this scalar product, the symmetry of the approach between the kinematic and static connection conditions (P3) will be ensured, as well as the possibility of treating arbitrary constitutive behaviours on either side of the connection (P4), since no aspect of the constitutive behaviour appears in the equilibrium equality used.

**The approach**

The 3D displacement field will be decomposed into a "beam" part and a "complementary" part. This will lead us quite naturally to define the kinematic connection conditions between the beam and the 3D solid as the equality of the beam displacement (torsor) and of the beam part of the 3D displacement field. Once this is done, the equality will allow us to interpret the connection conditions in static terms and thus to reach the static connection conditions.

These boundary conditions may be brought by a beam, but also by discrete elements. The 2D/beam connection must therefore satisfy the following requirements:

## 5 Solid 3D-3D and 2D-2D connection

### 5.1 Objective

The aim of this connection is to connect the solution at the interface of two potentially non-conforming meshes by using a link formulation by a weak variational method.
This more rigorous approach makes it possible to ensure a consistent and accurate transmission of the mechanical forces.
The methods presented are close to what is done for contact problems. It is restricted to the connection between two surfaces of the same topological dimension.

### 5.2 Continuous formulation

It is assumed that there are two domains $\Omega_1$ and $\Omega_2$ separated by the interface $\Gamma$ (see fig-schema-geom).
The normals $\normal_1$ and $\normal_2$ to $\Gamma$ for each domain are outward and of unit length.

![Figure](/figures/r3.03.03/geom_massif.svg)

*Domain decomposed into $\Omega_1$ and $\Omega_2$ separated by the interface $\Gamma$.*

The continuous problem with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions is the following: Find a displacement field $\disp = (\disp_1, \disp_2) \in H^1_0(\Omega = \Omega_1 \times \Omega_2)$ solution of .

$$
\begin{align}
- \nabla \cdot \stress(\disp) &= \vector{f} \quad \text{dans } \Omega, \\
\disp &= \vectorZero \quad \text{sur } \partial \Omega \setminus \Gamma.
\end{align}
$$

where $\stress$ is the Cauchy stress tensor and $\vector{f}$ the external body forces.
In the case of linear elasticity, $\stress$ is computed by Hooke's law

$$
\stress(\disp) = \lambda\,(\nabla \cdot \disp)\, \tensTwoUnit + 2\mu\, \strain(\disp).
$$

In addition, one has equality of the displacements and of the normal forces at the interface $\Gamma$.

$$
\disp_1 = \disp_2 \quad \text{sur } \Gamma.
$$

and

$$
\stress(\disp_1) \normal_1 = - \stress(\disp_2) \normal_2  \quad \text{sur } \Gamma.
$$

### 5.3 Discrete weak formulation

One wishes to impose the conditions and weakly by a variational approach.
Three methods will be considered: penalisation, the augmented Lagrangian and Nitsche.

| **Method** | **Advantages** | **Disadvantages** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Penalisation | Simple and primal | Numerical oscillations |
| Augmented Lagrangian | Accurate and robust | Saddle-point formulation |
| Nitsche | Accurate, robust and primal | Restricted to linear elasticity |

Let $\mathcal{M}^h$ denote a mesh of $\Omega$ which is potentially non-conforming at the interface.
Two finite-element approximation spaces $V_1^h$ and $V_2^h$, which may be different, are considered.
The equilibrium equation is discretised in the following manner: Find $(\disp_1^h, \disp_2^h) \in V_1^h \times V_2^h$ such that:

$$
a^h((\disp_1^h, \disp_2^h), (\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h))
:=
\left( \stress(\disp_1^h), \strain(\vector{v}_1^h) \right)_{\Omega_1}
+
\left( \stress(\disp_2^h), \strain(\vector{v}_2^h) \right)_{\Omega_2}
=
\ell^h((\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h))
:=
\left( \vector{f}, \vector{v}_1^h \right)_{\Omega_1}
+
\left( \vector{f}, \vector{v}_2^h \right)_{\Omega_2}, \quad  \forall (\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h) \in V_1^h \times V_2^h
$$

#### 5.3.1 Penalised method

For the penalised method, the kinematic constraint is imposed by penalising the difference
$\disp_1^h- \disp_2^h$ on $\Gamma$. The penalised weak problem is the following: Find $(\disp_1^h, \disp_2^h) \in V_1^h \times V_2^h$ such that:

$$
a^h((\disp_1^h, \disp_2^h), (\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h))
+
\frac{\gamma}{h} \left( \disp_1^h- \disp_2^h, \vector{v}_1^h- \vector{v}_2^h \right)_{\Gamma}
=
\ell^h((\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h)), \quad  \forall (\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h) \in V_1^h \times V_2^h
$$

where $\gamma$ is the penalisation parameter (homogeneous to a stress) and $h$ a characteristic mesh size (it is taken as the size of the mesh cell).

This problem is symmetric and well-posed because the penalisation term is the square of a norm at the energy level. This term disappears at convergence.
The larger $\gamma$ is, the more strongly the condition will be imposed, at the price of a degradation of the conditioning of the stiffness matrix. The disadvantage of this method is that stress oscillations may appear at the interface, because the penalisation term makes the discrete problem inconsistent.

#### 5.3.2 Nitsche's method

The idea of Nitsche's method is to add the terms missing from the penalised method in order to recover consistency and thus avoid problems of numerical oscillations. For this purpose, the equation is also used.
Thus, the Nitsche weak problem is the following: Find $(\disp_1^h, \disp_2^h) \in V_1^h \times V_2^h$ such that:

$$
a^h((\disp_1^h, \disp_2^h), (\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h))
+
\frac{\gamma}{h} \left( \disp_1^h - \disp_2^h, \vector{v}_1^h- \vector{v}_2^h \right)_{\Gamma}
+
\left( \stress(\disp_1^h) \normal_1, \vector{v}_1^h- \vector{v}_2^h \right)_{\Gamma}
+
\left( \disp_1^h- \disp_2^h, \stress(\vector{v}_1^h) \normal_1, \right)_{\Gamma}
=
\ell^h((\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h)), \quad  \forall (\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h) \in V_1^h \times V_2^h
$$

This problem is symmetric and well-posed if $\gamma > 0$ is chosen large enough. This formulation is restricted to linear elasticity for the time being.
The consistency of this formulation comes from the addition of the normal force.
The formulation is said to be biased here because only $\stress(\disp_1^h)\normal_1$ is used. The unbiased version is obtained by replacing $\stress(\disp_1^h)\normal_1$ by $\frac12 (\stress(\disp_1^h)\normal_1 - \stress(\disp_2^h)\normal_2)$. This version is not considered here because it complicates the implementation of the method.

#### 5.3.3 Augmented Lagrangian method

An augmented Lagrangian of *mortar* type will be used in order to have a robust method, that is to say without numerical oscillation.
For this purpose, a space $W_1^h$ is introduced for the Lagrange multiplier defined on $\Gamma$.
The idea is to replace, in the Nitsche formulation, the normal forces $\stress(\disp_1^h)\normal_1$ by the Lagrange multiplier $\lambda_1^h$.
The advantage is that this makes it possible to generalise to all constitutive laws, because they no longer intervene directly in the formulation, but this is done at the price of having a saddle-point problem to solve.

Thus, the Lagrangian weak problem is the following: Find $(\disp_1^h, \disp_2^h, \lambda_1^h) \in V_1^h \times V_2^h \times W_1^h$ such that:

$$
\begin{align}
a^h((\disp_1^h, \disp_2^h), (\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h))
+
\frac{\gamma}{h} \left( \disp_1^h- \disp_2^h, \vector{v}_1^h- \vector{v}_2^h \right)_{\Gamma}
+
\left( \lambda_1^h, \vector{v}_1^h- \vector{v}_2^h \right)_{\Gamma}
&=
\ell^h((\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h)), \quad  \forall (\vector{v}_1^h, \vector{v}_2^h) \in V_1^h \times V_2^h, \\
\left(\disp_1^h- \disp_2^h, \mu_1^h \right)_{\Gamma} &= 0, \quad \forall \mu_1^h \in W_1^h.
\end{align}
$$

The choice of the space $W_1^h$ is important for the robustness and the stability of the method. $W_1^h$ is defined as being the trace space of $V_1^h$ on the boundary $\Gamma$:

$$
W_1^h
=
(V_1^h)_{\vert \Gamma}
$$

This choice of mortar space guarantees the inf-sup condition, but the stability constant may depend on the mesh size and therefore, in certain cases, this may produce oscillations. The stability constant is independent of the mesh if the approximation is quadratic or, in the linear case, under mesh-regularity conditions. To guarantee independence in the linear case, it is sufficient to take $\gamma > 0$ sufficiently large, because it adds coercivity to the problem. One then has a stabilised augmented Lagrangian.
Note that at convergence, $h \rightarrow 0$, this term disappears and no longer influences the solution.

If one wishes to use an HHO formulation for $\disp_2^h$, the face unknown $\disp_{2,F}^h$ must be used. This is valid for all the methods presented.

### 5.4 Implementation

For the various formulations, it is necessary to compute integrals over $\Gamma$. One cannot integrate directly on one of the two sides as soon as the meshes are non-conforming. To overcome this, the integrals are computed on the intersection surfaces between two mesh cells. This intersection computation, and therefore the pairing, is carried out by reusing the algorithms developed for mortar contact.

## 6 What uses can be made of these modellings?

Besides the two uses aimed at in [§2] [Figure 2.1-a] and [Figure 2.1-b], this connection can also be used to:

- apply a force torsor on a known surface of a 3D modelling:

To this end, the user defines the surface of application of the force ($\mathit{lma}$ ), "connects" it with a node ($P$ ) of a discrete element (DIS_TR_N) **without stiffness** and then applies the desired torsor on this node (FORCE_NODALE).

In this way, the torsor is applied "gently", without generating spurious stresses on the surface.

- "hold" a structure without clamping it too strongly:

For example, if a pipe has been meshed in 3D and one wishes to prevent its rigid-body motions

![Figure](/figures/r3.03.03/100003AA00000BE300000B2A19F68F6259C30688.svg)

$(S)$ is connected to $P$ and then the 6 degrees of freedom of $P$ are blocked.

The structure is then held, without $(S)$ being clamped. In particular, the cross-section $(S)$ can ovalise.
