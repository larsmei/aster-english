# r3.03.01 Dualisation of the boundary conditions

**Summary:**

The principle of the Lagrange multipliers is explained in order to solve linear systems under affine constraints arising from the imposition of boundary conditions of kinematic type. The stiffness matrix obtained no longer being positive definite, certain solution algorithms therefore become unusable. A technique is therefore sought in order to be able to continue to use the factorisation algorithm ${\mathit{LDL}}^{T}$ without permutation and without elimination. The technique proposed is that of the "double Lagrange multipliers" (used in the Castem2000 code). It is shown that this technique is effective. A few indications are given on the conditioning of the matrices obtained by this technique.

The problem of the search for the eigenmodes of constrained systems is then examined. It is shown that one possible solution is to add the dualised boundary conditions to the stiffness matrix and not to modify the mass matrix.

**Contents**

## 1 Introduction

This document is concerned with the dualisation of the boundary conditions (said to be kinematic). Two distinct problems of linear algebra are examined:

- the solution of linear systems
- the search for the eigenmodes.

## 2 Dualisation of the kinematic boundary conditions, principle of the Lagrange multipliers

### 2.1 Objectives

The finite-element discretisation method leads one to solve numerous linear systems.

Often such a system can be considered as the algebraic expression of a problem of minimisation of a positive quadratic functional $J({\vector{u}})$ where ${\vector{u}}$ belongs to ${\setR}^{n}$ where $n$ is the number of nodal unknowns, while being constrained by a certain number of affine relations ${B}_{i}({\vector{u}})-{d}_{i}=0$ (boundary conditions of Dirichlet type).

It is this problem of minimisation under affine constraints that is of interest here. In the remainder of the document, the case (and the vocabulary) of linear static mechanics will be taken as an example. One will speak of a stiffness matrix and of a displacement vector, but the technique proposed remains valid for thermal evolution problems, in the linear or non-linear case.

Let the discretised problem be:

$$
\text{Pb1}: \underset{\vector{u} \in V \subset {\setR}^{n}}{\min}J({\vector{u}})
$$

where $J({\vector{u}})$ is a quadratic form (such as the total potential energy) which one writes

$$
J({\vector{u}})
=
\frac{1}{2} \left( \tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u} , \vector{u} \right )
-
\left( \vector{b} , \vector{u} \right)
$$

$\tensTwo{A}$ is a symmetric positive matrix (that is $\left( \tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}, \vector{u} \right) \ge 0 \quad \forall {\vector{u}}$) but not necessarily definite (that is, $\tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}=\vectorZero$ is possible for ${\vector{u}} \ne \vectorZero$ )

$V$ denotes the space of the kinematically admissible displacements; it is an affine subspace of ${\setR}^{n}$.

This discrete problem is solved numerically by expressing that the "derivative" of $J({\vector{u}})$ in $V$ is zero. One is then led to solve a linear system of equations.

The problem is to "differentiate" $J({\vector{u}})$ in $V \subset {\setR}^{n}$ .

In general, for practical reasons, the expression for $J({\vector{u}})$ is computed in the basis of all the nodal displacements (without taking the constraints into account): ${\vector{u}}$ then belongs to ${\setR}^{n}$ where $n$ is the total number of nodal unknowns.

If $V$ is a vector subspace of ${\setR}^{n}$ generated by the $(n-p)$ basic nodal displacements, the differentiation of $J({\vector{u}})$ in $V$ is done very simply: it is sufficient to "forget", in the matrix $\tensTwo{A}$ and the vector $\vector{b}$, the rows and columns corresponding to the removed degrees of freedom (dof) ($\vectorCmp{u}{i}=0$).

If the constrained degrees of freedom are not set to zero but assigned a given value $\vectorCmp{u}{i}={d}_{i}$, $\vector{b}$ must be modified.

Finally, if the constraints "mix" the dof among themselves (linear relations between unknowns), $\tensTwo{A}$ and $\vector{b}$ must be modified.

The principle of the Lagrange multipliers makes it possible to solve the problem without touching the matrices $\tensTwo{A}$ and $\vector{b}$. The price to be paid is an increase in the number of unknowns in the system to be solved.

Instead of solving the problem in the space $V$ of dimension $n-p$, one solves it in the space ${\setR}^{n+p}$, the additional unknowns denoted ${\lambda}_{i}$ being called Lagrange multipliers.

### 2.2 Principle and justification

Let us take up the previous problem again by making the space $V$ explicit

**Problem 1:**

$$
\begin{split}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \underset{{\vector{u}}\in V}{\min}J({\vector{u}})\\
    J({\vector{u}}) = \frac{1}{2}( \tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u} ,{\vector{u}}) - (\vector{b} ,{\vector{u}})\\
    V = \left\lbrace {\vector{u}}\in {\setR}^{n} \vert \, {B}_{i}({\vector{u}}) = {d}_{i}, \quad \forall i=1,\cdots,p
\right\rbrace \end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

The ${B}_{i}$ are linear forms on ${\setR}^{n}$ , the ${d}_{i}$ are given constants. It is furthermore assumed that the p forms ${B}_{i}$ are independent of one another: the dimension of the space generated by the ${B}_{i}$ is $p$ .

One can show (Cf. [r3.03.01-annexe]) that this problem is equivalent to the following:

**Problem 2:**

$$
\begin{split}\text{Trouver } {\vector{u}} \in {V} \text{ où }
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    V = \left\lbrace {\vector{u}} \in {\setR}^{n} \vert \, {B}_{i}({\vector{u}})={d}_{i}, \quad \forall i=1,\cdots,p\right\rbrace \\
    (\tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u} - \vector{b}) (\vector{v}) = 0 \quad \forall \vector{v} \in {V}_{0} \\
    {V}_{0} = \left\lbrace \vector{v} \in {\setR}^{n} \vert \,  {B}_{i} (\vector{v})=0, \quad \forall i=1,\cdots,p \right\rbrace
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

Let us rewrite problem 2 differently:

**Problem 3:**

$$
\begin{split}\text{Trouver } {\vector{u}} \in {\setR}^{n} \text{ où }
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \forall i ,\quad {B}_{i} (\vector{u}) - {d}_{i} = 0 \\
    \forall \vector{v} \in {V}_{0} ,\quad {B}_{i} (\vector{v}) = 0 \\
    \forall \vector{v} \in {V}_{0} ,\quad (\tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}-\vector{b})(\vector{v}) = 0 \\
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

The last two equations show (by identifying ${\setR}^{n}$ and its dual) that:

- $\forall i , \quad {B}_{i}$ is orthogonal to ${V}_{0}$
- $(\tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}-\vector{b})$ is orthogonal to ${V}_{0}$

${V}_{0}$ is the vector subspace of ${\setR}^{n}$ orthogonal to ${B}_{i}$ (${V}_{0}$  is of dimension $(n-p)$ because the $p$ conditions ${B}_{i}$ are assumed independent).

Since the decomposition of ${\setR}^{n}$ into a direct sum of two orthogonal subspaces is unique, one deduces from it that $(\tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}-b)$ belongs to the vector space generated by the ${B}_{i}$ .

There therefore exists a family of scalars ${\lambda}_{i}$ called Lagrange multipliers such that:

$$
(\tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}-\vector{b})
+
\sum_{i} {{\lambda}_{i} \, \vector{B}_i }
=
\vectorZero
$$

where $\vector{B}_i$ denotes the discrete representation of the linear form ${B}_{i}$. This equality is true in ${\setR}^{n}$

Problem 3 then becomes:

**Problem 4:**

$$
\begin{split}\text{Trouver } {\vector{u}} \in {\setR}^{n} \text{ et } {\lambda}_{i} \in {\setR}  \text{ pour } i=1,\cdots,p
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    {B}_{i} ({\vector{u}}) - {d}_{i} = 0 , \quad \forall i=1,\cdots, p \\
    (\tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}-\vector{b})+\sum_{i} {{\lambda}_{i} \, \vector{B}_i } = \vectorZero
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

The converse ($\text{Pb4} \Rightarrow \text{Pb3}$) is obvious.

If there exist ${\lambda}_{i}$ such that:

$$
(\tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}-\vector{b})
+
\sum_{i} {{\lambda}_{i} \, \vector{B}_i }
=
\vectorZero
$$

then

$$
\forall \vector{v} \in {V}_{0}, \quad (\tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}-\vector{b})\vector{v}
=
- \left( \sum_{i} {{\lambda}_{i} \, \vector{B}_i } \right ) \, \vector{v}
=
0
$$

Problem 4 is the problem sought. One will say that it is the problem with dualised kinematic conditions. In matrix form one can write it:

$$
\discMatr{K} \, \discVect{X}
=
\discVect{F}
$$

with

$$
\begin{split}\left[
    \begin{array}{cc}
      \tensTwo{A} & \transpose{\tensTwo{B}} \\
       \tensTwo{B} & \tensTwo{0}
    \end{array}
\right]
\left\lbrace
    \begin{array}{c}
        \vector{u}
        \\
        \vector{\lambda}
    \end{array}
\right\rbrace
=
\left\lbrace
   \begin{array}{c}
        \vector{b}
        \\
        \vector{d}
    \end{array}
\right\rbrace\end{split}
$$

with the vectors ${\vector{u}}\in {\setR}^{n}$, $\vector{\lambda} \in {\setR}^{p}$ and $\vector{X} \in {\setR}^{n+p}$.
And the matrices $\tensTwo{A} \in {\setR}^{n} \times  {\setR}^{n}$, $\tensTwo{B} \in {\setR}^{p} \times {\setR}^{n}$ and $\tensTwo{K} \in {\setR}^{n+p} \times  {\setR}^{n+p}$.

One observes that this system can be obtained by seeking to render extremal the functional:

$$
\mathcal{L}({\vector{u}}, \vector{\lambda} )
=
\frac{1}{2}( \tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u},{\vector{u}})
-
(\vector{b} , \vector{u})-
+
\vector{\lambda} ( \tensTwo{B} \vector{u} -\vector{d} )
$$

This functional is called the Lagrangian of the initial problem. The main interest of this method is to free oneself from the constraints: $\vector{u}$ and $\vector{\lambda}$ are sought in ${\setR}^{n}$ and ${\setR}^{p}$ ($\vector{X}$ in ${\setR}^{n+p}$ ).

The coefficients ${\lambda}_{i}$ are called the Lagrange coefficients of the problem (one will sometimes say the "Lagrange multipliers").

## 3 Disadvantages of this dualisation

One sees from the expression for the Lagrangian that the matrix $\tensTwo{K}$ is no longer positive (which was the case for $A$ ). Indeed:

$$
\exists {\vector{u}}_{0} \text{ tel que } \tensTwo{B}{\vector{u}}_{0} \ne \vectorZero
{\Rightarrow}
\exists {\vector{\lambda}}_{0} \text{ tel que } \mathcal{L} \left( {\vector{u}}_{0},{\vector{\lambda}}_{0} \right)
=
\frac{1}{2}({\tensTwo{A} \vector{u}}_{0},{\vector{u}}_{0})
+
{\vector{\lambda}}_{0}{{\tensTwo{B} \vector{u}}}_{0}<0
$$

The loss of the positivity of the matrix $\tensTwo{K}$ entails that the solution of the system $\tensTwo{K} \vector{X}=\vector{F}$ can no longer be carried out in general by the classical gradient algorithms or by the Cholesky factorisation. The factorisation algorithm $LDL^{T}$ without permutation of the rows and columns is no longer guaranteed either.

Let us illustrate the problem on an example.

A spring of stiffness $k$ connects two nodes ${N}_{1}$ and ${N}_{2}$

*Example 1 of the discrete system to be solved*

The two unknowns are ${u}_{1}$, ${u}_{2}$ and the two nodal forces are ${f}_{1}$ and ${f}_{2}$. With the notation of the previous paragraph, the size of the system is denoted $n=2$.
The stiffness matrix is therefore written

$$
\begin{split}\discMatr{A}
=
\left[\begin{array}{cc}
    k& -k\\
    -k& k
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

One adds a constraint $\alpha {u}_{1}+\beta {u}_{2}=\gamma$ (one therefore has $p=1$). The dualised problem is written:

$$
\tensTwo{K} \vector{X}
=
\vector{F}
$$

With the following numbering of the unknowns, identified by $\discVect{X^{1}}$:

$$
\begin{split}\discVect{X^{1}}
=
\left \lbrace \begin{array}{c}
    {u}_{1}\\
    {u}_{2}\\
    \lambda
\end{array}\right \rbrace\end{split}
$$

The matrix and the right-hand side are written:

**Reminder of the necessary and sufficient condition for the algorithm $LDL^{T}-{SP}$ (without permutation) to work**

$$
\begin{split}\discMatr{K^{1}}
=
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
    k& -k& \alpha \\
    -k& k& \beta \\
    \alpha & \beta & 0
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

$$
\begin{split}\discVect{F^{1}}
=
\left \lbrace \begin{array}{c}
    {f}_{1}\\
    {f}_{2}\\
    \gamma
\end{array}\right \rbrace\end{split}
$$

Let ${K}_{i}$ denote the sub-matrix of ${\tensTwo{K}}$ formed by the first $i$ rows and columns of $\tensTwo{K}$. (If $\tensTwo{K}$ is of order $n$ then ${\tensTwo{K}}_{n} = K$ and ${\tensTwo{K}}_{1}=[{k}_{11}]$ ).
There will be no zero pivot in the algorithm $LDL^{T}-{SP}$ if, and only if, ${\tensTwo{K}}_{i}$  is invertible for every $i$ between $1$ and $n$.
This condition will be denoted: cond1

The matrix $\tensTwo{K^{1}}$ above is written with, as numbering of the unknowns, the order of the components of $\discVect{X^{1}}$:

then the sub-matrix ${\tensTwo{K^{1}}}_{2}$ such that

$$
\begin{split}{\tensTwo{K^{1}}}_{2}
=
\left[\begin{array}{cc}
    k& -k\\
   -k&  k
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

does not satisfy condition cond1.

Let us now take another numbering such that

$$
\begin{split}\discVect{X^{2}}
=
\left \lbrace \begin{array}{c}
    \lambda \\
    {u}_{1}\\
    {u}_{2}\\
\end{array}\right \rbrace\end{split}
$$

then the matrix  $\tensTwo{K^{2}}$ is written

    0& \alpha & \beta \\

\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

the sub-matrix ${\tensTwo{K^{2}}}_{1}$ does not satisfy condition cond1.

Let us now take another numbering such that

$$
\begin{split}\discVect{X^{3}}
=
\left \lbrace \begin{array}{c}
    {u}_{1}\\
    \lambda \\
    {u}_{2}\\
\end{array}\right \rbrace\end{split}
$$

The stiffness matrix in this numbering is written

$$
\begin{split}\tensTwo{K^{3}}
=
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
    k& \alpha & -k\\
    \alpha & 0& \beta \\
   -k& \beta & k
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

The three sub-matrices have as determinant

$$
\det{\tensTwo{K^{3}}}_{1}
=
k
$$

$$
\det{\tensTwo{K^{3}}}_{2}
=
{\alpha}^{2}
$$

$$
\det{\tensTwo{K^{3}}}_{3}
=
-k{(\alpha +\beta )}^{2}
$$

Of course, $k$ is assumed strictly positive (stiffness of the spring). The matrix ${\tensTwo{K^3}}_{3}$ is invertible only if $\alpha + \beta \ne 0$ . The case $\alpha +\beta =0$ corresponds indeed to a "bad" physical constraint: the condition  ${u}_{1}-{u}_{2}=\text{cte}$ does not block the "rigid-body motions" for $\tensTwo{A}$ (without energy).

For the global problem to have a unique solution, it is indeed necessary that the conditions ${\tensTwo{B}}_{i} \vector{u}={\vector{d}}_{i}$ generate a space of admissible displacements which contains none of the rigid-body motions of $\tensTwo{A}$ .

With the notation of the previous paragraph one will write:

$$
\ker \tensTwo{A} \cap {V}_{0}
=
\left\lbrace 0\right\rbrace
$$

It will be assumed in the remainder of the document that this condition is verified. That is, that the constraints ${\tensTwo{B}}_{i}$ block at least the rigid-body motions of $\tensTwo{A}$ (they may of course be more numerous). When this condition is verified and the conditions ${\tensTwo{B}}_{i}$ are independent of one another, one will say that the problem is physically well posed.

And therefore the matrix ${\tensTwo{K^3}}_{2}$ is invertible only if $\alpha \ne 0$. In the end, one therefore sees that the numbering $\left({u}_{1},\lambda ,{u}_{2}\right)$ satisfies condition cond1 if $\alpha \ne 0$ .

If the constraint ${{\alpha u}}_{1}+{{\beta u}}_{2}=\gamma$ reduces to:

$$
{u}_{1} = \delta (\alpha ,\beta ) = (1,0)
$$

then it works. On the other hand if

$$
{u}_{2} = \delta (\alpha ,\beta ) = (0,1)
$$

then it does not work.

The symmetry of the problem shows that in order to be able to treat the problem $(\alpha ,\beta )=(0,1)$ one must number $\left({u}_{2},\lambda ,{u}_{1} \right)$ .

From this very simple example, one can draw a few general conclusions (all negative):

- if one numbers all the ${\lambda}_{i}$ after the ${u}_{i} et  si $\tensTwo{A}$ is singular, condition cond1 will not be verified for ${\tensTwo{K}}_{i}=\tensTwo{A}$ ,
- let a condition $\tensTwo{B} \vector{u}= \vector{d}$ and $\lambda$ the associated Lagrange multiplier. The equation $\tensTwo{B} \vector{u}= \vector{d}$ does not, in general, involve all the unknowns ${u}_{i}$ : the equation constrains certain degrees of freedom. If $\lambda$ is numbered before the dof which it constrains, condition cond1 will not be verified. Indeed, let us look at the sub-matrix ${\tensTwo{K}}_{j}$ where $j$ is the number of the equation giving $\lambda$ .

$$
\begin{split}{\tensTwo{K}}_{j}
=
\left[\begin{array}{cc}
    {\tensTwo{K}}_{j-1} & 0\\
    0& 0
\end{array}\right]`\end{split}
$$

- Consider a physical structure which, by misfortune, is "retained by its last physical dof", that is, such that if one does not constrain this dof the matrix is singular, and such that if one constrains it the matrix is invertible. The use of a Lagrange degree of freedom $\lambda$ for this constraint is impossible. Indeed, if one numbers $\lambda$ before the last physical degree of freedom, there will be a zero pivot at the level of $\lambda$ , and if one numbers it afterwards (therefore as the very last degree of freedom), the matrix ${\tensTwo{K}}_{n-1}$ will not be invertible since the constraint is not yet taken into account. It will be seen in  that the "double Lagrange" technique makes it possible to solve this problem.

To finish this paragraph we can make the following remark: If $\tensTwo{K}$ is invertible, one knows that there exists a numbering of the unknowns making it possible to factorise $\tensTwo{K}$ by $LDL^{T}$ . This numbering is for example that resulting from the algorithm $LDL^{T}$ with permutation (maximum pivot for example). But this renumbering concerns only the rows of the matrix; there is therefore a loss of the symmetry of $\tensTwo{K}$ . It is sufficient to consider the following example.

$$
\tensTwo{B}

## 4 Principle of the "double Lagrange multipliers"

### 4.1 Methodology

The method proposed here is that implemented in the CASTEM code (personal communication of Thierry Charras and Pierre Verpeaux). An intuitive presentation of it could be the following:

One sees that the dualised problem has zero terms on the diagonal: those corresponding to the Lagrange degrees of freedom. This property is also noticeable on the Lagrangian: there are no quadratic terms in $\vector{\lambda}$ .

This vanishing of the diagonal terms prevents certain permutations of rows and columns: one cannot place a Lagrange multiplier before the physical degrees of freedom which it constrains.

The idea is then to decompose each Lagrange coefficient $\vector{\lambda}$ into two equal parts ${\vector{\lambda}}^{1}$ and ${\vector{\lambda}}^{2}$. The equation $\tensTwo{B} \vector{u}=\vector{d}$ is then replaced by:

$$
\begin{split}\left \lbrace
    \begin{array}{l}
        \tensTwo{B} \vector{u}-\alpha \left({\vector{\lambda}}^{1}-{\vector{\lambda}}^{2} \right) = \vector{d}\\
        \tensTwo{B} \vector{u}+\alpha \left({\vector{\lambda}}^{1}-{\vector{\lambda}}^{2} \right) = \vector{d}
    \end{array}
\right .\end{split}
$$

where $\alpha$ is a non-zero constant.

Let us show the equivalence of the old problem and of the new one:

**Problem 1** : "simple Lagrange multiplier"

Find:

$$
\begin{split}\left \lbrace
    \begin{array}{l}
        \vector{u}\in {\setR}^{n}      \\
        \vector{\lambda} \in {\setR}^{p}
    \end{array}
\right .\end{split}
$$

such that:

$$
\begin{split}(S): \left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    \tensTwo{A} \vector{u} + \tensTwo{B}^{T} \vector{\lambda} = \vector{b} \\
    \tensTwo{B} \vector{u} = \vector{d}
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

One has the following equivalences

$$
\begin{split}(S)
\iff
\left \lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    {\vector{\lambda}}^{1} = {\vector{\lambda}}^{2}\\
     \vector{\lambda} = {\vector{\lambda}}^{1} + {\vector{\lambda}}^{2}\\
     \tensTwo{A} \vector{u}+{\tensTwo{B}}^{T}\vector{\lambda} = \vector{b}\\
     \tensTwo{B} \vector{u} = \vector{d}
\end{array} \right .
\iff
\left \lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \tensTwo{A} \vector{u}+{\tensTwo{B}}^{T} {\vector{\lambda}}^{1}+{\tensTwo{B}}^{T}{\vector{\lambda}}^{2}=\vector{b}\\
    \tensTwo{B} \vector{u}-\alpha \vector{\lambda}^{1}+\alpha \vector{\lambda}^{2}=\vector{d}\\
    \tensTwo{B} \vector{u}+\alpha \vector{\lambda}^{1}-\alpha \vector{\lambda}^{2}=\vector{d}
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

$\alpha$ is a constant $\ne 0$

Whence the problem equivalent to the previous one:

**Problem 2**  : "double Lagrange multiplier"

Find:

$$
\begin{split}\left \lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \vector{u}\in {\setR}^{n}\\ {\vector{\lambda}}^{1},{\vector{\lambda}}^{2}\in {\setR}^{p}\times {\setR}^{p}
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

such that

$$
\begin{split}(S^\prime)=:
\left \lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \tensTwo{A} \vector{u}+\tensTwo{B}^{T}{\vector{\lambda}}^{1}+\tensTwo{B}^{T}{\vector{\lambda}}^{2}=\vector{b}\\
    \tensTwo{B} \vector{u}-\alpha \vector{\lambda}^{1}+\alpha \vector{\lambda}^{2} = \vector{d}\\
    \tensTwo{B} \vector{u}+\alpha \vector{\lambda}^{1}-\alpha \vector{\lambda}^{2} = \vector{d}
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

The new problem can be written:

$$
\tensTwo{K^{\prime}}\vector{X^{\prime}}
=
\vector{F^{\prime}}
$$

with:

$$
\begin{split}\left \lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \vector{X^{\prime}} = ( \vector{u},{\vector{\lambda}}_{1},{\vector{\lambda}}_{2})\\
    \vector{F^{\prime}} = ( \vector{b} , \vector{d}, \vector{d})
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

and

$$
\begin{split}\discMatr{K^{\prime}}
=
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
    \tensTwo{A}& {\tensTwo{B}}^{T}& {\tensTwo{B}}^{T}\\
    \tensTwo{B}& -{\alpha \tensTwoUnit}& {\alpha \tensTwoUnit}\\
    \tensTwo{B}& \alpha \tensTwoUnit & - \alpha \tensTwoUnit
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

The problem corresponds to rendering extremal the functional:

$$
\mathcal{L^{\prime}} \left( \vector{u},{\vector{\lambda}}^{1},{\vector{\lambda}}^{2} \right)
=
\frac{1}{2} \left( \tensTwo{A} \vector{u} , \vector{u} \right)
-
\left( \vector{b} , \vector{u} \right)
+
\left({\vector{\lambda}}^{1}, \tensTwo{B} \vector{u}-\vector{d} \right)
 +
\left({\vector{\lambda}}^{2}, \tensTwo{B} \vector{u}-\vector{d} \right)
-
\frac{\alpha}{2}\left( {\vector{\lambda}}^{1}-{\vector{\lambda}}^{2},{\vector{\lambda}}^{1}-{\vector{\lambda}}^{2} \right)
$$

One can show (Cf Appendix 2) that if one observes a certain rule for numbering the unknowns, and by choosing the constant $\alpha >0$ , the matrix $\tensTwo{K^{\prime}}$ satisfies condition cond1.

This rule is the following:

Let a constraint relation $\tensTwo{B} \vector{u}-\vector{d}=0$ ; there correspond to it 2 Lagrange multipliers ${\vector{\lambda}}^{1}$ and ${\vector{\lambda}}^{2}$ . This relation involves a certain number of physical degrees of freedom.

**Rule ${R}_{0}$ :**

For each constraint relation, one must place ${\vector{\lambda}}^{1}$ before the first constrained physical degree of freedom and ${\vector{\lambda}}^{2}$ after the last constrained physical degree of freedom.
In order to reduce the memory occupation of the matrix $\tensTwo{K}$ , one must seek to minimise the bandwidth. This is what one does by "framing" the relations "as closely as possible": ${\vector{\lambda}}^{1}$ is placed just before the first constrained degree of freedom, ${\vector{\lambda}}^{2}$ is placed just after the last constrained dof.

### 4.2 Illustration

Let a problem with 4 physical degrees of freedom $\left( {u}_{1}, {u}_{2}, {u}_{3}, {u}_{4} \right)$ .

This system is subjected to 2 conditions:

$$
\begin{split}\left \lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    {a}_{11}{u}_{1} + {a}_{13}{u}_{3} = {b}_{1}\\
    {a}_{22}{u}_{2} + {a}_{24}{u}_{4} = {b}_{2}
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

let us call $\left({\lambda}_{1}^{1},{\lambda}_{1}^{2} \right)$ the two Lagrange degrees of freedom associated with the first condition and $\left({\lambda}_{2}^{1},{\lambda}_{2}^{2} \right)$ those associated with the second condition.

Assuming that the physical degrees of freedom have been numbered in the order $\left( {u}_{1}, {u}_{2}, {u}_{3}, {u}_{4} \right)$, the total numbering of the degrees of freedom retained is then  $\left( {\lambda}_{1}^{1},{u}_{1},{\lambda}_{2}^{1},{u}_{2}, {u}_{3} , {\lambda}_{1}^{2},{u}_{4},{\lambda}_{2}^{2} \right)$.

${\lambda}_{1}^{1}$ and ${\lambda}_{1}^{2}$ frame "as closely as possible" the constrained degrees of freedom (${u}_{1}$  and ${u}_{3}$), while ${\lambda}_{2}^{1}$ and ${\lambda}_{1}^{2}$ frame "as closely as possible" the constrained degrees of freedom (${u}_{2}$  and ${u}_{4}$).

The "double Lagrange" technique associated with the rule ${R}_{0}$ therefore makes it possible to solve any physically well-posed linear system with the algorithm of $LD{L}^{T}$ without permutation. The proof nevertheless assumes that the **matrix** $\tensTwo{A}$ **is symmetric and positive**  (not necessarily definite).

- ${A}_{1}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}0& 1\\ 1& 0\end{array}\right]$ is symmetric but not positive
- ${A}_{2}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}0& 1\\ -1& 1\end{array}\right]$ is positive but not symmetric

## 5 Additional advantage

It will be shown in this paragraph that the "double Lagrange" technique can make it possible to solve economically a series of problems which would differ only by their kinematic boundary conditions (for example a variable contact zone).

**Note**

This possibility is not used at present in the code.

Let a system with constraints $\tensTwo{K} \vector{X}=\vector{F}$ . Let us write this system by bringing out a particular constraint (this computation obviously remains valid when there are several constraints) $\tensTwo{C} \vector{u}-\vector{d}=\vectorZero$. In order to simplify the writing, one chooses $\alpha =1$ .

Let:

- ${\vector{\lambda}}^{1}$ the first Lagrange degree of freedom associated with the constraint
- ${\vector{\lambda}}^{2}$ the second Lagrange degree of freedom associated with the constraint
- $\vector{U}=\vector{X}-\left\lbrace {\vector{\lambda}}^{1},{\vector{\lambda}}^{2}\right\rbrace$
- $\tensTwo{\tilde{K}}$ the matrix $\tensTwo{K}$ projected onto vector{U} and $\vector{b}$ the vector $\vector{F}$ projected onto $\vector{U}$

Then the system is written with these variables:

$$
\begin{split}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
    \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}& \tensTwo{C}^{T}& \tensTwo{C}^{T}\\
    \tensTwo{C}& -\tensTwoUnit& \tensTwoUnit\\
    \tensTwo{C}& \tensTwoUnit& -\tensTwoUnit
\end{array}\right]
\,
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}
    \vector{U}\\
    {\vector{\lambda}}^{1}\\
    {\vector{\lambda}}^{2}
\end{array} \right \rbrace
=
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}
    \vector{b}\\
    \vector{d}\\
    \vector{d}\\
\end{array} \right \rbrace\end{split}
$$

Let us change the coefficient situated in this matrix at the position $({\vector{\lambda}}^{2},{\vector{\lambda}}^{2})$ from $-1$ to $3$ and let us write the new system

$$
\begin{split}(S): \left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    \tensTwo{\tilde{K}} \vector{U} + \left( {\vector{\lambda}}^{1} + {\vector{\lambda}}^{2} \right ) \, \tensTwo{C}^{T} = \vector{b} \\
    \tensTwo{C}  \vector{U} - {\vector{\lambda}}^{1} + {\vector{\lambda}}^{2} = \vector{d} \\
    \tensTwo{C}  \vector{U} + {\vector{\lambda}}^{1} + 3 \, {\vector{\lambda}}^{2} = \vector{d} \\
\end{array} \right .
\iff
\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    \left( {\vector{\lambda}}^{1} + {\vector{\lambda}}^{2} \right ) = \vectorZero \\
    \tensTwo{C}  \vector{U} - {\vector{\lambda}}^{1} + {\vector{\lambda}}^{2} = \vector{d} \\
    \tensTwo{\tilde{K}} \vector{U} = \vector{b} \\
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

This latter system is decoupled: one can solve the third equation in order to obtain $\vector{U}$ and then compute ${\vector{\lambda}}^{1}$ and ${\vector{\lambda}}^{2}$ .

One notes that the solution of this third equation corresponds to the initial problem without the constraint $\tensTwo{C} \vector{u}-\vector{d}=\vectorZero$ . The values of ${\vector{\lambda}}^{1}$ and ${\vector{\lambda}}^{2}$ then no longer have the same physical meaning. In other words, the system $(S)$ has the same solution in $U$ as the subsystem $\tensTwo{\tilde{K}} \vector{U} = \vector{b}$ ; the two additional unknowns ${\vector{\lambda}}^{1}$ and ${\vector{\lambda}}^{2}$ do not disturb the solution in $\vector{U}$ . The total system may appear of a size larger $(+2)$ than what is necessary, but from the software point of view this can be very convenient

Indeed, let us now imagine that we know in advance that certain kinematic relations are liable to be released. Let us number the ${\vector{\lambda}}^{2}$ associated with these relations at the end of the system. We can then partially triangulate the system once and for all, stopping before these dof. The triangulated part of the matrix is the most important in volume: all the physical degrees of freedom and all the ${\vector{\lambda}}^{1}$ . When a concrete problem arises, that is, when one knows the list of the active linear relations, it is sufficient to update the last rows of the matrix ($-\alpha$  if the relation is active, $3 \, \alpha$ if it is not). One can then finish the triangulation and solve the problem economically.

## 6 Note on the conditioning of the system

When one looks at the expression for the matrix which one is finally going to factorise $\tensTwo{\tilde{K}}$, one sees that its various sub-matrices $\tensTwo{A}$ , $\tensTwo{B}$ , $\alpha \tensTwoUnit$ can be of very different orders of magnitude. One knows that in general this situation is not favourable numerically (limited precision of computers).

It must be noted that the constraint equations $\tensTwo{C} \vector{u}-\vector{d}=\vectorZero$ can be multiplied by an arbitrary constant $\beta$ without changing the problem. Moreover, we have seen that the matrices $\alpha \tensTwoUnit$ were likewise arbitrary $\alpha >0$ . We therefore have two parameters making it possible to "adjust" the conditioning of the matrix.

We shall not give a general proof, but we content ourselves with examining the most trivial case there is: a spring, one dof, one constraint.

The matrix $\tensTwo{\tilde{K}}$ is written if $k$ is the stiffness of the spring:

$$
\begin{split}\tensTwo{\tilde{K}}
=
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
    k & \beta & \beta \\
    \beta & -\alpha & +\alpha \\
    \beta & \alpha & -\alpha
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

The conditioning of this matrix is linked to the scatter of its eigenvalues ${m}_{i}$ :

Let us compute the characteristic polynomial of $\tensTwo{\tilde{K}}$ :

$$
\begin{split}P(\mu)
=
\left(\mu + 2 \, \alpha \right)
\left( -{\mu}^{2}+k\mu + 2 \, \beta^{2} \right)
\iff
\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    {\mu}_{1} = -2 \, \alpha<0\\
    {\mu}_{2} = \frac{+k+\sqrt{k^2 + 8 \, \beta^{2}}}{2}>0\\
    {\mu}_{3} = \frac{+k-\sqrt{k^2 + 8 \, \beta^{2}}}{2}<0
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

$k$ is the eigenvalue of the unconstrained system. This eigenvalue is the order of magnitude sought for $\left({\mu}_{1},{\mu}_{2},{\mu}_{3}\right)$ .

One notes that ${\mu}_{1}<0$, ${\mu}_{3}<0$ and ${\mu}_{2}>0$ that is, that the two eigenvalues added by the Lagrange coefficients are negative (it is moreover because of this that $LD{L}^{T}-{SP}$ is not guaranteed without precautions).

One seeks to obtain eigenvalues of one and the same order of magnitude:

$$
\vert {\mu}_{1} \vert \simeq \vert {\mu}_{2} \vert \simeq \vert {\mu}_{3} \vert
$$

Which is expressed by

One sees that if $\beta << k$ then ${\mu}_{3} \simeq 0$ and  ${\mu}_{2} \simeq k$, this is not the result sought. If $\beta >> k$ , then $\vert {\mu}_{2}\vert \simeq \vert {\mu}_{3} \vert \simeq \sqrt{2} \beta \simeq \vert {\mu}_{1} \vert$

The three eigenvalues are then, in absolute value, of the order of $\beta$, which is an arbitrary constant very large compared with $k$. This solution is not the one which will be retained, because the value $k$ is, in the general case (with a large number of degrees of freedom), of an order of magnitude comparable to the other eigenvalues of the system.

One will rather choose $\beta = \alpha \simeq k$

$$
\begin{split}\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    {\mu}_{1} \simeq - 2 \, k\\
    {\mu}_{2} \simeq 2 \, k \\
    {\mu}_{3} \simeq -k
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

In practice, one chooses a single value of $\alpha$ for the whole system. This value is the mean of the extremal values of the diagonal terms associated with the physical degrees of freedom: $(\min({a}_{ii})+\max({a}_{ii}))/2$. Moreover, one takes $\beta =\alpha$ .

## 7 Eigenmodes and Lagrange parameters

### 7.1 Introduction

This paragraph aims to answer the following two questions:

* Q1: What is the **reduced** system of eigenvalues (and eigenvectors) to be solved when a mechanical model is subjected to **homogeneous linear** kinematic constraints?
* Q2: What is the dualised model (with Lagrange parameters) equivalent to the previous one?

### 7.2 Mechanical problem to be solved

A mechanical system already discretised by finite elements is assumed.

The nodal unknowns are denoted $\vector{U}=\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right \rbrace_{(i=1,n)}$ .

The nodal displacements are not all independent: there exist $p < n$ homogeneous linear relations between these displacements: $\tensTwo{B}_{j} \, \vector{U} = \vectorZero  \text{ pour } j=1,\cdots,p$ .

These linear relations are independent of one another, that is, the rank of the matrix $\tensTwo{B}$ containing the coefficients of the $p$ linear relations is $p$.

Let $\tensTwo{K}$ be the stiffness matrix of the mechanical system without constraints, and $\tensTwo{M}$ the mass matrix of the mechanical system without constraints.

What is the eigenvalue system to be solved in order to find the eigenmodes of the constrained structure?

### 7.3 Reduced system

Let us note that if one writes the kinematic linear relations in the form:

$$
\tensTwo{B}
\,
\vector{U}
=
\vectorZero
$$

where $\tensTwo{B}$ is a matrix $p \times n$ and $\vector{U}$ is the vector of the nodal unknowns with $\vector{U} \in \setR^{n}$.

then the relation is also valid for the velocities.

$$
\tensTwo{B}
\,
\vector{\dot{U}}
=
\vectorZero
$$

Moreover, if $\tensTwo{B}$ is of rank $p$ , then there exists a **square**  sub-matrix of $\tensTwo{B}$ of rank $p$ . Let us denote this sub-matrix by $\tensTwo{B}_{1}$ .

Let us then make a partition of the unknowns of $\vector{U}$ into $\vector{U}_{1}$ and $\vector{U}_{2}$ such that:

$$
\begin{split}\tensTwo{B}
\,
\vector{U}
=
\vectorZero
\iff
\left[ \begin{array}{ll}
    \tensTwo{B}_{1} & \tensTwo{B}_{2}
\end{array} \right]
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \vector{U}_{1} \\
    \vector{U}_{2}
\end{array}  \right \rbrace
=
\vectorZero\end{split}
$$

with $\vector{U}_{1}\in {\setR}^{p}$, $\vector{U}_{2}\in {\setR}^{n-p}$, $\tensTwo{B}_{1} \in p \times p$ and $\tensTwo{B}_{2} \in p \times (n-p)$.

The linear relations can then be written:

$$
\tensTwo{B}_{1} \vector{U}_{1}
+
\tensTwo{B}_{2} \vector{U}_{2}
=
\vectorZero
$$

which makes it possible to express the unknowns $\vector{U}_{1}$ as a function of $\vector{U}_{2}$ since $\tensTwo{B}_{1}$ is invertible.

$$
\vector{U}_{1}
=
- \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \tensTwo{B}_{2}  \vector{U}_{2}
$$

### 7.4 Reduced stiffness matrix

The elastic strain energy of the unconstrained discretised structure is ${W}_{\text{def}}=\frac{1}{2} \left( \transpose{\vector{U}}\tensTwo{K} \, \vector{U} \right)$ . If one partitions the matrix $\tensTwo{K}$ in the same manner as one has partitioned $\vector{U}$ , one obtains:

$$
\begin{split}\tensTwo{K}
=
\left[\begin{array}{cc}
    \tensTwo{K}_{1}& \tensTwo{K}_{12}\\
    \transpose{\tensTwo{K}_{12}} & \tensTwo{K}_{2}
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

then:

$$
2\, {W}_{\text{def}}
=
\transpose{\vector{U}_{1}}\tensTwo{K}_{1} \vector{U}_{1}
+
\transpose{\vector{U}_{2}}\tensTwo{K}_{2} \vector{U}_{2}
+
\transpose{\vector{U}_{2}}\tensTwo{K}_{12}\vector{U}_{1}
+
\transpose{\vector{U}_{1}} \transpose{\tensTwo{K}_{12}} \vector{U}_{2}
$$

Let us then introduce the linear constraints

$$
2\, {W}_{\text{def}}
=
\transpose{\vector{U}_{2}} \transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{2}} \inverseTranspose{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \tensTwo{K}_{1} \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \tensTwo{B}_{2}\vector{U}_{2}
+
\transpose{\vector{U}_{2}} \tensTwo{K}_{2} \vector{U}_{2}
-
\transpose{\vector{U}_{2}} \tensTwo{K}_{12} \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \tensTwo{B}_{2}\vector{U}_{2}
-
\transpose{\vector{U}_{2}} \transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{2}} \inverseTranspose{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \transpose{\tensTwo{K}_{12}}\vector{U}_{2}
=
\transpose{\vector{U}_{2}} \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{2} \vector{U}_{2}
$$

with:

$$
\tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{2}
=
\tensTwo{K}_{2}
+
\transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{2}} \inverseTranspose{{B}} \tensTwo{K}_{1} \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \tensTwo{B}_{2}
-
\tensTwo{K}_{12} \inverse{\tensTwo{B}} \tensTwo{B}_{2}
-
\transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{2}} \inverseTranspose{{B}} \transpose{\tensTwo{K}_{12}}
$$

One therefore sees that one has expressed the strain energy of the reduced structure as a bilinear form in $\vector{U}_{2}$ . The nodal unknowns of $\vector{U}_{1}$ **have been eliminated** . The nodal unknowns $\vector{U}_{2}$ **are no longer constrained** .

### 7.5 Reduced mass matrix

Let us adopt the same partition for the mass matrix $\tensTwo{M}$. We can write

$$
\tensTwo{B}_{1}{ \vector{\dot{U}}}_{1}
+
\tensTwo{B}_{2}{ \vector{\dot{U}}}_{2}
=
\vectorZero
$$

The same computation as previously then leads us to the following expression for the kinetic energy

$$
2\, {W}_{\text{cin}}
=
\transpose{{\vector{\dot{U}}}_{2}}  \tensTwo{\tilde{M}}_{2} { \vector{\dot{U}}}_{2}
$$

with:

$$
\tensTwo{\tilde{M}}_{2}
=
\tensTwo{M}_{2}
+
\transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{2}}  \inverseTranspose{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \tensTwo{M}_{1} \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}}\tensTwo{B}_{2}
-
\tensTwo{M}_{12} \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}}\tensTwo{B}_{2}
-
\transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{2}} \inverseTranspose{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \transpose{\tensTwo{M}_{12}}
$$

### 7.6 Eigenvalue system

The system to be solved in order to find the eigenmodes (and the eigenfrequencies) of the constrained structure consists in finding the $(n-p) \left({\vector{X}}_{i},{\omega}_{i}^{2} \right)\in {\setR}^{n-p} \times \setR$ such that

$$
\left(
    \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{2}-{\omega}_{i}^{2}\tensTwo{\tilde{M}}_{2}
\right )
{\vector{X}}_{i}
=
\vectorZero
$$

If one applies this to the case of blocked degrees of freedom, one has

whence: $\tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{2}=\tensTwo{K}_{2}$ and $\tensTwo{\tilde{M}}_{2}=\tensTwo{M}_{2}$, that is, it is sufficient to "forget" in $\tensTwo{K}$ and $\tensTwo{M}$ the rows and columns corresponding to the blocked degrees of freedom.

### 7.7 Dualised system

We have seen that taking the (double) Lagrange coefficients into account in a matrix $\tensTwo{A}$ led to the matrix $\tensTwo{A}^{\prime}$ :

where $\tensTwo{B}$ is the matrix of the kinematic conditions ($\tensTwo{B} \vector{U}=\vectorZero$) , $\alpha \in {\setR}^{+}$ arbitrary and $\alpha \ne 0$, $\beta \in \setR$ arbitrary and $\beta \ne 0$.

Let us apply the dualisation of the boundary conditions to the matrices $\tensTwo{K}$ and $\tensTwo{M}$ , partitioning the degrees of freedom into $\vector{X}_{1}$ and $\vector{X}_{2}$ as in the previous paragraph. We obtain the following eigenvalue problem:

$$
\begin{split}(S) :
\left(
\left[\begin{array}{cccc}
    \tensTwo{K}_{1} & \tensTwo{K}_{12}& {\beta}_{k} \transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{1}}& {\beta}_{k} \transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{1}}\\

    {\beta}_{k}\tensTwo{B}_{1}& {\beta}_{k}\tensTwo{B}_{2}& {\alpha}_{k} \tensTwoUnit & -{\alpha}_{k} \tensTwoUnit
\end{array}\right]

    \tensTwo{M}_{12}& \tensTwo{M}_{2} & {\beta}_{m} \transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{2}}& {\beta}_{m} \transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{2}}\\
    {\beta}_{m}\tensTwo{B}_{1}& {\beta}_{m}\tensTwo{B}_{2}& -{\alpha}_{m}\tensTwoUnit& {\alpha}_{m}\tensTwoUnit\\
    {\beta}_{m}\tensTwo{B}_{1}& {\beta}_{m}\tensTwo{B}_{2}& {\alpha}_{m}\tensTwoUnit& -{\alpha}_{m}\tensTwoUnit
\end{array}\right]

$$

for a natural circular frequency $\omega$ and an eigenvector $\vector{X}$ of this system, one can write:

$$
\begin{split}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{1} \vector{X}_{1} + \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{12}\vector{X}_{2} + \beta \transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{1}}( \vector{\lambda}_{1} + \vector{\lambda}_{2}) = \vectorZero \\
    \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{12}\vector{X}_{1} + \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{2}\vector{X}_{2} + \beta \transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{2}} (\vector{\lambda}_{1}+\vector{\lambda}_{2})= \vectorZero \\
    \beta (\tensTwo{B}_{1}\vector{X}_{1}+\tensTwo{B}_{2}\vector{X}_{2})-\alpha ({\vector{\lambda}}_{1}-{\vector{\lambda}}_{2})= \vectorZero \\
    \beta (\tensTwo{B}_{1}\vector{X}_{1}+\tensTwo{B}_{2}\vector{X}_{2})+\alpha ({\vector{\lambda}}_{1}-{\vector{\lambda}}_{2})= \vectorZero \\
\end{array}  \right .\end{split}
$$

with: $\tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{i}=\tensTwo{K}_{i}-{\omega}^{2}\tensTwo{M}_{i}$, $\beta ={\beta}_{k}-{\omega}^{2}{\beta}_{m}$ and $\alpha ={\alpha}_{k}-{\omega}^{2}{\alpha}_{m}$.

The system $(S)$ is of order $n+2p$ if $n$ is the number of physical dof and $p$ the number of kinematic relations.

The characteristic polynomial in ${\omega}^{2}$ is **a priori**  of degree $n+2p$ . Its term of highest degree is: $\prod_{i=1}^{n}(-{m}_{i})\cdot {(+{\alpha}_{m})}^{2p}$ if ${m}_{i}$ is the  i-th  diagonal term of $\tensTwo{M}$ .

One therefore sees **that if** ${\alpha}_{m} \ne 0$ , the term of highest degree is $\ne 0$ (because the ${m}_{i}$ are $>0$ ) and therefore the dualised system $(S)$ has more eigenvalues than the reduced system: $(n-p)$ . The two systems are therefore not equivalent.

Let us choose ${\alpha}_{m}={\beta}_{m}=0$. Then one has, from the third and the fourth equation

$$
\begin{split}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    {\vector{\lambda}}_{1}={\vector{\lambda}}_{2} \\
    \vector{X}_{1} = - \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \tensTwo{B}_{2} \vector{X}_{2}
\end{array}  \right .\end{split}
$$

then, from the first equation

$$
{\vector{\lambda}}_{1}
=
{\vector{\lambda}}_{2}
=
- \frac{1}{2 \, \beta }  \inverseTranspose{\tensTwo{B}_{1}}
\left(
    -\tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{1} \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \tensTwo{B}_{2}
    +
    \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{12}
\right)  \vector{X}_{2}
$$

by injecting into the second equation

$$
-
\tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{12}  \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \tensTwo{B}_{2} \vector{X}_{2}
+
\tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{2} \vector{X}_{2}
-
\transpose{\tensTwo{B}_{2}}
\inverseTranspose{\tensTwo{B}_{1}}
\left(
    -
    \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{1} \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}}  {\tensTwo{B}_{2}}
    +
    \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{12}
\right) \vector{X}_{2}
=
\vectorZero
\iff
\left( {\tilde{K}}_{2}-{\omega}^{2}{\tilde{M}}_{2} \right) \vector{X}_{2}
=
\vectorZero
$$

with

$$
\begin{split}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{2} = - \transpose{\tensTwo{K}_{12}} \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} {\tensTwo{B}_{2}}
                      +

                       +

                       +

$$

One observes that the definitions of ${\tilde{K}}_{2}$ and ${\tilde{M}}_{2}$ are identical to those given by the non-dualised system.

One therefore sees that every eigenvector $\vector{X}_{2}$ of the dualised system is also an eigenvector of the reduced system (with the same natural circular frequency) if one projects it onto the space $\vector{U}_{2}$. Conversely, every eigenvector $\vector{X}_{2}$ of the reduced problem can be extended into an eigenvector of the dualised system $\transpose{\vector{X}}=\left[\transpose{\vector{X}_{1}},\transpose{\vector{X}_{2}},\transpose{\vector{\lambda}},\transpose{\vector{\lambda}_{2}}\right]$ .

with:

$$
\begin{split}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \vector{X}_{1} = - \tensTwo{B}_{1}^{-1} \tensTwo{B}_{2} \vector{X}_{2} \\

                                -

                                +
                                \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{12}
                            \right ) \vector{X}_{2} \\
    \vector{\lambda}_{2} = -\frac{1}{2 \, \beta}
                           \inverseTranspose{\tensTwo{B}_{1}}
                            \left(
                                -
                                \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{1} \inverse{\tensTwo{B}_{1}} \tensTwo{B}_{2}
                                +
                                \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{12}
                            \right ) \vector{X}_{2} \\
\end{array} \right.\end{split}
$$

The two systems are therefore equivalent; they have the same eigenmodes and the same eigenvalues. The dualised system, although of dimension higher than the reduced system, does not have more eigenvalues than the reduced system (the dimension of the eigenspace is the same).

**Conclusion**

The dualised system is equivalent to the reduced system as soon as one chooses ${\alpha}_{m}={\beta}_{m}=0$, that is, if one takes the dualised stiffness matrix but does not modify the mass matrix.

One therefore has

$$
\begin{split}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \tensTwo{B}_{1} = \tensTwoUnit \\
    \tensTwo{B}_{2} = \tensTwoZero
\end{array}  \right .\end{split}
$$

$$
{\tilde{K}}_{2}
=
k \left( 1+\gamma \right)^{2}
$$

and

$$
{\tilde{M}}_{2}
=
m \left( 1+{\gamma}^{2} \right)
$$

whence the following expression for the circular frequency

$$
\begin{split}\tensTwo{A}^{\prime}
=
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
    \tensTwo{A} & \beta \transpose{\tensTwo{B}}& \beta \transpose{\tensTwo{B}}\\
    \beta \tensTwo{B} & -\alpha \tensTwoUnit & +\alpha \tensTwoUnit\\
    \beta \tensTwo{B} & \alpha \tensTwoUnit& -\alpha \tensTwoUnit
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

$$
{\tilde{\omega}}^{2}
=
\frac{k}{m}\frac{{(1+\gamma )}^{2}}{1+{\gamma}^{2}}
$$

and ${X}_{2}=1$.

One observes that the eigenvalue of the constrained system ${\tilde{\omega}}^{2}$ depends on the ratio $\gamma =\frac{\beta}{\alpha}$ and that it does indeed respect the bracketing between the two eigenvalues of the unconstrained system. Indeed, one has:

- If $\gamma =0,{\tilde{\omega}}^{2}=\frac{k}{m}$ (case of the fixed-free spring)
- If $\gamma =1,{\tilde{\omega}}^{2}=\frac{2k}{m}$ (the rigid mode is blocked)
- If $\gamma \to \infty ,{\tilde{\omega}}^{2} \to \frac{k}{m}$ (case of the fixed-free spring)

Let us choose $\alpha=\beta=1$ in order to simplify, and let us write the dualised system:

$$
\begin{split}\left( \left[\begin{array}{cccc}
    k& -k& {\beta}_{k}& {\beta}_{k}\\
    -k& k& {\beta}_{k}& {\beta}_{k}\\
    {\beta}_{k}& {\beta}_{k}& -{\alpha}_{k}& {\alpha}_{k}\\
    {\beta}_{k}& {\beta}_{k}& {\alpha}_{k}& -{\alpha}_{k}
\end{array}\right]
-
\lambda \left[\begin{array}{cccc}
    m& 0& {\beta}_{m}& {\beta}_{m}\\
    0& m& {\beta}_{m}& {\beta}_{m}\\
    {\beta}_{m}& {\beta}_{m} & -{\alpha}_{m}& {\alpha}_{m}\\
    {\beta}_{m}& {\beta}_{m}& {\alpha}_{m}& -{\alpha}_{m}
\end{array}\right]\right)
\left\lbrace \begin{array}
    {c}{x}_{1}\\
    {x}_{2}\\
    {\lambda}_{1}\\
    {\lambda}_{2}
\end{array}\right\rbrace
=
\vector{0}\end{split}
$$

The eigenvalues of this system are:

$$
{\omega}^{2}
=
\left\lbrace
    \frac{{\beta}_{k}}{{\beta}_{m}},
    \frac{{\beta}_{k}}{{\beta}_{m}},
    \frac{{\beta}_{k}}{{\beta}_{m}},
    \frac{2k}{m}
\right\rbrace
$$

One observes that one recovers the relevant eigenvalue (the fourth), but that one also finds three spurious eigenvalues due to the non-vanishing of ${\alpha}_{m}$ and ${\beta}_{m}$ .

If one chooses ${\alpha}_{m}={\beta}_{m}=0$, the computation shows that the characteristic polynomial is of degree 1 and that its only solution is:

$$
\begin{split}\left \lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    {\tilde{\omega}}^{2}=\frac{2k}{m}\\
    X = \left( -1, +1 \right)
\end{array} \right.\end{split}
$$

which is the solution sought (blocked rigid mode).

### 7.9 Conclusions

If $\tensTwo{K}$ and $\tensTwo{M}$ are the stiffness and mass matrices of an unconstrained system and if the linear constraints can be written in the form

$$
\begin{split}\left[
    {\tensTwo{B}}_{1} {\tensTwo{B}}_{2}
\right]
\left \lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    {\vector{U}}_{1}\\
    {\vector{U}}_{2}
\end{array}\right \rbrace
=
\vectorZero\end{split}
$$

with ${\tensTwo{B}}_{1}$ invertible. Then the eigenmodes of the constrained structure are those of the reduced system:

$$
\left( \tensTwo{\tilde{K}}_{2}-{\omega}^{2}\tensTwo{\tilde{M}}_{2} \right) \vector{X}_{2}
=
\vectorZero
$$

The dualised system (double Lagrange multipliers) which is written:

$$
\left( \tensTwo{\tilde{\tilde{K}}}-{\omega}^{2} \tensTwo{\tilde{\tilde{M}}} \right) \vector{\tilde{\tilde{X}}}
=
\vectorZero
$$

with as unknowns

$$
\transpose{\vector{\tilde{\tilde{X}}}}
=
\left[   {\vector{X}}_{1}   {\vector{X}}_{2}   \vector{\lambda}_{1}  \vector{\lambda}_{2} \right]
$$

and for matrices

$$
\begin{split}\tilde{\tilde{\tensTwo{M}}}
=
\left[\begin{array}{cccc}
    {\tensTwo{K}}_{1} & {\tensTwo{K}}_{12}& \transpose{{\tensTwo{B}}_{1}} & \transpose{{\tensTwo{B}}_{1}}\\
    \transpose{\tensTwo{K}_{12}}& {\tensTwo{K}}_{2}& \transpose{{\tensTwo{B}}_{2}}& \transpose{{\tensTwo{B}}_{2}}\\
    {\tensTwo{B}}_{1} & {\tensTwo{B}}_{2}& -\tensTwoUnit& \tensTwoUnit\\
    {\tensTwo{B}}_{1} & {\tensTwo{B}}_{2}& \tensTwoUnit& -\tensTwoUnit
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

and

$$
\begin{split}\tilde{\tilde{\tensTwo{K}}}
=
\left[\begin{array}{cccc}
    {\tensTwo{M}}_{1}& {\tensTwo{M}}_{12} & \tensTwoZero & \tensTwoZero \\
    \transpose{{\tensTwo{M}}_{12}} & {\tensTwo{M}}_{2} & \tensTwoZero & \tensTwoZero \\
    \tensTwoZero & \tensTwoZero & \tensTwoZero & \tensTwoZero \\
    \tensTwoZero & \tensTwoZero & \tensTwoZero & \tensTwoZero
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

then has the same solutions as the reduced system.

## 8 Appendix: proofs of a few results

### 8.1 Equivalent problems

Let the problem $(Pb1)$ be:

$$
\begin{split}(Pb1):
\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    \underset{\vector{u} \in V}{\min}J(\vector{u}) \\
    \text{avec } J(\vector{u}) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \tensTwo{A} \vector{u} , \vector{u} \right)-\left(\vector{b} , \vector{u}\right)
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

$V$ is an affine subspace of ${\setR}^{n}=\left\lbrace \vector{u} \in {\setR}^{n}\text{ tel que }\tensTwo{\tensTwo{B}}_i \vector{u}-\vector{d}_i= \vector{0}, \, \forall i=1,p \right\rbrace$. $\tensTwo{A}$ and $b$ are defined on ${\setR}^{n}$ and $\tensTwo{A}$ is a symmetric positive matrix of order $n$ .

Let the problem $(Pb2)$ be:

$$
\begin{split}(Pb2):
\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    \text{Trouver } \vector{u} \in V \\
    \text{Tel que } (\tensTwo{A} \vector{u},  \vector{v}_0)-\left(  \vector{b} , \vector{v}_0 \right)= \vectorZero \, \forall {v}_{0} \in {V}_{0})
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

with the space $V=\left\lbrace \vector{u} \in  {\setR}^{n} \text{ tel que }\tensTwo{\tensTwo{B}}_i \vector{u}-\vector{d}_i= \vectorZero, \,\forall i=1,p\right\rbrace$ and ${V}_{0}=\left\lbrace {v}_{0}\in {\setR}^{n}\text{ tel que }\tensTwo{\tensTwo{B}}_i \vector{u}=\vectorZero, \, \forall i=1,p\right\rbrace$

It will be shown that the two previous problems are equivalent.

Let us first note that the problem $(Pb2)$ is equivalent to the problem $(Pb2^{\prime})$.

$$
\begin{split}(Pb2^{\prime}):
\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    \text{Trouver } \vector{u} \in V \\
    \text{Tel que } (\tensTwo{A} \vector{u} , \vector{v}-\vector{u})-(\vector{b},\vector{v}-\vector{u})=\vectorZero \, \forall v\in V)
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

with the space $V=\left\lbrace \vector{u} \in  {\setR}^{n}\text{ tel que }\tensTwo{\tensTwo{B}}_i \vector{u}-\vector{d}_i=\vectorZero \,\forall i=1,p\right\rbrace$. There is indeed a bijection between the set of $\left\lbrace \left(\vector{v}-\vector{u}\right) , \vector{u} \in  V , \vector{v} \in V \right\rbrace$ and the set ${V}_{0}$ .

Let us show that the problem $(Pb2^{\prime})$ is equivalent to the problem $(Pb1)$.

Let $\vector{u}$ be a solution of $(Pb2^{\prime})$. Then $\forall \vector{v} \in V$:

$$
J(\vector{v})-J(\vector{u})
=
\frac{1}{2} \left( \left( \tensTwo{A} \vector{v}, \vector{v} \right) \right)
-
(\vector{b} , \vector{v})
-
\frac{1}{2} \left( \left( \tensTwo{A} \vector{u}, \vector{u} \right) \right)
+
(\vector{b}, \vector{u})
$$

$$
J(\vector{v})-J(\vector{u})
=
\frac{1}{2} \left( \left( \tensTwo{A} \vector{v}, \vector{v} \right) \right)
-
\left(\tensTwo{A} \vector{u} , \vector{v}-\vector{u} \right)
-
\frac{1}{2} \left( \left( \tensTwo{A} \vector{u}, \vector{u} \right) \right)
$$

$$
J(\vector{v})-J(\vector{u})
=
\frac{1}{2} \left(
    \left( \tensTwo{A} \vector{v}, \vector{v} \right)
    -
    2 \left( \tensTwo{A} \vector{u} , \vector{v}  \right)
    +
    \left( \tensTwo{A} \vector{u} , \vector{u}  \right)
\right)
=
\frac{1}{2} \left(
\left( \tensTwo{A} \vector{u} - \vector{v} , \vector{u} - \vector{v} \right)
\right) \ge 0
$$

Let us now compute the derivative of $J(\vector{u})$. One has:

$$
\begin{split}\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    \forall \vector{u} \in V \text{ et } \forall \vector{v}_{0} \in {V}_{0} \\
    J^{\prime}(\vector{u}) \cdot \vector{v}_{0}
    =
    \underset{\varepsilon \to 0}{\lim} \frac { J \left( \vector{u}+\varepsilon \vector{v}_{0} \right)-J(\vector{u})}{\varepsilon}
    =
    \underset{\varepsilon \to 0}{\lim} \left[
        \left( \tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}, \vector{v}_{0} \right)
        +
        \frac{\varepsilon}{2} \left( {\tensTwo{A} \, \vector{v}}_{0}, \vector{v}_{0} \right)
        -
        \left( \vector{b}, \vector{v}_{0} \right)
    \right]
    =
    \left( \tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u} - \vector{b} \right ) \, \vector{v}_{0}
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

Let $\vector{u}$ be the solution of $(Pb1)$, then $\forall \vector{v} \in V$ and ${\vector{v}}_{0}\in {V}_{0}$ one sets ${\vector{v}}_{0}=\vector{v}-\vector{u}$. Then one has:

$$
\forall \varepsilon, \quad
\frac{J \left( \vector{u}+\varepsilon \vector{v}_{0} \right)-J \left( \vector{u} \right)}{\varepsilon} \ge 0 \quad
\Rightarrow
J^{\prime}(\vector{u}) \cdot \vector{v}_{0} \ge 0, \quad
\forall \vector {v}_{0}\in {V}_{0}
$$

One sees that $J^{\prime}(\vector{u})$, which is a linear form on ${V}_{0}$, must be systematically positive. This is possible only if this form is identically zero.

One concludes from this that:

$$
J^{\prime}(\vector{u}) \cdot ( \vector{v}-\vector{u})
=
\left( \tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}- \vector{b} \right) \left( \vector{v}- \vector{u} \right )=0, \quad \forall \vector{v} \in V
$$

### 8.2 Proof of the rule ${R}_{0}$

#### 8.2.1 Definitions and notation

$\tensTwo{A}$ is the unconstrained stiffness matrix of size $\left({n}^{\prime} \times n \right)$ (symmetric and positive). $\tensTwo{B}$ is the constraint matrix such that $\tensTwo{B} \vector{u}-\vector{d}=\vectorZero$ with $\tensTwo{B}$ the matrix of size  matrix $\left( {n}^{\prime} \times p \right)$ (with $p<n$).

$\vector{u}$ is the vector of the physical degrees of freedom in ${\setR}^{n}$, $\vector{\lambda}^{1}$ is the vector of the first Lagrange degrees of freedom in ${\setR}^{p}$ and $\vector{\lambda}^{2}$ is the vector of the second Lagrange degrees of freedom in ${\setR}^{p}$.

One denotes by $\vector{x}$ the vector of the unknowns $\left( \vector{u}, \vector{\lambda}^{1}, \vector{\lambda}^{2} \right) \in {\setR}^{n}\times {\setR}^{p} \times {\setR}^{p}$. One denotes:

- $\discVect{U}$ the set of the physical degrees of freedom,
- $\discVect{\Lambda}^{1}$ the set of the first Lagrange degrees of freedom,
- $\discVect{\Lambda}^{2}$ the set of the second Lagrange degrees of freedom.
- $\alpha \in {\setR}^{+}$ coefficients
- $\discMatr{K}$ the matrix of order $2p+n$, symmetric, such that

$$
\begin{split}\discMatr{K}
=
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}
    \tensTwo{A} & \transpose{\tensTwo{B}} & \transpose{\tensTwo{B}}\\
    \tensTwo{B} & -\alpha \tensTwoUnit    & \alpha \tensTwoUnit        \\
    \tensTwo{B} & \alpha \tensTwoUnit     & -\alpha \tensTwoUnit
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

The matrix $\discMatr{K}$ written above corresponds to a certain numbering of the unknowns $\discVect{X} = \left( \discVect{U}, \discVect{\Lambda}^{1}, \discVect{\Lambda}^{2} \right)$. The true matrix $\discMatr{K}$ of which one seeks to show that it is factorisable by $LDL^{T}$ without permutation is not written with this numbering. The only numbering rule taken into account is the following:

**Rule ${R}_{0}$**

For each constraint relation, one must place ${\vector{\lambda}}^{1}$ before the first constrained physical degree of freedom and ${\vector{\lambda}}^{2}$ after the last constrained physical degree of freedom.
In order to reduce the memory occupation of the matrix $\tensTwo{K}$ , one must seek to minimise the bandwidth. This is what one does by "framing" the relations "as closely as possible": ${\vector{\lambda}}^{1}$ is placed just before the first constrained degree of freedom, ${\vector{\lambda}}^{2}$ is placed just after the last constrained dof.

### 8.3 Proof

The two Lagrange degrees of freedom associated with a constraint equation ${\tensTwo{B}}_{i} \vector{U}-\vector{d}_i= \vectorZero$ frame the physical dof constrained by this equation. In what follows, in order to simplify the writing, one will take $\alpha=1$.

One seeks to show that every sub-matrix $\discMatr{K}_{i}$ of $\discMatr{K}$ is invertible. Let a given sub-matrix $\discMatr{K}_{i}$ . It corresponds to a partition of the degrees of freedom: those of rank $\le i$ , those of rank $\ge i$ .

We shall denote:

- ${\tilde{U}}$ the subset of the set ${U}$ corresponding to the degrees of freedom of rank $\le i$ .
- ${\tilde{\tilde{U}}}$ the subset of the set ${U}$ corresponding to the degrees of freedom of rank $> i$ .
- ${L}_{1}$ is the set of the pairs $(\vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1},\vector{\lambda}_{1}^{2})$ such that $\rank(\vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}) < \rank(\vector{\lambda}_{1}^{2}) \le i$. This set can be separated into two subsets

- ${L}_{3}$ is the set of the pairs $(\vector{\lambda}_{3}^{1},\vector{\lambda}_{3}^{2})$ such that $i<\rank(\vector{\lambda}_{3}^{1}) < \rank({\vector\lambda}_{3}^{2})$. This set can be separated into two subsets

$$
{L}_{1}^{1} = \left\lbrace \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1} \right\rbrace
\text{ et }
{L}_{1}^{2} = \left\lbrace \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{2} \right\rbrace
$$

- ${L}_{2}$ is the set of the pairs $(\vector{\lambda}_{2}^{1},\vector{\lambda}_{2}^{2})$ such that $\rank(\vector{\lambda}_{2}^{1}) \le i< \rank(\vector{\lambda}_{2}^{2})$. This set can be separated into two subsets

$$
{L}_{3}^{1} = \left\lbrace \vector{\lambda}_{3}^{1} \right\rbrace
\text{ et }
{L}_{3}^{2} = \left\lbrace \vector{\lambda}_{3}^{2} \right\rbrace
$$

$$
{L}_{2}^{1} = \left\lbrace \vector{\lambda}_{2}^{1} \right\rbrace
\text{ et }
{L}_{2}^{2} = \left\lbrace \vector{\lambda}_{2}^{2} \right\rbrace
$$

The resulting set $L$ is therefore written

$$
\begin{split}L = \underset{\begin{array}{c} i=1,3 \\ j=1,2 \end{array}} {\cup}{L}_{i}^{j}\end{split}
$$

The matrix $\tensTwo{B}$ can be split into three parts $\tensTwo{B}_1$, $\tensTwo{B}_2$ and $\tensTwo{B}_3$ corresponding to the splitting $({L}_{1},{L}_{2},{L}_{3})$. Each matrix ${\tensTwo{B}}_{i}$ can be split into two parts $\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{i}$ corresponding to the splitting $(\vector{\tilde{U}},\vector{\tilde{\tilde{U}}})$.

The matrix $\tensTwo{A}$ can be split into four parts corresponding to the splitting $(\vector{\tilde{U}},\vector{\tilde{\tilde{U}}})$.

$$
\begin{split}\discMatr{A}
=
\left[\begin{array}{cc}
    \tensTwo{\tilde{A}} & \tensTwo{\tilde{A}}\\
    \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{A}}} & \tensTwo{\tilde{\tilde{A}}}
\end{array}\right]\end{split}
$$

With the aid of this notation, the problem to be solved is to show that the matrix $\discMatr{K}_{i}$ is invertible.

$$
\begin{split}\discMatr{K}_{i} =
\left[\begin{array}{cccc}
    -\tensTwoUnit &  \tensTwoUnit &  \tensTwoZero & {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{1} \\
     \tensTwoUnit & -\tensTwoUnit &  \tensTwoZero & {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{1} \\
     \tensTwoZero &  \tensTwoZero & -\tensTwoUnit & {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{2} \\
     \transpose{{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{1}} & \transpose{{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{1}} &
     \transpose{{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{2}} & \tensTwo{\tilde{A}}
\end{array} \right]\end{split}
$$

This matrix corresponds to the vector ${\discVect{X}}_{i}=\left\lbrace\begin{array}{c} \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}\\ \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{2}\\ \vector{\lambda}_{2}^{1}\\ \vector{\tilde{U}} \end{array}\right \rbrace$. It must be shown that:

$$
\tensTwo {K}_{i}  {\vector{X}}_{i} = \vectorZero \Rightarrow {\vector{X}}_{i} = \vectorZero
$$

This problem is equivalent to:

$$
\begin{split}(S):
\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    -\vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1} + \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{2} + \tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{1} \vector{\tilde{u}} = \vectorZero \\
     \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1} - \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{2} + \tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{1} \vector{\tilde{u}} = \vectorZero \\
    -\vector{\lambda}_{2}^{1} + \tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2} \vector{\tilde{u}}  = \vectorZero \\
     \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{1}} \left(
        \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}
        +
        \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{2} \right )
    +
    \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}}{\vector\lambda}_{2}^{1}
    +
    \tensTwo{\tilde{A}} \, \vector{\tilde{u}}  = \vectorZero \\
\end{array} \right .
\Rightarrow
\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1} = \vectorZero \\
    \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{2} = \vectorZero \\
    \vector{\lambda}_{2}^{1} = \vectorZero \\
    \vector{\tilde{U}} = \vectorZero \\
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

**General case**

It is assumed that ${\tilde{U}} \ne \varnothing$, ${L}_{1} \ne \varnothing$ and  ${L}_{2}\ne \varnothing$. Then the problem $(S)$ is written

$$
\begin{split}(S):
\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1} = \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{2} \\
    {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{1} \vector{\tilde{u}} = \vectorZero \\
    \vector{\lambda}_{2}^{1} = {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{2 } \vector{\tilde{u}}
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

and therefore

$$
2 \, \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{1}} \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}
+
\left (
    \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}} {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}}
    +
    \tensTwo{\tilde{A}}
\right )
\vector{\tilde{u}}
=
\vectorZero
$$

From this equation, one deduces:

$$
2 \, \transpose{\vector{\tilde{u}}}
\transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{1}} \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}
+
\transpose{\vector{\tilde{u}}}
\left (
    \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}} {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}}
    +
    \tensTwo{\tilde{A}}
\right )
\vector{\tilde{u}}
=
0
$$

Since ${\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{1} \vector{\tilde{u}} = \transpose{\vector{\tilde{u}}} \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{1}} = \vectorZero$, one obtains:

$$
\transpose{\vector{\tilde{u}}}
\left (
    \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}} {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}}
    +
    \tensTwo{\tilde{A}}
\right )
\vector{\tilde{u}}
=
0
$$

and therefore, by expanding again:

$$
\transpose{\vector{\tilde{u}}}
\transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}} {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}}
\vector{\tilde{u}}
+
\transpose{\vector{\tilde{u}}}
\tensTwo{\tilde{A}}
\vector{\tilde{u}}
=
0
$$

Now $\tensTwo{\tilde{A}}$ is symmetric positive (as a sub-matrix of a positive matrix) and $\transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}}{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{2}$ is also a symmetric positive matrix, therefore this sum can be zero only if the two terms are zero.

$$
\begin{split}\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    \transpose{\vector{\tilde{u}}}
     \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}} {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}}
    \vector{\tilde{u}}=0\\
    \transpose{\vector{\tilde{u}}}
    \tensTwo{\tilde{A}}
    \vector{\tilde{u}} =0
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

From the first equation, one has ${\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}} \vector{\tilde{u}}= \vectorZero$ and therefore $\vector{\lambda}_{2}^{1}=\vectorZero$. For the second equation, since $\tensTwo{\tilde{A}}$ is a positive matrix, one wants to show that

$$
\transpose{\vector{\tilde{u}}}  \tensTwo{\tilde{A}}  \vector{\tilde{u}} = 0
\Rightarrow
\vector{\tilde{u}} = \vectorZero
$$

Let us extend $\vector{\tilde{u}}$ onto ${\setR}^{n}$ by $\vector{\tilde{\tilde{u}}}=\vectorZero$. One therefore has $\vector{u}=\left( \vector{\tilde{u}}, \vector{\tilde{\tilde{u}}} \right)$.

$$
\transpose{\vector{u}}  \tensTwo{A} \vector{u}
=
\transpose{\vector{\tilde{u}}}  \tensTwo{\tilde{A}}  \vector{\tilde{u}}
=
0
\Rightarrow
\vector{u} \in \ker{\tensTwo{A}}
$$

One has

$$
\begin{split}\tensTwo{B} \vector{u}
=
\left \lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    {\tensTwo{B}}_{1} \vector{u} \\
    {\tensTwo{B}}_{2} \vector{u} \\
    {\tensTwo{B}}_{3} \vector{u}
\end{array}\right \rbrace
=
\left \lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{1} \vector{\tilde{u}} \\
    {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{2} \vector{\tilde{u}} \\
    {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{3} \vector{\tilde{u}}
\end{array}\right \rbrace
=
\left \lbrace \begin{array}{l}
    \vectorZero
\end{array}\right \rbrace\end{split}
$$

The first two rows are zero by virtue of the previous results. Moreover, $\tensTwo{{\tilde{B}}}_{3}=\tensTwoZero$ because otherwise there would exist dof of $\vector{\tilde{u}}$ constrained by equations not yet taken into account ( of rank $>i$ ), which is contrary to the rule ${R}_{0}$.

The extension $\vector{u}$ of $\vector{\tilde{u}}$ is therefore in the kernels of $\tensTwo{A}$ and $\tensTwo{B}$ . It will be shown that it is then zero. Let us take up again the problem with the "simple Lagrange multipliers".

$$
\begin{split}({S}_{2}):
\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{l}
    \tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u} + \transpose{\tensTwo{B}} \vector{\lambda} = \vector{b} \\
    \tensTwo{B} \vector{u} = \vector{d}
\end{array} \right .\end{split}
$$

One sets $\vector{u}_{0} \ne \vectorZero$ such that ${\tensTwo{A} \, \vector{u}}_{0}= \vectorZero$ and ${\tensTwo{C} \, \vector{u}}_{0}= \vectorZero$ . If ${u}_{1}$ is a solution of $({S}_{2})$, one sees that then $\vector{u}_{1}+\mu \, \vector{u}_{0}$ is also a solution. Which is impossible because we assume that our problem is physically well posed.

One concludes from this that $\vector{u}=\vectorZero \Rightarrow \vector{\tilde{u}} = \vectorZero$ .

It only remains to prove that $\vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}=\vectorZero$. One starts again from the equation

$$
\transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{1}} \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}
+
\left (
    \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}} {\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{2}}
    +
    \tensTwo{\tilde{A}}
\right )
\vector{\tilde{u}}
=
\vectorZero
$$

One has just proved that $\vector{\tilde{u}} = \vectorZero$ . One therefore now has only

$$
\transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{1}} \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}
=
\vectorZero
$$

In the same way as the rule ${R}_{0}$ imposing $\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}_{3}=\tensTwoZero$ , one sees that $\tensTwo{\tilde{\tilde{\tensTwo{B}}}}_{1}=\tensTwoZero$.

$$
\begin{split}\transpose{\tensTwo{B}}_{1} \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}
=
\left\lbrace\begin{array}{l}
    \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{B}}}_{1} \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1} \\
    \transpose{\tensTwo{\tilde{\tilde{B}}}}_{1} \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}
\end{array}\right \rbrace
=
\left\lbrace\begin{array}{l}
    \vectorZero \\
    \vectorZero
\end{array}\right \rbrace
=
\vectorZero\end{split}
$$

Let us reason by contradiction: if $\vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}\ne \vectorZero$ is such that $\transpose{\tensTwo{B}}_{1} \vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}=\vectorZero$ , it is because there exists a linear combination of the rows of ${\tensTwo{B}}_{1}$ which is zero, which is contradictory with the fact that the rows of ${\tensTwo{B}}_{1}$ are independent of one another (physically well-posed problem). Therefore one does indeed have $\vector{\lambda}_{1}^{1}=\vectorZero$.

**Particular case**

When one (or more) of the sets $\tilde{U}$, ${L}_{1}$ , ${L}_{2}$ is empty, the system $(S)$ is simplified. One can verify that the reasonings which one has made in the general case make it possible to prove analogous results.
