# r4.05.06 Equivalent linear method for the propagation of waves in 1D

**Summary**

This document describes the equivalent linear method and its implementation in *Code_Aster* in the command DEFI_SOL_EQUI [U4.81.31](doc:u4.81.31).

The equivalent linear method makes it possible to compute the response of a one-dimensional soil column (1D – one component) or of a multidimensional one (1D – three components), stratified horizontally, to the vertical propagation of shear waves (SV). It accounts in an approximate way for the behaviour of soils under cyclic loading. The solution method of the equivalent linear model is an iterative procedure, in which one evaluates at each iteration, for each soil layer, linear viscoelastic characteristics equivalent to those given by the degradation curves of the shear modulus $G$ and of the increase of the hysteretic damping measured experimentally.

The simplicity of the implementation, the quality of the results and the rapidity of the computations make it a method very widely used in engineering. The one-dimensional equivalent linear method is thus implemented in numerous analysis codes (SHAKE, FLUSH, CYBERQUAKE, DEEPSOIL, EERA,...).

The application of the equivalent linear method remains valid as long as the volumetric strain of the soil can be neglected. In the contrary case, it is necessary to turn to non-linear models integrating the shear–mean-pressure coupling (or deviatoric strains–dilatancy) such as the cyclic elastoplastic model of Hujeux [R7.01.23](doc:r7.01.23) (Cyclic constitutive law of Hujeux for soils).

Traditionally the equivalent linear model is accepted up to distortion strains in free field of the order of ${10}^{-3}$ (Guide ASN/2/01). This remains an order of magnitude of validity, which it is necessary to adapt to the situation and to the phenomena that one wishes to model.

## 1 Introduction

Idriss and Seed (1967) [1] were the first to propose an equivalent linear approach for the analysis of the site response to a seismic solicitation. Later, Schnabel et al. (1972) [2] implemented this method in the frequency domain with the program SHAKE. The equivalent linear method has since been implemented in several analysis codes (SHAKE, FLUSH, CYBERQUAKE, DEEPSOIL, EERA,...) and in **code_aster** under the operator DEFI_SOL_EQUI, [U4.84.31](doc:u4.84.31).

This method is suited to the evaluation of the response of soil profiles made up of horizontal layers subjected to the vertical propagation of seismic waves. A 1D modelling – one component considers only the propagation of shear waves in a horizontal direction, whereas a 1D modelling – three components considers the vertical propagation of three plane waves (SV, SH and P).

Indeed the soil can in practice be considered as linear viscoelastic for distortions satisfying $\gamma =2{\epsilon}_{xz}\le 1,0\times {10}^{-5}$ . For higher distortions, the behaviour of the soil is described by experimental curves giving the variation of the shear modulus $G$ and of the percentage of critical damping $D$ as a function of the distortion (denoted gamma $\gamma =2{\varepsilon}_{xz}$ ) (see figure ). one admits that this approach is acceptable up to distortions satisfying: $\gamma =2{\epsilon}_{xz}\le 1,0\times {10}^{-3}$ . beyond that, a non-linear constitutive model coupling dilatancy and distortions should be used.

## 2 One-dimensional viscoelastic stress–strain relation

The equivalent linear model is based on the viscoelastic rheological model of Kelvin-Voigt. The latter, represented in , is constituted in parallel of a linear spring and of a linear damper representing an internal resistance to the strain.

![Figure](/figures/r4.05.06/10000000000001080000011B8DBCCF4CB3C631F2.png)

Figure 2-1: Representation of the rheological model of Kelvin-Voigt.

The constitutive relation of this rheological model is the following:

$$
{\sigma}_{xz}=G\gamma +H\frac{\partial \gamma }{\partial t}=G\gamma +H\dot{\gamma}
$$

with ${\sigma}_{xz}$ the shear stress, $\gamma$ the distortion (equal to $2{\epsilon}_{xz}$ ), $G$ the shear modulus and $H$ the viscosity modulus. The dot represents the time derivative.

In the case of a one-dimensional soil column, denoting by $u(z,t)$ the horizontal displacement at a depth $z$ and at a given instant $t$ , the distortion and its time derivative are defined from the displacement field $u(z,t)$ :

$$
\gamma =\frac{\partial u(z,t)}{\partial z}
$$

And its time derivative:

$$
\dot{\gamma}=\frac{\partial \gamma (z,t)}{\partial t}=\frac{{\partial}^{2}u(z,t)}{\partial z\partial t}
$$

In the case of a harmonic loading $u(z,t)=U(z){e}^{i\omega t}$ , the distortion is written:

$$
\gamma (z,t)=\frac{\partial u(z,t)}{\partial z}=\frac{\mathit{dU}(z)}{\mathit{dz}}{e}^{i\omega t}=\Gamma (z){e}^{i\omega t}
$$

And its time derivative:

$$
\dot{\gamma}(z,t)=i\omega \gamma (z,t)
$$

with $U(z)$ and $\Gamma (z)$ respectively the amplitude of the displacement and of the distortion.

The constitutive stress–strain relation of the Kelvin-Voigt model is then defined:

$$
{\sigma}_{xz}(z,t)=G\gamma +H\dot{\gamma}=G(1+i\eta \omega )\gamma (z,t)={G}^{\ast }\gamma (z,t)
$$

with ${G}^{\text{*}}$ the complex shear modulus ${G}^{\ast }=(G+i\eta \omega )$ ; the loss factor is here equal to $\eta \omega$ .

During a symmetric cyclic loading, the response of the soil presents cycles or hysteresis loops in the stress–strain plane (see ).

![Figure](/figures/r4.05.06/100000000000018D00000184D300721119BC9B75.png)

Figure2-2. Response of a soil to a symmetric cyclic loading, ${\sigma}_{xz}$ the shear stress, $\gamma$ the distortion, $D$ the damping [4].

These loops represent the quantity of strain energy dissipated by the soil during the loading. This energy can be quantified by defining the reduced damping coefficient $D$ (see the ) of the soil by the following relation:

$$
D=\frac{{W}_{d}}{4\pi {W}_{s}}
$$

where ${W}_{d}$ represents the energy dissipated in a complete loading cycle (equal to the interior area formed by the stress–strain loop, in grey on the ), and ${W}_{s}$ the elastic energy stored by the soil during the loading for the distortion level ${\gamma}_{c}$ (lower triangle, hatched on the ).

These two energies are computed in the case of a harmonic loading $\gamma (t)={\gamma}_{c}{e}^{i\omega t}$ with the Kelvin-Voigt model:

$$
{W}_{d}=\underset{{{\sigma}_{xz}}_{c}}{\oint}{\sigma}_{xz}d\gamma =\underset{t}{\overset{t+\frac{2\pi }{\omega}}{\oint}}\mathrm{\Re }[{\sigma}_{xz}]\mathrm{\Re }[\frac{d\gamma }{\mathit{dt}}]\mathit{dt}=\pi \eta \omega {\gamma}_{c}^{2}
$$

And:

$$
{W}_{s}=\frac{1}{2}{\tau}_{c}{\gamma}_{c}=\frac{1}{2}G{\gamma}_{c}^{2}
$$

For the viscoelastic model of Kelvin-Voigt, the energy dissipated during a loading cycle therefore depends on the frequency of the solicitation. Now experiment shows that for soils, the energy dissipated during shear is almost independent of the strain rate. The damping results essentially from irreversible plastic strains at the scale of the grain. The damping in the soil is of a hysteretic rather than a viscous nature [5]. The Kelvin-Voigt model is thus modified so that the dissipative properties are equivalent to the real material.

## 3 Three-dimensional stress–strain relation

One is interested in this part in defining an equivalent scalar quantity starting from a state of tensorial stresses and strains. One starts from the tensorial decomposition of the strain tensor $\epsilon$ into a volumetric part and deviatoric parts:

$$
\epsilon ={\epsilon}_{d}+\frac{1}{3}\mathit{tr}(\epsilon )I
$$

where ${\epsilon}_{d}$ is the deviatoric strain tensor. The equivalent linear modelling being interested in the shear behaviour of the soil column, the norm of the deviatoric strain tensor ${\epsilon}_{d}$ is the most appropriate candidate because one can relate it to the shear strain $\gamma$ for a one-dimensional loading:

$$
\gamma =\sqrt{3}{\epsilon}_{d}
$$

This expression makes it possible, from the computation of ${\epsilon}_{d}$ obtained by the 1D – 3 components approach, to go back to the value of $\gamma$ to be used to update the elastic modulus.

For the case of the propagation of plane waves P, SV and SH perpendicularly to the planes of the stratigraphy (i.e. vertical incidence for a horizontal stratigraphy), the computation of the deviatoric strain reduces to the following expression:

$$
{\epsilon}_{d}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}\left({\epsilon}_{d}:{\epsilon}_{d}\right)}
{\epsilon}_{d}=\frac{2}{3}\sqrt{{\epsilon}_{yy}^{2}+3{\epsilon}_{xy}^{2}+3{\epsilon}_{yz}^{2}}
$$

The equivalent stress chosen is the deviatoric stress $q$ , computed in post-processing via by the following expression:

$$
q=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}S:S}
\textrm{et}
q=3G{\epsilon}_{d}
$$

where $S$ is the deviatoric tensor, defined by the following expression:

$$
S=\sigma -\frac{1}{3}\mathit{tr}(\sigma )I
S=\sigma -\frac{1}{3}\mathit{tr}(\sigma )I
$$

## 4 Formulations of the equivalent linear behaviour

### 4.1 Formulation of Schnabel

Schnabel et al. (1972) [2] relate the viscosity to the damping of the material $D$ , by $H\omega =2\mathrm{GD}$ . The critical damping of the material $D$ is the value measured in the laboratory and reported on the $D$ -gamma curves.

The complex shear modulus is then written:

$$
{G}^{\ast }=(G+i\eta \omega )=(G+2i.\mathit{GD})=G(1+2i.D)
$$

The stiffness $G$ is taken equal to the secant modulus ${G}_{s}$ of the curves (${\sigma}_{xz}-\gamma$ ) (see ). The value of the complex shear modulus is equal to:

$$
∣{G}^{\ast }∣=G\sqrt{1+4{D}^{2}}
$$

The modulus of the complex shear modulus $∣{G}^{\ast }∣$ and $D$ therefore increase jointly. The  represents the value of $\frac{∣{G}^{\ast }∣}{G}$ as a function of $D$ . For example, for a reduced damping coefficient $D$ of 20%, the modulus of the complex shear modulus is 8% higher than the value of $G$ .

![Figure](/figures/r4.05.06/10000000000001E000000120E9A00E05092A663F.png)

Figure4.1-1. Evolution of $\frac{∣{G}^{\ast }∣}{G}$ as a function of $D$ for the model of Schnabel, compared with that for the model of Lysmer.

To solve this problem, Lysmer formulated a second equivalent linear model.

### 4.2 Formulation of Lysmer

The formulation of Lysmer consists in rewriting the complex shear modulus ${G}^{\ast }$ in order to ensure that the ratio $\frac{∣{G}^{\ast }∣}{G}$ is always equal to one. The complex shear modulus is then written:

$$
{G}^{\ast }=G((1-2{D}^{2})+2\mathit{iD}\sqrt{1-{D}^{2}})
$$

The energy dissipated in a cycle is then:

$$
{W}_{d}=2\pi G\sqrt{1-{D}^{2}}{\gamma}_{c}^{2}
$$

### 4.3 Comparison of the energies dissipated by the two formulations

The figure shows that the complex shear modulus $∣{G}^{\ast }∣$ is overestimated with the formulation of Schnabel, and rigorously equal to $G$ for the formulation of Lysmer. The energy dissipated during a loading cycle is also compared between the two formulations as a function of $D$ .

![Figure](/figures/r4.05.06/100000000000018A0000013359B2A1D1E97D18F8.png)

Figure 4.3-1: Comparison of the energy dissipated during a loading cycle for the two formulations of the equivalent linear model as a function of the damping $D$ [5].

The energy dissipated between the two formulations is the same up to about $D=0,2$ , then the formulation of Lysmer dissipates less energy. The domain of validity of the equivalent linear model means that the domain of interest of these curves is on the interval $[0,0.3]$ . One then notes that even if ${D}_{\mathit{Schnabel}}<{D}_{\mathit{Lysmer}}$ , the model of Schnabel can dissipate more energy than the model of Lysmer.

## 5 Simplified taking into account of the water pressure via the Byrne model

The simplified modelling of the influence of the water pressure on the shear response of a soil column with DEFI_SOL_EQUI rests on the following assumptions:

- the plastic volumetric strains developed during a seismic loading are essentially due to the shear strain undergone there by the soil,
- incompressibility of the pore water: the variations of water pressure in a saturated sand during a cyclic shear loading can be estimated from the volumetric strains obtained during a cyclic shear test carried out under drained conditions at the same confinement stress.

With these assumptions, the evaluation of the increase of water pressure during an earthquake (cyclic shear loading) requires evaluating the plastic volumetric strains generated by this earthquake under saturated conditions. These strains are a function of the cumulative plastic volumetric strain and of the amplitude of the shear strain applied (Martin et al., 1975; Byrne1991). Martin et al. (1975) consider that the slip between grains generating an increment of plastic volumetric strain $\Delta {\epsilon}_{v}^{p}$ during a drained or undrained shear test is the same. Any hydrostatic stress transferred by the slip of the grains to the pore water generates an increase of the water pressure, according to the following relation:

$$
\Delta u={E}_{r}\Delta {\epsilon}_{v}^{p}
$$

where $\Delta {\epsilon}_{v}^{p}$ represents the increment of plastic volumetric strain accumulated during a period of the shear-strain history, $\Delta u$ is the increment of water pressure and ${E}_{r}$ is the unloading/reloading modulus of the skeleton for the current effective stress level (Martin et al. 1975).

- Evaluation of $\Delta {\epsilon}_{v}^{p}$

The evaluation of $\Delta {\epsilon}_{v}^{p}$ is carried out by means of the model of Byrne (1991):

$$
\frac{\Delta {\epsilon}_{v}^{p}}{\gamma}={C}_{1}\exp(-{C}_{2}\frac{{\epsilon}_{v}^{p}}{\gamma})
$$

Where ${\epsilon}_{v}^{p}$ is the cumulative volumetric strain (sum of the $\Delta {\epsilon}_{v}^{p}$ ) since the beginning of the computation and ${C}_{1}$ and ${C}_{2}$ are the parameters of the model, which depend on the relative density ${D}_{r}$ or on the value of the SPT test ${({N}_{1})}_{60}$ . Byrne (1991) proposes an empirical relation in order to identify the parameters ${C}_{1}$ and ${C}_{2}$ from the value ${({N}_{1})}_{60}$ :

$$
{C}_{1}=\frac{8,7}{{({N}_{1})}_{60}^{1,25}}
{C}_{2}=\frac{0,4}{{C}_{1}}
$$

These expressions make it possible to obtain the increment of plastic volumetric strain for a harmonic loading. For a seismic loading (i.e. non-uniform), the evaluation of the plastic strains is done by successive half-cycles in an incremental way, via a cycle-counting method by zero crossing of the shear strain. This counting method on the strain restricts the use of this approach to the 1D – 1 component case only.

- Evaluation of ${E}_{r}$

The evaluation of ${E}_{r}$ is done by means of the expression proposed by Wu (1995), determined from the experimental tests:

$$
{E}_{r}=M\sigma {'}_{v}=M(\sigma {'}_{v0}-u)
$$

where the value of $M$ depends on the relative density ${D}_{r}$ or on the value of the SPT test ${({N}_{1})}_{60}$ . Wu (2001) proposes the following correlation for the computation of $M$ :

$$
M=10{({N}_{1})}_{60}+160
$$

This expression is integrated into the command DEFI_SOL_EQUI during the evaluation of the modulus ${E}_{r}$ .

- Evaluation of the pore-pressure ratio ${r}_{u}$

By combining the equations and one can estimate directly the ratio ${r}_{u}$ by the following equation:

$$
{r}_{u}=\frac{\Delta u}{\sigma {'}_{v0}}=1-\exp(-M{\epsilon}_{v}^{p})
$$

The value of ${r}_{u}$ is estimated for each half-cycle in an incremental way, from the computation of ${\epsilon}_{v}^{p}$ obtained by means of the expression .

One assumes that the impact of the water pressure on the shear modulus can be estimated by means of the following expression:

$$
{G}_{max}={G}_{0max}{(1-{r}_{\mathit{ueff}})}^{0,5}
$$

where ${G}_{0max}$ is the maximum shear modulus at the in-situ confinement pressure and ${r}_{\mathit{ueff}}$ is estimated as a factor ${\mathrm{\chi }}_{\mathit{ru}}$ of the maximum value of ${r}_{u}$ , ${r}_{\mathit{umax}}$ , in a manner similar to the weighting coefficient of the classical equivalent linear method § 7 .

$$
{r}_{\mathit{ueff}}={\mathrm{\chi }}_{\mathit{ru}}{r}_{\mathit{umax}}
$$

Therefore, the method presented below and applied by Kteich et al. (2018) to the estimation of liquefaction and seismically induced settlements in the city of Urayasu in Japan during the 2011 earthquake consists in enriching the shear behaviour described by the curves $G/{G}_{\max}$ as a function of the shear strain, by taking into account the influence of the water pressure for the computation of the degraded shear modulus § 6.3 . Nevertheless, it does not intervene for the obtaining of the value of hysteretic damping from the curves $D$ as a function of the effective strain.

## 6 Implementation of the equivalent linear method in code_aster

The command DEFI_SOL_EQUI[U4.84.31] makes it possible to compute the response of a one-dimensional soil column to a seismic solicitation with the equivalent linear model (formulation of Schnabel or Lysmer) in convolution or in deconvolution.

The solution method of the equivalent linear model is an iterative procedure, in which at each iteration, for each layer, equivalent linear characteristics are evaluated from the degradation curves of the shear modulus $G$ and of the increase of the hysteretic damping.

The command supplies as output the time signals, the Oscillator Response Spectra (SRO), the time strains and stresses at various depths as well as the degraded soil properties in accordance with the levels of shear strain computed.

The user must give as input to the command:

- A table giving the ratio $G/{G}_{max}$ as a function of Gamma,
- A table giving the reduced damping of the material $D$ as a function of Gamma,
- A table describing the initial material characteristics of the various layers (Young's modulus E, density RHO, Poisson's ratio NU, the hysteretic damping AMOR_HYST, possibly the value of ${({N}_{1})}_{60}$ N1 for the obtaining of the parameters of the Byrne model). The hysteretic damping is equal to twice the critical damping $\mathit{AH}=2D$ (see the justification in § 4.2 .).
- The 2D/3D mesh of the column respecting a mesh-cell size ${L}_{max}<\frac{{V}_{s}}{8{f}_{\mathit{coupure}}}$ ,
- A time signal (one-component or three-component) imposed under a free-field condition or at outcropping rock.

### 6.2 Formulations of the equivalent linear model

The command models the damping by a hysteretic damping (operator DEFI_MATERIAU keyword AMOR_HYST). From Young's modulus $E$ and the hysteretic damping $\mathrm{AH}$ supplied in DEFI_MATERIAU, **code_aster** builds the complex modulus ${E}^{\ast }$ according to the equation:

$$
{E}^{\ast }=E(1+\mathrm{i.AH})
$$

The construction of the complex modulus therefore corresponds to the formulation of Schnabel described in § 4.1 if $\mathit{AH}=\mathrm{2D}$ . That is why one must indeed specify $2{D}_{\min}$ as the initial value in the soil table.

However if the user has chosen the formulation of Lysmer, the command computes from the material data $E$ and $\mathit{AH}$ , a modulus ${E}_{1}$ and an equivalent damping ${\mathit{AH}}_{1}$ such that the formulation of Lysmer is recovered:

$$
{E}_{1}\left(1+i{\mathit{AH}}_{1}\right)=E\left(\left(1-\frac{{\mathit{AH}}^{2}}{2}\right)+ii\mathit{AH}\sqrt{1-\frac{{\mathit{AH}}^{2}}{4}}\right)
$$

By equating the real and imaginary parts of the two preceding equations, the following relations are obtained:

$$
{E}_{1}=E\left(1-\frac{{\mathit{AH}}^{2}}{2}\right)
\textrm{et}
{\mathit{AH}}_{1}=\mathit{AH}\sqrt{1-\frac{{\mathit{AH}}^{2}}{4}}
$$

The equivalent linear method does not, in this form, present a dependence of the damping on the frequency.

## 7 Choice of the weighting coefficient of the effective strain

The weighting coefficient of the effective strain $\chi$ is a key parameter linking the loading conditions during the earthquake to the laboratory tests which are carried out to measure the curves $G/{G}_{\max}$ and $D$ -gamma (Kramer, 1996 [7]). Its value is taken less than $1$ . The introduction of this parameter into the theory of the equivalent linear model is practical because it makes it possible to adjust the computed results to a recorded response. However when one wishes to predict the response of a soil column, one must make the choice of a value of $\chi$ .

In the literature, the practices and the recommendations on the value of this coefficient are fairly different:

- In the user's guide of SHAKE (2012 and earlier versions) [8], Schnabel et al. (2012) recommend taking it equal to a value of $0.65$ . This value has today remained the reference value used in engineering practice without further consideration. However this recommendation remains empirical.
- In the user's guide of DEEPSOIL (2012) [9], Y. Hashash recommends taking this parameter at a value of $0,65$ .
- In the user's guide of SHAKE91 (1992) [10], Idriss and Sun recommend varying this coefficient as a function of the magnitude ${M}_{w}$ of the earthquake of the input signal, according to the formula:

$$
\chi =\frac{({M}_{w}-1)}{10}
$$

- According to Idriss and Sun [10], the values of $\chi$ are thus comprised in the interval $[0.4,0.75]$ , which corresponds to earthquakes of magnitude ${M}_{w}$ from $5$ to $8,5$ .
- In the user's guide of EERA (2000) [5], this coefficient is denoted ${R}_{y}$ and depends on the magnitude of the earthquake according to the formulation of Idriss and Sun [10] above.
- Dickenson (1994) [11] shows that the response computed with $\chi =0,65$ can be slightly improved by using a value from $0,35$ to $0,55$ for earthquakes of magnitude ${M}_{s}=6-7$ , and between $0,55$ and $0,7$ for earthquakes of magnitude ${M}_{s}=7-8$ , ${M}_{s}$ being the magnitude of the surface waves.
- Yoshida et al (2002) [12] estimate that the value of $\chi$ must vary according to the seismic level of the recorded signal and that it varies in reality between $0,2$ and $1$ . He specifies that there is no good method for the evaluation of $\chi$ . Yoshida et al. show that the use of the weighting coefficient for the evaluation of the shear modulus and of the damping is at the origin of two defects in the theory of the equivalent linear model:

  - The overestimation of the maximum shear stress which implies an overestimation of the peak maximum acceleration.

The mechanism is described on the . The solid line O-A-B represents the stress–strain curve of the soil computed from the relation $G$ -Gamma given by the test curve. Let us take the example where a maximum strain ${\gamma}_{\max}$ is computed. The estimation of the shear modulus (secant modulus – straight line OA) is made with respect to the effective strain ${\gamma}_{\mathrm{eff}}$ . Now this implies that with this secant shear modulus (OA) at the strain ${\gamma}_{\max}$ , the maximum shear stress becomes ${\tau}_{1}$ and no longer ${\tau}_{2}$ . There is therefore an overestimation of the maximum shear stress which implies an overestimation of the peak acceleration. This overestimation remains moderate at low strain but can be important for strong earthquakes. Yoshida et al. (2012) show that at large strain (on the plastic plateau), the overestimation is of $\frac{1}{\chi }$ , that is $\mathrm{50 }\text{\%}$ for $\chi =0,65$ . To overcome this difficulty, the first idea is to take $\chi$ equal to $1$ but this solution implies another problem explained below.

Figure 7-1: Diagram illustrating the overestimation of the maximum shear stress with the equivalent linear method [12].

  - The underestimation of the amplification at high frequency

Yoshida et al. [12] illustrate the second defect of the equivalent linear method by the  where the amplification (ratio of the FFTs of the accelerations) has been computed with SHAKE for an instrumented borehole in Tokyo Bay and compared with the measured ratio. The amplification computed by SHAKE is lower from $7\mathrm{Hz}$ . Yoshida et al. [12] show that the problem comes from the fact that the shear modulus and the damping are evaluated from the effective strain, and this for all frequencies. The shear modulus is then lower and the damping greater than necessary for the high-frequency domain, where the amplitudes are smaller than the effective strain. This point shows the simplification of using a single weighting coefficient of the effective strain for the behaviour at high and low frequencies.

This is the reason why certain authors recommend varying $\chi$ as a function of the magnitude. $\chi$ must take low values for weak earthquakes and higher values for stronger earthquakes.

![Figure](/figures/r4.05.06/10000000000001CA00000143B0411D37B4AB6938.png)

Figure 7-2. Comparison of the amplification between SHAKE and the borehole measurements [12].

Points 1 and 2 are therefore contradictory since one would have to increase $\chi$ in order to lower the overestimation of the maximum shear stress (and therefore of the peak acceleration) and reduce $\chi$ in order to avoid the underestimation of the amplification at high frequency.

The choice of $\chi$ must therefore be made according to the problem posed, taking the two preceding remarks into account.

Modifications of the equivalent linear model have been proposed to resolve these difficulties (FDEL model, Assimaki et al. [13], DYNEQ model Yoshida et al. [12]). In these models, the weighting coefficient of the effective strain, the shear modulus and the damping depend on the frequency.

These models nevertheless remain little used in practice because of the mixed results.

For the FDEL model, Assimaki et al [13] and Furumoto (2000) [14] show a good improvement of the results with respect to the classical model whereas Yoshida et al. [12] and Kwak et al. [15] put forward results less good than the classical model with the FDEL model.

For the DYNEQ model, Yoshida et al. [12] show a definite improvement in the quality of the results obtained particularly in deconvolution with respect to the classical model (described here) and to the FDEL model. This improvement is nevertheless to be qualified by the works of Kwak et al. [15].

## 8 Choice of the weighting factor of the effective water-pressure rise

The weighting factor of the effective water-pressure rise ${\chi }_{\mathit{ru}}$ is a key parameter making it possible to consider the effect of the water-pressure rise on the degradation of the shear modulus. The introduction of this parameter is practical because it makes it possible to adjust the influence of the increase of the water pressure in the shear behaviour of soils.

During the thesis of Kteich (2018), a parametric study on the real case of the city of Urayasu was carried out in order to calibrate this factor by comparing the observations of liquefaction in the city with the predictions for various values of ${\chi }_{\mathit{ru}}$ . The value making it possible to obtain all the observations of occurrence of liquefaction is equal to 0.6667.
