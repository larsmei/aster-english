# r4.04.02 Elasto-(visco)plastic modelling taking metallurgical transformations into account

**Summary:**

This document presents the modelling implemented in *code_aster* for the mechanical analysis of operations that generate metallurgical transformations. The various mechanical effects resulting from the structural transformations to be taken into account, and their modellings, are presented.

> Contents

## 1 Introduction

Certain materials undergo structural transformations when they are subjected to particular thermal evolutions , , . This is, for example, the case of low-alloy steels during operations of the welding and heat-treatment type, or again the Zircaloy alloys of fuel cladding for certain accidental situations (LOCA).

These transformations have a more or less strong influence on the thermal and mechanical evolutions.

From the thermal point of view, the structural transformations are accompanied by a modification of the thermal characteristics (volumetric heat capacity, thermal conductivity) of the material that undergoes them, as well as by a production or an absorption of energy (latent heats of transformation) . However, the latent heats of transformation in the solid state are relatively small compared with the latent heats of liquid-solid change of state, and one may therefore, as a first approximation, consider the thermal and structural evolutions as uncoupled. This is currently the case for the thermal and metallurgical analysis options implemented in *code_aster* . [U4.85.01](doc:u4.85.01)

From the mechanical point of view, the consequences of the structural transformations (in the solid state) are of four types:

- The mechanical characteristics of the material that undergoes them are modified. More precisely, the elastic characteristics (Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio) are little affected, whereas the plastic characteristics (the yield stress in particular) and the thermal expansion coefficient are strongly affected;
- The volumetric expansion or contraction that accompanies the structural transformations results in a (spherical) “transformation” strain which is superimposed on the strain of purely thermal origin. This effect is brought out on a dilatometry test and, in general, it is grouped with that due to the modification of the expansion coefficient, and one speaks globally of the influence of the transformations on the thermal strain;
- A transformation taking place under stress can give rise to an irreversible strain, even for stress levels very much lower than the yield stress of the material (at the temperature and in the structural state considered). This phenomenon is called “**transformation plasticity**”;
- During the metallurgical transformation one may have a phenomenon of **recovery of hardening**. The hardening of the parent phase is not transmitted to the newly created phases. These may then be born with a virgin hardening state, or inherit only part, possibly all, of the hardening of the parent phase.

Furthermore, the mechanical state also influences the metallurgical behaviour. The stress state may in particular accelerate or slow down the kinetics of the transformations and modify the temperatures at which they occur. However, the experimental characterisation of this influence, particularly in the case of complex situations (three-dimensional, under variable temperature and stress state), remains very delicate, and it is very common to consider the structural evolution as independent of the mechanical state. This is the case of the model of structural transformations implemented in *code_aster* .

If the various couplings of mechanical origin are neglected, the determination of the mechanical evolution associated with a process involving structural transformations therefore requires two successive and uncoupled analyses:

- An (uncoupled) thermo-metallurgical analysis allowing the determination of the thermal evolutions and then the structural ones;
- A mechanical (elasto-viscoplastic) analysis taking account of the effects due to the thermal and structural evolutions;

This document presents the mechanical modelling implemented in *code_aster* . The modelling is available for two materials:

- Steel, which undergoes, around $850°$ , an austenite-ferrite transformation (passage from cold ferritic phases of face-centred cubic structure to a hot austenitic phase of body-centred cubic structure). Steel presents four possible ferritic phases: ferrite, pearlite, bainite and martensite;
- Zircaloy alloys, which undergo, around $800°C$ , a transformation from a cold phase $\alpha$ of hexagonal close-packed structure to a hot phase $\beta$ of body-centred cubic structure ($\mathrm{cc}$ ).

The models are identical for the two materials; only the number of phases changes.

The model therefore comprises five phases for steel and three phases for Zircaloy. Modelling the behaviour of Zircaloy does indeed require considering two cold phases of different mechanical behaviour: a phase $\alpha$ considered as pure and a phase $\alpha$ mixed with $\beta$ [U4.85.01](doc:u4.85.01), .

## 3 Influence of the structural transformations on the thermal strain

### 3.1 Metallurgical phases

At any time instant $t$ and at each point M of the material, two groups of phases can be detected:

- The *ferritic* phases, called “cold phases”, of body-centred cubic structure. Steel presents four possible ferritic phases: ferrite, pearlite, bainite and martensite. ${Z}_{{k}_{f}=1,4}$ will denote the proportion of each of these phases;
- The *austenitic* phase, also called the “hot phase”, of face-centred cubic structure. ${Z}_{\gamma}$ will denote the proportion of austenite;

One has:

$$
{Z}_{\gamma}=1-\sum_{{k}_{f}=1}^{4}{Z}_{{k}_{f}}
$$

One will also denote by ${Z}_{f}$ the sum of all the phases of ferritic type, that is:

$$
{Z}_{f}=\sum_{{k}_{f}=1}^{4}{Z}_{{k}_{f}}
$$

### 3.2 Dilatometry test

A dilatometry test consists in measuring the (homogeneous) strain of a small specimen as a function of temperature (or of time) during an imposed thermal cycle (assumed identical at all points of the specimen). A dilatometry test on a steel is presented [Figure 3-1]. The thermal cycle comprises heating beyond the austenitisation temperature (that is, about $850°C$ ), then a hold at this temperature and, finally, a controlled cooling down to ambient temperature. One then obtains an evolution of the strain (variable according to the imposed cooling kinetics) such as that represented in the following figure.

### 3.3 Zones of thermal strain

The various zones brought out in the figure may be interpreted as follows:

| A-B: | Thermal expansion of the metal in its initial metallurgical structure (of ferrito-pearlitic, bainitic and/or martensitic type) up to the temperature of the start of austenitisation $T(B)$ |
| --- | --- |
| B-C: | Austenitisation and contraction of the specimen (specific volume of the austenitic phase smaller) |
| C-D: | Thermal expansion of the austenite (with an expansion coefficient different from that of the ferritic phases) |
| D-E: | Thermal contraction of the austenite |
| E-F: | First (partial) transformation of the austenite (for example austenite towards ferrito-pearlitic) which is accompanied by a volumetric expansion |
| F-G: | Zone without transformation, with thermal contraction of the mixture of remaining austenite and formed phase (with a certain apparent thermal expansion coefficient) |
| G-H: | Second transformation of the remaining austenite (for example austenite towards martensite) which is accompanied by a volumetric expansion |
| H-A: | Thermal contraction of the final structure (with the same expansion coefficient as during heating) |

### 3.4 Thermal strain

The ferritic, pearlitic, bainitic and martensitic structures have an identical thermal expansion coefficient (denoted ${\alpha}_{f}$ ), different from that of austenite (denoted ${\alpha}_{\gamma}$ ). A reference state is defined for which the thermal strain is considered to be zero; for this one chooses a reference metallurgical phase (austenitic phase or ferritic phase) and a reference temperature ${T}_{\text{ref}}$ . Let ${\varepsilon}_{\gamma}^{\text{th}}$ be the thermal strain of the austenitic phase:

$$
{\varepsilon}_{\gamma}^{\mathit{th}}={\alpha}_{\gamma}(T)(T-{T}_{\mathit{ref}})-(1-{Z}_{\gamma}^{R})\Delta {\varepsilon}_{f\gamma }^{{T}_{\mathit{ref}}}
$$

And ${\varepsilon}_{f}^{\text{th}}$ the thermal strain of the ferritic, pearlitic, bainitic and martensitic phases; we shall take:

$$
{\varepsilon}_{f}^{\mathit{th}}={\alpha}_{f}(T)(T-{T}_{\mathit{ref}})+{Z}_{\gamma}^{R}\Delta {\varepsilon}_{f\gamma }^{{T}_{\mathit{ref}}}
$$

With:

| ${T}^{\text{ref}}$ : | Reference temperature; |
| --- | --- |
| ${\alpha}_{\gamma}(T)$ : | Mean expansion coefficient of the austenitic phase at the current temperature $T$ , with respect to the reference temperature; |
| ${\alpha}_{f}(T)$ : | Mean expansion coefficient of the ferritic, pearlitic, bainitic and martensitic phases at the current temperature $T$ , with respect to the reference temperature; |
| ${Z}_{\gamma}^{R}$ : | Characterises the reference metallurgical phase: ${Z}_{\gamma}^{R}=1$ when the reference phase is the austenitic phase; ${Z}_{\gamma}^{R}=0$ when the reference phase is the ferritic phase. |

In fact, $\Delta {\varepsilon}_{f\gamma }^{{T}_{\mathit{ref}}}$ expresses the difference in compactness between the face-centred cubic crystallographic structures (austenite) and the body-centred cubic ones (ferrite) at the reference temperature ${T}_{\text{ref}}$ :

$$
\Delta {\varepsilon}_{f\gamma }^{{T}_{\mathrm{ref}}}={\varepsilon}_{f}^{\mathrm{th}}({T}_{\mathrm{ref}})-{\varepsilon}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{th}}({T}_{\mathrm{ref}})
$$

Under the assumption of a mixture law to define the thermal strain of a multiphase mixture (characterised by $Z$ ), one has:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{\mathit{th}}(\mathrm{Z},T)={Z}_{\gamma}\left[{\alpha}_{\gamma}(T-{T}_{\mathit{ref}})-(1-{Z}_{\gamma}^{R})\Delta {\varepsilon}_{f\gamma }^{{T}_{\mathit{ref}}}\right]+{Z}_{f}\left[{\alpha}_{f}(T-{T}_{\mathit{ref}})+{Z}_{\gamma}^{R}\Delta {\varepsilon}_{f\gamma }^{{T}_{\mathit{ref}}}\right]
$$

The $\alpha$ depend on the temperature and are computed for the temperature of the current Gauss point. The thermal strain is purely spherical; its three-dimensional generalisation is written:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{\mathit{th}}={\varepsilon}^{\mathit{th}}\mathit{Id}\text{car}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{th}}=0
$$

## 4 Transformation plasticity

Experimentally, one observes that the dilatometric record of a specimen undergoing a structural transformation is strongly influenced by the stress state, and that the application of a stress even lower than the yield stress of the material can nevertheless cause an irreversible strain (cf. [Figure 4-1]).

![Figure](/figures/r4.04.02/Cadre2.gif)

This phenomenon is called **transformation plasticity** and ${\varepsilon}^{\text{pt}}$ denotes the corresponding **irreversible strain**. The model of transformation plasticity most frequently used is, originally, the three-dimensional generalisation of the one-dimensional phenomenological model established by Desalos . If, from a dilatometric test, one plots the difference between the elongation $\varepsilon$ obtained for an applied stress different from zero and that obtained for a zero stress, as a function of the progress of the transformation, one observes that:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{\text{pt}}(\sigma ,b)=\varepsilon (\sigma ,b)-\varepsilon (0,b)=\mathrm{kF}(b)\sigma
$$

With:

| $k$ : | Constant homogeneous to the inverse of a stress; |
| --- | --- |
| $F$ : | Normalised function ($F(0)=0$ and $F(1)=1$ ); |
| $b$ : | Proportion of the transformed phase; |

A three-dimensional and temporal generalisation of the preceding experimental model, for a single transformation, was proposed by Leblond , , , , in the form:

$$
{\dot{\varepsilon}}_{ij}^{\mathit{pt}}=\frac{3}{2}K{\tilde{\sigma}}_{ij}F'(b)\dot{b}
$$

It rests on the following heuristic considerations:

- The relation must be “incremental”, that is, it must relate the plastic strain rate to the transformation rate;
- The transformation-plasticity strain rate must, as for classical plasticity, be proportional to the deviatoric part $\tilde{\sigma}$ of the stress tensor $\sigma$ . Transformation plasticity occurs without change of volume, hence a dependence on the stress deviator rather than on the stress field itself;
- The transformation-plasticity strain rate must be zero outside the transformation ranges;
- The integration of this relation in the uniaxial case at constant stress $\sigma$ must recover the experimental relation.

On the basis of experimental tests and for a transformation of bainitic type of a 16MND5 steel, for example, $K={10}^{\text{-4}}{\mathit{MPa}}^{\text{-1}}$ and $F(b)=b(2-b)$ .

The phenomenon of transformation plasticity can exist during structural transformations under stress of ferritic, pearlitic, bainitic and martensitic type, which may possibly appear simultaneously. On the other hand, this phenomenon is considered not to exist during the austenitic transformation. To lighten the writing, one writes for transformation plasticity:

$$
{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\left(Z,\dot{Z}\right)=\sum_{{k}_{f}=1}^{4}{K}_{{k}_{f}}{F}_{{k}_{f}}^{'}\langle {\dot{Z}}_{{k}_{f}}\rangle
$$

The general model implemented in *code_aster* is therefore:

$$
{\dot{\epsilon}}^{\mathit{pt}}\left(\sigma ,Z\right)=\sum_{{k}_{f}=1}^{4}{\dot{\epsilon}}_{{k}_{f}}^{\mathit{pt}}\left(\sigma ,Z\right)=\frac{3}{2}\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\left(Z,\dot{Z}\right)
$$

Where $\langle X\rangle ` denotes the positive part of a quantity. In *code_aster,* it is possible not to take the phenomenon of transformation plasticity into account. In the case where this phenomenon is taken into account, it appears as soon as there is a transformation, even if the structure yields. The model is more particularly dedicated to steel. The data :math:`{K}_{{k}_{f}}$ and ${F}_{{k}_{f}}^{'}$ are supplied by the user in DEFI_MATERIAU under the keyword META_PT.

## 5 Recovery of hardening

In the usual way, the hardening state of a material is characterised by its plastic history. Thus, for example, in the case of plasticity with *linear* isotropic hardening, one generally takes as the hardening variable the cumulative plastic strain denoted $p$ . The hardening term is then written:

$$
R={R}_{o}p
$$

Where ${R}_{o}$ is the linear hardening coefficient. This model is insufficient in two cases:

1. For viscoplastic materials, under the action of thermal agitation, a slow recovery of the crystalline structure of the metal occurs by annihilation of the dislocations and relaxation of the internal stresses;
2. During metallurgical transformations, there exist within the material atomic displacements of greater or lesser importance. These atomic displacements can annihilate dislocations that are at the origin of the hardening;

### 5.1 Viscous recovery of hardening

#### 5.1.1 Single-phase model

The phenomenon of viscous recovery of hardening, which leads to a partial evanescence of the hardening, is introduced into the modelling. The model used to describe this phenomenon is the following in the case of isotropic hardening:

$$
\dot{r}=\dot{p}-{g}^{\text{re},v}
$$

The evolution term of the hardening variable $r$ therefore comprises a hardening term due to the plastic strain $\dot{p}$ and a viscous-recovery term which will be denoted ${g}^{\text{re},v}$ :

$$
{g}^{\text{re},v}=(\mathit{Cr}{)}^{m}
$$

The model thus makes it possible to describe the phenomenon of primary creep (hardening) and of secondary creep (stabilisation of the hardening) with the parameters $C$ and $m$ . In the case of kinematic hardening, the model will be written:

$$
\dot{\alpha}={\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}+{h}^{\text{re},v}
$$

With the recovery term which will be denoted ${h}^{\text{re},v}$ :

$$
{h}^{\text{re},v}=\frac{3}{2}(C{\alpha}_{\mathit{eq}}{)}^{m}\frac{\alpha}{{\alpha}_{\mathit{eq}}}
$$

#### 5.1.2 Multiphase model

In the multiphase case, the viscous part of the recovery is expressed by replacing the hardening variable by an average value over the phases:

$$
r\to \overline{r}
$$

With an average taken over the **five** phases:

$$
\overline{r}=\sum_{k=1}^{5}{Z}_{k}{r}_{k}
$$

And therefore:

$$
{\overline{g}}^{\text{re},v}=(\overline{C}\overline{r}{)}^{\overline{m}}
$$

In the case of kinematic hardening, according to ( ), one has:

$$
{h}^{\text{re},v}=\frac{3}{2}(C{\alpha}_{\mathit{eq}}{)}^{m}\frac{\alpha}{{\alpha}_{\mathit{eq}}}
$$

One proceeds in the same way as for isotropic hardening:

$$
\alpha \to \overline{\alpha}
$$

With:

$$
\overline{\alpha}=\sum_{k=1}^{5}{Z}_{k}{\overline{\alpha}}_{k}
$$

And therefore:

$$
{\overline{h}}^{\text{re},v}=\frac{3}{2}(\overline{C}{\overline{\alpha}}_{\mathit{eq}}{)}^{\overline{m}}\frac{\overline{\alpha}}{{\overline{\alpha}}_{\mathit{eq}}}
$$

### 5.2 Metallurgical isotropic hardening recovery

In the multiphase case, the hardening term is written for each phase:

$$
{R}_{k}={R}_{0,k}p
$$

Where ${R}_{0,k}$ is the linear hardening coefficient of phase $k$ . During metallurgical transformations, there exist within the material atomic displacements of greater or lesser importance. These atomic displacements can annihilate dislocations that are at the origin of the hardening. In these cases, the hardening of the parent phase is not transmitted to the phase produced; this is the recovery of hardening. The new phase may then be born with a virgin plastic state, or inherit only part, possibly all, of the hardening of the parent phase. The cumulative plastic strain $p$ is no longer characteristic of the hardening state, and other hardening variables must be defined for each phase, denoted ${r}_{k}$ , which take account of the recovery of hardening. The hardening term of phase $k$ is then written:

$$
{R}_{k}={R}_{0,k}{r}_{k}
$$

#### 5.2.1 Two-phase model with one direction of transformation

To define the variables ${r}_{k}$ , the model proposed by Leblond is chosen. A two-phase volume element $V$ is considered, which undergoes a metallurgical transformation and a plastic strain. Phase 1 is the parent phase, characterised by its volume fraction ${V}_{1}$ , its phase proportion $(1-z)$ and a hardening variable ${r}_{1}$ . Phase 2 is the product phase, with its volume fraction ${V}_{2}$ , its phase proportion $z$ and its hardening variable ${r}_{2}$ . The evolution equations of the ${r}_{i}$ obtained by differentiation with respect to time are written:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{\dot{r}}_{1}=\dot{p}\\ \\ {\dot{r}}_{2}=\dot{p}-\frac{\dot{z}}{z}{r}_{2}+\frac{\dot{z}}{z}\theta {r}_{1}\end{array}
$$

The parameter $\theta$ characterises the proportion of hardening transmitted from the parent phase to the product phase, and $\dot{p}$ is the equivalent plastic strain rate. Here $p$ is no longer an internal variable of the problem as such. The only meaning of $\dot{p}$ here is to be the plastic multiplier, and it is equal to the equivalent plastic strain rate. Sjöström obtains the same equations using a phenomenological argument which is reported here in order to make the model explicit .

Let there be a time increment $\Delta t$ , such that between $t$ and $t+\Delta t$ :

- A fraction $\Delta {V}_{2}$ of the parent phase transforms into phase 2 and is therefore added to the volume ${V}_{2}$ of this product phase;
- The volume element $V$ undergoes a plastic strain $\Delta p$ .

|  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{V}_{2},{r}_{2}(t)\\ {V}_{1},{r}_{1}(t)\end{array}$ | $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\Delta t\\ \Delta p\\ \Delta {V}_{2}\end{array}$ | $\lbrace \begin{array}{}{V}_{2},{r}_{2}(t)+\Delta p\\ \Delta {V}_{2},\theta {r}_{1}(t)+\Delta p\\ {V}_{1},{r}_{1}(t)+\Delta p\end{array}$ |

It is assumed that, during the metallurgical transformation, the transformed fraction $\Delta {V}_{2}$ inherits only a part $\theta {r}_{1}$ of the hardening of the parent phase $0\le \theta \le 1$ . Then the hardening variables ${r}_{i}$ at the time instant $t+\Delta t$ are such that:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{r}_{1}(t+\Delta t)={r}_{1}(t)+\Delta p\\ \\ {r}_{2}(t+\Delta t)=\frac{{V}_{2}({r}_{2}(t)+\Delta p)+\Delta {V}_{2}(\theta {r}_{1}(t)+\Delta p)}{{V}_{2}+\Delta {V}_{2}}\end{array}
$$

That is, considering a standard incremental writing of the hardening variables such that:

$$
\Delta {r}_{i}={r}_{i}(t+{\Delta}_{t})-{r}_{i}(t)
$$

One obtains:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\Delta {r}_{1}=\Delta p\\ \\ \Delta {r}_{2}=\Delta p+\frac{\Delta {V}_{2}}{{V}_{2}+\Delta {V}_{2}}\theta {r}_{1}^{-}-\frac{\Delta {V}_{2}}{{V}_{2}+\Delta {V}_{2}}{r}_{2}^{-}\end{array}
$$

Equations () are obtained by passing to the limit. For the discretisation of the evolution laws of the ${r}_{i}$ , an explicit integration scheme is chosen, using equations () directly.

#### 5.2.2 Generalisation of the model to n phases with two-way transformations

For the case of steel, the existing phases are ferrite, pearlite, bainite, martensite and austenite $\gamma$ , of respective proportions ${Z}_{{k}_{f}=1,4}$ and ${Z}_{\gamma}$ . When one heats, the metallurgical transformations to be considered are the transformations of the ferritic phases (ferrite, pearlite, bainite and martensite) towards the austenitic phase. The proportion of austenite increases, and therefore:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}\text{Si}{Z}_{\gamma}>0& {\dot{r}}_{\gamma}={\dot{p}}_{\gamma}+{g}_{\gamma}^{\text{re},m}\\ \text{Sinon}& {\dot{r}}_{\gamma}=0\end{array}
$$

With ${g}_{\gamma}^{\text{re},m}$ the function of metallurgical isotropic hardening recovery for austenite:

$$
{g}_{\gamma}^{\text{re},m}=\frac{\sum_{{k}_{f}=1}^{4}\langle {-\dot{Z}}_{{k}_{f}}\rangle {\theta}_{{k}_{f},\gamma }{r}_{{k}_{f}}-\sum_{{k}_{f}=1}^{4}\langle {-\dot{Z}}_{{k}_{f}}\rangle {r}_{\gamma}}{{Z}_{\gamma}}
$$

Where ${\theta}_{{k}_{f},\gamma }$ is the proportion of hardening recovery during the transformation of a ferritic phase towards the austenitic phase. In the case of cooling, the metallurgical transformations to be considered are the transformations of austenite towards the ferritic phases (ferrite, pearlite, bainite and martensite). It is the proportions of the ferritic phases that increase, and one has:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}\text{Si}{Z}_{{k}_{f}}>0& {\dot{r}}_{{k}_{f}}={\dot{p}}_{{k}_{f}}+{g}_{{k}_{f}}^{\text{re},m}\\ \text{Sinon}& {\dot{r}}_{{k}_{f}}=0\end{array}
$$

With ${g}_{{k}_{f}}^{\text{re},m}$ the function of isotropic metallurgical hardening recovery for the ferritic phases:

$$
{g}_{{k}_{f}}^{\text{re},m}=\frac{\langle {\dot{Z}}_{{k}_{f}}\rangle {\theta}_{\gamma ,{k}_{f}}{r}_{\gamma}-\langle {\dot{Z}}_{{k}_{f}}\rangle {r}_{{k}_{f}}}{{Z}_{{k}_{f}}}
$$

${\theta}_{\gamma ,k}$ is the proportion of hardening recovery during the transformation of the austenitic phase into a ferritic phase.

For transformations with diffusion (for example: austenite towards ferrite, pearlite and bainite), involving large atomic displacements, one may take $\theta =0$ ; the dislocations at the origin of the plastic hardening are totally annihilated by the transformation. For transformations without diffusion (for example a martensitic transformation), one may take $\theta =1$ , the hardening being totally transmitted.

#### 5.2.3 Metallurgical kinematic hardening recovery

By analogy with the case of isotropic hardening, the kinematic hardening recovery in the multiphase case will be written, for the case of austenite:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}\text{Si}{Z}_{\gamma}>0& {\dot{\alpha}}_{\gamma}={\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}+{h}_{\gamma}^{\text{re},m}\\ \text{Sinon}& {\dot{\alpha}}_{\gamma}=0\end{array}
$$

With ${h}_{\gamma}^{\text{re},m}$ the function of metallurgical isotropic hardening recovery for austenite:

$$
{h}_{\gamma}^{\text{re},m}=\frac{\sum_{{k}_{f}=1}^{4}\langle {-\dot{Z}}_{{k}_{f}}\rangle {\theta}_{{k}_{f},\gamma }{\alpha}_{{k}_{f}}-\sum_{{k}_{f}=1}^{4}\langle {-\dot{Z}}_{{k}_{f}}\rangle {\alpha}_{\gamma}}{{Z}_{\gamma}}
$$

And for the ferritic phases:

$$

$$

## 6 (Visco)plastic strain models

The principal characteristic of the thermal evolutions concerned in this type of analysis is that they sweep a wide temperature range, which has an important effect on the mechanical behaviour of the material that undergoes the thermal evolution. One is in particular in temperature ranges where viscosity phenomena may no longer be negligible. It may therefore be necessary to use an elasto-viscoplastic constitutive model, especially when one remains in these ranges for a significant duration; for example during the stress-relief treatments associated with welding.

A viscoplastic model is therefore chosen whose characteristics are such that it makes it possible to describe, with one and the same formalism and therefore without changing model:

- A classical plastic behaviour, in order to model the cases at low temperature when the viscous effects are still negligible, or in order to model high-rate processes (welding);
- A hardening viscoplastic behaviour at high temperature, in order to model the creep and relaxation effects associated, for example, with stress-relief treatments or with multipass welding;
- A viscous-fluid type behaviour for temperatures higher than the melting temperature, in order to have a reasonable description of the molten zone.

The viscoplastic model chosen does indeed degenerate, for certain limit cases, into a time-independent plasticity model, or into a viscous-fluid model.

### 6.1 Partition of the strain

The total strain $\varepsilon$ is written as the sum of four components:

$$
\varepsilon ={\varepsilon}^{e}+{\varepsilon}^{\mathit{th}}+{\varepsilon}^{\mathit{vp}}+{\varepsilon}^{\mathit{pt}}
$$

Where ${\varepsilon}^{e}$ , ${\varepsilon}^{\mathit{th}}$ , ${\varepsilon}^{\mathit{vp}}$ and ${\varepsilon}^{\mathit{pt}}$ are, respectively, the elastic, thermal, viscoplastic and transformation-plasticity strains. The strains of plastic type are purely deviatoric and the thermal strain is purely spherical. For the deviatoric part, one therefore has:

$$
\tilde{\varepsilon}={\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{e}+{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}+{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{pt}}
$$

And for the spherical part:

$$
\mathit{tr}(\varepsilon )=\mathit{tr}({\varepsilon}^{e})+\mathit{tr}({\varepsilon}^{\mathit{th}})
$$

### 6.2 Constitutive laws

#### 6.2.1 Case of isotropic hardening

One places oneself here in the framework of von Mises (visco)plasticity with additive isotropic hardening. The threshold function is written:

$$
f(\sigma ,r;T,Z)={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-R(r;T,Z)-{\sigma}_{c}(T,Z)
$$

With ${\sigma}_{\text{eq}}$ the Von Mises equivalent stress such that:

$$
{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}={(\frac{3}{2}\tilde{\sigma}:\tilde{\sigma})}^{1/2}
$$

$R$ is the isotropic hardening term and ${\sigma}_{c}$ is the initial critical stress. It corresponds to the minimum initial stress to be applied in order to have a viscoplastic flow. These two quantities may depend on the temperature and on the metallurgical phases. Equation () can be rewritten in the form:

$$
{f}^{\text{*}}=f-{\sigma}_{v}={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-R(r;T,Z)-{\sigma}_{c}(T,Z)-{\sigma}_{v}=0
$$

That is, in this model, the stress can be interpreted as the sum of a flow-limit stress (which itself decomposes into an initial limit stress and a hardening term) and a “viscous” stress depending on the strain rate and zero at zero rate:

$$
{\sigma}_{v}=\eta {\dot{p}}^{\frac{1}{n}}
$$

The (visco)plastic flow rate is written:

$$
{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}={\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{vp}}=\dot{\lambda}\frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma }=\frac{3}{2}\dot{p}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}
$$

This tensor is purely deviatoric. The cumulative plastic strain $\dot{p}$ is viscous and is written:

$$
\dot{p}={(\frac{\langle {\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-R(r;T,Z)-{\sigma}_{c}(T,Z)\rangle }{\eta})}^{n}
$$

With $\eta$ and $n$ the material viscosity coefficients.

#### 6.2.2 Case of kinematic hardening

In a manner equivalent to the case with isotropic hardening, the threshold function is written:

$$
f(\sigma ,X;T,Z)=(\tilde{\sigma}-X{)}_{\mathit{eq}}-{\sigma}_{c}(T,Z)
$$

With $X$ the hardening tensor associated with the hardening variable tensor $\alpha$ such that:

$$
X=\frac{2}{3}{H}_{0}\alpha
$$

${H}_{0}$ is the kinematic hardening coefficient. In a manner similar to the isotropic case, equation () can be rewritten by introducing the viscous back-stress:

$$
{f}^{\text{*}}=f-{\sigma}_{v}=(\tilde{\sigma}-X{)}_{\mathit{eq}}-{\sigma}_{c}(T,Z)-{\sigma}_{v}=0
$$

The (visco)plastic flow rate is written:

$$
{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}={\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{vp}}=\dot{\lambda}\frac{\partial f}{\partial \sigma }=\frac{3}{2}\dot{p}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}-X}{(\tilde{\sigma}-X{)}_{\mathit{eq}}}
$$

The cumulative plastic strain $\dot{p}$ is viscous and is written:

$$
\dot{p}={(\frac{\langle (\tilde{\sigma}-X{)}_{\mathit{eq}}-{\sigma}_{c}(T,Z)\rangle }{\eta})}^{n}
$$

### 6.3 Limit cases

#### 6.3.1 Time-independent plastic model

One wishes to describe an instantaneous elasto-plastic behaviour and to cancel the viscous effects. For this, the viscous parameters $\eta$ and $C$ will be taken equal to zero. In order to avoid the numerical problems that taking $\eta$ and $C$ equal to zero may pose, and in a manner similar to the treatment carried out for the viscoplastic model of Taheri [R5.03.05](doc:r5.03.05), equation () is rewritten in the form:

$$
f-{\sigma}_{v}\le 0
$$

The strict inequality being obtained in the case $f<0$ and $\dot{p}=0$ , which corresponds to the elastic regime. In the domain of purely plastic behaviour ($\eta \to 0$ ), inequality () is then reduced to:

$$
f={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-R(r;T,Z)-{\sigma}_{c}(T,Z)\le 0
$$

So that $\dot{p}$ can no longer be determined except by the consistency equation $\dot{f}=0$ . One is therefore indeed back in the framework of instantaneous time-independent plasticity, with a numerical treatment identical to that classically used for the treatment of the latter. It will be noted that ${\sigma}_{c}$ then corresponds to the classical definition of the elastic limit ${\sigma}_{y}$ . The yield stress will be denoted ${\sigma}_{c}$ in viscoplasticity and ${\sigma}_{y}$ in time-independent plasticity *.*

#### 6.3.2 Viscous-fluid constitutive model

At very high temperature one has $R\to 0$ and ${\sigma}_{c}\to 0$ ; the threshold function is therefore reduced:

$$
f(\sigma ,r;T,Z)={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}
$$

If one takes $n\to 1$ , then:

$$
\dot{p}=\frac{\langle {\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}\rangle }{\eta}=\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}{\eta}
$$

The (visco)plastic flow rate is written:

$$
{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}=\frac{3}{2}\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}{\eta}
$$

That is, in one dimension:

$$
{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}=\frac{\sigma}{\eta}
$$

One thus obtains a constitutive model of Newtonian viscous-fluid type, of viscosity $\eta$ .

### 6.4 Multiphase plasticity

The metallurgical transformations lead to modifications of the mechanical characteristics of the material. The elastic characteristics (Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio) are little affected by the changes of metallurgical structures. Only their dependence on temperature is therefore taken into account. On the other hand, the plastic characteristics (the yield stress in particular) depend strongly on the metallurgical structure. The differences in plastic characteristics for each of the possible phases must therefore be taken into account. In the modelling, the strain and the stress are defined at the scale of the material point (macroscopic), which may be multiphase. One seeks to define the equivalent plastic behaviour of the material when it presents a multiphase structure, with in particular a single plasticity criterion.

#### 6.4.1 Linear mixture law

The definition of the behaviour of the equivalent material is made with the aid of a **mixture law** on the characteristics of the phases. More precisely, the definition of this equivalent material would correspond in 1D to a rheological model of $k$ bars in parallel such that:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}={\dot{\varepsilon}}_{k}^{\mathit{vp}}\\ \sigma =\sum_{k}{z}_{k}{\sigma}_{k}\text{avec}{\sigma}_{k}={\sigma}_{c,k}+{R}_{k}+\eta {\dot{\varepsilon}}_{k}^{\mathit{vp}}\end{array}
$$

##### 6.4.1.1 Viscoplasticity with isotropic hardening

In the case of von Mises viscoplasticity with isotropic hardening:

$$
f(\sigma ,r;T,Z)={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-R(r;T,Z)-{\sigma}_{c}(T,Z)
$$

The hardening of the multiphase material $\overline{R}$ is written by application of the mixture law:

$$
\overline{R}(r;T,Z)=\sum_{k=1}^{5}{Z}_{k}{R}_{k}({r}_{k};T)
$$

Where ${R}_{k}$ is the hardening of phase $k$ . The yield stress of the multiphase material ${\overline{\sigma}}_{c}$ is written likewise:

$$
{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}(T,Z)=\sum_{k=1}^{5}{Z}_{k}{\sigma}_{c,k}(T)
$$

Where ${\sigma}_{c,k}$ is the yield stress of phase $k$ . Which gives a new threshold function on the multiphase material:

$$
\overline{f}(\sigma ,r;T,Z)={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-\overline{R}(r;T,Z)-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}(T,Z)
$$

The plastic strain rate satisfies the consistency condition () (function ${f}^{\text{*}}$ ). With an average taken over the **five** phases:

$$
\overline{\eta}=\sum_{k=1}^{5}{Z}_{k}{\eta}_{k}
$$

The mixture law is applied to the viscous back-stress:

$$
{\overline{\sigma}}_{v}(T,Z)=\overline{\eta}{\dot{p}}^{\frac{1}{\overline{n}}}
$$

And therefore:

$$
{\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-\overline{R}(r;T,Z)-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}(T,Z)-{\overline{\sigma}}_{v}(T,Z)=0
$$

That is, when one is loading, $\dot{p}$ is such that:

$$
{\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-\overline{R}-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}-{\overline{\sigma}}_{v}=0
$$

##### 6.4.1.2 Viscoplasticity with kinematic hardening

In the case of von Mises viscoplasticity with kinematic hardening:

$$
f(\sigma ,X;T,Z)=(\tilde{\sigma}-X{)}_{\mathit{eq}}-{\sigma}_{c}(T,Z)
$$

The hardening of the multiphase material $\overline{R}$ is written by application of the mixture law:

$$
\overline{X}(\alpha ;T,Z)=\sum_{k=1}^{5}{Z}_{k}{X}_{k}({\alpha}_{k};T)
$$

The yield stress of the multiphase material ${\overline{\sigma}}_{c}$ is written in the same way as in the isotropic case ( ):

$$
{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}(T,Z)=\sum_{k=1}^{5}{Z}_{k}{\sigma}_{c,k}(T)
$$

Which gives a new threshold function on the multiphase material:

$$
\overline{f}(\sigma ,X;T,Z)=(\tilde{\sigma}-\overline{X}{)}_{\mathit{eq}}-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}(T,Z)
$$

The plastic strain rate satisfies the consistency condition () (function ${f}^{\text{*}}$ ). In a similar manner, the mixture law is applied to the viscous stress. That is, when one is loading, $\dot{p}$ is such that:

$$
{\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}=(\tilde{\sigma}-\overline{X}{)}_{\mathit{eq}}-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}(T,Z)-{\overline{\sigma}}_{v}=0
$$

#### 6.4.2 Non-linear mixture law

One recalls that ${Z}_{f}$ denotes the sum of all the phases of ferritic type, that is:

$$
{Z}_{f}=\sum_{{k}_{f}=1}^{4}{Z}_{{k}_{f}}
$$

One also gives the possibility of using a non-linear mixture law between the hot phase (austenitic) and the cold phases (ferritic), such that one has, in the one-dimensional case:

$$
\overline{\sigma}=(1-{f}_{h}({Z}_{f})){\sigma}_{\gamma}+{f}_{h}({Z}_{f}){\sigma}_{\alpha}
$$

The quantity ${f}_{h}$ is the mixture function. One then has the hardening, which is written:

$$
\overline{R}=(1-{f}_{h}({Z}_{f})){R}_{\gamma}+{f}_{h}({Z}_{f}){\overline{R}}_{\alpha}
$$

${\overline{R}}_{\alpha}$ is the mean hardening of the cold phases (ferritic phases):

$$
{\overline{R}}_{\alpha}=\frac{\sum_{{k}_{f}=1}^{4}{Z}_{{k}_{f}}{R}_{{k}_{f}}}{{Z}_{f}}
$$

The initial stress of the multiphase material is written likewise:

$$
{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}=(1-{f}_{h}({Z}_{f})){\sigma}_{c,\gamma }+{f}_{h}({Z}_{f}){\overline{\sigma}}_{c,\alpha }
$$

${\overline{\sigma}}_{c,\alpha }$ is the equivalent initial stress of the cold phases (ferritic phases):

$$
{\overline{\sigma}}_{c,\alpha }=\frac{\sum_{{k}_{f}=1}^{4}{Z}_{{k}_{f}}{\sigma}_{c,{k}_{f}}}{{Z}_{f}}
$$

${f}_{h}({Z}_{f})$ is a function defined by the user. The yield stress ${\overline{\sigma}}_{y}$ in the elasto-plastic (non-viscous) case uses the same mixture rule as ${\overline{\sigma}}_{c}$ . The whole is entered in the command DEFI_MATERIAU.

## 7 Numerical formulation

Only the viscoplastic constitutive law with isotropic hardening will be treated. One obtains the same thing for the case of kinematic hardening; ${R}_{0}$ is then replaced by the kinematic hardening coefficient ${H}_{0}$ .

### 7.1 Discretisation

Knowing the fields $\sigma$ , $u$ and $p$ at the time instant $t$ , an implicit scheme is chosen to discretise in time the equations of the continuous problem, except for the hardening parameters. One notes that, with an implicit discretisation, only two points differentiate the two types of behaviour, viscoplastic and time-independent plastic:

- The form of the yield function, for which there is a complementary term in the case of viscosity (see  6.4.1.1  and  6.4.1.2 );
- The presence of the hardening-recovery term in the evolution of the hardening variable for the viscoplastic case.

Moreover, classical incremental plasticity appears as the limit case (with no associated numerical difficulty) of incremental viscoplasticity when $\eta ,C\to 0$ and ${\sigma}_{c}\to {\sigma}_{y}$ . This type of treatment has already been carried out by Lorentz [R5.03.05](doc:r5.03.05). To compute the tangent operators, the convention of writing second-order symmetric tensors in the form of vectors with six components will be adopted. Thus, for a tensor $a$ :

$$
a={}^{t}\text{}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{a}_{xx}& {a}_{yy}& {a}_{zz}\end{array}\begin{array}{ccc}\sqrt{2}{a}_{xy}& \sqrt{2}{a}_{xz}& \sqrt{2}{a}_{yz}\end{array}\right]
$$

### 7.2 Integration of the metallurgical relations

#### 7.2.1 Expression of the stresses

The expression of $\sigma$ is given as a function of:

- $\Delta \varepsilon$ , unknown of the problem;
- The known terms, such as the variables computed at the previous step (stresses, internal variables ...), the material characteristics, the command variables (temperature, metallurgical phases);

One recalls the partition of the deviatoric strains:

$$
\tilde{\varepsilon}={\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{e}+{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}+{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{pt}}
$$

The stress tensor is separated into its deviatoric components and its spherical component:

$$
\sigma =\tilde{\sigma}+\mathit{tr}(\sigma )\mathit{Id}
$$

Applying Hooke's law:

$$
\sigma =A{\varepsilon}^{e}
$$

With its deviatoric part:

$$
\tilde{\sigma}=2\mu {\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{e}
$$

And its spherical part:

$$
\mathit{tr}(\sigma )=\mathrm{3K}\mathit{tr}({\varepsilon}^{e})
$$

By implicit discretisation, one obtains:

$$
\tilde{\sigma}=\frac{\mu}{{\mu}^{-}}{\tilde{\sigma}}^{-}+2\mu (\Delta \tilde{\varepsilon}-\Delta {\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}-\Delta {\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{pt}})
$$

Substituting ( ) and ( ):

$$
\tilde{\sigma}=\frac{\mu}{{\mu}^{-}}{\tilde{\sigma}}^{-}+2\mu (\Delta \tilde{\varepsilon}-\frac{3}{2}\Delta p\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}-\frac{3}{2}\tilde{\sigma}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}})
$$

For the spherical part of the stress tensor, one has:

$$
\mathit{tr}(\sigma )=\frac{\mathrm{3K}}{{\mathrm{3K}}^{-}}\mathit{tr}({\sigma}^{-})+\mathrm{3K}\mathit{tr}(\Delta \varepsilon )
$$

Because the strains of plastic type are purely deviatoric and therefore $\mathit{tr}({\Delta \varepsilon }^{\mathit{vp}})=\mathit{tr}({\Delta \varepsilon }^{\mathit{pt}})=0$ . Rearranging ( ):

$$
\tilde{\sigma}=\frac{1}{1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}}(\frac{\mu}{{\mu}^{-}}{\tilde{\sigma}}^{-}+2\mu \Delta \tilde{\varepsilon}-3\mu \Delta p\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}})
$$

One needs to develop the expression of $\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}$ . One starts from the expression of the partition of the deviatoric strains, that is:

$$
\Delta \tilde{\varepsilon}=\Delta {\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{e}+\Delta {\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}+\Delta {\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{pt}}
$$

Substituting the values of the strain increments:

$$
\Delta \stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}=\left(\frac{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}}{2\mu }-\frac{{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}}^{-}}{{2\mu }^{-}}\right)+\frac{3}{2}\Delta p\frac{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}+\frac{3}{2}\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}
$$

Multiplying by $2\mu$ :

$$
2\mu \Delta \stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}+\frac{2\mu }{{2\mu }^{-}}{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}}^{-}=\frac{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}\left[\left(1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\right){\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}+3\mu \Delta p\right]
$$

The deviatoric elastic stress is set:

$$
{\tilde{\sigma}}^{e}=2\mu \Delta \tilde{\varepsilon}+\frac{2\mu }{{2\mu }^{-}}{\tilde{\sigma}}^{-}
$$

And the equivalent elastic stress in the Von Mises sense:

$$
{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}=\left[(1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}){\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}+3\mu \Delta p\right]
$$

The expression of $\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}$ is then equal to:

$$
\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}=\frac{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{e}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}}
$$

#### 7.2.2 Expression of the plastic increment

The quantities ( ) and ( ) are all known at the current time instant, except the increment $\Delta p$ . To find this value, the consistency condition is used:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\overline{f}={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-\overline{R}-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}\\ {\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-\overline{R}-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}-{\overline{\sigma}}_{v}\\ \Delta p=0\text{si}\overline{f}<0\\ \Delta p\ge 0\text{si}\overline{f}=0\text{avec}{\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}=0\end{array}
$$

It is therefore sufficient to express ${\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}=0$ in order to find the expression of $\Delta p$ . Only ${\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}$ , $\overline{R}$ and the viscous stress ${\overline{\sigma}}_{v}$ depend on $\Delta p$ . That is, in incremental form:

$$
{\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-\overline{R}({p}^{-}+\Delta p)-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}-\overline{\eta}{(\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t})}^{\frac{1}{\overline{n}}}=0
$$

Using expression ( ), one finds:

$$
{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}=\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}-3\mu \Delta p}{1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}}
$$

One must therefore find the zero of the following function in order to determine $\Delta p$ :

$$
{\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}=\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}-3\mu \Delta p}{1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}}-\overline{R}({p}^{-})-\overline{R}(\Delta p)-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}-\overline{\eta}{(\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t})}^{\frac{1}{\overline{n}}}=0
$$

This equation is non-linear in $\Delta p$ . The solution is carried out in *code_aster* by a secant method with a search interval [R5.03.05](doc:r5.03.05).

### 7.3 Quantities in prediction

The aim of this paragraph is to compute the quantities necessary in prediction. Among others, the tangent operator ${K}_{i-1}$ (analysis option RIGI_MECA_TANG called at the first iteration of a new load increment) is evaluated from the results known at the previous time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ :

$$
{K}_{i-1}={\frac{d\sigma }{d\varepsilon }\mid }_{{t}_{i-1}}
$$

Since the quasi-static assumption is made, the various quantities depend on time only in an *implicit* manner, via the dependence of the material parameters on the command variables  1
$\beta (t)$ , themselves functions of time. In the present situation, there are two command variables: the temperature $T$ and the metallurgical phases $Z$ . For ${n}_{\mathrm{varc}}$ command variables, the total differential of a quantity $a$ is written:

$$
\frac{da}{dt}=\frac{\partial a}{\partial t}+\sum_{j=1,{n}_{\mathit{varc}}}^{}\frac{\delta a}{\delta {\beta}^{j}}\cdot \frac{\delta {\beta}^{j}}{\delta t}
$$

One starts from the expression of the incremental Hooke's law, for its deviatoric part:

$$
\dot{\tilde{\sigma}}=2\mu (\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}-{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{vp}}-{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{pt}})
$$

The principal difficulty of this expression is that the viscoplastic strain ${\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{vp}}$ has as its unknown the plastic multiplier, which is itself an unknown of the problem. For the spherical part, on the other hand, the expression is trivial, because it depends only on the thermal strain and on the elastic strain:

$$
\mathit{tr}(\dot{\sigma})=\mathrm{3K}\mathit{tr}(\dot{\varepsilon})-\mathrm{3K}\mathit{tr}({\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{th}})
$$

With the expression of the thermal strain given by ( ).

#### 7.3.1 Plastic multiplier

To establish the expression of the plastic multiplier, the consistency condition $\dot{\overline{f}}=0$ is written:

$$
\dot{\overline{f}}=\frac{d}{\mathit{dt}}({\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-\overline{R}-\overline{{\sigma}_{c}})=0
$$

The function $\overline{f}$ depends on the Von Mises equivalent stress, which is purely deviatoric and which is equal to:

$$
{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}={(\frac{3}{2}\tilde{\sigma}:\tilde{\sigma})}^{1/2}
$$

The contribution is then written:

$$
\frac{{d\sigma }_{\mathit{eq}}}{dt}=\frac{{\partial \sigma }_{\mathit{eq}}}{\partial t}+\frac{{\delta \sigma }_{\mathit{eq}}}{\delta T}\cdot \frac{\delta T}{\delta t}+\frac{{\delta \sigma }_{\mathit{eq}}}{\delta Z}\cdot \frac{\delta Z}{\delta t}
$$

The assumption (usual in *code_aster*) is made that the variation of the Hooke matrix $A$ as a function of the command variables is negligible. That is:

$$
\frac{{\delta \sigma }_{\mathit{eq}}}{\delta T}=\frac{{\delta \sigma }_{\mathit{eq}}}{\delta Z}=0
$$

Finally, the contribution ${\dot{\sigma}}_{\mathit{eq}}$ is then equal to:

$$
\frac{{d\sigma }_{\mathit{eq}}}{dt}={\dot{\sigma}}_{\mathit{eq}}=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}:A{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{e}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}
$$

The case of the hardening term $\overline{R}$ is now treated. The variation of the hardening modulus with respect to the command variables will be neglected. That is:

$$
\frac{\delta \overline{R}}{\delta T}=\frac{\delta \overline{R}}{\delta Z}=0
$$

The total differential in time is therefore written:

$$
\frac{d\overline{R}}{\mathit{dt}}=\sum_{k=1}^{5}{\dot{Z}}_{k}{R}_{0,k}{r}_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{5}{\dot{r}}_{k}{R}_{0,k}{Z}_{k}
$$

The variation of the metallurgical phases is neglected. The second term implies using the multiphase hardening law written in ( ) without the viscous-recovery term, which will be written in condensed form:

$$
{\dot{r}}_{k}={\dot{p}}_{k}+{g}_{k}^{\text{re},m}
$$

One therefore has:

$$
\frac{d\overline{R}}{\mathit{dt}}=\sum_{k=1}^{5}{\dot{Z}}_{k}{R}_{0,k}{r}_{k}+\sum_{k=1}^{5}(\dot{p}+{g}_{k}^{\text{re},m}){R}_{0,k}{Z}_{k}
$$

The equivalent modulus ${\overline{R}}_{0}$ is recognised:

$$
{\overline{R}}_{0}=\sum_{k=1}^{5}{R}_{0,k}{Z}_{k}
$$

The other terms will be gathered in the function $B$ :

$$
\frac{d\overline{R}}{\mathit{dt}}={\overline{R}}_{0}\dot{p}+B
$$

Since the effects of the variation of the metallurgical phases are neglected, this term $B$ is assumed to be zero. Finally:

$$
\frac{d\overline{R}}{\mathit{dt}}=\dot{\overline{R}}={\overline{R}}_{0}\dot{p}
$$

Finally, the case of the yield stress ${\sigma}_{y}$ is considered. A last simplification will be made by assuming that this term does not vary as a function of the metallurgical structure and of the temperature. That is:

$$
\frac{{d\overline{\sigma}}_{y}}{\mathit{dt}}={\dot{\sigma}}_{y}=\frac{{\partial \overline{\sigma}}_{y}}{\partial t}\dot{t}+\frac{{\partial \overline{\sigma}}_{y}}{\partial T}\dot{T}+\frac{{\partial \overline{\sigma}}_{y}}{\partial Z}\dot{Z}=C
$$

The quantity ${\overline{\sigma}}_{y}$ is constant in time. Its partial derivative with respect to time is therefore zero. The other quantities (dependence on temperature and on the metallurgical phases) are gathered in the function $C$ . This term $C$ will be assumed to be zero. Finally, one will have, trivially:

$$
\frac{{d\overline{\sigma}}_{y}}{\mathit{dt}}={\dot{\sigma}}_{y}=0
$$

Taking up ( ) and substituting into it the results of the differentiation of the terms ( ), ( ) and ( ), one finally has:

$$
\dot{\overline{f}}=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}:A{\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}}^{e}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}-{\overline{R}}_{0}\dot{p}=0
$$

The deviatoric component of Hooke's law is written in incremental form:

$$
\dot{\tilde{\sigma}}=2\mu {\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{e}
$$

Which gives a new (deviatoric) expression of the consistency condition:

$$
\dot{\overline{f}}=3\mu \frac{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}:\left(\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}-{\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}}^{\mathit{vp}}-{\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}}^{\mathit{pt}}\right)}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}-{\overline{R}}_{0}\dot{p}=0
$$

The elastic strain tensor must be expressed from expression ():

$$
{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{e}=\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}-{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{vp}}-{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{pt}}
$$

The first term is developed for the plastic strain:

$$
3\mu \frac{\tilde{\sigma}:{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{vp}}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}=3\mu \frac{\tilde{\sigma}:\frac{3}{2}\dot{p}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}
$$

Using ( ):

$$
3\mu \frac{\tilde{\sigma}:{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{vp}}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}=3\mu \dot{p}
$$

The second term is developed for transformation plasticity ( ):

$$
3\mu \frac{\tilde{\sigma}:{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{pt}}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}=3\mu \frac{\tilde{\sigma}:\frac{3}{2}\tilde{\sigma}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}(Z,\dot{Z})}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}
$$

It is important to note that the term ${g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}$ depends on the increment of the metallurgical phases $\dot{Z}$ . In what follows, in order to lighten the writing, it will not be written explicitly. Still using ( ):

$$
3\mu \frac{\tilde{\sigma}:{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{pt}}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}=3\mu {\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}
$$

Finally:

$$
\dot{\overline{f}}=3\mu \frac{\tilde{\sigma}:\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}-3\mu {\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}-({\overline{R}}_{0}+3\mu )\dot{p}=0
$$

Which allows us to express $\dot{p}$ :

$$
\dot{p}=\frac{3\mu }{{\overline{R}}_{0}+3\mu }\left(\frac{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}:\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}-{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\right)
$$

This expression is different from that given by ( ), because it is an approximation. Indeed, the consistency condition has been considered without the viscous term, as it is usual to proceed. But this expression does not need to be exact, since it concerns the tangent matrix in prediction, and it has the advantage of being an analytical formula, unlike ( ), which requires the use of a zero-search algorithm on a non-linear function.

#### 7.3.2 Stress increment

Starting from the definition of the viscoplastic strain increment ${\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{vp}}$ :

$$
{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{vp}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{ccc}\frac{3}{2}\dot{p}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}& \text{si}& {\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-\overline{R}-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}=0\\ 0& \text{si}& {\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-\overline{R}-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}<0\end{array}
$$

And from the expression of $\dot{p}$ obtained previously, one obtains:

$$
{\dot{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}=\lbrace  \begin{array}{ccc}\frac{9\mu }{2({\overline{R}}_{0}+3\mu )}\langle \frac{\tilde{\sigma}:\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}-{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\rangle \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}& \text{si}& {\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-\overline{R}-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}=0\\ 0& \text{si}& {\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}-\overline{R}-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}<0\end{array}
$$

Thanks to the expression of the incremental Hooke's law:

$$
\dot{\tilde{\sigma}}=2\mu (\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}-{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{vp}}-{\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}^{\mathit{pt}})
$$

Substituting the expression of the plastic strain ( ) and of the transformation plasticity, one obtains the expression of the stress increment:

$$
\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}}=2\mu \left[\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}-\frac{9\mu }{2{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}\left({\overline{R}}_{0}+3\mu \right)}\langle \frac{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}:\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}-{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\rangle \stackrel{̃}{\sigma}-\frac{3}{2}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}\right]
$$

The expression of $\dot{\tilde{\sigma}}$ depends on the sign of the following term (loading-unloading criterion):

$$
\frac{\tilde{\sigma}:\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}-{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}
$$

One can simplify by saying that unloading or loading does not depend on transformation plasticity; one will therefore approximate:

$$
\langle \frac{\tilde{\sigma}:\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}-{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\rangle \approx \frac{\langle \tilde{\sigma}:\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}\rangle }{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}-{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}
$$

In unloading, the second term will be neglected, and in loading it will be added to the other terms of ( ):

$$
\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}}=\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}2\mu \left[\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}-\frac{9\mu }{2}\frac{\langle \stackrel{̃}{\sigma}:\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}\rangle }{\left({\overline{R}}_{0}+3\mu \right){\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{2}}\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}-\frac{3}{2}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}\right]& \text{en décharge}& \\ 2\mu \left[\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}-\frac{9\mu }{2}\frac{\langle \stackrel{̃}{\sigma}:\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}\rangle }{\left({\overline{R}}_{0}+3\mu \right){\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{2}}\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}-\frac{3{\overline{R}}_{0}}{2\left({\overline{R}}_{0}+3\mu \right)}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}\right]& \text{en charge}& \end{array}
$$

The parameter $d$ is introduced, which is equal to 1 if one yields and if one is loading at the time instant $t$ , and 0 otherwise:

$$
\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}}=2\mu \left[\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}-\frac{9\mu }{2}\frac{\langle \stackrel{̃}{\sigma}:\dot{\stackrel{̃}{\epsilon}}\rangle }{\left({\overline{R}}_{0}+3\mu \right){\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{2}}\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}-\frac{3}{2}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\left(Z,\dot{Z}\right)\left(1-d\frac{3\mu }{3\mu +{\overline{R}}_{0}}\right)\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}\right]
$$

One notes that $\dot{\tilde{\sigma}}$ is an affine function of $\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}$ , but also that there exists a part which does not depend on $\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}$ but on the increment of the metallurgical phases $\dot{Z}$ .

#### 7.3.3 Second member in prediction – Contribution of metallurgy

Because of the dependence of ( ) on the increment of the metallurgical phases, there is a contribution $\Delta {L}^{\mathit{pt}}$ to the second member  2
of the time variation of the stress tensor. On simple test cases for which an analytical solution exists, it has been observed that neglecting the second member $\Delta {L}^{\mathit{pt}}$ could lead, in order to converge, to a large number of iterations. This is why this term is taken into account for the prediction phase. The contribution comes from transformation plasticity, which is a purely deviatoric quantity. Finally, one obtains:

$$
\Delta {L}^{\mathit{pt}}={\int}_{\Omega}3\mu \left[{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\left(Z,\dot{Z}\right)\left(1-d\frac{3\mu }{3\mu +{\overline{R}}_{0}}\right)\stackrel{̃}{\sigma}\right]\delta \epsilon d\Omega
$$

With $\delta \varepsilon$ the virtual strain.

#### 7.3.4 Tangent matrix in prediction (option RIGI_MECA_TANG )

One recalls that the tangent operator ${K}_{i-1}$ (analysis option RIGI_MECA_TANG called at the first iteration of a new load increment) is evaluated from the results known at the previous time instant ${t}_{i-1}$ :

\dot{\sigma}={K}_{i-1}\dot{\varepsilon}

In loading, one assumes that one yields, and therefore one has:

\langle \tilde{\sigma}:\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}\rangle =\tilde{\sigma}:\dot{\tilde{\varepsilon}}
$$

The deviatoric part of the prediction matrix is therefore equal to:

$$
{\tilde{K}}_{i-1}=2\mu \left[I-\frac{9\mu }{2({\overline{R}}_{0}+3\mu )}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}\otimes \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}\right]
$$

Expression ( ) gives the value of $\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}$ . The spherical part is equal to:

$$
\frac{1}{3}\mathit{tr}({K}_{i-1})=KI
$$

Finally:

$$
{K}_{i-1}=2\mu \left[I-\frac{9\mu }{2({\overline{R}}_{0}+3\mu )}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}\otimes \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}\right]+KI
$$

### 7.4 Quantities in correction

#### 7.4.1 Tangent matrix in correction (option FULL_MECA)

The tangent operator ${K}^{n}$ (analysis option FULL_MECA called at each Newton iteration) is such that:

$$
{K}^{n}=\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \varepsilon }
$$

This matrix has two contributions, deviatoric and spherical:

$$
{K}^{n}=\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \varepsilon }=\frac{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \varepsilon }+\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial \mathit{tr}(\sigma )}{\partial \varepsilon }I
$$

The spherical part is trivial:

$$
\frac{1}{3}\frac{\partial \mathit{tr}(\sigma )}{\partial \varepsilon }I=KI
$$

With K the bulk modulus. The deviatoric part of the tangent matrix must now be expressed. One has:

$$
\frac{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \varepsilon }=\frac{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}\frac{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}{\partial \varepsilon }
$$

One starts again from the expression of the deviatoric stresses:

$$
\tilde{\sigma}=2\mu (\tilde{\varepsilon}-{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}-{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{pt}})
$$

And one differentiates:

$$
\frac{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}\left[2\mu (\tilde{\varepsilon}-{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}-{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{pt}})\right]
$$

One finds:

$$
\frac{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=I
$$

For transformation plasticity:

$$
\frac{{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{pt}}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=\frac{3}{2}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\frac{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}
$$

For viscoplasticity:

$$
\frac{{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=\frac{3}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}(\Delta p\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}})=\frac{3}{2}\left[\frac{\partial (\Delta p)}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}\otimes \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}+\Delta p\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}(\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}})\right]
$$

One needs to evaluate $\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}(\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}})$ ; now expression ( ) established the value of $\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}$ :

$$
\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}=\frac{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{e}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}}
$$

Applying the usual differentiation rules:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}(\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}})=\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}(\frac{{\tilde{\sigma}}^{e}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}})=\frac{1}{{({\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e})}^{2}}({\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}\frac{{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}^{e}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}-{\tilde{\sigma}}^{e}\frac{{\partial \sigma }_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}})
$$

One has:

$$
{\tilde{\sigma}}^{e}=2\mu \Delta \tilde{\varepsilon}+\frac{2\mu }{{2\mu }^{-}}{\tilde{\sigma}}^{-}\to \frac{{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}^{e}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=2\mu I
$$

By Hooke's law:

$$
\frac{{\partial \sigma }_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=3\mu \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}
$$

Finally:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}(\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}})=\frac{1}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}}(2\mu I-3\mu \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}\otimes \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}})
$$

It remains to find the expression of $\frac{\partial (\Delta p)}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}$ . For this, the consistency condition ${\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}=0$ is taken up again:

$$
{\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}=\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}-3\mu \Delta p}{1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}}-\overline{R}({p}^{-})-\overline{R}(\Delta p)-{\overline{\sigma}}_{c}-\overline{\eta}{(\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t})}^{\frac{1}{\overline{n}}}=0
$$

And one differentiates with respect to $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ :

$$
\frac{\partial {\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}\left[\frac{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}-3\mu \Delta p}{1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}}-\overline{R}(\Delta p)-\overline{\eta}{(\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t})}^{\frac{1}{\overline{n}}}\right]
$$

Thanks to ( ), one already has $\frac{{\partial \sigma }_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}$ . It remains to compute the other terms. The derivative of the metallurgical hardening term is equal to:

$$
\frac{\partial \overline{R}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=\overline{{R}_{0}}\frac{\partial (\Delta p)}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}
$$

And that of the viscous stress:

$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}\left[\overline{\eta}{(\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t})}^{\frac{1}{\overline{n}}}\right]=\frac{\overline{\eta}}{\overline{n}\Delta t}{(\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t})}^{\frac{1-\overline{n}}{\overline{n}}}(\frac{\partial (\Delta p)}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}})
$$

Finally, ( ) has the expression:

$$
\frac{\partial {\overline{f}}^{\text{*}}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=\frac{3\mu }{1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}-\frac{3\mu }{1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}}\frac{\partial (\Delta p)}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}-\overline{{R}_{0}}\frac{\partial (\Delta p)}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}-\frac{\overline{\eta}}{\overline{n}\Delta t}{(\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t})}^{\frac{1-\overline{n}}{\overline{n}}}(\frac{\partial (\Delta p)}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}})=0
$$

Rearranging, one finds the final expression of $\frac{\partial (\Delta p)}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}$ :

$$
\frac{\partial (\Delta p)}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=\frac{3\mu }{3\mu +\left[\overline{{R}_{0}}+\frac{\overline{\eta}}{\overline{n}\Delta t}{(\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t})}^{\frac{1-\overline{n}}{\overline{n}}}\right]\left[1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\right]}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}
$$

Therefore, for the viscoplastic term:

$$
\frac{{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=\frac{3}{2}\left[\frac{3\mu }{3\mu +\left[\overline{{R}_{0}}+\frac{\overline{\eta}}{\overline{n}\Delta t}{(\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t})}^{\frac{1-\overline{n}}{\overline{n}}}\right]\left[1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\right]}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}\otimes \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}+\Delta p\frac{1}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}}(2\mu I-3\mu \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}\otimes \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}})\right]
$$

If one starts again from the formal expression of the deviatoric tangent matrix:

$$
\frac{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}\left[2\mu (\tilde{\varepsilon}-{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}-{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{pt}})\right]
$$

The expression of the term corresponding to transformation plasticity and to elasticity is substituted into it:

$$
\frac{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=2\mu I-2\mu \frac{{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}-2\mu \frac{3}{2}{g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\frac{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}
$$

And therefore:

$$
\frac{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}}\left[2\mu I-2\mu \frac{{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\mathit{vp}}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}\right]
$$

The deviatoric tangent matrix is decomposed into two terms:

$$
\frac{\partial \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \tilde{\varepsilon}}=\frac{1}{1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}}\left[\alpha I+\beta \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}\otimes \frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}\right]
$$

With the term $\alpha$ :

$$
\alpha =2\mu (1-\frac{3\mu \Delta p}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}})
$$

And the term $\beta$ :

$$
\beta =-{9\mu }^{2}\left[\frac{1}{3\mu +\left[\overline{{R}_{0}}+\frac{\overline{\eta}}{\overline{n}\Delta t}{(\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t})}^{\frac{1-\overline{n}}{\overline{n}}}\right]\left[1+3\mu {g}_{Z}^{\mathit{pt}}\right]}+\frac{\Delta p}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}}\right]
$$

### 7.5 Synthesis of the matrices

The prediction and correction matrices can be written in the same form, by varying coefficients according to the cases. The spherical part is the same in the two matrices. The deviatoric part is written in general form:

$$
\tilde{K}=\left[\frac{2\mu }{a}(1-{c}_{3}\frac{3\mu \Delta p}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}})I-\frac{{c}_{p}}{a}\tilde{\sigma}\otimes \tilde{\sigma}\right]
$$
