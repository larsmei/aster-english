# r4.05.03 Seismic response by the spectral method

**Summary:**

The study of the response of a structure under the effect of imposed motions of seismic type, with a single imposed motion (single-support) or a multiple one (multi-support) is possible in transient analysis (time history). One will refer to the note [R4.05.01](doc:r4.05.01).

For design studies, one may be interested only in an estimate of the maximum forces induced by the loadings, in order to evaluate the safety margin with construction regulations, without resorting to a transient analysis.

The spectral method rests on the notion of oscillator spectrum of a seismic accelerogram. One details the method of construction of this response spectrum available in the operator [CALC_FONCTION](doc:u4.32.04).

One shows how this oscillator spectrum may be used to evaluate an upper bound of the response in relative displacement of a simple oscillator. This approach is justified if one does not wish to know the history of the displacements and of the forces, limiting oneself to the analysis of the inertial effects.

The spectral method uses general notions of the modal recombination method [R5.06.01](doc:r5.06.01).

One describes the different combination rules that can be used in order to obtain a realistic but conservative upper bound of the maximum response of the structure. These methods are available in the operator [COMB_SISM_MODAL](doc:u4.84.01).

### 1.2 Oscillator spectrum of an accelerogram

The notion of oscillator spectrum was introduced initially in order to compare with one another the effects of different accelerograms. The Fourier spectrum of a signal $A(t)$ informs one about its frequency content. The response of a mechanical system to a motion imposed at the base depends largely on the dynamic characteristics of this system: eigenfrequencies and reduced damping $(\xi ,{\omega '}_{0})$. r4.05.03-A details this aspect.

If one wishes to know the maximum value of the response of a simple oscillator with the parameters, $(A,\xi ,{\omega '}_{0})$ one must evaluate the Duhamel integral  which supplies the response of the oscillator  to an excitation imposed at the base.

The interest of this pseudo-acceleration spectrum lies in the fact that $\texttt{SRO}^{\ddot{x}}(A,\xi ,{\omega}_{0})$ is a good approximation of the maximum of absolute acceleration $\ddot{X}(t)$. Indeed, at the time instant when the relative displacement is maximal, the relative velocity vanishes and the reduced equation is written $\ddot{x}+0+{\omega}_{0}^{2}{x}_{\max}=-\ddot{s}$, which shows us that

For this reason, this oscillator spectrum is called **absolute pseudo-acceleration spectrum** .

The asymptote of this spectrum at high frequency (acceleration at zero period) corresponds to the response of an oscillator of high eigenfrequency, that is to say very stiff. In this case, the mass tends to follow entirely the imposed motion of the base. This asymptote therefore corresponds to the maximum acceleration ${\vert A(t)\vert }_{\max}$ of the imposed motion (ground or attachment point of the oscillator). It is reached in practice from the cut-off frequency of the spectrum. For this reason, one says that an accelerogram is scaled, for example, to $0.15g$, when its maximum amplitude and its absolute pseudo-acceleration oscillator spectrum at zero period are equal to $0.15g$ ($g$ is the acceleration of gravity).

### 1.3 Determination of the oscillator spectrum

The determination of the oscillator spectrum of an accelerogram $A(t)$ is available in the operator [CALC_FONCTION](doc:u4.32.04) with the keyword `SPEC_OSCI`: it is obtained by numerical integration of the Duhamel equation by the Nigam method [Solution of a second-order differential equation by the NIGAM method](doc:r5.05.01). This command supplies the absolute pseudo-acceleration spectrum and, on request, the pseudo-velocity spectrum or the relative-displacement spectrum.

### 1.4 Representation and use of the oscillator spectra

#### 1.4.1 Tri-logarithmic representation

The oscillator response spectra are commonly represented by tri-logarithmic graphs which make it possible to read on a single graph the three quantities: the relative displacement, the relative pseudo-velocity, the absolute pseudo-acceleration.

This representation is obtained by plotting the relative pseudo-velocity spectrum $\texttt{SRO}^{\dot{x}}$ in $\log-\log$ coordinates such that $\log\texttt{SRO}^{\dot{x}}=f(\log{\omega}_{0})$, on which one carries two complementary graduations at $\pm 45°$ if the scale of the logarithmic graduations is the same on the two axes:

- a logarithmic graduation at $+45°$ in order to measure the relative displacements $\log\texttt{SRO}^{x}=\log\left(\frac{\texttt{SRO}^{\dot{x}}}{{\omega}_{0}}\right)=\log\texttt{SRO}^{\dot{x}} - \log{\omega}_{0}$
- a logarithmic graduation at $–45°$ in order to measure the absolute accelerations $\log\texttt{SRO}^{\ddot{x}} =\log\left({\omega}_{0}\texttt{SRO}^{\dot{x}}\right)=\log\texttt{SRO}^{\dot{x}} + \log{\omega}_{0}$

### 1.5 Oscillator spectra used for studies

For studies of industrial installations, such as nuclear power plants, the seismic analysis leads to establishing several models:

- a civil-engineering model for the design of the buildings in order to determine:
- the accidental loadings for the analysis of the frameworks of these buildings;
- the motions imposed at the attachment points of the equipment (reactor vessel, supports of the piping networks, electrical cabinets, ..) at different levels of the buildings;
- study models for the verification of each piece of equipment subjected to the imposed motions amplified by the dynamic behaviour of the buildings.

#### 1.5.1 Design ground spectrum and verification of the buildings

At this stage, the equipment is known only as inertial overloads and one may admit that it brings no stiffness to the building. The structures are in this case subjected to a ground spectrum.

The frequency content of an oscillator spectrum reflects that of the accelerogram used and is therefore "marked" by the properties of the ground at the recording site. In order to construct the ground spectrum at the design stage, it is therefore recommended to establish the oscillator spectra for several accelerograms and to construct an envelope spectrum which smooths the anti-resonances.

![Figure](/figures/r4.05.03/SpectreSol.svg)

*Example of a ground spectrum for a project*

**Note**

In many cases, one does not know the rotational motion imposed by the earthquake, since the known earthquake accelerograms come from recordings of seismographs, sensors with one translational degree of freedom.

#### 1.5.2 Floor spectrum for the verification of the equipment

The study of the dynamic behaviour of the equipment subjected to the motions imposed by the supporting structure at the support points is possible from the response accelerograms at these points, results of the transient analysis of the behaviour of the building: these accelerograms, called floor accelerograms, make it possible to construct floor spectra.

For a verification of the equipment, one may limit oneself to a spectral analysis from the floor spectra and the differential displacements imposed at the supports.

The floor spectra are representative of the dynamic amplification brought by the supporting structure: a smoothing of the spectrum may be useful in order to take into consideration the uncertainty on the position of the eigenfrequencies of the building, but one will take care to keep realistic margins, since the ground spectrum is already an upper bound of the seismic loading. The oscillator spectrum must be represented with a frequency discretisation sufficiently fine in order to "capture" the resonances of the structure.

**Note**

Techniques for the direct determination of the floor spectra, from the ground spectrum and the modes of the structure, have been developed , but are not available at present.

## 4 Rules for the combination of the modal responses

In order to evaluate an upper bound of the response $R$ of the structure, one must now combine the modal responses ${R}_{imax}^{k}$ defined previously. Several levels of combination are necessary:

- combination of the eigenmodes retained ${\varphi}_{i}$ , for $i=1,\cdots,{N}_{r}\le N$ ,
- static correction by pseudo-mode,
- effect of the different excitations applied to groups of supports,
- combination along the directions of seismic excitation.

### 4.1 Direction of the earthquake and directional response

Different considerations lead to studying the seismic behaviour separately along each direction of space:

- for the study of a building on a ground, the accelerogram of the motion imposed vertically is different from that describing the horizontal motion, itself different along two orthogonal directions of space;
- for the study of a piece of equipment, the floor spectra differ significantly along the three directions of space, since they integrate the participations of different modes of the building (bending of floors, bending or torsion of the framework, ..).

This leads to establishing a directional modal response ${R}_{x}$ from different oscillator spectra and from modal participation factors established in each direction $X$ representing one of the directions of the global frame of definition of the mesh ($X,Y,Z$ ) or a particular direction defined explicitly by the user.

### 4.2 Choice of the eigenmodes to be combined

In order to represent correctly the strain modes liable to be excited by the imposed motion, one would need to know all the eigenmodes of frequency lower than the cut-off frequency of the spectrum, beyond which there is no significant dynamic amplification. This condition may prove difficult to fulfil for complex structures having a large number of eigenmodes.

The size of the modal basis necessary (${N}_{r}$ modes) must therefore be evaluated in order to make sure that no mode having an important contribution in the internal forces and the stresses has been omitted in each direction studied.

The expression involves the effective modal mass ${p}_{iX}^{2}{\mu}_{i}$, which makes it possible to state the criterion of cumulation of the unit effective modal masses.

**Criterion of cumulation of the effective modal masses**

The quality of a modal basis, from the point of view of the representation of the inertial properties of the structure, is evaluated by cumulating, for this direction, the unit effective modal masses of the available modes. An admissibility threshold of 95% of the total mass is commonly admitted. The same criterion may be applied partially in the case of a multi-support excitation with ${N}_{r}$ modes by comparing ${\varphi}_{{s_j}}^{T} m {\varphi}_{{s_j}}$ and $\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{r}}{p}_{i,j}^{2}{\mu}_{i}$.

The sum of the effective modal masses is in fact equal to the total mass which works on the chosen modal basis. In other words, this total working mass is equal to the total mass minus the mass contributions which are carried by clamped degrees of freedom (which therefore do not work on the modal basis). Thus, for example, on a mass-spring system with one degree of freedom with a mass $\texttt{M1}$ at the top and another mass $\texttt{M2}$ at the level of the basemat, the working mass will be equal to $\texttt{M1}$ and the total mass to $\texttt{M1}+\texttt{M2}$. Consequently, the unit effective modal mass for the single mode of the system will be equal to $\frac{\texttt{M1}}{\texttt{M1}+\texttt{M2}}$. The total cumulation will therefore have the same value and, according to the ratio between $\texttt{M1}$ and $\texttt{M2}$, one will therefore not necessarily be able to reach 90% of the total mass $(\texttt{M1}+\texttt{M2})$, even by considering all the modes (one has only a single mode in this example). In practice, the finer and more realistic the finite-element model, the smaller the difference between the working mass and the total mass will be.

### 4.4 Generalities on the combination rules

The rules of combination or of cumulation of the different components, modal or directional, are multiple and more or less complex to implement.

One presents the "natural" methods from the point of view of their aptitude to supply a realistic upper bound of the loadings induced in a structure represented by a basis of real eigenmodes coming from a model in linear elasticity, an upper bound estimated without transient analysis for a quantity of component ${G}^{k}$ , which one will name ${G}_{\text{max}}^{k}$ . **Assumption**

The hypothesis which justifies this method of combination may be stated: "the probable maximum of the energy stored in the structure is the sum of the probable maxima of the energy stored on each of the modes and on each of the directional components of the earthquake". That is to say that, with respect to the energy, the eigenmodes and the components of the earthquake are decoupled. It is analogous to the rule of addition of Gaussian random variables with zero mean.

The validity of this assumption, which will be discussed for each particular case of use of this combination method, is not established and various proposals have been presented in order to obtain a better approximation in the cases where it fails.

### 4.6 Establishment of the directional response in multi-support

#### 4.6.1 Computation of the global response

The order of the combinations to be carried out differs according to whether the excitations of the supports (or of the groups of supports) may be considered as correlated or uncorrelated with one another.

##### 4.6.1.1 Case with uncorrelated groups of supports

The sequence of the combinations may be stated as follows, in order:

- for each direction $X$, for each mode $i$ and for each group of supports, one computes modal directional support responses (intra-group combination on the supports). Considering that the supports of one and the same group are correlated, one proposes a summation of the algebraic values;
- for each direction $X$, for each group of supports $j$  :

- computation of the combined response of the modal oscillators (combination on the modes);
- computation of the pseudo-mode ${R}_{Xj}^{c}$ (static correction of the neglected modes);
- computation of the entrainment motion ${R}_{Xj}^{e}$ ;
- computation of the directional support response: ${R}_{Xj}=\sqrt{{{R}_{Xj}^{m}}^{2}+{{R}_{Xj}^{c}}^{2}+{{R}_{Xj}^{e}}^{2}}$

- for each direction (inter-group combination on the groups of supports): computation of the directional response, according to a CQS quadratic summation because the groups are assumed uncorrelated with one another;
- computation of the total response (combination on the directions).

##### 4.6.1.2 Case with correlated supports

In the case where all the supports are correlated with one another (when one cannot exhibit uncorrelated groups of supports), one proposes the following scheme, which has the advantage of making it possible to establish a parallel between the treatment of the single-support case treated as such and the single-support case treated as a particular case of the multi-support case. The sequence of the combinations may be summarised as follows:

- for each direction $X$ and for each mode $i$ , one computes directional modal responses (combination on the supports): the supports being assumed correlated with one another, one proposes a summation of the algebraic values or of the absolute values;
- for each direction $X$ :

- computation of the combined response ${R}_{X}^{m}$ of the modal oscillators (combination on the modes);
- computation of the pseudo-mode ${R}_{X}^{c}$ (static correction of the neglected modes, algebraic summation on the supports of the support pseudo-modes ${R}_{Xj}^{c}$ );
- computation of the entrainment motion ${R}_{Xj}^{e}$ (algebraic summation on the supports of the support entrainment motions ${R}_{Xj}^{e}$ ) ;
- computation of the directional response: ${R}_{X}=\sqrt{{{R}_{X}^{m}}^{2}+{{R}_{X}^{c}}^{2}+{{R}_{X}^{e}}^{2}}$ ,

### 4.8 Warning on the combinations

Several remarks are called for in order to warn the user about the way of using the combination methods and the combined quantities in a study note.

**Note 1**

If one wishes to use arithmetic combinations (direction) and quadratic combinations (modes), the quadratic cumulations must always be carried out last.

**Note 2**

Any quadratic combination applies only to the quantities for which, in instantaneous values, the cumulation has the meaning of a sum: combination of the components of displacement, or of generalised force or of stress of each eigenmode. The modal or directional quadratic combination therefore cannot be applied to stress intensities (principal stresses, von Mises, Tresca).

**Note 3**

The results of a combination, whatever the cumulation rule, must not serve as data for computing other quantities: for example, a differential displacement between two points (or a strain) can be computed only from the modal differential displacements which one then combines. A fortiori the generalised forces and the stresses can be computed only mode by mode before any combination and not from inertia forces deduced from the acceleration fields obtained by combination of the modal accelerations.

### 4.9 Regulatory practices

#### 4.9.1 Partition of the primary and secondary components of the response

The different supports of a piping line may be animated by different motions. One and the same piping run may be distributed over different buildings, different levels or different pieces of equipment. It therefore undergoes a multiple excitation. This results in two types of loading:

- an excitation whose frequency content varies from one support to another and which constitutes a primary loading according to the RCC-M classification,
- Seismic Differential Displacements (DDS) inducing a stress state by displacements imposed at the supports and classified as secondary.

The generalised moments resulting from these 2 loadings intervene separately in the RCC-M design inequalities and at several levels.

With a view to a thorough RCC-M post-processing, it is therefore necessary to have available the primary and secondary components of the seismic response.

In a more general manner, the method of combination of the support responses may differ according to whether one treats the case of the inertial or differential components. Moreover, the number of supports concerned by these two summations may not be equal. One is often led to impose overall differential motions even for supports associated with different user spectra. On the other hand, DDS formulated in rotation are sometimes to be considered. They cannot be associated with an inertial loading (limited to the translations).

One therefore proposes two treatments:

- Determination of the global response:
- The inertial and static entrainment contributions are cumulated during the computation of the directional support responses.
- Partition of the primary and secondary components of the global response:

The two previous contributions are no longer cumulated during the computation of the directional responses and are the subject of 2 independent treatments:

- The inertial component is obtained by suppressing the entrainment term ${R}_{ej}$ in the computation of the global response.
- The static component is determined by combining the entrainment terms defined under the keyword `DEPL_MULT_APPUI`. The methods of combination of these DDS load cases are entered in the keyword `COMB_DEPL_APPUI`.

#### 4.9.2 Envelope-spectrum method

Even if the piping is subjected to a multiple seismic excitation, the current practice is to come back to the analysis of a single-supported structure while keeping the DDS load cases. This simplified approach implies defining a single spectrum per direction for all the supports of the piping. For each direction, one then adopts an “envelope” spectrum of the different spectra at the supports. The spectra retained for the horizontal directions $X$ and $Y$ are identical.

In almost all cases, this method generates a “design margin”.
