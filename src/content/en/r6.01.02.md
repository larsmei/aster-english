# r6.01.02 Generalities on the conjugate gradient: GCPC Aster and use of PETSc

**Summary:**

**In the framework of thermo-mechanical simulations, the bulk of the computational cost often comes from the construction and the solution of linear systems**. The choice of the right linear solver is therefore essential, on the one hand for its speed, but also for its robustness and the memory space it requires. Each time, a compromise has to be struck between these constraints.

**In this** **document**, **one details**  from a theoretical, algorithmic and *code_aster* modelling point of view, **the fundamentals of the GCPC and of the other Krylov solvers implemented in PETSc**. In particular, one highlights their links with the other linear solvers of the code and with continuous optimisation methods. One then goes on to their implementation difficulties, their parameter settings, their scopes as well as a few pieces of advice on use.

## 1 Problem statement

In the numerical simulation of physical phenomena, **a significant computational cost often comes from the construction and the solution of linear systems**. Structural mechanics is no exception to the rule! The cost of constructing the system depends on the number of integration points and on the complexity of the constitutive laws, whereas that of the solution is linked to the number of unknowns, to the modellings adopted and to the topology (bandwidth, conditioning). When the number of unknowns explodes, the second stage becomes predominant 1
and it is therefore this latter stage that will mainly interest us here. Moreover, when it is possible to be more efficient on this solution phase, thanks to access to a parallel machine, this advantage can propagate to the phase of constitution of the system proper (elementary computations and assemblies, cf. [U2.08.06](doc:u2.08.06)).

These **inversions of linear systems are in fact omnipresent in the codes and often buried deep inside other numerical algorithms**: non-linear scheme, time integration, modal analysis…. One seeks, for example, the vector of nodal displacements (or of displacement increments) $u$ satisfying a linear system of the type

$\mathrm{Ku}=f$                                         (1-1)

with $K$ a stiffness matrix and $f$ a vector expressing the application of generalised forces to the mechanical system.

In general, the **solution of this type of problem requires a (wider) questioning** than it appears:

- Does one have access to the matrix or does one merely know its action on a vector?
- Is this matrix sparse or dense?
- What are its numerical properties (symmetry, positive definite…) and structural properties (real/complex, banded, block…)?
- Does one want to solve a single system (1-1), several simultaneously 2 or consecutively 3? Or even several different and successive systems whose matrices are very close 4?
- In the case of successive solutions, can one reuse previous results in order to facilitate the next solutions (cf. restart technique, partial factorisation)?
- What is the order of magnitude of the size of the problem, of the matrix and of its factorised form relative to the processing capacities of the CPUs and of the associated memories (RAM, disk)?
- Does one want a very accurate solution or merely an estimate (cf. nested solvers)?
- Does one have access to linear-algebra libraries (and to their MPI, BLAS, LAPACK… prerequisites) or must one call upon “in-house” products?

**In** **Code_Aster**, one builds the matrix explicitly and stores it in MORSE format 5
. With most modellings, the matrix is sparse (because of the finite-element discretisation), potentially ill-conditioned 6

and often real, symmetric and indefinite 7
. In non-linear analysis, in modal analysis or during thermo-mechanical chaining, one often treats problems of the “multiple right-hand sides” type. Discrete frictional-contact methods take advantage of partial-factorisation capabilities. On the other hand, *Code_Aster* also uses scenarios of simultaneous solutions (Schur complements of contact and of substructuring…).

As for the sizes of the problems, even if they swell from year to year, they remain modest compared with CFD: of the order of a million unknowns but for hundreds of time steps or Newton iterations.

Furthermore, from a **“middleware and hardware” point of view**, the code now relies on numerous optimised and durable libraries (MPI, BLAS, LAPACK, METIS, SCOTCH, PETSc, MUMPS…) and is used mainly on SMP clusters (fast networks, large RAM and disk storage capacity). One therefore seeks above all to optimise the use of linear solvers with this in mind.

**For 60 years, two types of techniques have competed for supremacy in the field, the direct solvers and the iterative ones** (cf. [Che05][Dav06][Duf06][Gol96][LT98][Liu89][Meu99][Saa03]).

**The former** are robust and terminate in a finite number of operations (theoretically) known in advance. Their theory is relatively well completed and their declension according to many types of matrices and software architectures is very complete. In particular, their multilevel algorithmics is well suited to the memory hierarchies of present-day machines. However, they require storage capacities that grow rapidly with the size of the problem, which limits the extensibility of their parallelism 8
. Even if this parallelism can be decomposed into several independent layers, thus multiplying the performance.

By contrast, **iterative methods** are more “scalable” when one increases the number of processors. Their theory abounds in numerous “open problems”, especially in finite arithmetic. In practice, their convergence in a “reasonable” number of iterations is not always secured; it depends on the structure of the matrix, on the starting point, on the stopping criterion… This type of solver has more difficulty in breaking through in industrial structural mechanics, where one often accumulates heterogeneities, non-linearities and junctions of models that undermine the conditioning of the working operator. On the other hand, they are not suited to solving efficiently problems of the “multiple right-hand sides” type. Yet these are very frequent in the algorithmics of mechanical simulations.

Unlike their direct counterparts, it is not possible to propose THE iterative solver that will solve any linear system. The matching of the type of algorithm to a class of problems is done case by case. They nevertheless present other advantages which historically earned them a right of citizenship for certain applications. For an equivalent memory management, they require less than direct solvers, because one only needs to know the action of the matrix on an arbitrary vector, without truly having to store it. On the other hand, one is not subject to the “diktat” of the fill-in phenomenon which deteriorates the profile of the matrices; one can efficiently exploit the sparse character of the operators and control the accuracy of the results 9
.

In the numerical-analysis literature, a preponderant place is often given to iterative solvers in general, and to the variants of the conjugate gradient in particular. **The most seasoned authors** **agree that, even if its use gains “market share” over the years and the variants, certain fields remain refractory. Among these, structural mechanics, the simulation of electronic circuits…**

To paraphrase these authors, the use of direct solvers belongs to the domain of technique whereas the choice of the right iterative-method/preconditioner pair is rather an art! Despite its biblical simplicity on paper, the solution of a linear system, even a symmetric positive-definite one, is not “a long quiet river”. Between two evils, fill-in/pivoting and preconditioning, one must choose!

## 2 Descent methods

### 2.1 Positioning of the problem

Let $K$ be the stiffness matrix (of size $N$) to be inverted; if it has the “good taste” of being symmetric positive definite (SPD in the English-language literature), which is often [11]_
the case in structural mechanics, one shows by simple differentiation that the following problems are equivalent:

- Solution of the usual linear system with $u$ the solution vector (of the displacements or displacement increments, resp. in temperature….) and $f$ the vector expressing the application of generalised forces to the thermo-mechanical system

$({P}_{1})\text{Ku}=f$                                     (2.1-1)

- Minimisation of the quadratic functional representing the energy of the system, with $\text{< , >}$ the usual Euclidean scalar product,

$\begin{array}{}({P}_{2})u=\underset{v\in {\Re }^{N}}{\text{Arg}\min}J(v)\\ \text{avec}J(v):=\frac{1}{2}\langle \text{v,Kv}\rangle -\langle f,v\rangle =\frac{1}{2}{v}^{T}\text{Kv}-{f}^{T}v\end{array}$             (2.1-2)

Because of the “positive-definiteness” of the matrix which makes $J$ strictly convex, the vector cancelling [12]_
$\nabla J$ corresponds to the unique [13]_
global minimum $u$. This is illustrated by the following relation, valid whatever the symmetric $K$,

$J(v)=J(u)+\frac{1}{2}{(v-u)}^{T}K(v-u)$                         (2.1-3)

Thus, for any vector $v$ different from the solution $u$, the positive-definite character of the operator makes the second term strictly positive and therefore $u$ is also a global minimum.

$\begin{array}{}({P}_{3})u=\underset{v\in {\Re }^{N}}{\text{Arg}\min}E(v)\\ \text{avec}E(v):=\langle \text{Ke}(v),e(v)\rangle ={(\text{Ke}(v))}^{T}e(v)={\parallel e(v)\parallel }_{K}^{2}\end{array}$         (2.1-4)

From a mathematical point of view, this is nothing other than a matrix norm (legitimate since $K$ is SPD). One often prefers to express it *via*  a residual $r(v)=f-\text{Kv}$

$E(v)=\langle r(v),{K}^{-1}r(v)\rangle =r{(v)}^{T}{K}^{-1}r(v)$                 (2.1-5)

### 2.2 Steepest Descent

#### 2.2.1 Principle

This last remark prefigures the philosophy of the method called “of the steepest slope”, better known under its English name of ‘Steepest Descent’: one constructs the sequence of iterates ${u}^{i}$ by following the direction along which $J$ decreases the most, at least locally, that is ${d}^{i}=-\nabla {J}^{i}={r}^{i}$ with ${J}^{i}:=J({u}^{i})\text{et}{r}^{i}:=f-{\text{Ku}}^{i}$. At the *i* th iteration, one will therefore seek to construct ${u}^{i+1}$ such that:

${u}^{i+1}:={u}^{i}+{\alpha}^{i}{r}^{i}$                                     (2.2-1)

and

${J}^{i+1}<{J}^{i}$                                         (2.2-2)

Thanks to this formulation, one has therefore transformed a quadratic minimisation problem of size $N$ (in $J$ and $u$) into a one-dimensional minimisation (in $G$ and $\alpha$)

$\begin{array}{}\text{Trouver}{\alpha}^{i}\text{tel que}{\alpha}^{i}=\underset{\alpha \in \left[{\alpha}_{m},{\alpha}_{M}\right]}{\text{Arg}\min}{G}^{i}(\alpha )\\ \text{avec}{G}^{i}(\alpha ):=J({u}^{i}+\alpha {r}^{i})\end{array}$                     (2.2-3)

The following figures illustrate the functioning of this procedure on example no. 1: starting from the point ${u}^{0}={[-2,-2]}^{T}$ (cf. (a)) one seeks the optimal descent parameter, ${\alpha}^{0}$, along the line of steepest slope ${r}^{0}$; which amounts to seeking a point belonging to the intersection of a vertical plane and a paraboloid (b), signified by the parabola (c). Trivially this point cancels the derivative of the parabola (d)

$\frac{\partial {G}^{0}({\alpha}^{0})}{\partial \alpha }=0\iff \langle \nabla J({u}^{1}),{r}^{0}\rangle =0\iff \langle {r}^{1},{r}^{0}\rangle =0\iff {\alpha}^{0}:=\frac{{\parallel {r}^{0}\parallel }^{2}}{\langle {r}^{0},{\text{Kr}}^{0}\rangle }$         (2.2-4)

This orthogonality between two successive residuals (i.e. successive gradients) produces a characteristic path, said to be in “zigzag”, towards the solution (e). Thus, in the case of an ill-conditioned system producing narrow and elongated ellipses [14]_
, the number of iterations required can be considerable (cf. figure 2.2-1).

#### 2.2.2 Algorithm

Whence the unfolding

$\begin{array}{}\text{Initialisation}{u}^{0}\text{donné}\\ \text{Boucle en}i\\ (1){r}^{i}=f-{\text{Ku}}^{i}(\text{nouveau résidu})\\ (2){\alpha}^{i}=\frac{{\parallel {r}^{i}\parallel }^{2}}{\langle {r}^{i},{\text{Kr}}^{i}\rangle }(\text{paramètre optimal de descente})\\ (3){u}^{i+1}={u}^{i}+{\alpha}^{i}{r}^{i}(\text{nouvel itéré})\\ (4)\text{Test d'arrêt via}{J}^{i+1}-{J}^{i}(\text{par exemple})\end{array}$

*Algorithm 1: Steepest Descent.*

To save a matrix–vector product it is preferable to substitute for step (1) the following residual update

${r}^{i+1}={r}^{i}-{\alpha}^{i}{\text{Kr}}^{i}$                                 (2.2-5)

However, in order to avoid untimely accumulations of round-off, one periodically recomputes the residual with the initial formula (1).

#### 2.2.3 Elements of theory

One shows that this algorithm converges, for any starting point ${u}^{0}$, at the rate [15]_

$\begin{array}{}{\parallel e({u}^{i+1})\parallel }_{K}^{2}={\omega}^{i}{\parallel e({u}^{i})\parallel }_{K}^{2}\\ \text{avec}{\omega}^{i}:=1-\frac{{\parallel {r}^{i}\parallel }^{4}}{\langle {K}^{-1}{r}^{i},{r}^{i}\rangle \langle {\text{Kr}}^{i},{r}^{i}\rangle }\end{array}$                         (2.2-6)

By expanding the error on the basis of the eigenmodes $({\lambda}_{j};{v}_{j})$ of the matrix $K$

$e({u}^{i})={\sum}_{j}{\xi}_{j}{v}_{j}$                                         (2.2-7)

the attenuation factor of the error in energy becomes

${\omega}^{i}=1-\frac{{({\sum}_{j}{\xi}_{j}^{2}{\lambda}_{j}^{2})}^{2}}{({\sum}_{j}{\xi}_{j}^{2}{\lambda}_{j}^{3})({\sum}_{j}{\xi}_{j}^{2}{\lambda}_{j})}$                             (2.2-8)

In (2.2-8), the fact that the components ${\xi}_{j}$ are squared ensures the priority eviction of the dominant eigenvalues. One finds here one of the characteristics of modal methods of Krylov type (Lanczos, Arnoldi cf. [Boi01] §5/§6) which favour the extreme eigenmodes. For this reason, the Steepest Descent and the conjugate gradient are called “coarse” compared with the classical iterative solvers (Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel, SOR…) which are “smoother” because they eliminate without discrimination all the components at each iteration.

Finally, thanks to the Kantorovich inequality [16]_
, one greatly improves the readability of the attenuation factor. At the end of *i*  iterations, at worst, the decrease is expressed in the form

${\parallel e({u}^{i})\parallel }_{K}\le {(\frac{\eta (K)-1}{\eta (K)+1})}^{i}{\parallel e({u}^{0})\parallel }_{K}$                                 (2.2-9)

It ensures the linear convergence [17]_

of the process in a number of iterations proportional to the conditioning of the operator. Thus, in order to obtain

$\frac{{\parallel e({u}^{i})\parallel }_{K}}{{\parallel e({u}^{0})\parallel }_{K}}\le \varepsilon (\text{petit})$                                 (2.2-10)

one needs a number of iterations of the order of

$i\approx \frac{\eta (K)}{4}\ln\frac{1}{\varepsilon}$                                     (2.2-11)

An ill-conditioned problem will therefore slow down the convergence of the process, which one had already observed “visually” with the phenomenon of the “narrow and winding valley”.

In order better to apprehend the implication of the spectrum of the operator and of the starting point in the unfolding of the algorithm, let us simplify formula (2.2-8) by placing ourselves in the trivial case where $N=2$. Denoting by $\kappa =\frac{{\lambda}_{1}}{{\lambda}_{2}}$ the matrix conditioning of the operator and by $\mu =\frac{{\xi}_{2}}{{\xi}_{1}}$ the slope of the error at the *i* th iteration (in the coordinate system of the two eigenvectors), one obtains a more readable expression of the error attenuation factor (cf. figure 2.2-2)

${\omega}^{i}=1-\frac{{({\kappa}^{2}+{\mu}^{2})}^{2}}{(\kappa +{\mu}^{2})({\kappa}^{3}+{\mu}^{2})}$                             (2.2-12)

As for modal solvers, one observes that the importance of the conditioning of the operator is weighted by the choice of a good starting point: despite a poor conditioning, cases (a) and (b) are very different; in the first, the starting point almost generates an eigenspace of the operator and one converges in two iterations, otherwise one has the “everlasting zigzags”.

- This method of steepest descent was initiated by Cauchy (1847) and brought back into fashion by Curry (1944). In the framework of SPD operators, it is also sometimes called the gradient method with optimal parameter (cf. [LT98] §8.2.1). Despite its weak convergence properties, it had its “hour of glory” for minimising functions $J$ *that are quasi-arbitrary, in fact only differentiable.* *In this more general case, it then only makes it possible to reach stationary points, at best local minima.*
- To avoid zigzag effects various acceleration procedures have been proposed (cf. Forsythe 1968, Luenberger 1973 or [Min08] §2.3) which have a convergence speed similar to that of the conjugate gradient but for a much higher computational complexity. They have therefore gradually fallen into disuse.
- Note that variants of algorithm 1 have been introduced to treat non-SPD cases: Minimum-residual iteration and Steepest Descent with residual norm (cf. [Saa03]).
- *One finds through the Steepest Descent a key concept very widespread in numerical analysis: that of the solution of a problem, by projection of iterates onto an approximating subspace, here* ${K}_{i}:=\mathrm{vect}({r}^{i})$ *, perpendicularly to another subspace, here* ${L}_{i}:={K}_{i}$ *. One calls them, respectively, search or approximation space and constraint space. For the Steepest Descent, they are equal and reduced to their simplest expression but we shall see that for the conjugate gradient they take the form of particular spaces, called Krylov spaces.*

> *Formally, at each iteration* $i$ *of the algorithm, one thus seeks an increment* ${\delta}^{i}:={\alpha}^{i}{r}^{i}$ *such that:*

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{u}^{i}:={u}^{0}+{\delta}^{i}{\delta}^{i}:={\alpha}^{i}{r}^{i}\in {K}_{i}\\ \langle {r}^{0}-K{\delta}^{i},w\rangle =0\forall w\in {L}_{i}={K}_{i}\end{array}$                 (2.2-13)

> *This general framework constitutes what one calls the Petrov–Galerkin conditions.*

#### 2.2.4 Complexity and memory occupation

The major part of the computational cost of algorithm 1 resides in the update (2.2-5) and, more particularly, in the matrix–vector product $K{r}^{i}$. On the other hand, one has already mentioned that its convergence was secured and took place in a number of iterations proportional to the conditioning of the matrix (cf. (2.2-11)).

The computational complexity of the algorithm is therefore, if one takes into account the sparse character of the operator, of the order of $\Theta (\eta (K)\text{cN})$ where $c$ is the average number of non-zero terms per row of $K$.

Thus, with problems resulting from the finite-element discretisation of second-order elliptic operators [18]_
(resp. of fourth order), one has operator conditionings in $\eta (K)=\Theta ({N}^{2/d})$ (resp. $\eta (K)=\Theta ({N}^{4/d})$) where $d$ is the space dimension. The computational complexity of the Steepest Descent in this framework is thus written $\Theta ({\text{cN}}^{\frac{2}{d}+1})$ (resp. $\Theta ({\text{cN}}^{\frac{4}{d}+1})$).

As regards memory occupation, only the storage of the working matrix is possibly required [19]_
: $\Theta (\text{cN})$. In practice, the computer implementation of sparse storage imposes the management of additional integer vectors: for example, for the MORSE storage used in *Code_Aster*, vectors of the end-of-row indices and of the column indices of the non-zero elements of the profile. Whence an effective memory complexity of $\Theta (\text{cN})$ reals and $\Theta (\text{cN}+N)$ integers.

### 2.3 “General” descent method

#### 2.3.1 Principle

A crucial step of these methods is the choice of their descent direction [20]_
. Even if the gradient of the functional remains the main ingredient, one can perfectly well choose another version ${d}^{i}$ than that required for the Steepest Descent. At the *i* th iteration, one will therefore seek to construct ${u}^{i+1}$ satisfying

${u}^{i+1}:={u}^{i}+{\alpha}^{i}{d}^{i}$                                         (2.3-1)

with

${\alpha}^{i}=\underset{\alpha \in \left[{\alpha}_{m},{\alpha}_{M}\right]}{\text{Arg}\min}J({u}^{i}+\alpha {d}^{i})$                                 (2.3-2)

This is of course only a generalisation of the 'Steepest Descent' seen previously and one shows that its choices of the descent parameter and its orthogonality property generalise

$\begin{array}{}{\alpha}^{i}:=\frac{\langle {r}^{i},{d}^{i}\rangle }{\langle {d}^{i},{\text{Kd}}^{i}\rangle }\\ \langle {d}^{i},{r}^{i+1}\rangle =0\end{array}$                                         (2.3-3)

Whence the same “zigzag” effect during the unfolding of the process and a convergence similar to (2.2-6) with an error attenuation factor bounded from below by:

${\omega}^{i}:=1-\frac{{\langle {r}^{i},{d}^{i}\rangle }^{2}}{\langle {K}^{-1}{r}^{i},{r}^{i}\rangle \langle {\text{Kd}}^{i},{d}^{i}\rangle }\ge \frac{1}{\eta (K)}{\langle \frac{{r}^{i}}{\parallel {r}^{i}\parallel },\frac{{d}^{i}}{\parallel {d}^{i}\parallel }\rangle }^{2}$             (2.3-4)

From this result one can then formulate two observations:

- The conditioning of the operator intervenes directly on the attenuation factor and therefore on the speed of convergence,
- To ensure convergence (sufficient condition) it is necessary, at a given iteration, that the descent direction not be orthogonal to the residual.

The sufficient condition of this last item is of course verified for the Steepest Descent (${d}^{i}={r}^{i}$) and it will impose a choice of descent direction for the conjugate gradient. To remedy the problem raised by the first point, we shall see that it is possible to constitute a working operator $\tilde{K}$ whose conditioning is smaller. One then speaks of preconditioning.

Still in the case of an SPD operator, the unfolding of a “general” descent method is therefore written

$\begin{array}{}\text{Initialisation}{u}^{0},{d}^{0}\text{donnés,}{r}^{0}=f-{\text{Ku}}^{0}\\ \text{Boucle en}i\\ (1){z}^{i}={\text{Kd}}^{i}\\ (2){\alpha}^{i}=\frac{\langle {r}^{i},{d}^{i}\rangle }{\langle {d}^{i},{z}^{i}\rangle }(\text{paramètre optimal de descente})\\ (3){u}^{i+1}={u}^{i}+{\alpha}^{i}{d}^{i}(\text{nouvel itéré})\\ (4){r}^{i+1}={r}^{i}-{\alpha}^{i}{z}^{i}(\text{nouvel résidu})\\ (5)\text{Test d'arrêt via}{J}^{i+1}-{J}^{i},\parallel {d}^{i}\parallel \text{ou}\parallel {r}^{i+1}\parallel (\text{par exemple})\\ (6)\text{Calcul de la dd}{d}^{i+1}={d}^{i+1}(\nabla {J}^{k},{\nabla}^{2}{J}^{k},{d}^{k}\dots )\end{array}$

*Algorithm 2: Descent method in the case of a quadratic functional.*

This algorithm already prefigures well that of the Conjugate Gradient (CG) which we shall examine in the following chapter (cf. algorithm 4). It shows well that the CG is only a descent method applied in the framework of quadratic functionals and of specific descent directions. Finally, only step (6) will be fleshed out.

## 3 The Conjugate Gradient (CG)

### 3.1 General description

#### 3.1.1 Principle

Now that the foundations are in place we shall be able to tackle the Conjugate Gradient (CG) algorithm proper. It belongs to a subset of descent methods which groups the so-called “conjugate-direction” methods [22]_
. These recommend constructing progressively linearly independent descent directions ${d}^{0,}{d}^{1,}{d}^{2}\dots$ so as to avoid the zigzags of the classical descent method.

What linear combination then to recommend in order to construct, at step $i$, the new descent direction? Knowing of course that it must take into account two crucial pieces of information: the value of the gradient $\nabla {J}^{i}=-{r}^{i}$ and those of the previous directions ${d}^{0},{d}^{1}\dots {d}^{i-1}$.

$?{d}^{i}=\alpha {r}^{i}+{\sum}_{j<i}{\beta}_{j}{d}^{j}$                                 (3.1-1)

The trick consists in choosing a vector independence of $K$-orthogonality type (as the working operator is SPD, it does define a scalar product *via*  which two vectors can be orthogonal, cf. figure 3.1-1)

${({d}^{i})}^{T}K{d}^{j}=0i\ne j$                                 (3.1-2)

also called conjugation, whence the designation of the algorithm. It makes it possible to propagate the orthogonality step by step and therefore to compute only one Gram–Schmidt coefficient at each iteration. Whence a very appreciable gain in computational and memory complexity.

![Figure](/figures/r6.01.02/100000000000027B0000013D3FD3D80687CEAED4.png)

*Figure 3.1-1. Example of pairs of* $K$ *-orthogonal vectors in 2D: conditioning* *of* $K$ *arbitrary (a), perfect conditioning (i.e. equal to unity) = usual orthogonality (b).*

One can therefore content oneself with a linear combination of the type

${d}^{i}:={r}^{i}+{\beta}^{i}{d}^{i-1}$                                         (3.1-3)

And this, all the more so as it verifies the sufficient condition (2.3-4) and that, because of the orthogonality (2.3-3b), the one-dimensional search (2.3-2) which follows operates in an optimal space: the plane formed by the two orthogonal directions $({r}^{i},{d}^{i-1})$.

It therefore remains to determine the optimal value of the proportionality coefficient ${\beta}^{i}$. In the CG this choice is made so as to maximise the attenuation factor of (2.3-4), that is the term

$\frac{{\langle {\mathrm{r}}^{i},{\mathrm{d}}^{i}\rangle }^{2}}{\langle {\mathrm{K}}^{-1}{\mathrm{r}}^{i},{\mathrm{r}}^{i}\rangle \langle {\text{Kd}}^{i},{\mathrm{d}}^{i}\rangle }$                                     (3.1-4)

It leads to the expression

${\beta}^{i}:=\frac{{\parallel {r}^{i}\parallel }^{2}}{{\parallel {r}^{i-1}\parallel }^{2}}$                                         (3.1-5)

and induces the same orthogonality property of successive residuals as for the Steepest Descent (but without the zigzags!)

$\langle {r}^{i},{r}^{i-1}\rangle =0$                                     (3.1-6)

There is added a “residual–dd” condition

$\langle {r}^{i},{d}^{i}\rangle ={\parallel {r}^{i}\parallel }^{2}$                                     (3.1-7)

which imposes initialising the process *via* ${d}^{0}={r}^{0}$.

#### 3.1.2 Algorithm

In short, by recapitulating relations (2.2-5), (2.3-1), (2.3-3) and (3.1-3), (3.1-5), (3.1-7) there comes the classical algorithm

$\begin{array}{}\text{Initialisation}{u}^{0}\text{donné,}{r}^{0}={\text{f-Ku}}^{0},{d}^{0}={r}^{0}\\ \text{Boucle en}i\\ (1){z}^{i}={\text{Kd}}^{i}\\ (2){\alpha}^{i}=\frac{{\parallel {r}^{i}\parallel }^{2}}{\langle {d}^{i},{z}^{i}\rangle }(\text{paramètre optimal de descente})\\ (3){u}^{i+1}={u}^{i}+{\alpha}^{i}{d}^{i}(\text{nouvel itéré})\\ (4){r}^{i+1}={r}^{i}-{\alpha}^{i}{z}^{i}(\text{nouveau résidu})\\ (5)\text{Test d'arrêt via}\langle {r}^{i+1},{r}^{i+1}\rangle (\text{par exemple})\\ (6){\beta}^{i+1}=\frac{{\parallel {r}^{i+1}\parallel }^{2}}{{\parallel {r}^{i}\parallel }^{2}}(\text{paramètre de conjugaison optimal})\\ (7){d}^{i+1}={r}^{i+1}+{\beta}^{i+1}{d}^{i}(\text{nouvelle dd})\end{array}$

*Algorithm 4: Standard conjugate gradient (CG).*

### 3.2 Elements of theory

#### 3.2.1 Krylov space

Taking up again the Petrov–Galerkin analysis already mentioned for the Steepest Descent, one can synthesise in one sentence the action of the CG. It realises successive orthogonal projections onto the Krylov space ${\kappa}_{i}(K,{r}^{0}):=\text{vect}({r}^{0},{\mathrm{Kr}}^{0}\dots {K}^{i-1}{r}^{0})$ where ${r}^{0}$ is the initial residual: at the *i* th iteration ${K}_{i}={L}_{i}={\kappa}_{i}(K,{r}^{0})$. One solves the linear system (${P}_{1}$) by seeking an approximate solution ${u}^{i}$ in the affine subspace (search space of dimension $N$)

$A={r}^{0}+{\kappa}_{i}(K,{r}^{0})$                                     (3.2-1)

while imposing the orthogonality constraint (constraint space of dimension $N$)

${r}^{i}:=f-{\text{Ku}}^{i}\perp {\kappa}_{i}(K,{r}^{0})$                                 (3.2-2)

This Krylov space has the good property of facilitating the approximation of the solution, after $m$ iterations, in the form

${K}^{-1}f\approx {u}^{m}={r}^{0}+{P}_{m-1}(K)f$                             (3.2-3)

where ${P}_{m-1}$ is a certain matrix polynomial of order $m-1$. Indeed, one shows that the residuals and the descent directions generate this space

$\begin{array}{}\text{vect}({r}^{0},{r}^{1}\dots {r}^{m-1})={\kappa}_{m}(K,{r}^{0})\\ \text{vect}({d}^{0},{d}^{1}\dots {d}^{m-1})={\kappa}_{m}(K,{r}^{0})\end{array}$                         (3.2-4)

while allowing the approximate solution, ${u}^{m}$, to minimise the energy norm over the whole affine space *A*

${\parallel {u}^{m}\parallel }_{K}\le {\parallel u\parallel }_{K}\forall u\in A$                                 (3.2-5)

This result joined to property (3.2-4b) illustrates all the optimality of the CG: unlike the descent methods, the energy minimum is not realised successively for each descent direction, but jointly for all the descent directions already obtained.

**Note:**

- One distinguishes a great variety of projection methods onto spaces of Krylov type, more prosaically called “Krylov methods”. To solve linear systems [Saa03] (CG, GMRES, FOM/IOM/DOM, GCR, ORTHODIR/MIN…) and/or modal problems [Boi01] (Lanczos, Arnoldi…). They differ by the choice of their constraint space and by that of the preconditioning applied to the initial operator in order to constitute the working one, knowing that different implementations lead to radically distinct algorithms (vector or block version, orthonormalisation tools…).

#### 3.2.2 Orthogonality

As has already been pointed out, the descent directions are $K$-orthogonal to one another. Moreover, the choice of the optimal descent parameter (cf. (2.2-4), (2.3-3a) or step (2)) imposes, step by step, the orthogonalities

$\begin{array}{}\langle {d}^{i},{r}^{m}\rangle =0\forall i<m\\ \langle {r}^{i},{r}^{m}\rangle =0\end{array}$                             (3.2-6)

One therefore notes a small approximation in the appellation of the CG, because the gradients are not conjugate (cf. (3.2-6b)) and the conjugate directions do not comprise only gradients (cf. (3.1-3)). But let us not “quibble”, the designated ingredients are nevertheless there!

At the end of $N$ iterations, two cases present themselves:

- Either the residual is zero ${r}^{N}=0$ hence convergence.
- Or it is orthogonal to the $N$ previous descent directions which constitute a basis of the finite approximation space ${\Re }^{N}$ (as they are linearly independent). Whence necessarily ${r}^{N}=0$ hence convergence.

It would therefore seem that the CG is a direct method which converges in at most $N$ iterations; that is at least what one believed before testing it on practical cases! For what remains true in theory, in exact arithmetic, is undermined by the finite arithmetic of computers. Progressively, notably because of round-off errors, the descent directions lose their fine conjugation properties and the minimisation leaves the required space.

Put otherwise, one solves an approximate problem which is no longer quite the desired projection of the initial problem. The (theoretically) direct method has revealed its true nature! It is iterative and therefore subject, in practice, to numerous hazards (conditioning, starting point, stopping tests, precision of the orthogonality…).

To remedy this, one can impose during the construction of the new descent direction (cf. algorithm 4, step (7)), a reorthogonalisation phase. This practice, very widespread in modal analysis [Boi01] and appendix 2) and in domain decomposition [Boi08] can be declined under different variants: total, partial, selective reorthogonalisation… *via*  a whole panoply of orthogonalisation procedures (GS, GSM, IGSM, Househölder, Givens).

These reorthogonalisations require, for example, the storage of the ${N}_{\mathrm{orth}}$ first vectors ${d}^{k}(k=1\dots {N}_{\text{orth}})$ and of their product by the working operator ${z}^{k}={\mathrm{Kd}}^{k}(k=1\dots {N}_{\text{orth}})$. Formally, it is therefore a matter of substituting for the last two steps of the algorithm the following computation

${d}^{i+1}={r}^{i+1}-\sum_{k=0}^{\max(i,{N}_{\text{orth}})}\frac{\langle {r}^{i+1},{\text{Kd}}^{k}\rangle }{\langle {d}^{k},{\text{Kd}}^{k}\rangle }{d}^{k}(\text{nouvelle dd}K-\text{orthogonalisée})$     (3.2-7)

Through the different numerical experiments which have been conducted (cf. notably works of J.P.Grégoire and [Boi03]), it seems that this reorthogonalisation is not always effective. Its extra cost, due mainly to the new matrix–vector products $K{d}^{k}$ (and to their storage) is not always compensated by the gain in the number of global iterations.

#### 3.2.3 Convergence

Because of the particular structure of the approximation space (3.2-3) and of the minimisation property on this space of the approximate solution ${u}^{m}$ (cf. (3.2-5)), one obtains an estimate of the convergence speed of the CG

$\begin{array}{}{\parallel e({u}^{i})\parallel }_{K}^{2}={({\omega}^{i})}^{2}{\parallel e({u}^{0})\parallel }_{K}^{2}\\ \text{avec}{\omega}^{i}:=\underset{1\le i\le N}{\max}(1-{\lambda}_{i}{P}_{m-1}({\lambda}_{i}))\end{array}$                         (3.2-8)

where one denotes by $({\lambda}_{i};{v}_{i})$ the eigenmodes of the matrix $K$ and by ${P}_{m-1}$ an arbitrary polynomial of degree at most $m-1$. The famous Chebyshev polynomials, *via*  their good majoration properties in the space of polynomials, make it possible to improve the readability of this attenuation factor ${\omega}^{i}$ *.*  At the end of $i$ iterations, at worst, the decrease is then expressed in the form

${\parallel e({u}^{i})\parallel }_{K}\le 2{(\frac{\sqrt{\eta (K)}-1}{\sqrt{\eta (K)}+1})}^{i}{\parallel e({u}^{0})\parallel }_{K}$                         (3.2-9)

It ensures the superlinear convergence (that is $\underset{i\to \infty }{\lim}\frac{J({u}^{i+1})-J(u)}{J({u}^{i})-J(u)}=0$) of the process in a number of iterations proportional to the square root of the conditioning of the operator. Thus, in order to obtain

$\frac{{\parallel e({u}^{i})\parallel }_{K}}{{\parallel e({u}^{0})\parallel }_{K}}\le \varepsilon (\text{petit})$                             (3.2-10)

one needs a number of iterations of the order of

$i\approx \frac{\sqrt{\eta (K)}}{2}\ln\frac{2}{\varepsilon}$                                 (3.2-11)

Obviously, as we have remarked many times, an ill-conditioned problem will slow down the convergence of the process. But this slowing-down will be less noticeable for the CG than for the Steepest Descent (cf. figures 3.2-1). And in any case, the global convergence of the former will be better.

#### 3.2.4 Complexity and memory occupation

As for the Steepest Descent, the major part of the computational cost of this algorithm resides in step (1), the matrix–vector product. Its complexity is of the order of $\Theta (\text{kcN})$ where $c$ is the average number of non-zero terms per row of $K$ and $k$ the number of iterations required for convergence. To be much more efficient than a simple Cholesky (of complexity $\Theta (\frac{{N}^{3}}{3})$) one must therefore:

- Take well into account the sparse character of the matrices issuing from finite-element discretisations (MORSE storage, matrix–vector product optimised *ad hoc*): $c\text{<<}N$.
- Precondition the working operator: $k\text{<<}N$.

One has already remarked that for an SPD operator its theoretical convergence occurs in, at most, $N$ iterations and proportionally to the square root of the conditioning (cf. (3.2-11)). In practice, for large ill-conditioned systems (as is often the case in structural mechanics), it can be very slow to manifest itself (cf. Lanczos phenomenon of the following paragraph).

Taking into account the operator conditionings generally observed in structural mechanics and recalled for the complexity study of the Steepest Descent, whose notations one takes up again, the computational complexity of the CG is written $\Theta ({\text{cN}}^{\frac{1}{d}+1})$ (resp. $\Theta ({\text{cN}}^{\frac{2}{d}+1})$).

As regards memory occupation, as for the Steepest Descent, only the storage of the working matrix is possibly required: $\vartheta (\text{cN})$. In practice, the computer implementation of sparse storage imposes the management of additional integer vectors: for example for the MORSE storage used in *Code_Aster*, vectors of the end-of-row indices and of the column indices of the elements of the profile. Whence an effective memory complexity of $\Theta (\text{cN})$ reals and $\vartheta (\text{cN}+N)$ integers.

**Note:**

- *These considerations on the memory footprint do not take into account the storage problems of a possible preconditioner and of the working space that its construction may temporarily mobilise.*

### 3.3 Complements

#### 3.3.1 Equivalence with the Lanczos method

By “working over” the orthogonality property of the residual with any vector of the Krylov space (cf. (3.2-2)), one shows that the $m$ iterations of the CG lead to the construction of a Lanczos factorisation of the same order (cf. [Boi01] §5.2)

${\text{KQ}}^{m}={\mathrm{Q}}^{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{m}}-\underset{{\mathrm{R}}^{\mathrm{m}}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{\sqrt{{\beta}^{m}}}{{\alpha}^{m-1}}{\mathrm{q}}^{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{e}}_{\mathrm{m}}^{\mathrm{T}}}}$                             (3.3-1)

denoting:

- ${R}^{m}$ the specific “residual” of this factorisation,
- ${e}_{m}$ the *m* th vector of the canonical basis,
- ${q}^{i}:=\frac{{r}^{i}}{\parallel {r}^{i}\parallel }$ the residual vectors normalised to unity, called for the occasion Lanczos vectors,

#### 3.3.2 Nested solvers

As has already been remarked, linear solvers are often buried deep inside other algorithms, and in particular, non-linear solvers of Newton type. For *Code_Aster*  these are the most frequently used application cases: operators STAT_NON_LINE, DYNA_NON_LINE and THER_NON_LINE.

In such a configuration, a linear solver such as the CG can play its trump card (with respect to a direct solver). Its iterative character proves useful for making the stopping test of the algorithm evolve (cf. step (5) algorithm 4) as a function of the relative error of the non-linear solver which encapsulates it: this is the nested-solvers issue (one also speaks of relaxation) [CM01][GY97][BFG] of which CERFACS is the “spearhead” in France.

By relaxing at the opportune moment the precision required on the cancellation of the residual (for example a strategy in the case of a modal method of Krylov type + CG) or, on the contrary by hardening it (resp. modal method of power type + Gauss–Seidel + CG), one can thus gain in computational complexity on the CG without modifying the convergence of the global process. One must of course devise simple and inexpensive criteria so that the gain is substantial. This functionality could be inserted into *Code_Aster*  (internal and automatic piloting of the value RESI_RELA).

**Note:**

- Certain authors have also asked themselves the question of the order of chaining of these algorithms: “Newton–CG” or “CG–Newton”? If from a computer-science and conceptual point of view, the question is quickly settled: one prefers the first solution, more readable, more flexible and which does not disturb the existing, from a numerical and algorithmic point of view, the share is more nuanced. It can depend on the “technical innards” deployed by the non-linear solver (tangent matrix, one-dimensional minimisation…) and by the CG deployed (preconditioner). Nevertheless, it seems that the natural order, “Newton–CG”, is the most efficient and the most evolutive.

#### 3.3.3 Parallelism

The CG, like many iterative solvers, lends itself well to parallelism. It is fairly scalable [23]_

Concerning the preconditioners, one therefore has two criteria which tend to contradict each other: parallel efficiency and algorithmic efficiency.

The codes therefore tend to favour preconditioners that are not very efficient from an algorithmic point of view [25]_

Another difficulty of parallelism also comes from the distribution of the data. One must see to it that the different rules which govern the data setup of the analysis (attribution of the boundary-condition zones, assignment of the materials, choice of the finite elements…) are not affected by the fact that each processor knows them only partially. This is what one calls the “consistency” of the distributed data [Boi08b].

Likewise, one must provide in the code the communications *ad hoc*  during each global manipulation of data (stopping criteria, post-processings…).

## 4 The Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient (GCPC)

### 4.1 General description

#### 4.1.1 Principle

As one has been able to observe (and to hammer home!) in the previous paragraphs, the speed of convergence of the descent methods, and notably that of the conjugate gradient, depends on the conditioning of the matrix $\eta (K)$. The closer it is to its floor value, 1, the better the convergence.

The principle of preconditioning is then “simple”; it consists in replacing the linear system of the problem (${P}_{1}$) by an equivalent system of the type (left preconditioning):

$({\tilde{P}}_{1})\underset{\tilde{K}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{M}^{-1}K}}u=\underset{\tilde{f}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{M}^{-1}f}}$                                 (4.1-1)

such that, ideally:

- The conditioning of it is obviously improved (this theoretical property, like the following one, are only very rarely demonstrated. They are often warranted only by numerical experiments): $\eta (\tilde{K})\ll \eta (K)$ *.*
- Just like the spectral distribution: eigenvalues more tightly packed.
- ${M}^{-1}$ is inexpensive to evaluate (as for the initial operator, one often only needs to know the action of the preconditioner on a vector): $\text{Mv}=u$ easy to invert.
- Or even easy to implement and, possibly, efficient to parallelise.
- That it be as sparse as the initial matrix because it is a matter of limiting the additional memory footprint.
- That it conserve for the working matrix $\tilde{K}$ the same properties as the initial one: here, the SPD character.

In theory, the best choice would be ${M}^{-1}={K}^{-1}$ because then $\eta (\tilde{K}={I}_{N})=1$, but if one has to invert the operator completely by a direct method in order to construct this preconditioner, it is of little practical interest! Although, one will see later that this idea is not as far-fetched as that.

Put otherwise, the objective of a preconditioner is to pack the spectrum of the working operator; thus, as has already been mentioned, its “effective conditioning” will be improved together with the convergence of the GCPC.

Graphically, this translates into a more spherical shape of the graph of the quadratic form. Even on a system with $N=2$ dimensions and with a “crude” preconditioner (cf. figure 4.1-1), the effects are noticeable.

![Figure](/figures/r6.01.02/100000000000010200000109ADFD92FE0252D7F9.png)

![Figure](/figures/r6.01.02/10000000000000C7000000F7A1B5AB00B9A1A9CD.png)

*Figure 4.1-1. Effect of diagonal preconditioning on the paraboloid of example no. 1: (a) without,* $\eta (K)=3.5$ *, (b) with,* $\eta (\tilde{K})=2.8$ *.*

In the absolute, one can precondition a linear system on the left (‘left preconditioning’), on the right (resp. ‘right’) or by making a mixture of the two (resp. ‘split’). It is this last version which will be retained for our SPD operator, because one cannot directly apply the CG to solve $({\tilde{P}}_{1})$: even if ${M}^{-1}$ and $K$ are SPD, this is not necessarily the case of their product.

The trick then consists in using an SPD preconditioning matrix, $M$, for which one will therefore be able to define another matrix ($M$  being real symmetric, it is diagonalisable in the form $M={\text{UDU}}^{T}$ with $D:=\text{diag}({\lambda}_{i}){\lambda}_{i}>0$ and $U$ an orthogonal matrix). The sought SPD matrix then comes from the associated decomposition ${M}^{1/2}=\text{Udiag}(\sqrt{{\lambda}_{i}}){U}^{T}$ with ${M}^{1/2}$ defined such that ${({M}^{1/2})}^{2}=M$. Whence the new working problem, this time SPD

$({\hat{P}}_{1})\underset{\hat{\mathrm{K}}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\mathrm{M}}^{-1/2}{\text{KM}}^{-1/2}}}\underset{\hat{\mathrm{u}}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\mathrm{M}}^{1/2}\mathrm{u}}}=\underset{\hat{\mathrm{f}}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\mathrm{M}}^{-1/2}\mathrm{f}}}$                         (4.1-2)

on which one will be able to apply the standard CG algorithm in order to constitute what one calls a Preconditioned Conjugate Gradient (GCPC).

#### 4.1.2 Algorithm

In short, by substituting into algorithm 4 the expressions of the previous problem $({\hat{P}}_{1})$ and by working a little at the simplification of the whole so as to manipulate only expressions in $K$, $u$ and $f$, there comes the following unfolding.

$\begin{array}{}\text{Initialisation}{u}^{0}\text{donné,}{r}^{0}={\text{f-Ku}}^{0},{d}^{0}={M}^{-1}{r}^{0},{g}^{0}={d}^{0}\\ \text{Boucle en}i\\ (1){z}^{i}={\text{Kd}}^{i}\\ (2){\alpha}^{i}=\frac{\langle {r}^{i},{g}^{i}\rangle }{\langle {d}^{i},{z}^{i}\rangle }(\text{paramètre optimal de descente})\\ (3){u}^{i+1}={u}^{i}+{\alpha}^{i}{d}^{i}(\text{nouvel itéré})\\ (4){r}^{i+1}={r}^{i}-{\alpha}^{i}{z}^{i}(\text{nouveau résidu})\\ (5)\text{Test d'arrêt via}\langle {r}^{i+1},{r}^{i+1}\rangle (\text{par exemple})\\ (6){g}^{i+1}={M}^{-1}{r}^{i+1}(r\text{ésidu préconditionné})\\ (7){\beta}^{i+1}=\frac{\langle {r}^{i+1},{g}^{i+1}\rangle }{\langle {r}^{i},{g}^{i}\rangle }(\text{paramètre de conjugaison optimal})\\ (8){d}^{i+1}={g}^{i+1}+{\beta}^{i+1}{d}^{i}(\text{nouvelle dd})\end{array}$

*Algorithm 5: Preconditioned conjugate gradient (GCPC).*

But in fact, the symmetric character of the initial preconditioned problem $({\tilde{P}}_{1})$ is quite relative. It is inseparable from the underlying scalar product. If instead of taking the usual Euclidean scalar product, one uses a matrix scalar product defined with respect to $K$, $M$ or ${M}^{-1}$, it is possible to symmetrise the preconditioned problem which was not so initially. As for Krylov methods in modal analysis, it is the pair (working operator, scalar product) that one must modulate in order to adapt to the problem!

Thus, ${M}^{-1}K$ being symmetric with respect to the $M$-scalar product, one can substitute this new working operator and this new scalar product into the algorithm of the non-preconditioned CG (algorithm 4)

$\begin{array}{}K\Leftarrow {M}^{-1}K\\ \langle ,\rangle \Leftarrow {\langle ,\rangle }_{M}\end{array}$

And (Oh surprise!) by working a little on the expressions, one recovers exactly the previous GCPC algorithm (algorithm 5). One proceeds likewise with a right preconditioning, $K{M}^{-1}$, via an ${M}^{-1}$-scalar product. Therefore, right preconditioning, left preconditioning or “split in the SPD manner”, all lead rigorously to the same algorithm. This observation will be useful to us later when one realises that the matrices supplied by *Code_Aster*  (and also the preconditioners that one will associate with them) are not always in conformity with the ideal scenario $({\hat{P}}_{1})$.

**Notes:**

- *This variant of the GCPC, which is by far the most widespread, is sometimes called in the literature: untransformed preconditioned conjugate gradient (‘untransformed preconditioned conjugate gradient’). As opposed to the transformed version which manipulates the entities proper to the new formulation.*
- The general descent methods and, particularly the non-linear CGs, are also preconditioned (cf. [§2.3]). This time with a preconditioner approximating the inverse of the Hessian at the point considered, so as to make spherical the graph of the functional in the neighbourhood of this point.

#### 4.1.3 “Survey” of the principal preconditioners

The solution often retained for its simplicity of implementation, its “numerical efficiency/extra computational cost” ratio for problems that are not too ill-conditioned, consists in preconditioning by the diagonal of the initial operator

${M}_{J}:=\text{diag}({K}_{ii})$                                 (4.1-3)

This is what one calls diagonal or Jacobi preconditioning (JCG for ‘Jacobi Conjugate Gradient’) by reference to the stationary method of the same name.

**Notes:**

- *It is a solution often retained in CFD (for EDF R&D: Code_Saturne, TELEMAC). In these fields, great attention paid to the non-linear solution scheme and to the construction of the mesh produces a problem that is often better conditioned. This is what made the reputation of the JCG, transformed for the occasion into a veritable “racing beast” dedicated to large parallel computers.*
- *It is a solution proposed, in structural mechanics, by a good number of commercial codes: ANSYS, Zébulon, NASTRAN. It was present in Code_Aster but its lack of reliability led to its removal.*
- *This principle of a “poor man’s” preconditioner extends to the descent methods by taking this time the diagonal of the Hessian.*

Another very widespread preconditioner is the SSOR (for Symmetric Successive Over Relaxation). Like the previous one, it is deduced from a classical iterative method: the relaxed Gauss–Seidel method. By decomposing the initial operator in the usual form $K:=D+L+{L}^{T}$ where $D$ is its diagonal and $L$ its strictly lower triangular part, it is written (as a function of the relaxation parameter $\omega$)

${M}_{\text{SSOR}}(\omega ):=\frac{1}{\omega (\omega -2)}(D+\mathrm{\omega L}){D}^{-1}(D+{\mathrm{\omega L}}^{T})0<\omega <2$                 (4.1-4)

It presents the advantage of not requiring memory storage and extra computational cost, since it is elaborated directly from $K$, while being very simple to invert (a forward–backward substitution). On model problems (the famous “Laplacian on the unit square”) theoretical results have been unearthed in finite elements

$\eta (K):=\Theta (\frac{1}{{h}^{2}})\Rightarrow \eta ({M}^{-1}K):=\Theta (\frac{1}{h})$                             (4.1-5)

On the other hand, it has the faculty, for an SPD operator, of confining its spectrum in the band $]0,1]$. However, it has proved in industrial practice less efficient than the incomplete-Cholesky-type preconditioners which we are going to tackle in the following paragraph. And it poses the delicate problem of the choice of the optimal relaxation parameter, by nature very “problem-dependent”.

**Notes:**

- *This preconditioner was proposed by D.J.Evans (1967) and studied by O.Axelsson (1974). It comes in many versions: non-symmetric, by blocks, with optimal relaxation parameter…*
- *By setting* $\omega =1$ *one recovers the particular case of Symmetric Gauss–Seidel*

${M}_{\text{SGS}}(\omega ):=-(D+L){D}^{-1}(D+{L}^{T})$                         (4.1-6)

A host of other preconditioners has thus seen the light of day over some thirty years in the literature: explicit (polynomial [BH89], SPAI, AINV…), implicit (Schwarz, IC…), multilevel (domain decomposition, multigrids…) …. Some are specific to an application, others more general. “Fashion effects” have also done their work! For more information one may consult the monumental sum committed by G.Meurant [Meu99] or the books of Y.Saad [Saa03] and H.A.Van der Vorst [Van01].

### 4.2 Incomplete Cholesky factorisation

#### 4.2.1 Principle

One has just seen that the preconditioners are often inspired by linear solvers in their own right: Jacobi for the diagonal one, Gauss–Seidel for SSOR. The one based on an Incomplete Cholesky factorisation (IC) is no exception to the rule! But it relies this time, not on another iterative method, but on a direct method of Cholesky type. Whence the denomination of ICCG (‘Incomplete Cholesky Conjugate Gradient’) given to the coupling of this preconditioner with the GCPC.

The initial operator being SPD, it admits a Cholesky decomposition of the type $K={\mathrm{CC}}^{T}$ where $C$ is a lower triangular matrix. One calls incomplete Cholesky factorisation the search for a lower triangular matrix $F$ as sparse as possible and such that ${\mathrm{FF}}^{T}$ is close to $K$ in a sense to be defined. For example, by setting $B=K-{\mathrm{FF}}^{T}$ one will ask that the relative error (expressed in a matrix norm of one’s choice)

$\Delta :=\frac{\parallel B\parallel }{\parallel K\parallel }$                                             (4.2-1)

be as small as possible. On reading this “rather evasive” definition one glimpses the profusion of possible scenarios. Each one has come up with his own incomplete factorisation! The work of G.Meurant [Meu99] shows their great diversity: $\mathrm{IC}(n)$, $\mathrm{MIC}(n)$, relaxed, reordered, by blocks....

However, to simplify the task, one often imposes *a priori*  the sparse structure of $F$, that is its graph

$\Theta (F):=\left\lbrace (i,j),1\le j\le i-1,1\le i\le N,{F}_{ij}\ne 0\right\rbrace$                 (4.2-2)

It is obviously a matter of finding a compromise: the more extended this graph is, the smaller the error (4.2-1) will be but the more the computation and the storage of what is (in the case which interests us) only a preconditioner will be costly. Generally, the preconditioners are recursive and in their base level, they impose on $F$ the same structure as that of $C$: $\Theta (F)=\Theta (C)$.

**Notes:**

- *Initially, these incomplete factorisations were developed in order to solve iteratively a linear system of type* (${P}_{1}$)

${\text{FF}}^{T}{u}^{i+1}=f-{\text{Bu}}^{i}$                                 (4.2-3)

> *The “sinews of war” then being the spectral radius* $\rho \left[{({\text{FF}}^{T})}^{-1}B\right]$ *which a judicious choice of* $\Theta (F)$ *can contribute notably to bringing down.*

- *This principle of incomplete factorisation generalises without difficulty to the standard case where the operator is written* $K=\mathrm{LU}$ *with this time* $B=K-\mathrm{LU}$ *. One then speaks of Incomplete factorisation of LU type (ILU for ‘Incomplete LU’).*

#### 4.2.2 Strategy retained in Code_Aster

It is a preconditioner of ILU type (because we shall see in the following paragraph that the working matrices of *Code_Aster*  often lose their positive-definiteness) inspired by the works of H. Van der Vorst [Van01]. The matrices remaining nevertheless symmetric, one can write $K={\mathrm{LDL}}^{T}$ and $B=K-{\mathrm{LDL}}^{T}$ *.*

**Note:**

- *The matrix no longer being SPD but simply symmetric regular, one is not, a priori, assured of the existence of a factorisation* ${\mathrm{LDL}}^{T}$ *without having recourse to permutations of rows and columns (* $\mathrm{PK}={\mathrm{LDL}}^{T}$ *with* $P$ *a permutation matrix). A scenario which has not been provided for in the native linear solvers of Code_Aster (‘LDLT’, ‘MULT_FRONT’, ‘GCPC’) and would be difficult to implement efficiently given the MORSE storage of the matrices. Fortunately, one will see that a happy combination of circumstances makes it possible to unblock the situation (cf. §5.1)!*

Strictly speaking one should speak of incomplete factorisation of ILDLT type but in the literature and in the documentations of the codes, one already amalgamates ILU and IC, or even their variants, so there is no point in enriching the list of acronyms!

This incomplete factorisation, in the direct line of the previous theoretical reminders, rests on two observations which we are now going to substantiate:

- the notion of fill-in by levels,
- the weak magnitude of the terms resulting from this fill-in.

#### 4.2.3 Fill-in by levels

The construction of the factorisation is carried out, row by row, via the usual formula

${L}_{ij}=\frac{1}{{D}_{j}}({K}_{ij}-\sum_{k=1}^{j-1}{L}_{\text{ik}}{D}_{k}{L}_{\text{jk}})$                             (4.2-4)

Whence the phenomenon of progressive filling of the profile (‘fill-in’ in English): initially the matrix $L$ has the same fill as the matrix $K$, but in the course of the process, to a zero term of ${K}_{ij}$ there may correspond a non-zero term of ${L}_{ij}$. It suffices that there exist a column $k$ ($<j$) comprising a non-zero term for rows $i$ and $j$ (cf. figure 4.2-1).

![Figure](/figures/r6.01.02/Shape6.gif)

*Figure 4.2-1. Fill-in phenomenon during the factorisation.*

These non-zero terms being moreover able themselves to correspond to earlier fill-ins, whence a notion of level of recursivity which can be interpreted as so many “levels” of fill-in. One will thus speak of an incomplete factorisation of level 0 (stored in $L(0)$) if it reproduces identically the structure (but of course not the values, which are different) of the strict lower diagonal part of $K$ (i.e. the same graph). The factorisation of level 1 (resp. $L(1)$) will itself be able to include the fill-in induced by non-zero terms of $K$ (terms denoted ${r}^{1}$ in figure 4.2-2), that of level 2 (resp. $L(2)$) will be able to mix in the previous new non-zero terms (${r}^{1}$) in order to constitute possible new terms (denoted ${r}^{2}$), and so on recursively…

This is illustrated on the textbook case of a sparse pentadiagonal matrix resulting from the finite-difference discretisation of the Laplacian on a uniform 2D grid (cf. figure 4.2-b). One represents only the structure: $d$, spectator diagonal terms, * , initially non-zero terms, ${r}^{i}$, terms filled at step no. *i*.

The incomplete factorisation $L(3)$ leads here to a complete fill-in; the relative error will then be zero $\Delta =0$ and the GCPC will converge in one iteration. The interest of the exercise is of course purely didactic!

#### 4.2.4 Weak magnitude of the terms resulting from the fill-in

On the other hand, one empirically remarks a very interesting property of these new terms issuing from the fill-in: their absolute value decreases as one moves away from the zones already filled by the non-zero terms of $K$. That is, in the previous case, from the main sub-diagonal and from the outer one. To convince oneself of this it suffices to visualise the following function (cf. figure 4.2-3)

$y(k):={\log}_{10}(\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N-k}{L}_{{}_{k+i,i}}^{2}}{N-k}})$                                 (4.2-5)

which represents the orders of magnitude of the terms of the $k$ th sub-diagonal. For example $y(0)$ corresponds to the main diagonal.

![Figure](/figures/r6.01.02/Shape7.gif)

If one recapitulates, one therefore has:

- a recursive and parameterisable level of fill-in,
- a lesser importance of the terms corresponding to high fill-in levels.

Whence a certain “blank cheque” left to the incomplete factorisations $\mathrm{ILU}(p)$ neglecting these median terms.

This “cheap” approximation of ${K}^{-1}$ will then serve as preconditioner in algorithm 5 of the GCPC (${M}^{-1}={K}^{-1}$). In order to conserve a certain interest in the thing (otherwise one might as well solve the problem directly!), one limits oneself to the first fill-in levels: $p=0,1,2$ or even $3$. Everything depends on the number of linear systems that one will have to solve with the same matrix.

**Notes:**

- There exist few theoretical results on this type of preconditioner. Which does not prevent it from often proving effective [Greg].
- *It is a solution proposed by a good number of large codes in structural mechanics (ANSYS, Zébulon, NASTRAN…) and by all the linear-algebra libraries (PETSc, TRILINOS, HYPRE…). On the other hand, this preconditioning phase is not always parallelised. Only the Krylov solver (CG, GMRES…) is. One then finds again the problems of ‘parallel preconditioning’ already mentioned.*

#### 4.2.5 Complexity and memory occupation

As regards the additional computational cost with an incomplete factorisation, it is very difficult to estimate and, in practice, depends greatly on the manner in which it has been coded (to our knowledge, there exist no theoretical results on the matter). With a low fill-in level, one only hopes that it is very much less than the $\Theta (\frac{{N}^{3}}{3})$ of a complete factorisation.

For a compromise is to be found between memory occupation and complexity. To constitute these incomplete factorisations, one often has to allocate temporary working spaces. Thus, in the implementation of the $\mathrm{ILU}(p)$ preconditioning of *Code_Aster*, it has been chosen to facilitate the algorithmics (sorting, search for indices and coefficients in the profile, management of the recursivity and of the fill-in…) by provisionally creating matrices representing the full storage of the initial matrix and of its fill-in.

To this temporary working space is of course added the additional storage due to the final preconditioner. At level 0, it is theoretically at least of the same order as that of the matrix. Whence a minimum effective memory complexity of the GCPC of $\vartheta (2\text{cN})$ reals and $\Theta (2\text{cN}+\mathrm{2N})$ integers. When one goes up in completeness, only practice can bring a semblance of an answer.

In summary, one shows empirically with *Code_Aster* that one must foresee a total memory footprint of $\Theta (\alpha \text{cN})$ reals and $\Theta (\alpha \text{cN}+\mathrm{2N})$ integers with

- $\alpha =2,5$ in $\mathrm{ILU}(0)$ (default level in *Code_Aster*),
- $\alpha =4,5$ in $\mathrm{ILU}(1)$,
- $\alpha =8,5$ in $\mathrm{ILU}(2)$.

For more details on the computer implementation of the GCPC in *Code_Aster*  and its use on quasi-industrial test cases, one may consult the notes [Greg] or [Boi03].

## 5 The “native” GCPC of Code_Aster

Two types of conjugate gradient can be used:

1. The “historical” conjugate gradient [Greg] of the code (keyword SOLVEUR/METHODE=’GCPC’), completely integrated into its sources and adhering to its memory-management software package.
2. That of PETSC [PET] (keyword SOLVEUR/METHODE=’PETSC’+ALGORITHME=’CG’) called as an external library.

This chapter tackles the implementation difficulties in *Code_Aster* of the first. The following chapter deals more specifically with PETSC.

### 5.1 Particular difficulties

#### 5.1.1 Taking boundary conditions into account

In *Code_Aster*, there are two ways of taking the boundary conditions into account and this step is carried out during the effective construction of the stiffness matrix:

- By double dualisation [Pel01] (operators AFFE_CHAR_ACOU/MECA/THER) using specific degrees of freedom, called Lagrange, which encompass the groups of unknowns concerned and make it possible to verify all types of linear boundary conditions (generalised Dirichlet)
- $\mathrm{T}\mathrm{u}=0$                                         (5.1-1)

with $T$ a real matrix of size $p\times n$. This technique fleshes out the stiffness matrix into a new matrix, called “dualised”, which becomes the new working matrix

- $\tilde{\mathrm{K}}=(\begin{array}{ccc}\mathrm{K}& \beta {\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{T}}& \beta {\mathrm{T}}^{\mathrm{T}}\\ \beta \mathrm{T}& -\alpha \mathrm{Id}& \alpha \mathrm{Id}\\ \beta \mathrm{T}& \alpha \mathrm{Id}& -\alpha \mathrm{Id}\end{array})$                             (5.1-2)

where $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are two strictly positive reals.

- By simple elimination of the unknowns (operators AFFE_CHAR_CINE) by substituting and setting to zero the $p$ rows and columns concerned of the stiffness matrix. This is valid only for blockings of degrees of freedom; one therefore cannot take into account a linear relation. The stiffness matrix is then written

> $\tilde{\mathrm{K}}=(\begin{array}{cc}\bar{\mathrm{K}}& 0\\ 0& \mathrm{Id}\end{array})$                             (5.1-3)

denoting by $\bar{\mathrm{K}}$ its unchanged part.

Each of the two approaches has its advantages and its drawbacks: genericity, modularity but increase in the size of the problem, degradation of its conditioning and loss of its positive-definiteness for the first. Unlike the second which decreases its size but which is circumscribed to certain types of boundary conditions and is, from the computer-science point of view, more delicate to implement.

**Note:**

1. *Other approaches were conceivable: simple dualisation, taking the boundary conditions into account in the variational formulation, projected conjugate gradient…*

#### 5.1.2 Consequence for the GCPC

With AFFE_CHAR_CINE, the working operator $\tilde{\mathrm{K}}$ remaining SPD, all the theory recalled in the previous paragraphs applies. On the other hand, with AFFE_CHAR_ACOU/MECA/THER (the most frequent case in practice), this is no longer the case, because it becomes simply symmetric and loses its positive-definite character. The consequences are then of three orders:

- The global convergence of the GCPC (cf. (3.2-9)) is no longer guaranteed,
- When it occurs, it is slowed down (cf. (3.2-11)) by a degraded conditioning, $\eta (\mathrm{K})\ll \eta (\tilde{\mathrm{K}})$
- The preconditioning can no longer be carried out *via* an $\mathrm{IC}(p)$, but rather by an $\mathrm{ILU}(p)$ (cf. §4.2). It is still necessary that the factorisation ${\mathrm{LDL}}^{T}$ always be possible without having to permute row or column!

Fortunately, an adequate arrangement of the Lagrange multipliers with respect to the groups of degrees of freedom which they concern (they must encompass these degrees of freedom cf. [Pel01] §4), makes it possible to obtain without a hitch this incomplete factorisation (phew!).

**Notes:**

- In an EDF report B.Nitrosso [Ni92] shows that diagonal preconditioning is not conceivable *(cf. §4.1), because it leads to the cancellation of the scalar product in the denominator of step (7) of algorithm 5: computation of the optimal conjugation parameter. Therefore, unlike Code_Saturne, Code_Aster cannot propose this unreliable option.*
- It remains nonetheless that, with dualised boundary conditions, the conditioning of the working operator is degraded and therefore the convergence of the GCPC is slowed down. Other commercial codes, like ANSYS [PLM], have already made this observation.

#### 5.1.3 Memory footprint

Taking into account the elements of §4.2, one has empirically noted an effective memory complexity of the GCPC with the Incomplete Cholesky type preconditioner ('LDLT_INC'), in $\alpha$ times the size of $K$ with:

- $\alpha =2,5$ in $\mathrm{ILU}(0)$ (default level in *Code_Aster*),
- $\alpha =4,5$ in $\mathrm{ILU}(1)$,
- $\alpha =8,5$ in $\mathrm{ILU}(2)$.

With the preconditioner using a single-precision factorisation issuing from MUMPS ('LDLT_SP'), the memory consumption is larger (at least $\alpha >10$). But in non-linear analysis, this factorisation can be kept during several system solutions (keyword REAC_PRECOND). The extra computational cost due to its construction is therefore finally smaller.

**Notes:**

- *These figures are to be compared with the size of the complete factorisation which is of the order typically of* $\alpha =30$ *to* $100$ *(this depends notably on the renumberer used in pre-processing). The disproportion of these figures illustrates well the principal competitive advantage of the GCPC with respect to the direct solvers: the memory occupation required.*
- *However, unlike the direct solvers of* *Code_Aster, the GCPC has not been able to benefit from a memory paging. The whole matrix must be contained in RAM because it is used a great number of times* *via the matrix–vector product. Whereas the direct methods manipulate much larger objects but these are unloaded automatically and partially onto disk (software package* *JEVEUX and/or Out-Of-Core faculties of MUMPS).*

#### 5.1.4 Parallelism

Unlike other solvers of the code (MULT_FRONT, MUMPS, PETSC) and for problems of ‘parallel preconditioning’, the native GCPC is usable only in sequential mode (cf. §3.3).

For example, it does not benefit from the distribution of the tasks and of the data which the MPI parallelism set up around the direct solver MUMPS procures. Thus, a memory overhead of $\alpha =30$ with sequential In-Core MUMPS can very quickly come down to the level of a GCPC preconditioned by *ILU(0)* as soon as one uses a dozen processors and/or activates its Out-Of-Core faculties.

### 5.2 Scope of use

All the operators generating real symmetric systems except those requiring obligatorily a singularity detection (modal analysis, stability and buckling ...). In non-linear analysis, if the problem is real non-symmetric, one can use this solver provided that one forces the symmetrisation (cf. §5.3).

List of the *Code_Aster* operators that can use GCPC:

- CALC_FORC_AJOU,
- CALC_MATR_AJOU,
- CALC_PRECONT $\to$ Possible symmetrised approximation (cf. §5.3),
- DYNA_VIBRA,
- DYNA_NON_LINE $\to$ Possible symmetrised approximation,
- MACRO_MATR_AJOU,
- ASSEMBLAGE,
- MECA_STATIQUE,
- NUME_DDL/FACTORISER/RESOUDRE,
- MODE_STATIQUE,
- STAT_NON_LINE $\to$ Possible symmetrised approximation,
- THER_LINEAIRE,
- THER_NON_LINE $\to$ Possible symmetrised approximation,
- THER_NON_LINE_MO $\to$ Possible symmetrised approximation.

### 5.3 Symmetric character of the working operator

If the matrix is not symmetric two cases present themselves. Either the linear solver is inserted into a non-linear process (operators mentioned cf. list §5.2), or the latter is linear (the other operators).

In the first case, one transforms the initial problem $\text{Ku}=f$ into a new symmetrised problem $\frac{1}{2}(K+{K}^{T})u=f$. One supposes thereby that the encompassing non-linear solver (Newton algorithm) will compensate the approximation of the initial operator by $\tilde{K}:=\frac{1}{2}(K+{K}^{T})$. This is moreover not the only approximation of this non-linear process… the choice of the tangent matrix is, for example, another.

This symmetrised approximation does not harm the robustness and the consistency of the whole and avoids a more expensive non-symmetric solution. This operation is carried out by activating the keyword SYME=‘OUI’ (default ‘NON’) of the factor keyword SOLVEUR and it is legitimate for all the non-linear solvers of the code.

When the problem is purely linear, one cannot expect any compensation from any encompassing process and this approximation becomes impossible. Recourse to the native GCPC is illegitimate and one must solve this non-symmetric system *via*  the other linear solvers. The nature of the working operator is detected automatically; there is no need to notify it *via*  the keyword SYME.

### 5.4 Parameter setting and printout

To activate this functionality, one must initialise the keyword METHODE to ‘GCPC’ in the factor keyword SOLVEUR [U4.50.01]. Furthermore, two types of preconditioners are available (keyword PRE_COND):

- PRE_COND='LDLT_INC': A classical Incomplete Cholesky (cf. §4.2) whose fill-in level is piloted by the keyword NIVE_REMPLISSAGE=k (k=0 does not mean “without preconditioning” but preconditioning of ILU(0) type).
- PRE_COND='LDLT_SP': A single-precision factorisation carried out by the external direct solver MUMPS [R6.02.03]. This solution is, *a priori*, more costly than the first in terms of memory/time but the latter is shareable, in non-linear analysis, between different solutions of linear systems (keyword REAC_ITER). On the other hand, the robustness and the quality of this preconditioner can greatly accelerate the convergence of the GCPC.

**Notes:**

- To minimise the size of the profile of the working matrix and of its preconditioner (with the Incomplete Cholesky), a renumbering algorithm is available by default: ‘RCMK’ for ‘Reverse Cuthill Mac-Kee’ (cf. [LT98] §5.4.2). It can nevertheless be deactivated.
- *The principal “lever arm” for modifying the “CPU time/memory occupation” compromise of the GCPC remains the preconditioner and its different numerical parameters.*

Unlike the direct methods, one has had to fix a maximum number of iterations discriminating the converged iterative computations from the non-converged ones. This threshold (parameterisable) is arbitrarily fixed at half the degrees of freedom of the working problem $\hat{K}$. If after *i* =NMAX_ITER steps, the relative residual ${\delta}^{i}:=\frac{\parallel {r}^{i}\parallel }{\parallel f\parallel }$ is not less than RESI_RELA, the analysis stops in ERREUR_FATALE.

### 5.5 Advice on use

Overall, the best robustness/memory-footprint/CPU-cost compromise seems to go [Anf03][Boi03] to the two direct solvers: the multifrontal solver and MUMPS. Especially when one treats problems of the “multiple right-hand sides” type (e.g. operator DYNA/STAT/THER_NON_LINE without updating of the tangent matrix).

However, for problems that are rather well conditioned (thermal, simple geometry with a mesh and relatively homogeneous material characteristics…) or very greedy in memory (several million degrees of freedom), the GCPC can prove an interesting alternative. Especially with the LDLT_SP preconditioner, which is more costly but also more reliable..

Because of its natural modularity and of the simplicity of its constituents (matrix–vector product and scalar product), the GCPC remains much simpler to maintain and to make evolve than the other direct solvers. It is the “all-purpose” solver easy to implement (at least in its basic version) and very pedagogical. It is often plugged into more general processes (nested solvers, interface solvers of domain decomposition…) or adapted, case by case, for particular matrix structures.

## 6 The iterative Krylov solvers via PETSc

### 6.1 The PETSc library

The PETSc library [PET] (for ‘Portable Extensive Toolkit for Scientific Computation’) of the Argonne laboratory (DOE and NSF) proposes, besides a great variety of solvers (direct, iterative, modal, ODE…), utilities for the manipulation of distributed data and for the profiling of parallel computations. It is a public-domain product, interfaced with different languages (F77/90, C/C++, Python), parallelised in MPI and which has reached a certain maturity [27]_
.

Certain codes, such as MEF++ [Tar04] have not hesitated to back themselves completely onto PETSc in order to take full advantage of its toolkit aspects and of its parallelism faculties. This performance aspect [28]_
is the priority of the product which has long claimed records in the matter (solution of linear systems of 500 million unknowns in 2004).

### 6.2 Implementation in Code_Aster

The software work of the implementation of PETSc in Code_Aster is detailed in the note of T.De Soza [Des07] and traced in the feedback tool *Aster*. We synthesise here the main lines:

1. A PETSc execution *via Code_Aster*  unfolds in three steps: pre-allocation of the memory space and filling of the matrix, parameter setting of the chosen Krylov solver and of its preconditioner, supply of the right-hand side and effective solution.

2.  The conversion of the *Aster*  matrix format (CSC format ‘Compressed Sparse Column’) to the format proper to PETSc (CSR ‘Compressed Sparse Row’). In symmetric mode, one stores in *Aster*  only the upper triangular part whereas PETSc requires the whole matrix. On the other hand, PETSc needs in order to be efficient particular indicators: in sequential mode, the average number of non-zero terms per row, in parallel, that of the diagonal and extra-diagonal blocks of the block of rows associated with the processor. This phase of preallocation of the PETSc objects is tedious but fortunately inexpensive compared with the other steps [29]_

.

1. In parallel mode, the distribution of the data is entirely piloted by PETSc. The latter attributes a block of contiguous rows to each processor. The *Aster*  user cannot, for the moment, really intervene on this choice (apart from changing the number of processors!). It is the same philosophy as for the OpenMP parallelism of the *Aster* multifrontal solver. With MUMPS, the choice is wider: the user can distribute his data directly or *via*  automatic tools (possibly controllable).

4.  The indefinite character of most Aster matrices (Lagrange, mixed finite elements…) prevents the use of the renumberers and of the preconditioners proper to PETSc. In order to be able to function efficiently, one “bluffs” the tool by supplying it with matrices renumbered [30]_

by the RCMK [31]_
of *Aster*  (which specifically manages the Lagranges).

### 6.3 Scope of use

Concerning the *Aster* operators, it is the same as for the native GCPC of the code (cf. §5.2).

As regards the functionalities of the PETSc library, we have limited ourselves for the moment to a few iterative linear solvers of Krylov type:

- CG (‘CG’): standard conjugate gradient (the same as the native GCPC of *Aster; M.R.Hestenes and E.Stiefel 1952*),
- CR (‘CR’): conjugate residual (*M.R.Hestenes and E.Stiefel 1952*),
- GMRES (‘GMRES’): Generalised Minimal RESidual (*Saad and Schultz 1986*),
- FGMRES ('FGMRES'): Flexible Generalised Minimal RESidual
- GCR (‘GCR’): Generalised Conjugate Residual (*S. C. Eisenstat, H. C. Elman, and H. C. Schultz 1983*).

and to the preconditioners:

- ILU( *k* ) (‘LDLT_INC’): incomplete factorisation by level (the same as the native GCPC of *Aster*; cf. §4.2),
- JACOBI (‘JACOBI’): standard diagonal preconditioner (cf. §4.1),
- SOR (‘SOR’): Successive Over Relaxation (cf. §4.2).
- Boomeramg ('BOOMER'): algebraic multigrid supplied by the *Hypre* library.
- Multilevel ('ML'): algebraic multigrid supplied by the *ML* library.
- GAMG('GAMG'): algebraic multigrid of the PETSc library

Furthermore, as for 'GCPC', another preconditioner (internal to *Code_Aster*) is available: it is 'LDLT_SP' which relies on a single-precision factorisation renewed during several iterative solutions.

The preconditioner 'BLOC_LAGR' is a block preconditioner of augmented-Lagrangian type which applies to systems with Lagrange multipliers. It is a preconditioner internal to code_aster developed on a PETSc basis. A system with Lagrange multipliers presents the following saddle-point structure:

$$
\left(\begin{array}{cc}K& {B}^{T}\\ B& 0\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}{c}u\\ \lambda \end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}{c}f\\ g\end{array}\right)
$$

The preconditioner of augmented-Lagrangian type (see [Mer15]) is of the form:

$$
M=\left(\begin{array}{cc}K+\gamma {B}^{T}B& 2{B}^{T}\\ 0& -\frac{1}{\gamma}I\end{array}\right)
$$

Finally, it is possible to combine the iterative solver GMRES with the preconditioner LDLT_SP (called first-level preconditioner) and a limited-memory preconditioner “Limited Memory Preconditioner”, called second-level preconditioner. This combination makes it possible to accelerate the solution of a non-linear problem. Indeed, the solution of the non-linear problem is obtained by solving a succession of linearised problems. The second-level preconditioner is constructed from spectral information (Ritz pairs) issuing from the previous linear solutions (see [Mer15]).

**Notes:**

- *The CG and CR methods are to be reserved for the Aster modellings leading to SPD matrices (the most widespread case notwithstanding the double-Lagrange problems cf.* *§5.1/6.2). In the non-symmetric case, one must call upon the other methods (GMRES and GCR).*
- *All the PETSc preconditioners propose a host of parameters. For the moment, only the fill factor of the ILU is accessible to the user.*
- *In parallel mode, only JACOBI offers a preconditioner strictly equivalent to the sequential mode. The others, first and foremost ILU and SOR, precondition by using the diagonal block local to the processor.*

### 6.4 Symmetric character of the working operator

One can make the same remarks as for the native GCPC (§5.3). Except that, with PETSc, one can use a Krylov solver compatible with non-symmetric systems (GMRES, GCR). The parameter ‘SYME’ therefore loses much of its interest.

### 6.5 Parameter setting and printout

To activate this functionality, one must initialise the keyword METHODE to ‘PETSC’ in the factor keyword SOLVEUR [U4.50.01]. Overall, one can make the same comments as in §5.4.

## 7 Treatment of failures

### 7.1 Failures encountered

Unlike a direct solver, an iterative solver will in general tend to fail during the phase of solution of the linear system: for example if the convergence criterion is not verified after the maximum number of iterations previously fixed. This can be caused by a preconditioner that is not robust enough or a quasi-singular matrix.

Failures are also possible during the phase of construction of the preconditioner but are rarer: for example if MUMPS lacks memory during the single-precision factorisation in order to construct the preconditioner LDLT_SP. In this last case the solution is to relaunch the analysis with more memory or by increasing the value of PCENT_PIVOT.

### 7.2 Recovery in the event of failure

Like the direct solvers, the iterative solvers of *Code_Aster*  are capable of signalling the failure or the success of the linear solution. This functionality is exploited in the non-linear solver [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01) in order to allow:

- the cutting of the time step in the event of failure during the solution phase whatever the preconditioner
- the updating of the preconditioner when LDLT_SP is used

When LDLT_SP is used, one proceeds first to the updating, then one carries out again the current time step. If a new failure occurs, the time step is then cut.

It is to be noted that no recovery is provided for in the event of failure during the phase of construction of the preconditioner.

### 7.3 Implementation

The operator DEFI_LIST_INST makes it possible to activate the treatment of failures. In the presence of a cutting action (ACTION='DECOUPE') or of updating of the preconditioner (ACTION='REAC_PRECOND'), one activates in STAT_NON_LINE or DYNA_NON_LINE the capture of the errors in the iterative solvers.

### 7.4 Interception of the exception

In the event of failure of the cutting strategy or of updating of the preconditioner, an exception is raised. It can be intercepted in the command file by using its name: ResolutionError.
