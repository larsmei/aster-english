# u2.05.07 User's guide for the cohesive-zone models

**Summary:**

This document is intended to supply the information necessary to a user wishing to model the initiation and the propagation of a crack with the cohesive models (CZM).

In a first part one recalls the main lines of this type of model, their fields of application, advantages and limitations. The second part is intended to supply specific recommendations for the production of the mesh. Advice on use in various fields of fracture mechanics is detailed in the third part. Finally, this document ends with an example of a commented command file, referring to the advice given in the preceding parts.

## 1 Introduction

The ambition of the cohesive models is to represent the evolution of the damage and/or fracture surfaces of a solid in 2D or 3D, initially sound or already partially cracked, subjected to a very wide range of loadings (mixed mode, cyclic loadings, thermal loadings, etc.) in the quasi-static or dynamic regime, for a material which may or may not present non-linearities in its constitutive behaviour (elasticity, plasticity, viscosity, etc.). This ambition is, however, limited, for the moment, to an evolution of the surfaces whose direction is known *a priori*.

This approach, widely developed in the literature in recent years, is attractive for describing the phenomena of cracking. It is intermediate between the initiation criteria (energy release rate or stress criteria) and continuum damage mechanics. When the geometric location of the potential cracks is known, it supplies a safe and robust framework for predicting the propagation kinetics.

The objective of this document is to supply a user's guide to users. On the one hand to the research units, with the aim of maintaining these models and making them evolve; but also in order to facilitate their use by engineering. In a first part one briefly recalls the main lines of the models available in *Code_Aster*, their respective advantages and limits, as well as the references necessary for a finer understanding of their formulation. The precautions to be taken when producing the mesh are set out in the second part. The third is devoted to advice on use in the various fields of fracture mechanics. Finally, this document ends with a commented example of use.

## 2 Summary of the cohesive models

### 2.1 Theoretical framework

The cohesive models presented here are based on the variational approach to fracture proposed by Francfort and Marigo [bib2]. Founded on a principle of energy minimisation, it makes it possible to represent the initiation and the propagation of cracks, without a priori assumptions on their spatio-temporal evolutions and whatever the applied loading. This theory postulates that the equilibrium configuration of a structure is that which minimises, at each loading increment, its total energy, the sum of the potential energy and of the surface energy. The latter corresponds to the energy dissipated in order to create one or more cracks.

The numerical models proposed here are limited to the search for the equilibrium state (or satisfying the equations of motion in dynamics) of a structure for which one knows beforehand the potential cracking path.

### 2.2 Finite-element models

There exist in Code_Aster three types of finite elements intended to model cracking with a cohesive law. The first two presented below are classically used in the literature (see [bib5] or [bib6]); the third is innovative and makes it possible to combine the advantages of the two preceding ones even if, for the moment, it remains in the course of validation.

**The classical joint element (** $\mathrm{EJ}$ **)**, placed along the potential cracks, makes it possible to discretise the displacement jump with an order consistent with the other elements of the mesh. In return, it requires a regularisation of the cohesive law by penalisation of the contact and adhesion conditions, apparently a source of instabilities of the global response of the structure. Furthermore, it leads to spatial oscillations of the cohesive forces which may prove prohibitive, because of their collocation at the Gauss points of the elements.

Modellings: PLAN_JOINT, AXIS_JOINT, 3D_JOINT

Documentations: [R3.06.09](doc:r3.06.09) and [U3.13.14](doc:u3.13.14)

**The element with included discontinuity (** $\mathrm{ED}$ **)** discretises both the displacement jump (piecewise constant) and the surrounding bulk displacement field ($\mathrm{Q1}$ ). It fulfils its objective, namely the account taken, without regularisation, of the adhesion conditions. On the other hand, contrary to what had been hoped, the geometric position of the crack is not left free, unless one departs from an energy formulation. Moreover, its limitation to an elastic constitutive behaviour, in quasi-statics, and the insufficient order of discretisation make it of limited use in practice.

Modellings: PLAN_ELDI, AXIS_ELDI

Documentations: [R7.02.14](doc:r7.02.14) and [U3.13.14](doc:u3.13.14)

More recently, the development of a third family of finite elements has made it possible to reconcile the advantages of the two preceding ones. Placed likewise along the potential cracks, **the mixed interface element (** $\mathrm{EI}$ **)** supplies a discretisation of the displacement field ($\mathit{P2}$ ), of the cohesive forces ($\mathrm{P1}$ ) and of the displacement jump ($\mathit{P1}$ -discontinuous or $\mathit{P2}$ -discontinuous). In this way:

- it authorises the treatment of the adhesion conditions without any regularisation, whence more regular structural responses;
- it satisfies the LBB condition, which guarantees a satisfactory convergence with the refinement of the mesh, with the same rate as for the volume elements (quadratic convergence);
- it is compatible with any bulk constitutive law.
- on the other hand it is compatible only with a quadratic mesh

For the interface elements, one distinguishes a standard modelling *_INTERFACE and an under-integrated modelling *_INTERFACE_S. In the first, the displacement jump is modelled by a $\mathit{P2}$ -discontinuous field, whereas in the second it is modelled by a $\mathit{P1}$ -discontinuous field. Each of these modellings has advantages and disadvantages. The standard modelling limits the risk of the appearance of zero pivots in the tangent matrix, but does not strictly satisfy the LBB condition. To remedy this, one advises choosing an augmented-Lagrangian penalisation parameter that is not too large (this choice brings a little “flexibility” into the modelling). The under-integrated modelling satisfies the LBB condition better, but more often presents zero pivots in the tangent matrix. These pivots appear in particular when the interface lies on a plane of symmetry, or between a volume and a structural element (MEMBRANE, GRILLE or BARRE).

The first applications confirm the potential of this element, which will have succeeded in synthesising the two preceding families. Its application in dynamics in order to treat sudden propagations is planned in the short term.

Modellings: PLAN_INTERFACE, AXIS_INTERFACE, 3D_INTERFACE, PLAN_INTERFACE_S, AXIS_INTERFACE_S, 3D_INTERFACE_S

Documentations: [R3.06.13](doc:r3.06.13) and [U3.13.14](doc:u3.13.14)

### 2.3 Cohesive laws

The cohesive laws are a relation between the stress vector and the displacement jump at each Gauss point. All these laws are non-linear and must be used with the non-linear operators STAT/DYNA_NON_LINE (consequently they are not suited to MECA_STATIQUE). For the preceding models the cohesive laws are named:

> **EJ** : CZM_LIN_REG , CZM_EXP_REG
> **ED**  : CZM_EXP
> **EI** : CZM_OUV_MIX , CZM_TAC_MIX, CZM_EXP_MIX CZM_FAT_MIX, CZM_TRA_MIX, CZM_LAB_MIX

One gives an example, in opening mode (Figure 2.3), of the relation between the normal component of the stress vector ${\sigma}_{n}$ and the normal jump ${\delta}_{n}$. The internal threshold variable $\kappa$ makes it possible to handle the irreversibility of the cracking. The mechanical parameters of the law are the critical initiation stress ${\sigma}_{c}$ and the critical energy release rate ${G}_{c}$. The ratio of these two parameters defines a critical jump ${\delta}_{c}={\mathrm{2G}}_{c}/{\sigma}_{c}$ beyond which the stress vanishes.

![Figure](/figures/u2.05.07/100000000000047B0000019E0B14AC389E99B106.png)

**Figure 2.3**: Normal component of the stress vector as a function of the normal jump for the law CZM_OUV_MIX (threshold $\kappa$ zero on the left and positive on the right).

The principal differences between the CZM* laws concern the initiation phase and the shape of the post-peak law. The *REG laws take account of the contact/adhesion part in a penalised manner, whereas the others carry out these phases in a perfect manner (infinite slope at the origin, see Figure 2.3). Note that the regularisation of the cohesive laws has an influence on the results of the analysis (see part 15). Furthermore, the *EXP* laws (except CZM_EXP_MIX, whose law has been truncated deliberately) have an exponentially decreasing behaviour which tends asymptotically towards zero with the opening of the lips. Formally, these do not make it possible to define a crack tip; one will prefer to them the other laws with linear decrease, which supply explicitly the position of the crack tip (critical fracture jump, see Figure 2.3).

In a general manner, during the crack-propagation phase, the shape of the cohesive law (linear or exponential) has little influence on the cracking; on the other hand it may have a role in the initiation phase. For more details on these laws see [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11).

### 2.4 Definition of the local frame of the crack

For the integration of the cohesive law, which involves a displacement jump, a function of the nodal displacements expressed in the global frame, it is necessary to know a local frame $(n,\mathrm{t1},\mathrm{t2})$ of the element. For the EI and the EJ two different methods have been adopted, each having its advantages but also its limitations.

*For the* $\mathrm{EI}$*:* the user supplies the information necessary to define the local frame (in 2D, one angle of rotation is sufficient; in 3D one must know the three nautical angles). This is relatively simple for a rectilinear or plane crack. It becomes much more complex when this is no longer the case. On the other hand this method makes it possible to impose a single local frame on all the elements of a given plane crack and thus authorises a consistent visualisation of the displacement jumps in mode $\mathrm{II}$ and $\mathrm{III}$. We shall see that this is not the case for the $\mathrm{EJ}$ and $\mathrm{ED}$.

For a mesh-cell group of interface FISS in a given plane, the user supplies the characteristics of the EI in the following manner (nautical angles $(a,b,c)$ in degrees):

ORIEN_FI=AFFE_CARA_ELEM(

> MODELE=MO,

> MASSIF=(

_F(GROUP_MA='FISS',ANGL_REP=(a, b ,c)),

));

then CARA_ELEM = ORIEN_FI in STAT_NON_LINE.

In 2D it is easy to know the angle between the normal of the local frame $n$ and the vector $X$ of the global frame. This angle is the first nautical angle; the other two are set to zero. In 3D it is a little more difficult. In order to help the user one supplies a few examples of nautical angles with the definition of the local frame $(n,\mathrm{t1},\mathrm{t2})$ in the global frame $(X,Y,Z)$.

> $(0,0,0):(n,\mathrm{t1},\mathrm{t2})=(X,Y,Z)$
> $(90,0,0):(n,\mathrm{t1},\mathrm{t2})=(Y,-X,Z)$
> $(-90,0,-90):(n,\mathrm{t1},\mathrm{t2})=(-Y,-Z,X)$
> $(0,-90,-90):(n,\mathrm{t1},\mathrm{t2})=(Z,X,Y)$

*For the* $\mathrm{EJ}$ *and the* $\mathrm{ED}$: the computation of the local frame is carried out (hard-coded) from the geometric coordinates of the element. No information is given by the user; the account taken of non-rectilinear or non-plane cracks is therefore immediate. In return, for a plane crack, the elements all have the same normal but tangent vectors $(\mathrm{t1},\mathrm{t2})$ which differ from one element to another. This of course has no effect on the validity of the analyses, but on the other hand it prevents a clean visualisation of the components of the jump in mode $\mathrm{II}$ or $\mathrm{III}$.

## 3 Advice on the mesh

After a few general points on the various types of finite elements, one supplies advice on the production of the mesh as well as on its characteristics in order to model the cohesive crack correctly in various configurations.

### 3.1 General points

In a general manner, the cohesive models make it possible to represent a crack on a path $\Gamma$ given *a priori*. These models account for the evolution but not for the direction of the propagation. It is therefore necessary to produce a mesh compatible with the potential crack path. This consists in placing cohesive elements along this path.

![Figure](/figures/u2.05.07/1000000000000690000004B0A2B9BED2A81643D2.jpg)

**Figure 3.1-a**: 3D diagram of an element $\mathrm{EI}$ or $\mathrm{EJ}$ inserted between two tetrahedra.

For the surface models ($\mathrm{EJ}$  and $\mathrm{EI}$), in 2D the crack lips correspond to the long sides of the cohesive elements (degenerate quadrangles); in 3D the lips are defined by the triangular faces (for the degenerate pentahedra) or rectangular faces (for the degenerate hexahedra). For the element with internal discontinuity $\mathrm{ED}$, the crack passes through the centre of the element and is parallel to the two opposite sides. The models $\mathrm{EJ}$ and $\mathrm{ED}$ have a linear interpolation of the displacements; they are compatible with a linear volume mesh $\mathrm{Q1}$ or $\mathrm{P1}$. The $\mathrm{EI}$ has a quadratic interpolation of the displacements and is compatible with quadratic volume mesh cells $\mathrm{Q2}$ or $\mathrm{P2}$.

### 3.2 Creation of the mesh

One gives in this part the tools available in *Code_Aster* in order to guarantee the proper production of a mesh with a cohesive crack, then one supplies advice for the meshers GMSH and Salomé.

#### 3.2.1 Code_Aster tools

The mesh cells of the cohesive elements $\mathrm{EI}$ and $\mathrm{EJ}$ are surface mesh cells; their orientation is defined by a local frame, and it is therefore necessary that their local connectivity be defined *a priori*. This is also the case for the $\mathrm{ED}$, which are volume elements since they are crossed by a surface discontinuity.

Two techniques are available in *Code_Aster* for carrying out this task. The first, once the mesh has been produced, consists in checking that they have an *ad hoc* local numbering (orientation). The second consists in producing the mesh of the cohesive elements from two facing node groups (automatic creation).

*Notes* **:**

- *The thickness of the elements* $\mathrm{EJ}$ *and* $\mathrm{EI}$ *has no influence on the solution. The latter may however modify the dimensions of the structure; the user must produce the mesh accordingly. When this is possible one advises producing mesh cells of zero thickness.*
- *For the post-processing in 2D a small thickness may however facilitate the visualisation of the variables at the Gauss points (this problem does not arise in 3D because one visualises a surface).*
- *On the other hand, the* $\mathrm{ED}$ *necessarily have a non-zero thickness. One recommends choosing a size of the same order of magnitude as that of the surrounding non-cohesive elements.*

*Orientation*

The keyword ORIE_FISSURE (command MODI_MAILLAGE) makes it possible to ensure the correct local numbering of the mesh cells and to renumber them correctly if necessary. For more details on the use of this procedure see [U4.23.04](doc:u4.23.04). One advises using this command systematically; it works in 2D and in 3D (provided that the thickness of the mesh cells is non-zero) and whatever the type of cohesive elements (linear or quadratic). One gives an example of use for the cohesive layer 'JOINT':

MA=MODI_MAILLAGE(reuse =MA,

MAILLAGE=MA, **ORIE_FISSURE** =_F(GROUP_MA='JOINT'),)

*Automatic creation*

Another possibility: CREA_FISS allows the creation of cohesive mesh cells from two node groups (facing one another or coincident two by two). Certain precautions are necessary for the use of this command; it is therefore advised to read the documentation of CREA_MAILLAGE [U4.23.02](doc:u4.23.02). This functionality is available in 2D (the 3D equivalent is possible with a Salomé Python script, see 12 below); here is an example from the node groups GN1 and GN2.

MA = CREA_MAILLAGE(

MAILLAGE = MA_INI,

**CREA_FISS**  = (

_F(NOM='JOINT', GROUP_NO_1='GN1', GROUP_NO_2='GN2', PREF_MAILLE='MJ'),

))

*Notes*:

- Once the command CREA_FISS has been carried out, it is not necessary to run ORIE_FISSURE if one produces a linear mesh.
- *If one wishes to use this procedure with the interface elements (hence a quadratic mesh), one must first carry out the preceding procedure on a linear mesh, then switch to quadratic (* LINE_QUAD/TOUT='OUI') *and finally run* ORIE_FISSURE *again on the cohesive layer, because the command* LINE_QUAD *may have modified the local numbering.*

Finally, tools are under development in order to facilitate the creation of cohesive mesh cells. In particular in Salomé version 5 (see 12).

#### 3.2.2 Advice for GMSH

With the free mesher GMSH, it is fairly easy to build a layer of cohesive finite elements intended to be joint or interface elements, whatever the nature of the volume mesh. It is sufficient to use the command “Extrude” and to take a few precautions.

*In 2D:* one extrudes a line n of an edge of the domain, of a thickness ep (small but non-zero) in the direction -Y, and one specifies that one wishes only one layer of elements with the keyword “Layers”; finally one adds “Recombine” so that the mesher creates quadrangles (triangles by default).

> ep=0.001
> NbCouche = 1;
> JOINT[]=Extrude Line {n, {0,-ep,0}}

> {

> Layers{NbCouche,1}; Recombine;

> };;

One may then define a “Physical Line” of the new line created, or a “Physical Surface” of the surface created, in the following manner:

> Physical Line(a) = {JOINT[0]};
> Physical Surface(b) = {JOINT[1]};

The first may serve for example for the application of symmetry boundary conditions; the second corresponds to the layer of cohesive elements. If one wishes to include the crack inside a domain, one proceeds as previously, then one extrudes the new line JOINT[0] in order to create the second part of the volume.

*In 3D*: one proceeds in the same manner with the extrusion of a boundary surface of the domain; “Recombine” makes it possible to create hexahedra if the trace of the mesh on the extruded surface comprises quadrangles, and pentahedra if the latter comprises triangles.

Finally, the “physical” entities created in the .geo are used in the .comm command file in the usual way:

> Physical Line(i) corresponds to 'GMi' if the mesh is in .msh format
> Physical Surface(j) corresponds to 'GMj' if the mesh is in .msh format
> Physical Line(k) corresponds to 'G_1D_k' if the mesh is in .med format
> Physical Volume(l) corresponds to 'G_3D_l' if the mesh is in .med format

This bug is listed on the GMSH web site and should be corrected in future versions.*

*Notes* **:**

- *The mesh cells created by GMSH are topologically correct but the local numbering of the nodes is not necessarily properly carried out. In Code_Aster the latter must be defined in a specific manner in order to distinguish the orientation of the cohesive elements. After reading the mesh in .msh or .med format it is therefore necessary to pass through a* MODI_MAILLAGE *with the command* ORIE_FISSURE*, which carries out this renumbering operation of the cohesive elements automatically (see part* 10*).*
- *Note that the extrusions mentioned above are also easily achievable in an interactive manner with the mouse. Once the .geo has been generated automatically, one must however add “* Recombine *” in order to create quadrangles in 2D or hexahedra/pentahedra in 3D.*

#### 3.2.3 Advice for Salomé

In Salomé, it is possible to extrude a layer of surface elements from an edge of the domain. This makes it possible to create cohesive mesh cells whose connectivity one will change if necessary by means of the command ORIE_FISSURE (see part 10).

*Note* **:**

*Whatever the technique adopted for meshing the potential crack path, it is necessary that the latter cross the structure from one side to the other. Indeed, there do not exist cohesive finite elements intended to model a crack tip (a triangle, for example) which cannot propagate. This would moreover not really make sense, since it is contrary to the logic of the modelling of a potential path.*

#### 3.2.4 Case of advanced geometric configurations

When one employs the interface finite elements (EI), the presence of two families of degrees of freedom, the displacements and the (augmented) cohesive forces, complicates the handling of advanced geometric configurations. This expression refers to situations in which several interfaces cross one another, emerge onto one another, emerge onto a kinematic boundary condition, or else present a boundary in the volume and not on an edge of the structure.

Advice on the manner of meshing such configurations has been given in the note [bib9]. One observes there that it is indeed the nodes which carry the degrees of freedom corresponding to the cohesive forces that require particular care.

### 3.3 Modelling of the cohesive zone

A CZM crack is defined by three zones: the first corresponds to a stress-free crack, the second, called the cohesive zone, in which the cohesion forces are non-zero, and finally a sound zone (or potential cracking zone) in which the initiation stress has not been reached (see Figure 3.3).

The cohesive zone makes it possible to achieve a progressive transition from the sound material to a true crack. The size of the latter is linked to the characteristic length ${G}_{c}/{\sigma}_{c}$ of the cohesive model; it adapts in order to remove the singularity of the stresses at the crack tip. The smaller this length, the more the size of the cohesive zone will be reduced. In the limit, if it tends towards zero, the cohesive zone disappears and one finds oneself in the case of classical fracture mechanics, with the theoretical possibility of having infinite stresses at the crack tip. Formally, this amounts to taking an infinite fracture stress ${\sigma}_{c}$.

One emphasises here the importance, both from the mechanical point of view and from the numerical point of view, of simulating this cohesive zone correctly. In a general manner and whatever the type of analysis, one advises meshing this zone sufficiently so that it is composed of at least four or five cohesive finite elements. This makes it possible to represent finely the evolution of the crack from a mechanical point of view, but also to avoid numerical convergence problems linked to too coarse a discretisation of this zone of strong stress gradient.

![Figure](/figures/u2.05.07/10000000000004A1000002B578234E15122F2FAF.jpg)

**Figure 3.3**: Diagram of the cohesive crack.

In the literature [bib7] there exists an estimate of the size of the cohesive zone ${S}_{\mathrm{zc}}$ for a crack in an infinite elastic material:

> ${S}_{\mathrm{zc}}\simeq \frac{E}{1-{\nu}^{2}}\frac{{G}_{c}}{{\sigma}_{c}^{2}}$

with $E$ Young's modulus, $\nu$ Poisson's ratio. This value is given as an indication in order to estimate the size of the cohesive zone; however the latter may vary with various parameters of the mechanical problem.

### 3.4 Definition of a cohesive pre-crack

In most cases, one places the cohesive elements in a cracking plane or in the extension of an existing crack. The latter is in general represented by a free surface. However, in certain cases, it may be useful to place cohesive mesh cells on an initial crack. These may for example serve as contact elements (small strains) or serve to define initial crack fronts, plane or semi-elliptical for example.

In order to define an initial crack, it is necessary to build a mesh with two contiguous zones of cohesive elements, one for the pre-crack and the other for the potential crack. The idea then consists in imposing the internal variables of the pre-crack in such a way that they correspond to a stress-free zone, and in not modifying those of the potential crack (i.e. imposing them at 0). To do this, one uses the commands CREA_CHAMP in order to build a field of internal variables at the Gauss points. The latter is then used as the initial state on input to STAT_NON_LINE with the command ETAT_INIT. Note that it is necessary to define all the internal variables of the structure, including those of the volume part. In the case of an elastic material this operation is trivial; in the non-linear case it is not feasible to know them easily, unless one imposes them as zero, which is not necessarily realistic.

*Note* **:**

*It is not possible to define an initial crack by imposing the material parameters* ${\sigma}_{c}$ *and* ${G}_{c}$ *as zero.*

### 3.5 Multi-cracking

It is possible to model several cracks with all the types of cohesive elements, provided that the mesh is produced accordingly. With the ED element one may for example place layers of elements in order to take account of a network of parallel cracks (see example [bib5]). For the EJ and EI elements the test of the plate with a hole (ssnp133 [V6.03.133](doc:v6.03.133)) makes it possible to model the simultaneous advance of two cracks.

However, with this type of approach one advises against placing cohesive elements everywhere (i.e. on each edge of the mesh). Besides the difficulty of producing such a mesh, numerous works in the literature show that the results for the cracking path depend strongly on the mesh. Furthermore, for the EJ, the regularisation of the cohesive law may also contribute to the variability of the results and play a harmful role in the quality of the solution. Finally, this raises the non-trivial question of the conditions to be satisfied at the intersection of two cracks. No particular treatment is carried out for the models presented here.

## 4 Advice on use

The cohesive models cover a fairly wide spectrum of use in the field of fracture mechanics. One begins the advice on use with general points concerning the use of the parameters and the loadings, valid whatever the type of simulation. One continues by giving specific advice for the analyses in brittle fracture (linear or non-linear material), the fatigue analyses and the dynamic analyses. Subsequently one emphasises the importance of relying on numerical tools in order to improve the convergence of the analyses. Finally one ends with advice for the post-processing of the results.

### 4.1 General points

#### 4.1.1 Mechanical parameters

The mechanical parameters of the law are the critical initiation stress ${\sigma}_{c}$ and the critical energy release rate ${G}_{c}$. On Figure 2.3 on the left, the stress ${\sigma}_{c}$ corresponds to the peak of the law; the density ${G}_{c}$ is linked to the area under the triangle. The user defines a material and (non-zero) values of these parameters under the keyword RUPT_FRAG. In the case of a thermal loading it is possible to make the latter depend on the temperature. One uses in this case the keyword RUPT_FRAG_FO; see the example in the test SDNS105a.

The ratio of these two parameters defines a critical jump ${\delta}_{c}={\mathrm{2G}}_{c}/{\sigma}_{c}$ beyond which the stress vanishes for the laws CZM_LIN_REG, CZM_OUV_MIX, CZM_TAC_MIX. For the two laws CZM_EXP_REG and CZM_EXP the stress tends asymptotically towards zero. For the law CZM_EXP_MIX, a critical jump is defined at ${\delta}_{c}={\mathrm{3.2G}}_{c}/{\sigma}_{c}$. This jump corresponds to an error of 1% on the critical energy release rate. For the fatigue law CZM_FAT_MIX, the value of the jump beyond which the stress remains zero depends on the loading. For the ductile law CZM_TRA_MIX the shape parameters supplied by the user influence this critical jump. For more details see the documentation [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11).

*Note* **:**

When one imposes symmetry conditions on the crack lips it may be necessary to divide the value of ${G}_{c}$ by two; see part 16.

#### 4.1.2 Numerical parameters

*Elements with internal discontinuity*: there is no numerical parameter to be supplied for this model.

*Joint element*: this model has two numerical parameters intended to penalise the adhesion PENA_ADHERENCE and the contact PENA_CONTACT. One replaces the infinitely rigid parts (contact and adhesion) by strongly rigid elastic contributions (see doc [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11)). By default PENA_CONTACT is equal to 1 and leads to a penalisation identical to that of the adhesion; this is the value that one recommends. The parameter PENA_ADHERENCE corresponds to a percentage of the internal length of the cohesive law; it must be non-zero and less than 1. The smaller the value, the greater the penalisation slope.

The results of the analyses depend strongly on the value of this parameter. In a general manner, one observes:

- Oscillations (local snap-backs) in the global response of the structure, linked to the successive opening of the joints. But also oscillations of the stress profile along the crack, all the more marked as the stiffness of the joint is high.
- An important influence of the parameter on the quality of the solution in stress. For fairly low values of PENA_ADHERENCE the error is reasonable; for higher values this may lead to very large discrepancies.
- The convergence of the global Newton algorithm is all the more difficult as the penalisation is high.

One therefore observes antagonistic effects of the penalisation parameter, which imply making a compromise between convergence of the analysis and accuracy of the solution. It is therefore difficult to recommend a general rule supplying the right value of this parameter. One therefore advises, before carrying out a numerical simulation, performing a sensitivity test with respect to this parameter. In every case, the initial stiffness of the joint elements must be greater than the stiffness of the neighbouring volume elements.

*Interface element:* this model has a numerical parameter PENA_LAGR which corresponds to the penalisation of the augmented Lagrangian (see [R3.06.13](doc:r3.06.13)). The results of the analysis do not depend on the value of this parameter. One advises taking the default value (100). This value must be strictly greater than 1 in order to ensure the uniqueness of the solution during the integration of the cohesive law.

Furthermore the laws CZM_OUV_MIX and CZM_TRA_MIX, intended for opening in mode I only, have a parameter named RIGI_GLIS which makes it possible to impose a non-zero stiffness in sliding and thus to avoid the problems of zero pivot at the fracture of the elements. One advises using the default value of 10. The solution is not affected by this parameter provided that the sliding modes are not activated.

#### 4.1.3 Loadings and boundary conditions

All the types of mechanical and thermal loadings may *a priori* be applied on a structure comprising cohesive elements: imposed displacements, surface forces, body forces, temperature fields coming from a prior thermal analysis.

In order to achieve a saving in run time, taking account of the symmetries of the problem, it is often useful to simulate a half-structure. Symmetry conditions on the lips of the cohesive crack (i.e. the faces of the cohesive elements), leading to an opening in pure mode I, may be envisaged in two different ways:

- The first consists in blocking the normal displacements of the faces of the cohesive elements which are not connected to the volume. These then coincide with the axis of symmetry of the structure. In this case, a precaution is to be taken on the value of the critical surface-energy density entered by the user. Since one models only one crack lip, one must, for a given material value of ${G}_{c}$, supply G_C = ${G}_{c}/2$ in the command file. In other words, it costs twice less energy to open a half-crack.
- The second consists in making the crack open entirely. On the crack lips one imposes displacements that are symmetrical with respect to an axis passing between them (command AFFE_CHAR_MECA/LIAISON_GROUP). This type of modelling makes it possible to take the crack into account as a whole and does not require dividing ${G}_{c}$ by two. On the other hand this poses a problem for the energy balance, since one models a half-structure (hence a volume energy half as large) with a complete crack. This method is therefore to be used with a great deal of caution; one rather advises adopting the preceding one.

#### 4.1.4 Convergence criterion

The convergence criterion of the Newton algorithm RESI_REFE_RELA makes it possible to build a reference nodal force, which serves to estimate the (approximate) vanishing of the residual (see [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01)). It makes it possible to improve the convergence in the case where the residuals are heterogeneous, that is to say when one treats degrees of freedom of different nature. This is the case of the mixed model EI, for which one recommends the use of this criterion.

With the cohesive models, the force is defined from a stress and from its dual, a displacement. In order to use this criterion one therefore supplies a reference stress and a reference displacement: SIGM_REFE and DEPL_REFE. In order to choose these values, one proposes those coming from the cohesive law: the initiation stress ${\sigma}_{c}$ for the first, and the characteristic length ${G}_{c}/{\sigma}_{c}$ for the second. With the models EJ and ED it is not possible (and *a priori* not useful) to use this type of residual.

#### 4.1.5 Linear solver

The method of solution of the global linear system MUMPS is indispensable for the mixed elements having degrees of freedom of Lagrange type. It is therefore necessary to use it with the EI. Its principal interest lies in its ability to pivot rows and/or columns of the matrix during the factorisation in the event of a small pivot. The other finite-element models EJ and ED do not have this restriction; they may be used with the default method (multifrontal).

Furthermore, the tangent matrix of the cohesive laws is symmetric; the use of the keyword SYME='OUI', intended to symmetrise the latter, is not necessary.

### 4.2 A linear interface in order to validate one's data setup

In the same way as one recommends carrying out a linear elastic analysis prior to a plastic analysis, it is preferable to begin a cracking analysis by employing a **linear** cohesive constitutive relation. The model CZM_ELAS_MIX has been introduced with this aim; see [U4.51.11](doc:u4.51.11). This makes it possible to validate one's data setup (orientation of the interfaces, numbering of the degrees of freedom, assignment of the characteristics and of the loadings, etc.) in a framework simple enough for the mechanical intuition (or the failure of the analysis) to suffice to detect possible aberrant results. Caution, however: this constitutive relation is available only with the interface elements (EI).

This interface model is elastic, with a stiffness in the normal direction and another in the two tangent directions. They are defined via DEFI_MATERIAU [U4.43.01](doc:u4.43.01) under the factor keyword CZM_ELAS. It is also possible to consider limit behaviours via perfect normal and/or tangential adhesion conditions, still under the same factor keyword (in this case, the stiffnesses no longer play any role, since the displacement jump in the normal or tangent direction is forced to zero). Typically, with perfect adhesion conditions (normal and tangential), the response of the structure must be very close to that which it would have without an interface (very close but not necessarily equal, because of the mixed formulation used with the EI element).

Finally, a last adhesion condition is proposed: it consists of contact conditions in closure, together with an elastic stiffness in opening. The analysis becomes more complex because the problem is no longer linear. But this makes it possible to ensure that the zones where the interface opens and those where it remains closed are indeed in conformity with intuition.

### 4.3 Analyses in brittle fracture

**Possible models**: EJ, EI, **Recommended model**: EI

The cohesive models are suited to modelling brittle fracture in a linear material (all of them) or a non-linear material (EJ and EI only). One gives here a few pieces of advice specific to this type of simulation. Numerical tools make it possible to guarantee a good robustness of the solution algorithms; one also supplies advice on their use.

#### 4.3.1 Linear material

For a linear elastic material, there are no specific precautions of implementation other than the preceding general points (part 15) and those on the mesh (part 9). For the robustness of the analyses, the use of the piloting of the loading is essential. One devotes a part below to advice on the use of this method.

#### 4.3.2 Non-linear material

The cohesive models EJ and EI may interact with a surrounding non-linear material, *a priori* whatever it may be. The simulations that may be envisaged remain in the domain of brittle fracture in the presence of plasticity, the latter being confined to the neighbourhood of the crack tip. No specific numerical treatment is necessary for this type of analysis, other than the usual precautions on the fineness of the mesh (see part 9) in order to capture the cohesive zone but also the plastic band along the crack lips. The convergence of the Newton–Raphson algorithm is slowed (more Newton iterations for a given load step) and the run time is increased, but this remains entirely reasonable and does not hinder the robustness of the cohesive models in this field of application.

From a mechanical point of view, the plastification of the material and the cracking are two phenomena which interact. Thus, particular attention will have to be paid to the choice of the ratio of the initiation stress of the cohesive law and of the yield stress of the material.

Depending on the values of these parameters, one may find oneself either in the case where the cracking occurs without development of the plasticity, or in the opposite case where the stress in the material reaches the plastic threshold and evolves without exceeding the initiation stress, or finally in the case where plasticity and cracking evolve jointly. In this last case, one observes a plastic zone which initiates at the crack tip and which evolves with the propagation of the latter.

Furthermore, a few analyses with viscoplastic materials have been carried out by way of illustration; the limited feedback of experience in this field does not allow us to supply specific advice.

#### 4.3.3 Numerical tools

*Line search*

It is possible to use the line search with the cohesive models. This may occasionally improve the convergence of the analyses. This method is however not the best suited to getting past the instabilities linked to the sudden propagation of a crack in quasi-statics. In this case, one will prefer to it the piloting of the loading.

*Piloting of the loading*

The piloting of the loading, available for all the cohesive models, makes it possible to ensure the convergence of the Newton–Raphson algorithm in quasi-statics despite an instability in the global response of the structure (sudden propagation of a crack, for example). This method is valid for the softening laws which may lead to this type of response, and in particular for the cohesive laws. The idea consists in authorising the release of the loading in order to restore part of the potential energy and to make the cracking advance progressively by controlling locally the damage of the cohesive zones.

The user supplies the direction of the loading (displacement or surface force) from an AFFE_CHAR_MECA; it is multiplied by the scalar piloting parameter $\eta$, a new unknown of the problem which gives its intensity. This last parameter replaces the multiplicative function of the loading FONC_MULT. During the solution of the mechanical problem, one adds a supplementary equation (control of the piloting) which closes the system. For more details on this technique, the reader may refer to the general documentation [R5.03.80](doc:r5.03.80) and to the documentations specific to the piloting for the cohesive models [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11) and [R7.02.14](doc:r7.02.14).

This numerical tool is particularly important for ensuring the convergence of the algorithm. It is often necessary, including when the global response does not admit a snap-back. Indeed, in certain configurations, local snap-backs, linked to a sudden opening at the element level, may occur. All the cohesive laws may be piloted by elastic prediction (PRED_ELAS); the ratio of the time step $\Delta t$ to the coefficient COEF_MULT makes it possible to manage the evolution of the cracking. At the level of the constitutive law this amounts to controlling the exit from the threshold. The higher this ratio, the more rapidly one wishes to get past the instability, and therefore the higher $\eta$ will be.

In the command STAT_NON_LINE, one chooses to pilot the load TRACTION (supplied in the classical way) in the following manner:

```text
EXCIT = _F(CHARGE = TRACTION , TYPE_CHARGE = 'FIXE_PILO'),

On ajoute ensuite les données propres au pilotage :

PILOTAGE = _F(

SELECTION = 'RESIDU',

TYPE = 'PRED_ELAS',

GROUP_MA = 'JOINT',

COEF_MULT = c_mult_pilo,

),
```

*Notes and advice:*

- *One advises defining the direction of the* piloted *loading by a unit vector. Thus, the piloting parameter* $\eta$ *will correspond exactly to its intensity.*
- *One advises the user to begin by choosing a* ratio *of* $\Delta t$ *to* COEF_MULT *equal to 0.05. In this way, one seeks to damage by 5% the most highly loaded Gauss point.*
- *If the algorithm does not converge despite the piloting, one advises reducing the preceding ratio: automatic or manual subdivision of the step and/or increase of* COEF_MULT*. The smaller the ratio, the slower the cracking, and conversely.*
- *It is* sometimes *necessary to break the symmetry of the problem in order to avoid the piloting oscillating between two values of* $\eta$ *if these supply an identical cracking state. For this, one advises carrying out a first non-linear analysis with a very small non-piloted load step. One thus remains in the linear domain of the response and the analysis converges rapidly.*
- *Another way of avoiding the oscillations of the value of the piloting consists in supplying an* ETA_PILO_R_MIN *equal to zero in order to authorise only positive values of the piloting parameter.*
- *It is necessary to pilot finely (small exit from the criterion) the first piloted load steps in order to capture correctly the peak value of the global response.*
- *If the* piloting *is used in a situation in which one could do without it, the solution is not modified. The only difference is that one does not directly control the intensity of the load.*
- *It is* not *possible to pilot thermal loadings.*

### 4.4 Analyses in ductile fracture

**Possible models**: EI

The law CZM_TRA_MIX was introduced in version 10.4 in order to seek to model ductile fracture. The use of the latter must be confined to an upstream research framework. Indeed, it is in the course of validation and will certainly be led to evolve.

The law for ductile fracture is inspired by the laws classically proposed in the literature. In addition to the parameters of critical stress and of critical energy density, one adds two parameters linked to the shape of the law (COEF_EXTR and COEF_PLAS). The latter is chosen trapezoidal. Sensitivity analyses with respect to the parameters, as well as tests on CT specimens (SSNP151) and AE specimens (SSNA120), have been carried out. The first conclusions show that this model makes it possible to reproduce correctly the global response and the propagation kinetics observed with a model of Gurson type (Rousselier) on a 2D CT specimen (SSNP151a). One notes however that the fitting of the parameters of the law in order to approach the fine analysis of the local approach remains difficult. On the other hand the gain in run time and in robustness is significant. When one moves to a 3D CT specimen (SSNP151b) the results are less conclusive beyond a certain loading level. It seems that this is due in part to the poor account taken of the volume effects in the cohesive model, and in particular the confinement effect. Work is in progress in order to try to take account of the influence of the triaxiality. Other limits are also to be emphasised: as for the other cohesive models, the crack path must be known *a priori*. The cracking can take place only in mode I (it is however conceivable to introduce the sliding modes if this proves judicious). Finally, large crack openings are not taken into account; one may however use large strains in the surrounding material.

No specific numerical treatment is necessary for this type of analysis, other than the usual precautions on the fineness of the mesh (see part 9) in order to capture the cohesive zone but also the plastic band along the crack lips. The convergence of the Newton–Raphson algorithm is slowed (more Newton iterations for a given load step) and the run time is increased, but this remains entirely reasonable and does not hinder the robustness of the cohesive models in this field of application.

From a mechanical point of view, the plastification of the material and the cracking are two phenomena which interact. Thus, particular attention will have to be paid to the choice of the ratio of the initiation stress of the cohesive law and of the yield stress of the material. Depending on the values of these parameters, one may find oneself either in the case where the cracking occurs without development of the plasticity, or in the opposite case where the stress in the material reaches the plastic threshold and evolves without exceeding the initiation stress, or finally in the case where plasticity and cracking evolve jointly. In this last case, one observes a plastic zone which initiates at the crack tip and which evolves with the propagation of the latter.

#### 4.4.1 Numerical tools

*Line search*

It is possible to use the line search. This may occasionally improve the convergence of the analyses.

*Piloting of the loading*

One considers that the cracks propagate in a progressive manner in the ductile fracture mode. The piloting of the loading is therefore not developed for this type of law.

### 4.5 Analyses in fatigue

**Possible model**: $\mathrm{EI}$

The law available for treating the phenomenon of fatigue in mode $I$ is CZM_FAT_MIX (see doc [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11)), usable with the interface elements. One may carry out analyses in 2D or 3D whatever the material and for all types of mechanical loadings (of constant or variable amplitude), or thermal loadings. One may also refer to the tests SSNP118JKL and SSNP139B and to part 25.

In order to account for fatigue with this law, it is necessary to go through all the cycles of the loading. One readily understands that it is not feasible to simulate a large number of cycles. Nevertheless, the first results bring out a certain number of phenomena observed in fatigue (see report [bib3]). For an elastic material:

- One succeeds in reproducing evolution laws of Paris type, whose coefficients one identifies *a posteriori*,
- One notes the weak influence of an overload on the advance,
- One observes the absence of a sequence effect at variable amplitude,
- One simulates realistic evolutions of a 3D crack front.

In the presence of plasticity several effects observed experimentally are reproduced:

- One brings out the overload effect, which leads to the slowing of the propagation due to the compression induced by the increase of the plastic zone,
- the slowing is all the more pronounced as the overload is large,
- the increase of the load ratio contributes to reducing the delay at a fixed overload ratio and load amplitude.

Furthermore, work on the cycle jump is envisaged. It will make it possible, by extrapolation, to simulate a large number of cycles.

*Advice on the discretisation of the loading:*

- *When discretising the cyclic loading a few precautions are to be taken in order to optimise the run time.* The *risk is of carrying out at each cycle an automatic subdivision of the step which may become very costly in CPU time. The feedback of experience allows us to advise the user to carry out about 4 loading increments per cycle when the material is elastic and to double this value when the latter has a non-linear constitutive behaviour. This rule is not necessarily valid in all situations and will have to be adapted case by case in order to optimise the time spent in each cycle.*

*Advice on the definition of the cyclic function:*

*For the construction of the multiplicative function of the loading one may use* DEFI_FONCTION *or Python in the* command *file. One proposes below an example of a sawtooth function of variable amplitude. The odd time instants are the ends of the loading ramp (maximum amplitude) and the even time instants are the ends of the unloading (minimum amplitude). In this way, one finishes the* $i$ *th cycle at the time instant* $\mathrm{2i}$*. In the example below the loading varies*

*From* val_inf *to* val_sup *in the course of cycles 1 to* nb_cy_1,

*From* val_inf *to* val_surch *in the course of cycles* nb_cy_1+1 *to* nb_cy_2,

*From* val_inf *to* val_sup *in the course of cycles* nb_cy_2+1 *and* nb_cy_3

Example of a cyclic function f of variable amplitude:

> f = []
> for i in xrange(nb_cy_1) :
> f.extend((2*i,val_inf,2*i+1,val_sup))
> for i in range(nb_cy_1,nb_cy_2) :
> f.extend((2*i,val_inf,2*i+1,val_surch))
> for i in range(nb_cy_2,nb_cy_3) :
> f.extend((2*i,val_inf,2*i+1,val_sup))

The definition of the function concept in *Code_Aster* is then carried out in the usual way:

> FCT_FAT = DEFI_FONCTION(

> NOM_PARA = 'INST',
> PROL_GAUCHE='LINEAIRE',
> VALE = f,

> )

*Advice on the storage of the data*

*In order to* avoid *too large a storage of the databases, one advises keeping only the values of the fields at the peak of each cycle by means of the keyword* ARCHIVAGE *of* STAT_NON_LINE*. *To do this, it is of course necessary to carry out a discretisation of the loading which passes through the peaks.*

### 4.6 Analyses in dynamics

**Possible models**: $\mathrm{EJ}$, $\mathrm{EI}$ (in the course of validation), **Recommended model**: $\mathrm{EJ}$

Taking the dynamic aspects into account introduces an additional complexity into the modelling. Besides the usual precautions in quasi-statics (sufficient mesh refinement in order to discretise the cohesive zone, relatively small for the characteristics of steels (of the order of 1 mm)), the dynamic regime imposes supplementary rules. One proposes the following few practical pieces of advice, resting on the feedback of experience. One may also refer to the report [bib1].

- The integration scheme first of all: the Newmark scheme known as “average acceleration” ($d=1/2$, $a=1/4$), unconditionally stable and non-dissipative in a linear medium, seems the best suited (no numerical dissipation detected in the presence of cohesive zones). Very few tests have been tried with other schemes (explicit, Wilson, etc.) but they have led to convergence problems. A thesis is in progress (D. Doyen) in order to optimise schemes in the presence of shocks.
- As regards the time discretisation, everything depends on the phenomena that one seeks to simulate. In order to capture finely the reflections of the waves on the cohesive zone (causing a perturbation of the kinematics of the crack), one must (still for the usual characteristics of steels) consider time steps less than or equal to ${10}^{-8}s$. If a coarser tracking of the kinematics is sufficient and the important phenomenon is the initiation and the arrest, then steps of the order of ${10}^{-7}\le \Delta t\le {10}^{-5}s$ are sufficient. Above that, convergence problems appear ($\Delta t\approx {10}^{-6}s$ seems to be a good compromise).
- Very particular attention must be paid to the passage from a stable phase to an unstable phase (more precisely a phase in which the cohesive zone extends in a progressive manner, then in which it propagates in a sudden manner). In this case, one must adapt the time steps in order to capture the instant at which the cohesive zone accelerates, on pain of completely smoothing out “the dynamic effect”. The use of the piloting of the loading in quasi-statics, associated with the observation of the snap-back of the global response, may supply information on this critical instant.
- The small parameter PENA_ADHERENCE representing the stiffness of the joint before decohesion (regularisation parameter) must be taken less than ${10}^{-4}$. Indeed, below this value the results are no longer sensitive to this parameter.

In a general manner, the feedback of experience is limited in this field of application. The use of these models in dynamics remains, for the moment, intended for specialists.

### 4.7 Post-processing of the cracking and command POST_CZM_FISS

*Visualisation of the cracking for the* $\mathrm{EJ}$ *and* $\mathrm{EI}$

In order to visualise a cohesive crack, several solutions are possible:

- The internal variable at the Gauss point (VARI_ELGA) $\mathrm{V3}$ is a **cracking indicator** which makes it possible to know whether the Gauss point is sound ($\mathrm{V3}=0$), damaged ($\mathrm{V3}=1$) or broken ($\mathrm{V3}=2$). See the example in Figure 4.5.
- The variable $\mathrm{V4}$ varies continuously from 0 (sound) to 1 (broken); it corresponds to the **percentage of dissipated surface energy** and may be interpreted as a damage indicator. See the example in Figure 4.5.
- One may also visualise the values of the **displacement jump** in the three fracture modes I, II and III respectively from the internal variables $\mathrm{V7}$, $\mathrm{V8}$ and $\mathrm{V9}$.
- A **dissipation indicator** $\mathrm{V2}$ makes it possible to know whether the Gauss point dissipates energy or is unloading (the values taken by this internal variable depend on the cohesive law; see doc [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11)).
- Finally one may visualise the components of the cohesive force in the three fracture modes $I$, $\mathrm{II}$ and $\mathrm{III}$ respectively from the stress vector at the Gauss point SIEF_ELGA: SIGN, SITX and SITY. The sign of SIGN makes it possible to know whether the point is in contact or not.

Caution: for the $\mathrm{EJ}$ the visualisation of the tangential jumps in mode $\mathrm{II}$ and $\mathrm{III}$ (respectively $\mathrm{V8}$ and $\mathrm{V9}$) has no meaning, given that the tangential vectors of the local frame are independent from one element to another (see on this subject part 7).

For the $\mathrm{ED}$ the internal variables are not the same; the percentage of dissipated energy is stored in $\mathrm{V5}$ and the dissipation indicator in the variable $\mathrm{V4}$. POST_CZM_FISS *: computation of the length of cohesive cracks in 2D*

This command makes it possible to know, at each time instant, the size of the cohesive crack as well as the size of the cohesive zone from the coordinates of a point POINT_ORIG and of a direction vector VECT_TANG given by the user. One will refer to the U4 documentation of POST_CZM_FISS for more information. An example of use is proposed in the tests ssnp139a and ssnp139c.

*Computation of the crack length in 3D*

It is conceivable to extend the macro-command above to 3D, but this is not planned in the short term.

## 6 Conclusion

In a general manner, the use of the interface elements ($\mathrm{EI}$) is recommended when several choices are possible. Indeed, this approach combines the qualities of the two others and best meets the requirements of robustness, reliability and performance desired for models intended in due course for engineering. In dynamics, the EI model is in the course of validation; one will prefer to it the joint model. The latter may meet a certain number of expectations provided that one uses it with caution. Finally, the element with internal discontinuity has the advantage of not regularising the energy; on the other hand it accumulates the disadvantages of the other approaches. One advises using it only in very particular cases, for example for multi-cracking in a direction given *a priori* (see [bib5]).
