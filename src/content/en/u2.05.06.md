# u2.05.06 Carrying out quasi-static damage analyses (brittle fracture)

**Summary:**

This document aims to advise the user of *Code_Aster* on the carrying out of simulations using damage models and, in particular, on the techniques to be implemented in order to obtain a solution of good quality and in a robust manner.

## 1 Introduction and warning

To study the degradation of a structure, several tools are available and in particular (by increasing level of complexity and analysis time):

- fracture mechanics with initiation criteria based on stress criteria or the energy release rate;
- cohesive-zone models, which make it possible to study the initiation and the propagation of cracks when one knows a priori the potential cracking path;
- damage models, which make it possible to model the degradation of the material in a diffuse or localised manner.

User's guides for implementation in *Code_Aster* exist for the first 2 families of method. These are the documents [U2.05.01](doc:u2.05.01) and [U2.05.07](doc:u2.05.07). This document aims to complete the range of user's guides by concentrating on the implementation of quasi-static damage analyses.

However, it must be stated that, compared with the other techniques, damage analyses are the most complicated to implement, the most costly in computation time, but also those which are the least well mastered, both from the point of view of robustness and from the point of view of the quality of the result, in particular because:

- damage problems do not in general have a unique solution at the scale of the structure;
- the solutions obtained with the local damage models depend strongly on the mesh;
- one does not yet know how to propose means of identifying the characteristic lengths of the materials, and these lengths are not transferable from one regularisation method to another;
- the handling of broken mesh cells often poses a problem, in particular with the regularised models. Depending on the modellings, one may observe non-physical widenings of the damaged bands rather than the appearance of multi-cracking, or very severe convergence difficulties.

One must therefore always take a critical look at the results obtained.

## 2 Choice of the damage model

To represent the degradation of a material, or even its fracture, one of the possible methods (in particular when one does not know the failure mode) is to use a “softening” constitutive law, that is to say such that, once a threshold (in stress or in strain) has been passed, the stress decreases when the strain increases. This type of constitutive behaviour can be obtained:

- by introducing a damage variable $D$ lying between $0$ and $1$ (= damage mechanics as introduced by Kachanov or Lemaitre): the laws ENDO_FRAGILE, ENDO_ISOT_BETON, ENDO_ORTH_BETON, MAZARS in *Code_Aster*;
- by using plasticity models with a negative “hardening”, such as for example the laws BETON_DOUBLE_DP, DRUCK_PRAGER, but also the various soil laws available in *Code_Aster*;
- by introducing a variable such as the porosity coupled with plasticity for ductile fracture: the ROUSSELIER law in *Code_Aster*.

Whichever method is employed, all these “local” softening laws lead to the same difficulty: there comes a moment when the problem becomes ill-posed and the solution obtained becomes strongly dependent on the mesh. Indeed, the damage localises in a band whose thickness is a single finite element (whence a fracture energy which tends towards zero when one refines the mesh) and there is a dependence of the cracking “path” on the topology of the mesh. The relevance of these analyses (although still fairly widespread in the literature) is therefore highly questionable, and making the dissipated energy depend on the mesh-cell size provides only a very partial solution.

To get round this problem, it is commonly accepted that one must introduce into the problem to be solved a characteristic length, which will control the thickness of the damaged zone independently of the mesh and make it possible once again to have convergence of the solution when one refines the mesh.

Several regularisation methods have been developed in recent years, each of which has its advantages and its limits. In *Code_Aster*, the available methods all rely on a notion of gradient (as opposed to methods of integral type, also widely used in the literature).

One distinguishes the methods which introduce:

- the gradient of the damage variable (model GRAD_VARI, see documentation [R5.04.01](doc:r5.04.01));
- the second-gradient modellings and the dilatation second-gradient modellings, which introduce an energy depending wholly, or in part, on the components of the strain gradient (models 2DG and DIL, cf. doc [R5.04.03](doc:r5.04.03));
- the swelling-gradient modellings (model INCO_UPG, cf. doc [R3.06.08](doc:r3.06.08)).

In every case, the principle is to penalise, from an energy point of view, the localisation of the damage.

The table below summarises, for the various constitutive laws, which regularised modelling is available (and valid) in *Code_Aster*.

| Constitutive law | Modelling |
| --- | --- |
| ENDO_FRAGILE | GRAD_VARI / 2DG |
| ENDO_ISOT_BETON | GRAD_VARI / 2DG |
| MAZARS | 2DG |
| ENDO_ORTH_BETON | 2DG |
| DRUCK_PRAGER | 2DG/DIL |
| ROUSSELIER | INCO_UPG |

Table 1: correspondence between damage law and non-local modelling

Notes:

1. There is no non-local version of the model BETON_DOUBLE_DP. The implemented version does however include a regularisation of Hillerborg type in order to avoid the problem of the energy which tends towards zero when the size of the finite elements tends towards zero.
2. All the constitutive laws can be used with the modellings 2DG and DIL. However, the 2DG modelling has so far been used only with the soil laws, and the DIL modelling makes sense only for regularising “volumetric damage”: it is therefore well suited to treating the case of dilatant materials and hence of soils. Besides DRUCK_PRAGER, it is therefore possible to use the laws CAM_CLAY (which is a particular case of the Hujeux law) and HUJEUX (cf. thesis of Alexandre Foucault and CR-AMA-09-154), VISC_DRUCK_PRAG (see note H-T64-2009-03498) and LETK, BARCELONE, CJS, HOEK-BROWN; but the feedback of experience with these laws is still limited.
3. The modelling INCO_UPG is for the moment poorly mastered, and must be the subject of further studies and developments. Its use is not recommended.
4. The CPU cost of the regularised modellings is high, on the one hand because they require a fairly fine mesh (see § 3 ) and on the other because they introduce additional degrees of freedom, making the matrices to be inverted much larger and less sparse.

## 3 The mesh

For a mechanical analysis with local damage, one may use linear or quadratic meshes indifferently. On the other hand, it is advisable to have fairly homogeneous mesh-cell sizes so that the dissipation is likewise homogeneous (reminder: the dissipation in the local case is linked to the size of the mesh cell). It is also advisable to have carried out the identification of the post-peak parameters on tests using roughly the same mesh-cell sizes.

Most of the non-local models rely on quadratic meshes. The following table summarises the finite elements available for each modelling.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| GRAD_VARI | TRIA6 QUAD8 | TRIA6 QUAD8 | TETRA10 HEXA20 PENTA15 PYRAM13 |
| 2DG | TRIA7 QUAD9 |  |  |

Concerning the fineness of the mesh, one recalls that, for the regularisation of the problem to be effective, at least three mesh cells, or even ten mesh cells (for the GRAD_VARI modelling), are needed for a damage band. Thus, if the size of the damaged zone is $1\mathit{cm}$ , the size of the mesh cells in that zone must be less than $3\mathit{mm}$ , or even $1\mathit{mm}$ for GRAD_VARI.

The 3D version will be available in version 10

## 4 Solution of the problem with STAT_NON_LINE

The aim of this chapter is to give advice on the manner of solving softening problems with STAT_NON_LINE. A certain gradation of the tools is proposed here according to their originality, their effectiveness and their difficulties of implementation, ..., enabling a new user to gain his or her own experience. Depending on the constitutive law used, the modelling and the structure, it may be more judicious to use directly one or other of the methods (in particular piloting or the mixed line search). However, it is also possible to increase slightly the convergence criterion (RESI_GLOBA_RELA = ${10}^{-6}$  by default) in order to reduce the computation time and to help convergence for strongly damaged structures. One must not exceed ${10}^{-4}$ .

### 4.1 Simple Newton–Raphson (METHODE='NEWTON')

This is the simplest method to use, and the one which guarantees the quality of the solution obtained, provided of course that a sufficiently small convergence criterion is used. However:

1. the problems being softening, it is recommended to work in imposed displacement rather than in imposed force. Otherwise, as soon as the global force to be applied tends to decrease, there will no longer be any means of converging.
2. In the case of a brittle material, one often observes an instability of the solution, that is to say a sudden fracture of the material (snap-back in the global force–displacement response). In this case, there is little chance that Newton alone will get past this instability (except sometimes in the case of small instabilities or of the passage to the fully broken state), and one should switch to the mixed line search or, above all, to piloting.

Figure 1: example of an unstable response

It is recommended in general to activate the automatic subdivision of the time step (several levels, see the command DEFI_LIST_INST) and to use the tangent matrix (updated for laws of type ENDO_** , not necessarily updated for Mazars) but to allow, if need be, the switch to the secant matrix when one has subdivided the time step a great deal (keyword PAS_MINI_ELAS). Once the structure is strongly damaged, the secant matrix is sometimes more effective. It should be noted that, for the soil laws, it is pointless to update the secant matrix, because one uses the elastic matrix (REAC_ITER_ELAS=0).

With the tangent matrix, one may allow between 10 and 30 Newton iterations according to the constitutive law used (ITER_GLOB_MAXI). On the other hand, with the secant matrix, convergence is much slower and one must allow many more iterations (ITER_GLOB_ELAS100).

### 4.2 Newton–Raphson + line search

In certain cases, the classical line search (keyword RECH_LINEAIRE, METHODE='CORDE') can help convergence. But the MIXTE method is distinctly more robust.

### 4.3 Newton–Raphson + mixed line-search method

The mixed line-search method (keyword RECH_LINEAIRE, METHODE='MIXTE') is capable of seeking solutions further away than the basin of attraction of Newton and will thus make it possible to get past snap-backs. To avoid seeking solutions that are too remote (damage problems do not admit a unique solution), one advises:

- using small time steps,
- not activating the subdivision of the time step (or only very little), because in the presence of a large instability the size of the time step matters little
- allowing a large number of Newton iterations (ITER_GLOB_MAXI=1000),
- using the updated tangent matrix (REAC_ITER=1),
- allowing a large number of line-search iterations so as to give the method the opportunity to find the solution in the descent direction (ITER_LINE_MAXI=50)

This is in general the most robust method, but it can prove relatively costly, since one allows many iterations. On the other hand, it is in general no longer very effective when the structure is completely broken. In that case, it tends to perform many iterations and can sometimes artificially increase the size of the localisation band.

In the event of large snap-backs, corresponding to a sudden fracture of the specimen, identifiable on the force–displacement curve by a sudden decrease of the applied force, it is advisable to use piloting, which seeks a continuous solution in order to ensure the quality of the solution.

### 4.4 Piloting

Piloting, or the continuation method, is a method which makes it possible to follow the response of a structure in the event of instability (keyword PILOTAGE, see documentation [R5.03.80](doc:r5.03.80)). To do this, the intensity of the loading becomes a new unknown of the problem. Consequently, this method is not applicable when the problem depends explicitly on time (damage coupled with creep, thermal loading, etc.).

Two piloting modes suited to damage problems exist in Code_Aster. They are, on the one hand, piloting by strain increment (TYPE='DEFORMATION'), and, on the other hand, piloting by elastic prediction (TYPE='PRED_ELAS'). Piloting by elastic prediction is in general more efficient, but it is not generic (unlike piloting by strain); it is available for the laws ENDO_FRAGILE, ENDO_ISOT_BETON and ENDO_ORTH_BETON.

#### 4.4.1 Preliminary remarks valid for the 2 types of piloting

When you decide to pilot a loading, you no longer control its evolution in the course of time. Indeed, the intensity of the loading becomes an unknown of the problem: it may increase or decrease in the course of the increments. You can nevertheless fix the rapidity with which it will evolve, thanks to the time increments and to the parameter COEF_MULT (see further on).

You can pilot only one loading, or else several loadings which are proportional and therefore defined in the same occurrence of AFFE_CHAR_MECA and multiplied by the same multiplier function. (e.g. tension + shear simultaneously). Thus, if the forces are decomposed into two terms: the fixed forces ${F}_{\mathrm{fixe}}$ and the piloted forces ${F}_{\mathrm{pilo}}$ , the loading will be equal to $F={F}_{\mathit{fixe}}+\eta {F}_{\mathit{pilo}}$ where $\eta$ will be the intensity of the piloted force.

Let us suppose that the loading is composed of the boundary conditions and of a tensile force.

TRAC = AFFE_CHAR_MECA(DDL_IMPO=(_F(GROUP_MA='HAUT', DY = 0.0002)

FCT = DEFI_FONCTION( VALE = 0. , 0., 1., 1.)

If TRAC is the loading to be piloted, in STAT_NON_LINE it is sufficient to replace:

EXCIT=_F(CHARGE= TRAC, FONC_MULT=FCT)

by

EXCIT=_F(CHARGE= TRAC, TYPE_CHARGE='FIXE_PILO').

In the course of the time steps, you can follow the intensity of the loading thanks to the value ETA_PILOTAGE indicated in the convergence tables of STAT_NON_LINE (see example below). In our case, at the end of the time step considered, the displacement will be equal to:

$\mathit{dy}=0,0002\times \text{eta\_pilotage}=0,0002\times 8,66245\times {10}^{-3}$

You can control the bounds of the loading by fixing, in the keyword PILOTAGE, the parameters ETA_PILO_MAX and ETA_PILO_MIN. When these maximum or minimum values are reached, the analysis will stop, whatever the time instant. ETA_PILO_MIN is above all useful when one works with an imposed force, in order to stop the analysis when the force becomes very small (because the structure is broken), and ETA_PILO_MAX when one works with an imposed displacement, when the desired displacement has been reached.

One may also supply the values ETA_PILO_R_MIN and ETA_PILO_R_MAX, which fix the physical values for ETA. ETA_PILO_R_MIN is in particular indispensable for ENDO_FRAGILE, which presents the same constitutive behaviour in tension and in compression, on pain of jumping from one solution to the other.

Finally, it may be of interest to specify the GROUP_MA on which to carry out the piloting. Indeed, if the damage is localised, this can make it possible to limit the computation times. On the other hand, it is indispensable in the case where there are several constitutive laws in the model, some of which are not “pilotable” (for piloting of type PRED_ELAS).

In general, the analysis with piloting requires more time steps than a direct analysis, because one limits the evolution of the damage. If the analysis has stopped because it has reached the end of the list of time instants, and not the loading, it is sufficient to “extend” the initial list of time instants at the moment of the restart (POURSUITE) of the analysis.

#### 4.4.2 Advice on the implementation of PRED_ELAS piloting.

For the laws ENDO_FRAGILE and ENDO_ISOT_BETON, the coefficient COEF_MULT is related to the time step and to the minimum damage variation sought:

$\text{COEF\_MULT}=\frac{\Delta t}{\Delta D}$

Thus, if you wish that there be at least one point which sees its damage increase by 20%, and if your time steps are equal to $\Delta t=1$ , you must define $\text{COEF\_MULT}=\frac{1.}{0.2}=5$ . Thus, the smaller COEF_MULT is, the more rapidly the damage progresses.

For the other laws, the coefficient COEF_MULT ensures that at least one Gauss point leaves the linearised elasticity threshold ${f}_{\mathrm{pred}\text{\_}\mathrm{elas}}$ by a quantity $\frac{\Delta t}{\text{COEF\_MULT}}$ ; one therefore has $\text{COEF\_MULT}=\frac{\Delta t}{\underset{\text{pts de Gauss}}{\mathrm{MAX}}({f}_{\text{pred\_elas}})}$ . In this case, the value of the parameter to be supplied is not obvious and depends on the laws. One advises putting 1 to begin with. Depending on the progression of the damage observed, one will be able to accelerate the phenomenon by decreasing COEF_MULT or to slow it down by increasing COEF_MULT. One may also, in an equivalent manner, prefer to modify the list of time steps.

One advises in general opting for SELECTION='RESIDU',

When one pilots the loading, one should not hesitate to allow a great deal of subdivision (by using the command DEFI_LIST_INST), because certain passages are delicate.

#### 4.4.3 Advice on the implementation of DEFORMATION piloting.

For piloting in strain, it is obligatory to carry out at least one load increment without the piloting. One advises in general switching over to the piloting only when one no longer converges with the other methods.

The idea here is to ensure that at least one point of the structure sees its strain progress in a monotonic manner:

$\text{COEF\_MULT}\times \underset{\text{pts de Gauss}}{\mathit{MAX}}(\frac{{\varepsilon}^{-}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{\varepsilon}^{-}\mathrm{\parallel }}.\Delta \varepsilon )=\Delta t$ .

The value of COEF_MULT is not always easy to calibrate, but they are in general large values, since it is inversely proportional to the strain increment (example for steps of $0,1s$ and a strain increase of ${10}^{\text{-6}}$ , $\text{COEF\_MULT}=\frac{0,1}{{10}^{\text{-6}}}=100000$ ). After a first test, one will adjust if need be the value of this coefficient by increasing the value in order to load less quickly, and by decreasing it in order to damage more quickly. One may also prefer to act on the list of time steps.

### 4.5 Dynamics

As a last resort, one may also try to embark upon a dynamic analysis. In certain cases, this can provide solutions, but it is to be used with a great deal of caution. A U2 documentation has specially been written in order to gather the advice; it is the doc [U2.04.07](doc:u2.04.07), which it is indispensable to consult before embarking upon a simulation in dynamics (in order to solve a quasi-static problem).
