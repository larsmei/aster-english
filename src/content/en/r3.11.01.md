# r3.11.01 Formulation of a thermal model for thin shells

**Summary:**

The model presented here comes from the asymptotic analysis of the heat equations when the thickness of the structure tends to zero.

The temperature is described by 3 fields defined on the middle surface of the shell.

One shows, on a few examples, the capabilities of the model by reference to 3D solutions.

The applications aimed at are thermomechanical shell analyses, the thermal feedback of the wall for the thermohydraulics of pipework, and identification problems.

> **Contents**

## 1 Introduction

The mechanical models of thin structures (shells and plates) have reached an extremely advanced stage of development, at least for elastic structures that are homogeneous through the thickness. The problem has been known for a very long time and various theories have appeared, generally dedicated to specific problems (thick shells, buckling, etc.). However a basic model, that of LOVE-KIRCHHOFF, is unanimously accepted in the most common applications. The difficulties lie rather in its numerical analysis, because, on the one hand, of the necessity of approximating correctly the surface of the shell (in particular its curvature), and on the other hand of the high order of the partial differential equations which must be solved (4th order).

In heat transfer, on the other hand, the situation is much less clear and a large number of approaches coexist. It is indeed only recently that the problem has arisen with the possibilities (and the necessity) of thermomechanical analyses. The first models neglect conduction parallel to the middle surface in order to retain only the heat transfers through the thickness of the shell; this approach is entirely opposed to that of thin structures where, on the contrary, the small thickness of the structure leads to simplifying hypotheses on the variation through the thickness of the fields of physical quantities.

The most recent modellings are inspired by the mechanical ideas of thin shells and attach themselves to the second approach; one can classify them according to an entirely similar order.

1. Models involving a more or less extensive polynomial expansion of the temperature through the thickness , , . These are essentially finite-element formulations.
2. Models associated with the theories of surfaces with directors (COSSERAT surfaces) , . The director here is the gradient of the temperature through the thickness. The problem of these approaches lies in the constitutive law to be introduced. Consistency with the three-dimensional law leads to choices which are interpreted as a hypothesis of linear distribution of the temperature through the thickness. This formalism therefore practically rejoins the previous models (the introduction of several directors being identified with various orders of expansion of the polynomials).
3. Models of degenerated finite elements: starting from a three-dimensional finite element, the introduction of constraints between the degrees of freedom situated on one and the same normal to the middle surface makes it possible, by condensation, to deduce a “thermal shell” element. In practice, here again, the basic element using a parabolic interpolation through the thickness, the shell element corresponds to a linear distribution through the thickness.

In parallel with these numerical approaches (1) and (3) or those based on a priori hypotheses (2), results on the form of the temperature field of a thin plate and on the problem of which it is the solution have been obtained by asymptotic methods , .

As for the mechanical model, these make it possible to justify the a priori hypotheses made in the theories of thin shells, or even to obtain the equations of the shell problem. The results of  are recalled below and will serve as a basis for the model proposed. Let us simply note here that the idea underlying any approach of asymptotic type is to introduce a small parameter $\varepsilon$ (here the ratio of the thickness of the plate to a characteristic dimension of the latter), then, having obtained the limit problem when $\varepsilon$ tends to zero from the three-dimensional problem, to approximate in the applications (where $\varepsilon$ obviously takes a non-zero value) the solution by its limit.

From a practical point of view, the limit obtained for the equations of steady heat transfer seems to be too “poor” to be of real interest (an illustration will be given in [r3.11.01-application]). More precisely, the values of $\varepsilon$ to be reached in order to identify the solution with its limit are very small in the real situations encountered.

This is the reason why one proposes in this note to keep **the form of the limit solution**  (parabolic distribution through the thickness) **but to use it as an a priori hypothesis on the three-dimensional solution**  making it possible to reduce oneself to a problem posed on the middle surface.

One thus has an **approximate model of a thin structure converging towards the limit model of the three-dimensional equations** . In this sense, it is “optimal” since a hypothesis of linear distribution through the thickness leads to a model **which does not converge towards the limit solution**  and a model based on a richer expansion through the thickness sees its terms of order higher than two **converge towards zero**  when the shell is thin.

The plan of the note is as follows:

- one begins by recalling the equations of the steady thermal problem for the three-dimensional solid and their expressions in a coordinate system adapted to the cases where the solid is a “thin shell”,
- then, having recalled the results of an asymptotic study of these equations carried out in the case of a plate, one gives the complete description of the model proposed,
- one then applies the model to a certain number of geometries and thermal loadings and a comparison is made with respect to analytical solutions or three-dimensional numerical analyses,
- finally, one gives a few indications on the numerical aspects of the use of the model in an analysis by surface and line finite elements.

## 2 Presentation of the model

### 2.1 Statement of the thermal problem in shells

In this paragraph, we shall first of all recall the description of the geometry of shells, seen as thin three-dimensional solids. One will then pose the problem of heat conduction.

#### 2.1.1 Description of the geometry

A shell is defined as being a solid $\Omega$ , thin perpendicularly to a middle surface $\omega$ . One denotes by $\mathrm{2h}$ the thickness of the shell; one chooses a coordinate system $({x}_{1,}{x}_{2})$ on the surface $\omega$ . One denotes by $g$ the associated metric tensor, $\overrightarrow{n}$ the normal vector, $c$ the curvature tensor of $\omega$ .

![Figure](/figures/r3.11.01/10000000000003E6000001E3E893D05C520E65AC.png)

**Figure: 2.1.1-a**

The shell $\Omega$ is described by the coordinate system $({x}_{\gamma},{x}_{3})$ , ${x}_{3}$ along $\overrightarrow{n}$ : $\Omega =\omega \times \text{] - h, h[}$

(The Greek indices $\alpha ,\beta ,\gamma$ are dedicated to the surface coordinates on $\omega$ ).

This description is of course suitable for a shell of thickness $\mathrm{2h}$ smaller than the smallest radius of curvature of $\omega$ .

At an arbitrary point $({x}_{\gamma},{x}_{3})$ of the shell $\Omega$ , the metric tensor $G$ is expressed as a function of the fundamental tensors $g$ and $c$ of the middle surface $\omega$ by:

and $\begin{array}{cc}\sqrt{\detG}& =\sqrt{\detG}(1-{x}_{3}\text{tr}c)\\ & =\sqrt{\detG}(1+{x}_{3}(\frac{1}{{R}_{1}}+\frac{1}{{R}_{2}}))\end{array}$

where ${R}_{1,}{R}_{2}$ are the principal radii of curvature of $\omega$ at the point ${x}_{\gamma}$ considered.

**Note:**

*One knows indeed that the trace* $(\mathrm{tr})$ *of a tensor is an invariant (under change of basis). One is in the habit, however, of writing the quantities (grandeurs) in a physical basis, that is to say an orthonormal one. Thus: .* ${G}_{\alpha \beta }^{\text{phy}}({x}_{\gamma},{x}_{3})={\delta}_{\alpha \beta }-2{x}_{3}{C}_{\alpha \beta }^{\text{phy}}({x}_{\gamma})$

*And if the basis is a principal basis of curvature:* ${C}_{\alpha \beta }^{\text{phy}}=\frac{-1}{{R}_{\beta}}{\delta}_{\alpha \beta }$ *(without summation).*

One will denote: $\frac{1}{{R}_{1}}+\frac{1}{{R}_{2}}={H}_{1}$ and $\frac{1}{{R}_{1}}-\frac{1}{{R}_{2}}={H}_{2}$ .

One has limited oneself here to the terms of first order in ${x}_{3}\text{tr}C$ ; as one will do throughout what follows. In practice, indeed, the thinness of the shell allows such a simplification. It will also be advantageous to place oneself in a principal, orthonormal frame of curvature. The tensor $g$ is then the identity, $C$ is diagonal. This is what one will do henceforth.

#### 2.1.2 Heat equation

The equations of three-dimensional heat conduction are written (for a rigid conductor):

$-div(K\overrightarrow{\mathrm{grad}}T)+\rho {C}_{t}T=r$ **eq 2.1.2-1**

where $K$ denotes the conductivity tensor, $\rho C$ the heat capacity and $r$ the possible sources.

It is advantageous to write the expression of the differential operator according to the metric $G$ generated by the middle surface $\omega$ . One will indeed consider conductivity tensors $K$ transversely isotropic along these coordinate axes (cf. multilayer materials).

${K}_{j}^{i}=(\begin{array}{cc}{k}_{\beta}^{\alpha}& 0\\ 0& K\end{array}),{k}_{\beta}^{\alpha}\mathrm{et}K\mathrm{pouvant}\mathrm{varier}\mathrm{avec}{x}_{1,}{x}_{2,}{x}_{3}$

The expression of the operator:

$-div(K\overrightarrow{\mathrm{grad}}T)=-{(\detG)}^{-1/2}.{\partial}_{i}(\det{G}^{1/2}{K}_{j}^{i}{G}^{ij}{\partial}_{j}T)$

is then written, to first order in ${x}_{3}/\text{tr}c$ , for a conductivity orthotropic along the principal directions of curvature:

If the curvatures are constant, this becomes:

$-(1-2{x}_{3}/{R}_{1}).({\partial}_{1}({k}_{11}{\partial}_{1}T))-(1-2{x}_{3}/{R}_{2}).({\partial}_{2}({k}_{22}{\partial}_{2}T))-{H}_{1}(1-{x}_{3}{H}_{1})K{\partial}_{3}T-{\partial}_{3}(K{\partial}_{3}T)$

The effect of the curvature is therefore of the same type as a modified distribution of conductivity through the thickness.

#### 2.1.3 Heat transfer for a thin structure

The equations of steady heat transfer on the shell can be written in the form of a minimisation problem.

One assumes in particular that the boundary conditions on the edges $\partial \omega \times I$ of the shell $\Omega$ are of the same type over the whole thickness $I$ . One partitions $\partial \omega \times I$ into:

$\partial {\omega}_{T}\times I$ (zone with imposed temperature),

and $\partial {\omega}_{\varphi}\times I$ (zone with exchange condition or imposed flux).

One denotes:

- $V=\left\lbrace \theta \in {H}^{1}(\omega \times I),\theta =0\mathrm{sur}\partial {\omega}_{T}\times I\right\rbrace$ .
- The boundary conditions on ${\omega}^{+}\cup {\omega}^{-}\cup (\partial {\omega}_{\varphi}\times I)$ are of the exchange or imposed flux type $\varphi$ :

$\overrightarrow{\Phi}\overrightarrow{n}=\lambda T-\varphi =-(K\Delta T).\overrightarrow{n}$ $\lambda$ being an exchange coefficient.

The conductivity term in $A(T,\theta )$ is written:

${\int}_{\Omega}K\Delta \mathrm{T.}\Delta \theta d\Omega$

$={\int}_{\omega}{\int}_{I}\left[{k}_{\alpha \beta }(1-{x}_{3}(\frac{1}{{R}_{\alpha}}+\frac{1}{{R}_{\beta}})){\partial}_{\alpha}T{\partial}_{\beta}\theta +K{\partial}_{3}T{\partial}_{3}\theta \right](1+{x}_{3}{H}_{1}){\mathrm{dx}}_{1}{\mathrm{dx}}_{2}{\mathrm{dx}}_{3}$

this, in a principal orthonormal frame of curvature of $\omega$  (${k}_{\alpha \beta }$  and $K$ are then the physical components of the conduction tensor $K$ ).

The exchange terms on the surfaces ${\omega}^{+}$ and ${\omega}^{-}$ are:

${\int}_{{\omega}^{+}\cup {\omega}^{-}}\varphi .\theta d{\omega}^{\pm}={\int}_{\omega}{\lambda}^{\pm}{T}^{\pm}{\theta}^{\pm}.(1\pm h.{H}_{1}){\mathrm{dx}}_{1}{\mathrm{dx}}_{2}$

The object of a thermal shell model is therefore to reduce from three to two space variables the dependence of the temperature field $T$ in the expression of the differential operator corresponding to [] or [], subject to the choice and the justification of appropriate hypotheses.

### 2.3 Formulation of the steady thermal shell model

One has seen, from the results of the asymptotic study of the three-dimensional equations on the solid $\Omega =\omega \times I$ , that the limit model obtained comprised a mean temperature solution of a 2nd-order problem posed on $\omega$ , and that the additional parabolic term was determined only locally (point by point on $\omega$ ). This therefore had the disadvantage of supplying discontinuous solutions when the thermal “loadings” are discontinuous.

One therefore presents in this paragraph a representation of the temperature, still parabolic through the thickness, but avoiding the previous pitfall. One describes the equations obtained, and their properties.

#### 2.3.3 Link with the asymptotic model

One can easily verify that the model proposed here does indeed have as limit, when the thickness $\varepsilon h$ tends to $0$ , the results of the asymptotic expansion presented in [] and [].

One has seen the interest of the additional term $\rho$ for describing the evolutions of temperature through the thickness ${x}_{3}$ (whereas $\tilde{{T}_{1}}({x}_{\alpha})$ is constant through the thickness).

Now the previous result proves that the term $\hat{{T}_{2}}$ of the model with $2$ fields does not converge towards $\rho$ : one needs at least a representation with $3$ fields for that. Yet, knowing that the mechanical models of shells consider thermal strains affine through the thickness, one might have believed a thermal model with $2$ fields sufficient. One will see in [r3.11.01-plaque-infinie-couple-symetriques] an example illustrating (for a given thickness) the effect of the parabolic term ${T}_{3}$ on the mean temperature ${T}_{1}$ between the various models.
Other authors propose richer thermal models (cf. for example , , , probably interesting for thick shells, but whose terms higher than order 2 become useless for thin structures.

Indeed, as the previous diagram shows, the higher-order terms come only to correct (when $\varepsilon \ne 0$ ) expressions whose principal parts are given by ${T}_{1}$ on the one hand, ${T}_{2}$ and ${T}_{3}$ on the other hand. Qualitatively, they therefore bring nothing (unlike ${T}_{2}$ and ${T}_{3}$ ); quantitatively their contribution quickly becomes negligible in general compared with the principal parts.

## 3 Validation of the model on a few examples

One presents here applications on cylinders and plates. The first in fact treats a one-dimensional case through the thickness and makes it possible to evaluate the effect of the curvature terms, notably in the right-hand side of the equations. The others make it possible to judge the capacity of the model to treat the case of discontinuous thermal loadings, by reference to 3D solutions.

## 4 Remarks on the numerical discretisation

In this paragraph one limits oneself to a few observations concerning the numerical solution of the equations of the thermal shell model: first of all on the use of a finite-element method and then on the numerical locking appearing when the thickness $2h$ is small. The latter comes from the intervention of $h$ at different powers in the coefficients of the equations.

### 4.1 Solution by finite elements

The thermal shell model described in [r3.11.01-modele-thermique-stationnaire] presents the following characteristics:

- it leads to an operator of order $2$ acting on the three scalar fields $T=({T}^{m},{T}^{s},{T}^{i})$ ;
- these three fields are defined on a surface domain $\omega$ , embedded in ${ℝ}^{3}$ ;
- the curvature of the surface $\omega$ intervenes, possibly, only in the expression of the coefficients $A,B,C,D$ .

In the general case of a shell of arbitrary shape embedded in ${ℝ}^{3}$ , one can discretise the geometry of its middle surface $\omega$ by a mesh in plane triangular elements (this method certainly presents the defect of not being able to take into account explicitly the curvature of $\omega$ ).

The thermal problem (see [r3.11.01-synthese]) being scalar, with $3$ fields, of second order, one proposes the usual finite elements: the plane triangles $\mathit{P1}$ (with $3$ nodes) or $\mathit{P2}$ (with $6$ nodes).

Their formulation is the same, whether $\omega$ is plane or curved: one thus neglects the metric corrections in the stiffness operators $A$ and $B$ (one has seen in the validation cases that this had little effect in practice). On the other hand the user, if he knows the expression of the curvature, will have an interest in taking it into account in the values of the coefficients ${\lambda}^{\pm}$ and of the fluxes ${\varphi}^{\pm}$ , as in the expressions [] and [].

In the case of composite materials (just as if one wished to take the curvature into account), one has to provide for a pre-processing supplying the coefficients $A,B,C,D$ , as well as a post-processing making it possible to reconstruct the temperature and the fluxes at any point of the thickness.

There exist situations where the problem no longer depends on more than one space variable: these are the shells of revolution with axisymmetric loading, or the “slices of shells”, of axis $\overrightarrow{{e}_{3}}$ .

The geometry is then represented by a meridian: (see [r3.11.01-fig-meridien]). The mean curve is then:

- revolution case:            ${H}_{1}=(\frac{1}{R}+\frac{\cos\alpha }{x})$
- “slice”, or arc, case:    ${H}_{1}=\frac{1}{R}$

where $R$ denotes the radius of curvature of the meridian line $AB$ .

![Figure](/figures/r3.11.01/10000000000000F0000000CA7FE13416096F7C7A.png)

*Geometry represented by a meridian*

For these types of problems, one also proposes a finite element $\mathit{P2}$ with $3$ nodes, using the same formulation, where one neglects the metric correction through the thickness, for the coefficients $A$ and $B$ . One uses a quadrature formula with 4 GAUSS points.

This element is exactly associated with the one proposed in mechanics for chained thermomechanical studies [r3.07.02](doc:r3.07.02).

### 4.2 Numerical locking of a thermal shell finite element

Locking is a phenomenon appearing in the numerical solution by finite elements of certain problems such as that of thin shells or of arcs when the element is curved (membrane locking), that of shells or of beams with taking shear into account (shear locking), or again that of plasticity (plastic incompressibility locking). It was encountered initially in the mechanics of incompressible fluids and it is in this framework that its theoretical study began.

This locking phenomenon manifests itself by a very great loss of precision and important oscillations on certain computed quantities when a physical parameter of the model becomes “small”. The illustration of these inconveniences is given in the note HI-71/7131, (§4.2). The origin of these problems lies in the difference of order of magnitude which appears between certain components of the bilinear “stiffness” form when the physical or geometrical parameter tends to zero (thickness of the shell for membrane locking, inverse of the tangent compressibility modulus for plastic locking for example). Here, it is the thickness of the shell which will play the role of small parameter.

Let us take up again the equations of the steady thermal problem posed on a plate in variational form; let us denote by $2\varepsilon h$ its thickness ($\varepsilon$  a dimensionless real):

with:

> $A(T,\theta )=\mathrm{2kh}{\int}_{\omega}[\nabla T].\left[\begin{array}{ccc}1& 0& 0\\ 0& \frac{1}{3}& 0\\ 0& 0& \frac{1}{5}\end{array}\right].[\nabla \theta ]d\omega$

The $.$ denoting the usual scalar product,

> $B(T,\theta )=\frac{\mathrm{2K}}{h}{\int}_{\omega}[T].\left[\begin{array}{ccc}1& 0& 0\\ 0& 1& 0\\ 0& 0& 3\end{array}\right].[\theta ]d\omega$
> $[\nabla \theta ]=(\overrightarrow{\nabla}{\theta}_{1},\overrightarrow{\nabla}{\theta}_{2},\overrightarrow{\nabla}{\theta}_{3})\text{(gradients surfaciques)}$

An equivalent mixed formulation of this problem is obtained with the variables ${\mathrm{q}}_{2}$ and ${\mathrm{q}}_{3}$ , heat fluxes through the thickness (cf. [An 2]): one denotes $\theta ={({L}^{2}(\omega ))}^{2}$ .

where:

$\begin{array}{}[q]=({q}_{2,}{q}_{3})\\ M(\theta ,q)={\int}_{\omega}({\theta}_{2}{q}_{2}+{\theta}_{3}{q}_{3})d\omega \\ \bar{B}(p,q)=\frac{h}{v}\mathrm{2K}{\int}_{\omega}[p]\left[\begin{array}{cc}1& 0\\ 0& \frac{1}{3}\end{array}\right][q]d\omega \end{array}$

On this formulation, the numerical locking appears clearly (at least formally). Indeed, the discretisation of $W\times Q$ having been carried out (one denotes it ${W}_{d}\times {Q}_{d}$ ), the problem tends formally when $\varepsilon$ tends to zero towards the following problem:

> $\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}A({T}_{d},\theta )-M(\theta ,{p}_{d})& =F(\theta )\forall \theta \in {W}_{d}\\ M({T}_{d},q)& =0\text{}\forall q\in {Q}_{d}\end{array}$

Which amounts to solving on the kernel of $M$ :

The locking appears when the discretised kernel of $M$ is too small or reduced to zero: the solution of [] is done on a very small space or even reduced to zero. Even if the mesh is fine, the solution is then of very poor quality.

The kernel of $M$ in ${W}_{d}$ being by definition the space:

> $\mathrm{Ker}{M}_{d}=\left\lbrace \theta \in {W}_{d}\mid M(\theta ,q)=0\text{}\forall q\in {Q}_{d}\right\rbrace$

One sees that the choice of the discretisation of $Q$ is not innocent and strongly conditions the behaviour of the solution when $\varepsilon$ tends to zero. There exists a convergence condition bearing on the spaces ${W}_{d}$ and ${Q}_{d}$ which assures the good numerical behaviour of the solution with  small; it is the so-called discrete LBB condition, a version adapted to the discrete case of the continuous LBB condition (LADYJENSKAIA - BREZZI - BABUCHKA). We refer to  for a case study (plasticity) and a bibliography on this subject.

In parallel with the theoretical studies rapidly mentioned above, a practical remedy for the locking, appearing once the discretisation in ${W}_{d}$ has been chosen if this choice has been unfortunate, consists in under-integrating the “locking” term in the construction of the stiffness, that is to say the term B here. Certain choices of under-integration, in the primal formulation [] are interpreted as choices of interpolation of ${W}_{d}$ and ${Q}_{d}$ in the mixed formulation and can thus, via the verification (sometimes laborious) of the discrete LBB condition, be justified on the theoretical level.

Let us indeed consider a triangular finite element with 3 nodes and interpolation $\mathit{P1}$ in order to solve the problem []. Let us then choose for the discretisation of $Q$ a discontinuous interpolation $\mathit{P0}$ , that is to say a representation of $[q]$ constant per element.

The second equation of [] is then a local equation, that is to say to be solved on each element separately since $p$ is arbitrary on each element $E$ .

$-\frac{\varepsilon h}{\mathrm{2K}}{\int}_{E}({p}_{2}{q}_{2}+\frac{1}{3}{p}_{3}{q}_{3})-{\int}_{E}{T}_{2}{q}_{2}+{T}_{3}{Q}_{3}=0\text{}\forall ({q}_{2,}{q}_{3})$

whence the immediate solution if $\mid E\mid$ is the area of $E$ .

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}{p}_{2}=-\frac{\mathrm{2K}}{\varepsilon h}\frac{1}{\mid E\mid }{\int}_{E}{T}_{2}\\ {p}_{3}=-\frac{\mathrm{6K}}{\varepsilon h}\frac{1}{\mid E\mid }{\int}_{E}{T}_{3}\end{array}$

Reporting these results in the form $M$ , one has on the element $E$ :

> $\mathrm{M}(\theta ,\mathrm{p})=\frac{\mathrm{2K}}{\varepsilon \mathrm{h}}\left[\frac{1}{\mid \mathrm{E}\mid }{\int}_{\mathrm{E}}{\mathrm{T}}_{2}{\int}_{\mathrm{E}}{\theta}_{2}+\frac{3}{\mid \mathrm{E}\mid }{\int}_{\mathrm{E}}{\mathrm{T}}_{3}{\int}_{\mathrm{E}}{\theta}_{3}\right]$ for any $\theta \in {W}_{d}$

having thus eliminated $p$ , one is brought back to a primal formulation on $T$ only:

$M(\theta ,p)=\frac{\mathrm{2K}}{\varepsilon h}\left[\frac{1}{\mid E\mid }{\int}_{E}{T}_{2}{\int}_{E}{\theta}_{2}+\frac{3}{\mid E\mid }{\int}_{E}{T}_{3}{\int}_{E}{\theta}_{3}\right]\text{pour tout}\theta \in {W}_{d}$

which corresponds very exactly to the formulation [] in which the elementary term:

${B}_{E}(T,\theta )=\frac{\mathrm{2K}}{\varepsilon h}{\int}_{E}({T}_{2}{\theta}_{2}+3{T}_{3}{\theta}_{3})$

is under-integrated by a scheme with one GAUSS point:

${\int}_{E}\mathrm{f.g}=\frac{1}{\mid E\mid }.{\int}_{E}f.{\int}_{E}g.$

## 5 Conclusion

An asymptotic analysis of the heat equations in a thin structure when the thickness tends to zero leads to a limit model characterised by a mean temperature, solution of a boundary-value problem, and a complementary parabolic term through the thickness, defined locally.

One has deduced from it the formulation of a model with $3$ scalar fields defined on the middle surface of the shell, giving a parabolic representation of the temperature through the thickness. The differential operator obtained is of order $2$ ; the thickness of the shell appears in its coefficients.

This model appears as “optimal” for thin structures:

- its limit when the thickness tends to zero is identical to the asymptotic limit model;
- any additional terms would tend towards zero with the thickness.

In a standard version the curvature of the middle surface of the shell does not intervene directly. Test examples show a good agreement of the temperature obtained with complete three-dimensional solutions.

This model therefore appears entirely qualified to:

- be used in a finite-element formulation in order to compute the temperature in a thin shell of arbitrary shape; the solution obtained being able to be easily injected into a thermomechanical analysis of the shell; one thus proposes surface and line elements for the cases where one space variable does not intervene;
- be introduced directly (or by coupling) into a method of solution of the equations governing the thermohydraulic state of a pipework for example, in order to take into account the thermal feedback of the wall on the fluid;
- be used as an integrated model in the solution of identification problems (inverse problem) from experimental measurements (for example for stratified pipes);
- look for analytical solutions in cases with simple geometry.

The model described here can also be used in thermal evolution problems, provided that the thermal loadings do not vary too rapidly.

Finally, it remains to study the numerical methods to be used in order to avoid the locking which could appear in a finite-element analysis, when the thickness becomes small.

**Mixed formulation of the steady problem for the plate**

In the case of the plate, the variational problem [] is equivalent to a minimisation problem (by bringing out explicitly the thickness $\varepsilon h$ in $B$ ) of the functional:

> $J(\theta )=\frac{1}{2}A(\theta ,\theta )+\frac{1}{2\varepsilon }B(\theta ,\theta )-F(\theta )$

In order to obtain the mixed formulation, let us remark that:

**Proposition:**

$\frac{1}{2\varepsilon }B(\theta ,\theta )=\underset{({q}_{2,}{q}_{3})\in {\left[{L}^{2}(\omega )\right]}^{2}}{\text{Sup}}\left[-{\int}_{\omega}({q}_{2}{\theta}_{2}+{q}_{3}{\theta}_{3})-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\frac{h}{K}{\int}_{\omega}({q}_{1}^{2}+\frac{1}{3}{q}_{3}^{2})\right]$

**Proof:**

Let us write the extremality condition of the functional in brackets (its opposite is strictly convex, coercive and lower semi-continuous) and let us denote by p the pair where the sup is attained:

> ${q}_{2}{\theta}_{2}+{q}_{3}{\theta}_{3}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{h}{K}({q}_{2}{p}_{2}+\frac{1}{3}{q}_{3}{p}_{3})=0\text{}\forall q$

whence: $\lbrace \begin{array}{}{p}_{2}=-\frac{2K}{\varepsilon h}{\theta}_{2}\\ {p}_{3}=-\frac{6K}{\varepsilon h}{\theta}_{3}\end{array}$

The value of the functional at this point is therefore:

${\int}_{\omega}+\frac{\mathrm{2K}}{\varepsilon h}{\theta}_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{6K}}{\varepsilon h}{\theta}_{3}^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon h}{4K}\left[\frac{4{K}^{2}}{{\varepsilon}^{2}{h}^{2}}{\theta}_{2}^{2}+\frac{{\mathrm{36K}}^{2}}{3{\varepsilon}^{2}{h}^{2}}{\theta}_{3}^{2}\right]=\frac{1}{\varepsilon h}\left[\frac{\mathrm{2K}}{h}{\theta}_{2}^{2}+\frac{\mathrm{3K}}{h}{\theta}_{3}^{2}\right]$

that is indeed the announced result.

One therefore has a formulation equivalent to the minimisation of $J$ on $W$ :

> $\underset{\theta \in \mathrm{W}}{\mathrm{Min}}\underset{\mathrm{q}\in \mathrm{Q}}{\mathrm{Max}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{A}(\theta ,\theta )-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\bar{\mathrm{B}}(\mathrm{q},\mathrm{q})-\mathrm{M}(\theta ,\mathrm{q})-\mathrm{F}(\theta ).\right]$

denoting:

> $M(\theta ,q)={\int}_{\omega}{q}_{2}{\theta}_{2}+{q}_{3}{\theta}_{3},\bar{B}(q,q)={\int}_{\omega}\frac{h}{\mathrm{2K}}({p}_{2}{q}_{2}+\frac{1}{3}{p}_{3}{q}_{3})$

The saddle-point condition of this Lagrangian leads to the formulation []:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}A(T,\theta )-M(\theta ,p)=F(\theta )\text{}\forall \theta \in W\\ -\varepsilon \bar{B}(p,q)-M(T,q)=0\text{}\forall q\in Q\end{array}$
