# r4.10.03 Spatial residual error indicator for transient thermal analysis

**Summary:**

In numerical simulations by finite elements, obtaining a raw result is no longer sufficient. The user increasingly requires a **spatial error calculation with respect to the mesh**. **Methodological support and advanced “numerical-computational” tools are needed in order to gauge the quality of studies and to improve them.**

To this end, a posteriori spatial error indicators make it possible to locate, on each element, an error map on which the remeshing tools can rely: a first analysis on a coarse mesh makes it possible to recover the error map from the discretised data and from the discretised solution (hence the term “a posteriori”), the refinement then being carried out locally by ranking this information.

The **new a posteriori indicator** (referred to as “pure residual”) which has just been implemented **in order to post-process the thermal solvers of** **Code_Aster** is based on their local residuals extracted from the semi-discretisations in time. Via the option ‘ERTH_ELEM’ of CALC_ERREUR, it uses the thermal fields (EVOL_THER) coming from THER_LINEAIRE and from THER_NON_LINE. **It thus completes the range of advanced tools offered by the code, making it possible to improve the quality of studies, their pooling and their comparisons**.

**The aim of this note** is to set out in detail the theoretical, numerical and software work which governed its implementation. As regards the theoretical study we have, in a first stage, restricted ourselves to the linear thermal analysis of a fixed structure discretised by the standard isoparametric finite elements. In practice, however, **the scope of use of this option has been partially extended to non-linear thermal analysis**.

The reader is given the theoretical and practical properties and limitations of the indicator obtained, while relating these considerations, which may sometimes appear somewhat “ethereal”, to a precise parameter setting of the operators concerned and to the modelling choices of the code. An attempt has been made constantly to link the various items addressed, while setting out, at least in outline, somewhat technical proofs that are rarely made explicit in the specialist literature.

## 1 Problem statement – Description of the document

In numerical simulations by finite elements, obtaining a raw result is no longer sufficient. **The user increasingly requires a spatial error calculation with respect to the mesh**. **Methodological support and advanced numerical tools are needed in order to gauge the quality of studies and to improve them.**

For example, the accuracy of the results is often degraded by local singularities (corners, heterogeneities…). One then seeks the right strategy for identifying these critical regions and for refining/coarsening them in order to optimise the local/global error compromise. And this with the greatest possible accuracy, automatically, reliably (the error calculation must itself be as little approximate as possible!), robustly and at the lowest cost.

For each type of finite element, one generally has **a priori estimates of the spatial error**, . But these are verified only asymptotically (when the size $h$ of the elements tends to zero) and they require a certain level of regularity which is precisely not attained in the problem regions. Moreover, these upper bounds underlie two types of strategy for improving the analysis:

- the $\text{p}$-methods, which consist in locally increasing the order of the finite elements,
- the $\text{h}$-methods, which refine locally in order to reduce the geometric characteristics of the elements.

We are interested here in the second strategy, but through another class of indicators: the **a posteriori error indicators**. Since the founding work of I. BABUSKA and W.RHEINBOLDT , the importance of this type of indicator is well established and they are attracting growing interest, both in pure numerical analysis , , and in the field of applications , . They have in particular been implemented and used in N3S, TRIFOU and *code_aster* (for linear mechanics cf. [r4.10.01](doc:r4.10.01), [r4.10.02](doc:r4.10.02)). For a survey of the existing indicators, one may consult the reference work of R. VERFURTH or the report of X. DESROCHES .

To take up an argument of M.FORTIN (Cf. pp468-469), the development of a posteriori estimation is motivated principally by three reasons:

- the first is the need to establish the accuracy of the results obtained by a finite-element analysis: how much credit should be given to them? Are all the phenomena and all the data involved properly taken into account in the modelling?
- the second objective is to allow anyone to use an analysis code without having to intervene in the construction of the mesh in order to obtain the required global accuracy,
- finally, the third line of study is more particularly oriented towards three-dimensional problems, for which the size of the meshes is limited by the available memory space and by the cost of the solution.

**These specifications bring out two dual problems**: estimating the accuracy of the solution obtained on the principal parameters of the simulation, and proposing means of calculating a new solution which respects a minimum accuracy. The first problem is truly that of error estimation, whereas the second concerns the associated adaptive methods (refinement/coarsening, remeshing, displacement of points, boundary tracking…).

These indicators thus make it possible to locate on each element an error map on which the remeshing tools can rely.

**Note:**

*The name “indicator” is preferred to the usual terminology “estimator” (a literal translation of the English “error estimator”). In view of the fact that it has the same theoretical limitations as those of the finite-element solver (which it “post-processes”), that it is itself often marred by numerical approximations and that it is obtained via equivalence relations involving numerous constants depending on the problem… the information which it carries truly gives “only an order of magnitude” of the spatial error sought. Despite these restrictions, these a posteriori error maps remain no less important, and in any case they constitute the only type of information available in this field.*

A first analysis on a coarse mesh makes it possible to associate, with each element of the triangulation, an indicator calculated from the discretised data and from the first discrete solution. The refinement is then carried out locally by ranking this information.

In short, and in a non-exhaustive way, **the use of an indicator possibly coupled with a remesher:**

- provides a certain estimate of the spatial discretisation error,
- procures a better distribution of the error due to local singularities,
- makes it possible to improve the modelling of the data of the problem (materials, loadings, sources…),
- makes it possible to optimise (the same accuracy at the lowest cost) and to make reliable the mesh-convergence process,
- to estimate and to qualify an analysis for a given class of mesh.

These considerations clearly show that the calculation of these estimators (which is ultimately only a post-processing of the problem considered) must:

- be much less costly than that of the solution,
- require only the discretised data and the calculated solution,
- be capable of being localised,
- be equivalent (in a particular form) to the exact error.

**We shall see that, with residual indicators, one can obtain only a global upper bound on the exact error together with a local lower bound on that same error**. But these upper and lower bounds on the error complement each other because the first assures us that a solution has been obtained within a certain tolerance, while the second allows us to optimise locally the number of points in order to respect this accuracy and not to overestimate it. They involve constants which do not depend on the spatial and temporal discretisations.

**The aim of this note is to set out in detail the theoretical, numerical and software work which governed the implementation of an a posteriori error indicator making it possible to “post-process” the thermal solvers of** **Code_Aster. It is a pure residual indicator initiated by the option ‘ERTH_ELEM’ of** **CALC_ERREUR.**

As regards the theoretical study we have, in a first stage, restricted ourselves to the linear thermal analysis of a fixed structure discretised by the standard isoparametric finite elements. In practice, however, the scope of use of this option has been partially extended to non-linear thermal analysis. For further details on the scope of use and on the functional scope of the thermal indicator, and for an example of use, one may refer to [r4.10.03-mise-oeuvre-code-aster].

**The a posteriori indicator which we propose is a pure residual indicator based on the local residuals of the strong equation semi-discretised in time**. For certain elements of the theoretical study (and in particular its framework) we have drawn inspiration from the innovative work of C.BERNARDI and B. METIVET . They themselves extended, from the elliptic to the parabolic case, the results of R. VERFURTH . They were interested in particular in the calculation of indicators on the model case of the heat equation with a homogeneous Dirichlet condition, semi-discretised in time by an implicit Euler scheme. We have extended these results to the problems actually treated by the linear thermal operator of the code, THER_LINEAIRE. These are inhomogeneous mixed boundary-value problems (Cauchy-Dirichlet-Neumann-Robin), linear, with variable coefficients and discretised by a $\theta$-method.

**A thorough piece of work has therefore been carried out in order to grasp properly the theoretical mechanisms of the underlying thermal problem and in order to extrapolate the results of the preceding model problem**. This is so as to try to approach the modellings and the scope of the code, while setting out mathematical subtleties that are often glossed over in the papers of the field. A particular effort has been made to put into perspective the choices made in *Code_Aster* with respect to past and current research, and to make explicit the general philosophy of these indicators.

The reader is given the theoretical and practical properties and limitations of the indicators obtained, while relating these considerations to a precise parameter setting of the operator CALC_ERREUR involved in this post-processing. An attempt has been made constantly to link the various items addressed, to limit the recourse to long mathematical digressions, while setting out at least in outline numerous somewhat “technical” proofs that are rarely made explicit in the specialist literature.

## 2 The boundary-value problem

### 2.1 Context

Consider a **fixed body occupying a bounded connected open set** $O$ of ${R}^{q}$ ($q$ = 2 or 3) with **regular boundary** $\partial \Omega :=\Gamma :=\underset{i=1}{\overset{3}{\cup}}{\Gamma}_{i}$, characterised by its volumetric heat capacity at constant pressure $\rho {C}_{p}(x)$ (the vector variable $x$ here stands for the pair $(\text{x,y})$ (resp. $(x,y,z)$) for $q$ = 2 (resp. $q$ = 3)) and its isotropic thermal conductivity coefficient $\lambda (x)$.

**Note:**

*No account will therefore be taken of a possible displacement of the structure (cf. THER_NON_LINE_MO* [r5.02.04](doc:r5.02.04)).

These material data are assumed independent of time (THER modelling of *Code_Aster*) and constant per element (${P}_{0}$ discretisation).

**Note:**

*With the* *THER_FO* *modelling these characteristics may depend on time. From the very first versions of the code, and before the introduction of* *THER_NON_LINE, it made it possible to simulate “pseudo” non-linearities. In view of its rather marginal use, this modelling will not be considered here.*

One is interested in the evolutions of the temperature at every point $x$ of the open set and at every time instant $t\in [0,\tau [(\tau >0)$, when the body is subjected to boundary conditions and to loadings independent of the temperature but which may depend on time. These are a volumetric source $s(x\text{,t})$, boundary conditions of prescribed-temperature type $f(x\text{,t})$ (on the portion of external surface ${\Gamma}_{1}$), imposed normal flux $g(x\text{,t})$ (on ${\Gamma}_{2}$) and convective exchange $h(x\text{,t})$ and ${T}_{\text{ext}}(x\text{,t})$ (on ${\Gamma}_{3}$).

One thus places oneself within the scope of application of the operator THER_LINEAIRE [r5.02.01](doc:r5.02.01) of *Code_Aster*, retaining only the conductive aspects of this linear thermal problem.

**Note:**

Non-linearities pose serious theoretical problems for proving the existence, uniqueness and stability of the possible solution. Some are still completely open… But in practice, this in no way prevents one from “stretching” the scope of use of the error estimator which will be derived rigorously for linear thermal analysis, to non-linear thermal analysis (operator *THER_NON_LINE* [r5.02.02](doc:r5.02.02)).

This **mixed** boundary-value problem (of inhomogeneous **Cauchy-Dirichlet-Neumann-Robin** type (also called a Fourier condition), linear and with variable coefficients) is formulated

$$
(P_{0}) \left(    \begin{array}{l}   \rho C_{p} \dfrac{\partial T}{\partial t} - \operatorname{div}(\lambda \nabla T) = s \quad \Omega \times ]0,\tau[ \\   T = f \quad \Gamma_{1} \times ]0,\tau[ \\   \lambda \dfrac{\partial T}{\partial n} = g \quad \Gamma_{2} \times ]0,\tau[ \\   \lambda \dfrac{\partial T}{\partial n} + hT = hT_{\text{ext}} \quad \Gamma_{3} \times ]0,\tau[ \\   T(x,0) = T_{0}(x) \quad  \Omega   \end{array}   \right.
$$

**Notes:**

- *In this theoretical study of the mixed problem* $({P}_{0})$ *, one assumes that the boundary splits into portions on which a non-homogeneous boundary condition necessarily acts. This hypothesis is in fact not essential, and one may assume the existence of a portion* ${\Gamma}_{4}$ *, such that* ${\Gamma}_{4}:=\Gamma -\underset{i=1}{\overset{3}{\cup}}{\Gamma}_{i}\ne \varnothing$ *, on which a homogeneous Neumann condition acts (thus, when one constructs the variational formulation associated with the strong formulation* $({P}_{0})$ *, the boundary terms linked to this zone disappear. The problem then remains well posed, since it is thermally unconstrained in this zone. In the software, this is indeed what happens, since the boundary terms are initialised to zero). In practice, this is moreover often the case.*
- *One will assume that the exchange coefficient* $h(\text{t,}\mathrm{x})$ *is positive, which is the case in* code_aster (cf. [u4.44.02](doc:u4.44.02) §4.7.3). And this will make things a little easier for us in the proofs to come (cf. for example property 5).
- *The Robin condition modelling the convective exchange (keyword* *ECHANGE* *) on a portion of the boundary of the domain may be duplicated in order to take account of exchanges between two facing sub-parts of the boundary (keyword* *ECHANGE_PAROI* *). This boundary condition models an interface thermal resistance*

$$
\text{Avec } \Gamma_3 = \Gamma_{12} \cup \Gamma_{21}, \quad T_i = T_{\mid \Gamma_{ij}}, \text{ on a }   \left\lbrace   \begin{array}{ll}   \lambda \dfrac{\partial T_1}{\partial n} + h T_1 = h T_2 & \text{sur } \Gamma_{12} \times ]0,\tau[ \\   \lambda \dfrac{\partial T_2}{\partial n} + h T_2 = h T_1 & \text{sur } \Gamma_{21} \times ]0,\tau[   \end{array}   \right.
$$

  *So as not to weigh down the writing of the problem, and in so far as this option is similar to the Robin condition with the external medium, we shall not mention it specifically in the calculations which follow.*

- *The Dirichlet condition may be generalised in the form of linear relations between the degrees of freedom (keywords* *LIAISON_** *) in order to simulate, in particular, geometric symmetries of the structure.*

$$
\text{Avec } \Gamma_{1} = \Gamma_{12} \cup \Gamma_{21}, \quad T_{i} = T_{\mid \Gamma_{ij}} \text{ on a (LIAISON\_GROUP)}
\sum_{i} \beta_{1i} T_{1}^{i}(x,t) + \sum_{j} \beta_{2j} T_{2}^{j}(x,t) = \gamma(x,t) \quad \text{sur } \Gamma_{1} \times ]0,\tau[
\text{ ou plus simplement } \sum_{i} \beta_{i} T_{i}(x,t) = \gamma(x,t) \quad \text{sur } \Gamma_{1} \times ]0,\tau[
$$

  *Likewise the functionalities* *LIAISON_UNIF* *and* *LIAISON_CHAMNO* *make it possible to impose one and the same (unknown) temperature on a set of nodes. They constitute an additional layer on the preceding conditions by imposing particular pairs* $(\beta  ,\gamma )$ *. So as not to weigh down the writing of the problem, and in so far as these options are only particular cases of the generic Dirichlet condition, we shall not mention them specifically in the calculations which follow.*

- *When the material is* **anisotropic** *the conductivity is modelled by a diagonal matrix expressed in the orthotropy frame of the material. This does not fundamentally change the following calculations, which take account only of the isotropic case. One* *must simply take care no longer to commute, in the Neumann and Robin boundary conditions, the scalar product with the normal and the multiplication by the conductivity.*
- *For a* **transient analysis** *, the initial temperature may be chosen in three different ways: by carrying out a steady-state analysis at the first* *time instant, by fixing it at a uniform or arbitrary value created by a* *CREA_CHAMP*, *and by performing a restart from a previous transient analysis. This choice of the Cauchy condition has no bearing on the theoretical study which follows.*
- *We shall not treat the case in which (almost) all the loadings are multiplied by one and the same time-dependent function (option* *FONC_MULT* *; this functionality, well suited to certain mechanical problems, is not recommended in thermal analysis, because it may conflict with the time dependence of the loadings and, moreover, it applies selectively to each of them. It has moreover not been taken over into* *THER_NON_LINE* *).*

One shows that the most general and most convenient functional framework for “getting to grips” with this parabolic problem is the following.

| --- | --- | --- |
| $\Gamma$ | manifold of dimension $q-1$, Lipschitz or piecewise ${C}^{1}$ | (H2) |

**Note:**

*Let* $(X,{\parallel \parallel }_{X})$ *be a Banach space; one denotes by* ${L}^{p}(0,\tau ;X)$ *the space of functions* $t\to v(t)$ *strongly measurable for the measure* $\text{dt}$ *such that* ${\parallel \nu  \parallel }_{0,\tau ;p,X}={({\int}_{0}^{\tau}{\parallel \nu  (t)\parallel }_{X}^{p}\text{dt})}^{\frac{1}{p}}\text{<+}\infty$ *. It is a Banach space, and therefore a Hilbert space for the associated norm.*

The introduction of these particular “space-time” Hilbert spaces stems from the **need to separate the variables** $x$ and $t$. Any function $u:(x,t)\in {Q}_{\tau}:=\Omega \times ]0,\tau [\to u(x,t)\in \Re$ may in fact be identified (using Fubini’s theorem) with another function $\tilde{u}:t\in ]0,\tau [\to \left\lbrace \tilde{u}(t):x\in \Omega \to \tilde{u}(t)(x)=u(x,t)\right\rbrace$. The transformation $u\to \tilde{u}$ constituting an isomorphism, one will subsequently simplify the expressions by writing *u* for what ought to have been denoted $\tilde{u}$.

**Notes:**

- *Separating, first, time from the space variable makes it possible to draw heavily on the conceptual tools developed for elliptic problems. This is moreover entirely consistent with the sequence “semi-discretisation in time/full discretisation in space” which usually governs the determination of a formulation usable in practice.*
- *The hypotheses on the geometry assure us of the 1-extension property of the open set* $O$ *. One will thus be able to identify the Hilbert space*

${B}^{1}(\Omega ):=\left\lbrace u\in {L}^{2}(\Omega )/\nabla \mathrm{u}\in {({L}^{2}(\Omega ))}^{q}\right\rbrace$
*on which it is convenient to work, with the space*
${H}^{1}(\Omega ):=\left\lbrace u\in D'(\Omega )/\exists U\in {H}^{1}({\mathrm{\Re }}^{q})\text{}\text{avec}u={U}_{\mid \Omega }\right\rbrace$
*for which the standard theoretical results on traces, on space densities and on equivalent norms are legitimate.*

- *In view of the Lipschitz character of the boundary, the theoretical results which follow will be applicable to* **structures containing corners** *(salient or re-entrant). On the other hand, the treatment of* **spikes** *or of* **cusps** *falls outside this general theoretical framework. Likewise, the fact that the open set must lie locally on the same side of its boundary prevents (theoretically) the treatment of a* **crack** *. In order to treat this type of problem rigorously, one approach consists in correcting the basis functions of the finite elements by a suitable function centred on the internal tip of the crack (cf. P. GRISVARD. Ecole d’Analyse Numérique CEA-EDF-INRIA on fracture mechanics, pp183-192, 1982).*
- **The residual indicator using the solution of the temperature problem, its theoretical limitations are therefore, at best, identical to those of the said problem.**

- *This* **theoretical lifting**, which may appear somewhat “ethereal”, has a thoroughly concrete anchoring in the numerical techniques implemented in order to solve this type of problem. $\left[\begin{array}{cc}A& 0\\ 0& \text{Id}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}T\\ {T}_{{\Gamma}_{1}}:={\text{Rf}}_{{\Gamma}_{1}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}\hat{s}:=s-\sum_{j>J}{a}_{ji}{f}_{j}\\ f\end{array}\right]$

The regularity hypotheses on the boundary also assure us of the following good properties for the working spaces. One will then be able to place oneself in the usual abstract variational framework.

**Lemma 1**

Under the hypotheses (H1) and (H2) the working spaces $W$ and $V$ are Hilbert spaces equipped with the norm induced by ${H}^{1}(O)$.

**Proof:**

The result comes simply from the fact that the trace map ${\gamma}_{0,1}:{H}^{1}(\Omega )\to {L}^{2}({\Gamma}_{1})$ is the composition of the usual trace map ${\gamma}_{0}:{H}^{1}(\Omega )\to {H}^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Gamma )\subset {L}^{2}(\Gamma )$, linear, continuous and surjective (in view of the hypotheses adopted), and of the operator of restriction to ${\Gamma}_{1}$, itself also linear, continuous and surjective. By their definition, one deduces that $W$ and $V$ are closed subspaces of ${H}^{1}(\Omega )$. They are therefore Hilbert spaces equipped with the norm ${\parallel \parallel }_{1,\Omega }$.

**Lemma 2**

Under the hypotheses (${H}_{1}$) and (${H}_{2}$), the norm and the semi-norm induced by ${H}^{1}(O)$ are equivalent on the functional space $V$. One will denote by $P(O)>0$ the Poincaré constant conveying this equivalence

$\forall \nu  \in V{\parallel \nu  \parallel }_{1,\Omega }\le P(\Omega ){\mid \nu  \mid }_{1,\Omega }$

**Note:**

One will subsequently denote ${\parallel u\parallel }_{\infty ,\Omega }:=\underset{\text{pp}.t\in \Omega }{\text{supess}\mid u(t)\mid }$ and $\forall (u,\nu  )\in {({H}^{m}(\Omega ))}^{2}{(u,\nu  )}_{m,\Omega }:=\sum_{\mid \alpha \mid \le m}{({\partial}^{\alpha}u,{\partial}^{\alpha}\nu  )}_{{L}^{2}(\Omega )},{\parallel u\parallel }_{m,\Omega }^{2}:=\sum_{\mid \alpha \mid \le m}{\parallel {\partial}^{\alpha}u\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(\Omega )}^{2}\text{et}{\mid u\mid }_{m,\Omega }^{2}:=\sum_{\mid \alpha \mid =m}{\parallel {\partial}^{\alpha}u\parallel }_{{L}^{2}(\Omega )}^{2}$.

**Proof:**

This result is a corollary of the Poincaré inequality satisfied by the so-called “Nikodym” open sets, of which $\Omega$ is one in view of the hypotheses adopted. There are however two cases:

- either the problem is truly mixed and includes boundary conditions other than those of Dirichlet, $\text{mes}(\Gamma -{\Gamma}_{1})\ne 0$ (see the proof §III.7.2 pp922-925),
- or one takes into account only conditions of prescribed-temperature type, $\text{mes}(\Gamma -{\Gamma}_{1})=0$, $V={H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega )$, and one recovers the standard result of equivalence of the norm and of the semi-norm on this space (see for example the proof pp18-19).

The compilation of the preceding results makes it possible to delineate the **Abstract Variational Framework** (CVA) on which the weak formulation will rely:

- ${H}_{0}^{1}(\Omega )\subset V\subset {H}^{1}(\Omega )$ ,
- $V\subset H:={L}^{2}(\Omega )=H'\subset V'\subset {H}^{-1}(\Omega )$ identifying $H$ with its dual,

### 4.5 Complements

The constant ${K}_{3}$, just like its preceding counterpart ${K}_{2}$, **depends intrinsically on the type of boundary conditions enriching the initial heat equation as well as on the type of temporal and spatial discretisation**. In an attempt to free oneself from this last constraint, SR.GAGO proposes (on a 2D model problem) a dependence of the constant ${K}_{2}$ as a function of the **type of finite element used**. It is written

$$
{K}_{2}:=\frac{{\tilde{K}}_{2}}{\sqrt{24{p}^{2}}}
$$

where $p$ is the degree of the interpolation polynomial used ($p=1$ for the TRIA3 and QUAD4, $p=2$ for the TRIA6 and QUAD8/9). Whence the idea, once the global error indicator has been calculated, of multiplying it by this “corrective” constant $\frac{1}{\sqrt{24{p}^{2}}}$. This strategy has been implicitly adopted for the calculation of the error indicator in mechanics (option ‘ERME_ELEM’ of CALC_ERREUR, cf. [[r4.10.02](doc:r4.10.02) §3]). We have not however adopted it for thermal analysis because this constant has been determined only empirically on the 2D Laplace equation. We do not wish in this way to bias the values of the indicators.

Until now, only maps of spatial error indicators calculated at a given time instant of the analysis transient have been in question. But, in fact, **there exist several ways of constructing an error indicator on a parabolic problem**:

- one may very well, first, semi-discretise the strong formulation in space and control its spatial error by a posteriori error indicators adapted to the steady case (in our case elliptic). One then applies a solver, of variable step and order, treating the ordinary differential equations (for example ),
- a second strategy consists in semi-discretising in time and then in space and in determining the spatial error indicator at a given time instant (for example ) from the local residuals of the semi-discretised form. One applies a linear solver to the variational form making it possible to construct iteratively the solution at a given time instant from the solution at the preceding time instant,
- another possibility consists in discretising simultaneously in time and in space on appropriate finite elements and in controlling their “space-time” errors in a coupled manner (for example ).

This **last scenario is the most attractive** from a theoretical point of view because it proposes a complete control of the error and it makes it possible to avoid unfortunate decouplings as regards possible refinements/coarsenings driven by one criterion with respect to the other (cf. the following paragraph). It is however very heavy to put in place in a large industrial code such as *Code_Aster*. It assumes indeed, in order to be optimal, nothing less than a separate management of the time step by finite element. Which, from the point of view of the architecture supporting the finite elements of the code, is a real challenge!

**One therefore prefers the second scenario**, which has the great advantage of being able to be implemented directly in a finite-element code because it relies above all on the solution of the fully discretised system. It is this type of indicator which has been put in place in N3S, TRIFOU and *Code_Aster*.

Within the framework of a **genuine “space-time” discretisation of the problem** (scenario 3), one obtains, strictly speaking, a **“space-time” indicator for each discretisation element** $K\times \left[{t}_{n},{t}_{n+1}\right]$ which is the weighted sum of three terms:

1. the residual of the calculated solution and of the discretised data with respect to the strong formulation of the problem $({P}_{0})$ evaluated on $K\times \left[{t}_{n},{t}_{n+1}\right]$,
2. the spatial jump across $\partial K\times \left[{t}_{n},{t}_{n+1}\right]$ of the associated trace operator (which naturally links the weak and strong formulations via Green’s formula),
3. the temporal jump across $K\times \partial \left[{t}_{n},{t}_{n+1}\right]$ of the calculated solution.

The solution which has been put in place obviously does not make it possible to bring out explicitly the **temporal jump term**. **It reappears** however **implicitly**, because of the particular method of temporal semi-discretisation, in **all the terms in** $\theta$ **of Definitions 10 and 12**.

**On the other hand, the fact of being interested principally only in the spatial discretisation and in its possible refinement/coarsening must not obscure certain contingencies with respect to the management of the time step**. Indeed, during **transient analyses comprising abrupt variations of loadings and/or of sources in the course of time**, for example thermal shocks, the calculated temperature fields ${T}^{n}(0<n\le N)$ may **oscillate spatially and temporally**. Moreover, they may violate the “**maximum principle**” by taking values outside the bounds imposed by the Cauchy condition and the boundary conditions. In order to overcome this parasitic numerical phenomenon one shows, on a canonical case without an exchange condition (cf. [[r3.06.07](doc:r3.06.07) §2]), that the time step must remain between two bounds:

$$
\Delta {t}_{\min}(h)<\Deltat <\Delta {t}_{\max}(\theta )
$$

In practice, it is difficult to have an order of magnitude of these bounds; one therefore has trouble, if one detects oscillations, in modifying the time step in order to respect []. Moreover, this type of operation is not always possible because one must sometimes take precise account of the abrupt variations of the loadings (in particular when $\Delta t$ is too small).

When $\Delta t$ **is too large** one may operate in **implicit Euler** $(\theta =1)$, which will have the effect of removing the upper bound.

On the other hand **when it is too small**, two palliative strategies are available to the user:

- **diagonalise the mass matrix via the lumped elements** (cf. [[r3.06.07](doc:r3.06.07) §4] [r4.10.03-recapitulatif]) proposed in the code (this requires arrangements in order to treat the *P* 2 elements or the 2D_AXI modelling),
- **reduce the size of the mesh cells** (this increases the calculation and memory complexities required).

It is in this perspective that the **refinements/coarsenings carried out on the strength of our indicator may have an effect**. Refining will pose no problem; on the other hand, by coarsening one may very well deteriorate the lower bound of []. One must therefore be very circumspect if one uses the coarsening option of the HOMARD software (encapsulated for *Code_Aster* in MACR_ADAP_MAIL, option ‘DERAFFINEMENT’ [u7.03.01](doc:u7.03.01)) on a test case comprising a thermal shock.

We shall now summarise the principal contributions of the preceding theoretical chapters and what is at stake in them with respect to the thermal analysis put in place in *Code_Aster*.

But, in fact, **there exist several ways of constructing an error indicator on a parabolic problem** (cf. [r4.10.03-complements]). The one which we have retained does not allow a complete control of the error and it always requires a **certain vigilance when one treats problems of shock type** (the same as for the post-processed problem!). To finish, it must be emphasised that this indicator is therefore composed of four terms:

- the **principal term**, called the **volumetric error term**, controlling the proper satisfaction of the heat equation,
- to which are added **three secondary terms** checking the proper holding of the **spatial jumps** and of the boundary conditions: **flux and exchange terms**.

In 2D-PLAN or in 3D (resp. in 2D-AXI), if the unit of the geometry is the metre, the unit of the first is the *W.m* (resp. $W.m.{\text{rad}}^{-1}$) and that of the other terms is the $W.{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}$ (resp. $W.{m}^{\frac{1}{2}}.{\text{rad}}^{-1}$). **Beware, therefore, of the units taken into account for the geometry when one is interested in the raw value of the indicator and not in its relative value!**

## 6 Implementation in code_aster

### 6.1 Particular difficulties

In order to calculate this type of indicator one must come to terms with the “elementary calculation + assembly” vision generally deployed in all finite-element codes. Now the estimation, at the local level, of $\eta (K)$ requires not only knowledge of its local fields, but also that of its neighbouring mesh cells. **One therefore needs to carry out a “global calculation” at the scale of** ${\Delta}_{K}$ **, inside the local calculation!** A strategy modelled on what had been put in place for the estimator in mechanics consists in transmitting this type of information in **the components of extended maps**, which will themselves be transmitted as an input argument of CALCUL. It is this type of contingency which explains the heterogeneity of treatment, during the overloading of loadings, between the thermal solvers and the calculation of our indicator (cf. r4.10.03-environnement).

**Another type of difficulty, more numerical this time, concerns the calculation of the volumetric term**. Indeed, it requires a double differentiation which one carries out in three stages, because in *Code_Aster* one does not recommend the use of the second derivatives of the shape functions.

**Note:**

They have recently been introduced in order to treat the differentiation of the energy release rate (cf. [r7.02.01](doc:r7.02.01) § Appendix 1).

On the one hand, one calculates (in the thermal operator) the flux vector at the Gauss points, then one extrapolates the corresponding values to the nodes by local smoothing (cf. [r3.06.03](doc:r3.06.03) CALC_CHAMP with THERMIQUE=’FLUX_ELNO’ and [r4.10.03-environnement]) in order to calculate its divergence at the Gauss points. With quadratic finite elements the intermediate operation is only approximate (one assigns as value at the mid-side nodes the half-sum of their values at the end nodes). However, (limited) numerical tests have shown that this approach does not provide results very different from those obtained by a direct calculation via the proper second derivatives.

Finally, it was necessary to determine various geometric characteristics (diameters, normals, Jacobians…), the connectivities of the facing elements and to access the data which they underlie in all the cases provided for by the code (symmetrised and/or heterogeneous part of the mesh, function or real loading, non-linear material, all the 2D/3D isoparametric elements and all the thermal loadings).

Beyond these painstaking developments, **a large effort of “geometric-software” validation has been deployed** in an attempt to track down possible bugs in this tangle of small formulae. These laborious tests on small model test cases (TPLL01A/H for 2D_PLAN/3D and TPNA01A for 2D_AXI) proved fruitful (including for the indicator in mechanics and the lumped elements!) and indispensable. For one does not have, to my knowledge, theoretical values making it possible to validate these indicators in certain situations: **“nothing looks more like an indicator value… than another indicator value!”**. **Failing anything else, and although in a validation process this is not a panacea, one must therefore try to establish the maximum confidence in all these constituent elements**.

### 6.2 Necessary environment/parameter setting

**The calculation of this indicator is carried out, via the option ‘ERTH_ELEM’ of the post-processing operator** **CALC_ERREUR,** on an EVOL_THER (supplied to the keyword RESULTAT) coming from a previous thermal analysis (linear or not, transient or steady, isotropic or orthotropic, via THER_LINEAIRE or THER_NON_LINE, cf. a more precise scope [r4.10.03-perimetre-utilisation]).

As has already been emphasised, it **requires beforehand recourse to the option ‘FLUX_ELNO’** of CALC_CHAMP, which determines the values of the heat-flux vector at the nodes (cf. example of use [r4.10.03-exemple-utilisation]).

This indicator is made up of fifteen components per element and for a given time instant. In order to be able to post-process them via POST_RELEVE or GIBI one needs to extrapolate these fields by element into fields at the nodes by element. The addition of the option ‘**ERTH_ELNO**’ (after the call to ‘ERTH_ELEM’) makes it possible to carry out this purely software transformation. For a given time instant and finite element, it merely duplicates the fifteen components of the indicator onto each node of the element.

In order to carry out properly the complete post-processing of the thermal analysis desired, one must:

- **Carry it out on the whole geometry,** **TOUT=’OUI’** (default value, otherwise the analysis stops in ERREUR_FATALE). This provisional choice has been driven by software and functional contingencies, because in this way all the finite elements are assigned a homogeneous indicator calculated with the same number of terms (otherwise what becomes of the notion of jump term and of boundary-condition term on the edge of the domain considered?). Moreover the refinement/coarsening tool of the code (the HOMARD software encapsulated in MACR_ADAP_MAIL), the natural outlet of our error maps, does not allow one to treat only parts of meshes.

**Note:**

*This poses problems of propagation of subdivisions in order to preserve the conformity of the triangulation. In fact, in order to get round this type of contingency, one would have either to define a buffer zone making the junction between the “dead” zone of the mesh and the “active” zone to be treated, or, in a more optimal way but also much more difficult from an architectural point of view, to reduce it to a layer of joint elements.*

- **Supply the same temporal parameter setting**: value of $\theta$ (default value equal to 0.57) supplied to the keyword PARM_THETA; where appropriate, if one is treating a transient problem, one must fill in the usual fields TOUT/NUME/LIST_ORDRE with values admissible with respect to the thermal analysis. The calculation of the history of the indicator may then be carried out from any time instant of a transient, it being understood that at the first increment one carries out the calculation as in the steady state ($\theta =1$, $n+1=0$ and no finite-difference term, cf. []).

Moreover, in the steady state, if the user supplies a value of $\theta$ different from 1, one imposes this latter value on the user after having informed him of it.

In a related way, one detects the request to supply error maps between non-contiguous order numbers (one has an ALARME) or the datum of an EVOL_THER not comprising a temperature field and a flux vector at the nodes (the analysis stops in ERREUR_FATALE). The value of $\theta$ and the number of order numbers taken into account are traced in the message file [r4.10.03-résultats-calcul-erreur]. The order number and the corresponding time instant also accompany each occurrence of the error indicator in the result file ([r4.10.03-résultats-calcul-erreur]).

- **Use the same loadings and respect the particular overloading rules** of the error-calculation options of this operator. Thus, in the thermal (and mechanical) solvers one aggregates the boundary conditions of one and the same type, whereas in the error calculations of CALC_ERREUR (and therefore also with our indicator) one can take into account, for a given type of boundary condition, only the last one supplied to the keyword EXCIT. **The order of these loadings is therefore of crucial importance!**

**Note:**

*This restriction finds its basis in the first note of the preceding paragraph. To do the thing properly one would have either to concatenate, on the skin elements concerned, all the boundary conditions, or to supply to the elementary calculations maps of variable size containing exhaustively all the loadings. The first solution seems by far the most optimal but also the most laborious to implement. One would then also have to do the same thing for the residual indicator of mechanics (OPTION=’ERRE_ELGA_NORE’).*

However, in the event of a conflict between loadings of one and the same type, one can often and easily find a palliative solution via the appropriate AFFE_CHAR_THER. The user is warned of the presence of several occurrences of one and the same type of loading by an ALARME message, and the list of the loadings actually taken into account is traced in the message file ([r4.10.03-résultats-calcul-erreur]).

The code stops, on the other hand, in ERREUR_FATALE if the loadings supplied pose certain problems (interpolation of function loadings, access to the components, presence of the CHAMPGD of the exchange coefficient and absence of the CHAMPGD of the external temperature or vice versa….),

- **Within the same general framework**: value of the model (parameter MODELE), of the required materials (CHAM_MATER), of the EVOL_THER structure supplied (RESULTAT) and of the result (assignment of CALC_ERREUR with possibly a re-entrant “reuse”). They are traced in the message file ([r4.10.03-résultats-calcul-erreur]).

**If the user does not respect this necessary homogeneity of parameter setting (up to the overloading rules) between the thermal solver and the post-processing tool, he exposes himself to biased or even completely wrong results (without an ALARME message or an** **ERREUR_FATALE necessarily stopping him; one cannot control and/or forbid everything!). He then remains the sole judge of the relevance of his results.**

### 6.3 Presentation/exploitation of the results of the error calculation

The option ‘ERTH_ELEM’ in fact supplies not one, but **fifteen components per finite element** $K$ **and per time step** ${t}_{n+1}$. Indeed, **for each of the four terms** of [], the principal volumetric term and the three secondary surface terms _, one calculates not only **the absolute error**, but also **a normalisation term** (the theoretical value of the discretised loadings which one ought to have found) and **the associated relative error**. Summing these three families of four contributions, one also establishes the total absolute error, the total normalisation term and the total relative error. Which does indeed make up the count!

**The fact of dissociating the contributions of each component of this indicator makes it possible to compare their relative importances and to target refinement/coarsening strategies on a certain type of error. Even though the volumetric term (representing the proper satisfaction of the heat equation) and the jump term (linked to the finite-element modelling) remain the preponderant terms, it may prove useful to gauge the errors due to a certain type of loading in order to refine their modelling or to remesh the boundary zones concerned**.

Moreover this type of strategy can easily be diverted from its primary aim in order to carry out refinement/coarsening by zone: it suffices to impose, only in this zone, a type of fictitious boundary condition (with a very bad value in order to give rise to a large error).

### 6.4 Scope of use

**This indicator has been developed, for the moment, only on the isoparametric elements (TRIA3/6, QUAD4/8/9, TETRA4/10, PENTA6/13/15 and HEXA8/20/27) and for the modellings PLAN, PLAN_DIAG, AXIS, AXIS_DIAG, 3D and 3D_DIAG**. It therefore does not calculate the contributions of the structural elements of shell type (COQUE_PLAN, COQUE_AXIS, COQUE), of the pyramids (PYRAM5 and PYRAM13) and of the Fourier modelling (AXIS_FOURIER). Nor does it make it possible to calculate the jump terms of these elements with the authorised elements. **However, if a mesh comprises licit and illicit elements, the calculation is not interrupted** and, via OPTION –2 in the appropriate element catalogues, **the user is warned that the said elements are not taken into account**.

Indeed, in order to carry out this post-processing, one must beforehand call, explicitly, the option ‘FLUX_ELNO’ (calculation of the heat-flux vector at the nodes) and, implicitly, ‘INIT_MAIL_VOIS’ (determination of the characteristics of the neighbourhood ${\Delta}_{K}$ of an element $K$). **One is therefore dependent on their respective scopes of use**.

One must also keep in mind **a few more minor rules** which may however take on a quite particular importance for very precise studies:

- The calculation of the indicator treats only the elements of the mesh belonging to the model designated by the keyword MODELE of the command CALC_ERREUR. One can thus work with (uncleaned) meshes comprising “rough mesh cells” to which one assigns a different model.
- In a **mesh of dimension** $q$, one calculates the jump and loading terms only on **skin elements of dimension** $q-1$. One therefore treats the relations of the TRIA/QUAD with the SEG and the relations of the TETRA/PENTA/HEXA with the FACE. For example, in the event of the presence of segments in a three-dimensional mesh, the option will not stop but it will not take into account their (possible) contributions.
- The option ‘ERTH_ELEM_TEMP’ and its preliminary options do not know the **PYRAM**. Their contributions will be ignored. This gap comes from their introduction into *code_aster* being more recent than that of the preliminary options already cited.

**Note:**

*In any case these elements are in the minority in a 3D mesh and are generated only by the free volumetric mesher of GIBI, which creates them locally in order to complete portions of hexahedral meshes.*

- In 2D, **one must not accidentally insert a segment between two triangles or quadrangles**, otherwise the jump term of these elements will not be calculated and one will wrongly enquire into the existence of a possible boundary condition. The calculation will not be interrupted but, at this interface, the value of the indicator will be incomplete. However, for particular needs (density of internal loadings localised in a structure, crack...), one may of course allow oneself this kind of situation. In 3D, the problem of course also arises when one inserts quadrangles or triangles between two contiguous FACEs.
- the same type of imbroglio occurs when **two points of the mesh are geometrically superimposed**. Here again, the calculation ought not to be interrupted, but the value of the indicator will be incomplete at the level of this overlap zone,
- **If one works with a mesh which results from symmetrisation operations**, one must try not to find oneself in the two preceding cases. Moreover, on either side of the axis of symmetry, **the neighbouring mesh cells do not necessarily (in particular with the GIBI mesher) have orientations which meet the standard of** **Code_Aster** (they ought to be reversed). The calculation of the indicator, for which this information is crucial (in order to define the outward normals to each mesh cell and the facing connectivities), detects the problem by calculating the Jacobian of each mesh cell. In 2D, a substitution algorithm makes it possible to get round the problem and to reconstruct the connectivity tables “nodes of the current element / nodes of its neighbours”. In 3D, the problem is much more arduous and particular to each element; the code therefore stops in ERREUR_FATALE in the event of a problem.
- **If one wishes to refine or coarsen one's mesh with MACR_ADAP_MAIL** [u7.03.01](doc:u7.03.01), the mesh must comprise only triangles or tetrahedra. Concerning the surface or volumetric loadings, “good practice” consists in using only mesh-cell groups. If one uses node groups, one must expect falsified calculations, because after a few refinements other points will probably have been inserted geometrically into the zone concerned by the GROUP_NO without being assigned any loading (one cannot modify the composition of a GROUP_NO during the session!).

For point loadings or for recording points (on which, for example, POST_RELEVE_T will rely) the GROUP_NO is licit. On the other hand, it is not recommended to use directly mesh cells (MA) or nodes (NO) (outside a group), because in this case, as renumberings occur, HOMARD will probably lose track of them. It can retain the memory of the mesh cells or of the nodes only through a name of GROUP_MA or of GROUP_NO. Thanks to this mechanism, it can adopt a Lagrangian vision of the fate of these mesh cells or of these points!

**The calculation of the indicator operates indiscriminately on an EVOL_THER coming from THER_LINEAIRE or from THER_NON_LINE, steady or transient, isotropic or orthotropic, and on a fixed structure meshed by elements meeting the preceding criteria.**

In the non-linear case one takes into account the non-linearities of the materials and the modification of the problem into enthalpy. However one does not treat the possible contributions of non-linear loadings (FLUX_NL and RAYONNEMENT). The user is warned of this by an ALARME, just as he is warned that a boundary condition of type ECHANGE_PAROI is not taken into account. Indeed, in the linear case one recognises, for the moment, only the contributions of the loadings SOURCE, FLUX_REP and ECHANGE. For the taking into account of these loadings, particular overloading rules are applied (cf. r4.10.03-environnement).

### 6.5 Example of use

In order to familiarise oneself with the use of this indicator in thermal analysis and with its possible coupling with the encapsulation of HOMARD via MACR_ADAP_MAIL [u7.03.01](doc:u7.03.01), one may draw inspiration from the test case TPLL01J [v4.02.001](doc:v4.02.001).

In this other example taken from the HOMARD website, the coupling ERTH_ELEM/MACR_ADAP_MAIL [u7.03.01](doc:u7.03.01) simulates the circulation of a “hot” fluid on either side of an elbowed metal part (at the top and at the bottom, via a time-dependent ECHANGE condition in AFFE_CHAR_THER_F). The circulation of the fluid takes place from left to right.

Accuracy is required above all at the ends of the structure, at the level of the propagation of the fluid: thanks to the coupling of the error indicator with the refinement-coarsening tool, the mesh therefore remains fine at the edge of the part, in the zone where the “hot” fluid is concentrated. Finally it is coarsened at the rear, once the fluid has passed.

One also observes that, as predicted by the theory (cf. notes [r4.10.03-formulation]), the solution of the thermal problem is “blunted” in the re-entrant corners and that the error indicator (even though it too is penalised in these zones) signals this state of affairs (even when the part has cooled down).

## 7 Conclusion – Prospect

In numerical simulations by finite elements, obtaining a raw result is no longer sufficient. **The user increasingly requires a spatial error calculation with respect to the mesh. He needs methodological support and advanced “numerical-computational” tools in order to gauge the quality of his studies and to improve them.**

To this end, a posteriori spatial error indicators make it possible to locate, on each element, an error map on which the remeshing tools can rely: a first analysis on a coarse mesh makes it possible to recover the error map from the discretised data and from the discretised solution (hence the term “a posteriori”), the refinement then being carried out locally by ranking this information.

The new a posteriori indicator which has just been implemented in order to post-process the thermal problems of *code_aster* is based on their local residuals extracted from the semi-discretisations in time. Via the option ‘ERTH_ELEM’ of CALC_ERREUR, it uses the thermal fields (EVOL_THER) coming from THER_LINEAIRE and from THER_NON_LINE.

This new indicator completes the range of advanced tools offered by the code, making it possible to improve the quality of studies, their pooling and their comparisons. Indeed, error indicators in mechanics and a refinement/coarsening macro-command MACR_ADAP_MAIL [u7.03.02](doc:u7.03.02) are already available. It remains to complete the scope of use of these tools and to extend them, in particular in order to manage better the non-linearities and the interactions between spatial error and temporal error.

**Note:**

*Estimator by smoothing of the stresses of Zhu & Zienkiewicz (CALC_ERREUR+* *OPTION‘ERZ1/ERZ2_ELEM’* [r4.10.01](doc:r4.10.01)) and pure residual indicator (*‘ERME_ELEM’* [r4.10.02](doc:r4.10.02)).

Subsequently, the prospects of this work are of several kinds:

- **From a functional point of view,** the completeness of this indicator could also be improved by taking into account possible non-linear boundary conditions (FLUX_NL and RAYONNEMENT) and exchanges between walls (ECHANGE_PAROI). In the longer term, one ought also to be able to rely on structural finite elements (shell…), on pyramids, and to be able to treat convection-diffusion problems (operator THER_NON_LINE_MO [r5.02.04](doc:r5.02.04)).
- **From a theoretical point of view**, when one uses new boundary conditions and/or when one relies on new modellings (shell, beam...), a “numerical-functional” study similar to that of this document ought to be carried out in order to judge the theoretical and practical limitations (with respect to *code_aster*) of such an indicator and to derive its ad hoc formulation.
- Let us recall finally that a **host of a posteriori error indicators are available**, and that rather few have been tested and validated on industrial cases. In order to refine diagnoses, to establish comparisons and to put in place remeshing strategies by class of problem, it would be interesting to extend the list of available indicators. Various residual indicators plus a local problem have thus proved more effective (but also more costly) during numerical tests (in the elliptic case) in N3S.

**Note:**

*The indicator is the norm of the solution of a local problem, of the same type as the initial problem, but discretised on spaces of higher degree and whose right-hand side is the residual. According to the boundary conditions applied to this local problem, one distinguishes different types of them. They thus mix the “hierarchical bases” vision and the “residual” aspects of a posteriori error indicators.*

- The ideal consists in discretising simultaneously in time and in space on appropriate finite elements and in controlling their “space-time” errors in a coupled manner. **This “space-time” indicator** gives access to a complete control of the error and it makes it possible to avoid unfortunate decouplings as regards possible refinements/coarsenings driven by one criterion with respect to the other (cf. discussion [r4.10.03-complements]). It is however very heavy to put in place in a large industrial code such as *Code_Aster*. It assumes indeed, in order to be optimal, nothing less than a separate management of the time step by finite element. Which, from the point of view of the architecture supporting the finite elements of the code, is a real challenge!
