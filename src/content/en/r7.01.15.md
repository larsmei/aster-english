# r7.01.15 Constitutive law of LAIGLE

**Summary:**

The rheological model of Laigle makes it possible to analyse the mechanical behaviour of rocks. The development of this constitutive model was initiated following the difficulty of correctly capturing the response of the rock mass during the excavation of an underground cavity, with the aim:

- of defining the need for, and the nature of, any supports to be installed;
- of determining the extent of the ground around a structure that is influenced by the excavation.

The implementation of this elastoplastic model has been focused principally on the simulation of the post-peak behaviour of the rock. From this standpoint it is assumed that there is no hardening of the rock prior to its failure. This is expressed by a linear elastic behaviour up to the strength peak (the rock may nevertheless be damaged while the material is not yet at failure). The plasticity criterion defined is of generalised Hoek and Brown type and accounts for the influence of the stress level on the shear strength. The softening of the material is associated with a progressive decrease in the cohesion and friction-angle properties, accompanied by a change in volume. It is driven by the cumulative plastic deviatoric strain, taken as the sole hardening variable.

In order to facilitate the integration of this model into *Code_Aster*, the law initially developed in the formalism of the principal stresses was rewritten with stress invariants on a basis of the Cambou‑Jafari-Sidoroff (CJS) model. The numerical formulation is implicit with respect to the criterion and explicit with respect to the flow direction.

The sign convention used for the formulation of the equations, within the framework of this note, is that of continuum mechanics.

## 2 Introduction

The purpose of this note is to present the rheological model for analysing the mechanical behaviour of rocks, adapted to the simulation of underground structures, introduced into *Code_Aster* and developed by the CIH [bib1]. The aim of this model is that it should be able to be implemented rapidly and industrially, in order to answer the principal questions that the engineer asks when analysing and designing an underground cavity. The rheological law must for this purpose remain relatively simple, both in the identification of the parameters and in its implementation and in the interpretation of the results.

### 2.1 Phenomenology of the behaviour of soils

One particularity of a rock, compared with a soil, is that its mechanical behaviour is, over a wide stress range, driven by the cohesion. This cohesion is associated with a cementation of the medium, induced over the geological history of the rock mass, and is essentially epitaxial in nature. Conversely, the strength of a soil is governed more particularly by the friction and/or dilatancy term. The cohesion, of essentially capillary origin, then has an influence only for very low states of confining stress.

This distinction between a soil and a rock is important because it guides the choice and the basic assumptions of the constitutive model.

The principal rheological phenomena associated with this context are the following:

- In the domain of small strains, the response of a rock, in particular under low states of confinement, may be likened to a linear elastic behaviour, weakly dependent on the stress state. Non-linearities of the behaviour are liable to appear prior to the strength peak, in the case of soft rocks, for a stress level of the order of 70 to 80% of the maximum value. This threshold decreases with the increase in the mean pressure, until it almost vanishes when the overconsolidation stress is reached (cap-model). Under very low confining stresses, representative of those prevailing in the vicinity of underground structures, these non-linearities are generally weak, all the more so as the cementation is significant, and therefore the level of overconsolidation of the rock is high.
- The dilatancy (increase in volume) is initiated when the non-linearities appear on the stress–strain curve. This dilatancy increases until localisation occurs within the specimen. At that moment, the dilatancy rate (or the dilatancy angle $\psi$ ) is maximum, and thereafter decreases progressively and vanishes at very large strains.
- The strength peak is reached for stresses describing a failure criterion, generally curved in the Mohr plane or in the plane of the major and minor principal stresses. The assumption of a linear Mohr–Coulomb criterion is therefore only a simplifying assumption, which tends, for low confining stresses, to overestimate the cohesion of the medium.
- Once the maximum strength has been reached, the strength of the rock decreases. This post-peak softening is all the more rapid and significant (in intensity) as the confining stress is low. This decrease is linked to a more or less localised damage of the rock, depending on the confinement level. Whatever this stress, beyond the peak, **the rock can no longer be considered as continuous**. Its behaviour is then driven by the conditions of strain and of strength at the level of the strain-localisation zone.
- The appearance of one or more kinematic discontinuities within the rock is associated with a loss of cohesion. The post-peak behaviour is then governed by the conditions of friction and of dilatancy along the discontinuity planes or within a strain-localisation band. It follows from this reasoning that, for very large strains, the behaviour of the rock, likened to a “structure”, is purely frictional, and is characterised by an ultimate friction angle $\phi$ . This angle is an intrinsic datum of the material, a function of the minerals constituting the rock. It therefore does not depend directly on the cohesion conditions, and above all it may be considered as independent of the dimensions of the specimen.
- When the behaviour becomes purely frictional, it is associated with no volumetric strain. The dilatancy has therefore vanished, and no longer exists at large strains.
- The evolution between the peak strength and the critical state corresponding to large strains is more or less progressive depending on the state of the applied stresses. For a zero state of confinement (uniaxial compression), the behaviour is driven solely by the cohesion, and failure results in an immediate and sudden loss of all strength. The softening will be more progressive as the confining stress increases, until it becomes non-existent beyond a certain confining stress that bounds the ductile and brittle behavioural domains.

### 2.2 Context of the study and simplifying assumptions of the model

The wish to develop a model that is easy to implement necessarily entails simplifications, arising from a compromise between the objectives sought, the conditions of use of the model (quality of the input data, time and cost available…) and the means implemented to carry out these developments. These compromises are essentially the following:

- **A linear elastic behaviour** up to the strength peak. This amounts to assuming that there is no hardening of the rock prior to its failure.
- **Only a shear-failure criterion is retained**. This means that if the rock is crushed in an isotropic manner, the behaviour remains elastic, and that there is no damage and no hardening of the material under this type of path. During the excavation phases of an underground structure with the installation of a light support, the mean pressure in the rock mass situated nearby can only decrease (or remain constant in the ideal case of a circular cavity subjected to an isotropic loading, for a linear elastic behaviour). Plastification under isotropic stress, which one may find on a Cap-Model or on a Cam-Clay type law, did not appear indispensable to us in view of the objectives sought, and in the case of an isothermal and short-term loading.

During the development of this model, we deliberately focused on the study and the simulation of the post-peak behaviour of the rock. In this behavioural domain, the strength of the material is assumed to be driven, as a function of the stress state and of the level of damage of the rock, by the cohesion, the dilatancy or the friction.

The cohesion defines the strength of the material so long as the latter remains continuous. It is active up to the strength peak, and has only a slight influence on the softening behaviour, unless the cohesion is representative of a ductile “glue” (case of soils injected with silicate gel,…).

As the cohesion deteriorates through damage, the dilatancy increases, until it reaches its maximum value at the loss of continuity of the medium. At that moment, under the effect of the shearing of the induced discontinuity, this dilatancy degrades progressively and slowly. The rheology of the rock then evolves towards a purely frictional behaviour.

## 6 Numerical treatment adapted to non-regular models

The evolution law of the plastic mechanism, defined in chapter [§3], must satisfy the kinematic condition [eq 3.2.4-2]. The projection proposed onto the normal to the strain hypersurface may lead to a “non-solution”, which results in a failure of the numerical treatment (see the graphical interpretation of chapter [§6.1.3.3]). In this chapter it is proposed to define projection rules making it possible to handle so-called “non-regular” models by imposing on them a projection said to be “at the apex of the cone”.

Moreover, as for other constitutive relations, one adds the possibility of locally subdividing (at the Gauss points) the time step in order to facilitate the numerical integration.

### 6.2 Local subdivision of the time step

As for other constitutive relations (the CJS model for example) the possibility has been added, for the LAIGLE model, of locally subdividing (at the Gauss points) the time step in order to facilitate the numerical integration. This possibility is managed by the operand ITER_INTE_PAS of the keyword CONVERGENCE of the operator STAT_NON_LINE. If the value of ITER_INTE_PAS (itepas) is equal to 0, 1 or –1 there is no subdivision (note: 0 is the default value). If itepas is positive the subdivision is systematic; if it is negative the subdivision is taken into account only in the event of numerical non-convergence.

The subdivision consists in carrying out the integration of the plastic mechanism with a strain increment whose components correspond to the components of the initial strain increment divided by the absolute value of itepas (cf. document STAT_NON_LINE [U4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03)).

## 7 The internal variables

For the computer implementation the following 4 internal variables have been retained:

### 7.1 V1: the cumulative deviatoric plastic strain

The hardening variable ${\gamma}^{p}$ is proportional to the second invariant of the deviatoric strain tensor.

${\gamma}^{p}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}{e}_{ij}^{p}{e}_{ij}^{p}}$

with ${e}_{ij}^{p}={\varepsilon}_{ij}^{p}-\frac{\text{tr}({\varepsilon}_{ij}^{p})}{3}{\delta}_{ij}$

The volumetric plastic strain is defined by the relation presented in paragraph [§3.2.4] on the evolution law of the plastic mechanism: $\dot{{\varepsilon}_{v}^{p}}=\dot{\lambda}G$

### 7.3 V3: the behavioural domains of the rock

Five behavioural domains, numbered from 0 to 4 (cf. figure), are identified in order to allow a relatively simple representation of the damage state of the rock, from the intact rock to the rock in the residual state. These domains are a function of the cumulative deviatoric plastic strain ${\gamma}^{p}$ and of the stress state. Each increment of the domain number defines the passage into a higher damage domain.

- If the deviator is less than 70% of the peak deviator, then the material is in domain 0;
- Otherwise:
- If ${\gamma}^{p}=0$ then the material is in domain 1;

1. If $0<{\gamma}^{p}<{\gamma}^{e}$ then the material is in domain 2;

## 8 Detailed presentation of the algorithm

An implicit formulation with respect to the criterion and an explicit formulation with respect to the flow direction are retained: the criterion must be satisfied at the end of the step, whereas the flow direction will be that computed at the beginning of the step (and therefore the value of the dilatancy will likewise be that computed at the beginning of the time step).

One places oneself at a material point, and one considers that the following are given:

- The strain-increment tensor $\Delta \varepsilon$ from which one deduces $\Deltae$ and $\Delta {\varepsilon}_{\nu  }$;
- The stresses at the beginning of the step ${\sigma}^{-}$ , from which one deduces ${s}^{-}$ and ${I}_{1}^{-}$ ;
- The values of the internal variables at the beginning of the time step (only the cumulative plastic strain ${\gamma}^{{p}^{-}}$ is necessary).

The aim is to compute:

- The stresses at the end of the time step $\sigma$ ;
- The internal variables at the end of the time step (${\gamma}^{p}$ , ${\varepsilon}_{\nu  }^{p}$ , the behavioural domains);
- The tangent behaviour at the end of the step: $\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \varepsilon }$

### 8.1 Computation of the elastic solution

$\begin{array}{}\Delta {\varepsilon}^{e}=\Delta {\varepsilon}^{-}-\alpha \Deltat \\ {s}^{e}={s}^{-}+2\mu \Deltae \\ {I}_{}^{e}={I}_{1}^{-}+\mathrm{3K}\Delta {\varepsilon}_{\nu  }\end{array}$

### 8.2 Computation of the elastic criterion
