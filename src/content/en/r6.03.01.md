# r6.03.01 Solution of non-regular systems by a singular value decomposition method

**Summary:**

This document is devoted to the solution of non-regular systems of linear equations. The matrices taken into account may be square and non-invertible, or rectangular.

After recalling the theoretical framework of solutions in the least-squares sense, we concentrate the presentation on the singular value decomposition method, which provides, on the one hand, a tool for diagnosing the degree of regularity of the system and, on the other hand, a family of solution algorithms that are both more general and more stable than those derived from the normal-equations approach.

Finally, we detail the algorithm implemented in *Code_Aster*, which replaces the equivalent functionality of the *Nag* library (F04JDF for version 12 and F04JDE for version 15) used for the modelling of the metallurgical behaviour of steels [R4.04.01](doc:r4.04.01).

## 1 Introduction

Given a real matrix $A$ of order $m\times n$ and a vector $b$ element of ${ℝ}^{m}$ , we consider the problem of determining a vector $x$ element of ${ℝ}^{n}$ which satisfies the following linear system:

$\text{Ax}=b$                                             eq 1-1

It is well known ([bib3] p. 9) that this system admits one, and only one, solution, for every $b$ element of ${ℝ}^{m}$ , under the necessary and sufficient conditions that it be equally constrained $(m=n)$ and that its matrix $A$ be regular. Thus, the investigation of the under-constrained $(m\le n)$ and over-constrained $(m\ge n)$ cases will confront us with one of the following three situations:

1. The linear system [eq 1-1] admits one and only one solution,
2. The linear system [eq 1-1] admits no solution,
3. The linear system [eq 1-1] admits an infinity of solutions.

In practice, situation 2) is generally encountered in the case of an over-constrained system, whereas singular equally constrained systems and under-constrained systems generally lead to situation 3).

In order to claim to solve a linear system of type [eq 1-1], we must first define what we shall call a **solution**. This is the subject of **paragraph 2**, which relies mainly on the notion of **least squares** and on **differentiable optimisation** in order to define, whatever the type of system, a solution which is always unique.

**Paragraph 3** is devoted to **the singular value decomposition** of matrices (abbreviated SVD: *Singular Value Decomposition*), which not only constitutes a tool for diagnosing which of the three preceding situations corresponds to the linear system under study, but also provides a method for determining the solution defined in paragraph 2.

The method using the **SVD decomposition** is presented in **paragraph 4** and is compared there with the method of the **normal equations**.

**Paragraph 5** details, from the algebraic point of view, the application of the SVD method to **the solution of an equally or under-constrained linear system** as it is implemented in *Code_Aster*.

In the following paragraphs, we shall use the notations below:

- $\parallel x\parallel$ and $(\text{x,y})$ for, respectively, the Euclidean norm of the vector $x$ and the associated scalar product of the vectors $x$ and $y$ , elements of ${ℝ}^{m}$ or of ${ℝ}^{n}$ ,
- ${M}^{T}$ for the transpose of the matrix $M$ ,
- $\text{Ker}M$ and $\text{Im}M$ for, respectively, the kernel and the image of (the linear mapping associated with) the matrix $M$ ,
- ${X}^{\perp }$ for the orthogonal complement of the subspace $X$ of ${ℝ}^{m}$ or of ${ℝ}^{n}$ .

## 2 Solution of a rectangular linear system

In this paragraph we shall define a notion of *solution* for the linear system [eq 1-1] which enjoys the properties of **existence** and of **uniqueness.** The approach proceeds in two stages:

- First, by a least-squares type of approach we construct a differentiable and convex optimisation problem (section 2.1) which always admits at least one solution (section 2.2). Situation 2) of paragraph 1 is then eliminated,
- Then, analysing the uniqueness property (section 2.3) and observing that it is not always guaranteed, we shall impose an additional constraint (section 2.4) on the solution characterised in section 2.1 so as to restore uniqueness.

### 2.1 Least-squares formalism

The unique solution of a linear system $\text{Ax}=b$ with a square and regular matrix realises the minimum of the quantity $\parallel \text{Ay}-b\parallel$ when $y$ ranges over ${ℝ}^{n}$ . This property opens the way which leads to a notion of solution for a general linear system of type [eq1-1] which confers on it the same properties as those of the particular case of the regular system. We shall therefore say of a point $x$ of ${ℝ}^{n}$ that it is a solution of the system [eq1-1] if it is a solution of the **optimisation problem**:

$\parallel \text{Ax}-b\parallel =\underset{y\in {ℝ}^{n}}{\text{Min}}\parallel \text{Ay}-b\parallel$                                 eq 2.1-1

This approach is natural because it defines a solution whose residual $r=\text{Ax}-b$ is zero in the case where the right-hand side is an element of $\text{Im}A$ and is of minimal norm in the contrary case, which is the best that one can expect.

In order to analyse the problem [eq 2.1-1], it is convenient to replace it by the following equivalent unconstrained optimisation problem:

$\text{trouver}x\in {ℝ}^{n}\text{tel que}J(x)=\underset{y\in {ℝ}^{n}}{\text{MinJ}}(y)$                     eq 2.1-2

where $J(.)$ is the functional defined by:

$J:y\in {ℝ}^{n}\to J(y)=\frac{1}{2}\parallel \text{Ax}-b\parallel$

The interest of the problem [eq 2.1-2] lies in the fact that the functional $J(.)$ satisfies the following properties:

- $J(.)$ is twice continuously differentiable:

$\text{DJ}(x):h\in {ℝ}^{n}\to \text{DJ}(x)h=({A}^{T}\text{Ax}-{A}^{T}\text{b,h})\in ℝ$                 eq 2.1-3

${D}^{2}J(x):(\text{h,k})\in {ℝ}^{n}\times {ℝ}^{n}\to \text{DJ}(x)(\text{h,k})=({A}^{T}\text{Ah},k)\in ℝ$             eq 2.1-4

- $J(.)$ is quadratic and convex.

Thus, the problem [eq 2.1-2] falls within the framework of differentiable and convex optimisation, so that we have the following results at our disposal ([bib1] p. 156 and 146):

1. From convexity: every local optimum is in fact a global optimum, that is, a solution of [eq2.1-2],
2. From differentiability: every local optimum satisfies the Euler equation $\text{DJ}(x)=0$ on Än which, in view of [eq 2.1-3], leads to the characterisation by the so-called **normal** **equations**:

${A}^{T}\text{Ax}={A}^{T}b$                                     eq 2.1-5

### 2.2 Existence of optima

In [bib1] p. 171 one finds a proof of the existence of at least one solution of the normal equations [eq 2.1-5]. This proof relies on arguments intended to take into account the infinite-dimensional case (theorem of projection onto a closed convex set of a Hilbert space).

Our case being distinctly simpler, we give a proof of the result which uses only simple algebraic arguments which, moreover, will be useful to us in paragraph 3. Showing that, for every $b$ element of ${ℝ}^{m}$ , the normal equations [eq 2.1-5] admit a solution is equivalent to establishing the inclusion $\text{Im}{A}^{T}\subset \text{Im}{A}^{T}A$ . Now, for every real matrix $M$ of order $m\times n$ we have $\text{Im}{M}^{T}={(\text{Ker}M)}^{\perp }$ ([bib3] p. 28). Thus, the inclusion to be established, which is equivalent to ${(\text{Ker}A)}^{\perp }\subset {(\text{Ker}{A}^{T}A)}^{\perp }$ , is itself equivalent to $\text{Ker}{A}^{T}A\subset \text{Ker}A$ . Let therefore $x\in \text{Ker}{A}^{T}A$ ; then $\text{Ax}\in \text{Ker}{A}^{T}$ , that is $\text{Ax}\in {(\text{Im}A)}^{\perp }$ . Since $\text{Ax}$ is also an element of $\text{Im}A$ , it can only be zero, which means that $x\in \text{Ker}A$ and completes the proof.

At this stage of the discussion, we can say that every system of type [eq 1-1] admits at least one solution in the sense of [eq 2.1-3] and that all these solutions are characterised as the solution, in Cramer's sense, of the normal equations of [eq 2.1-5]. Situation 2) of paragraph 1 is eliminated.

It remains to eliminate situation 3), that is, to guarantee uniqueness.

### 2.3 Uniqueness of the optimum and rank of the system

It is clear that the normal equations [eq 2.1-5], which characterise the optima that we seek, admit a unique solution under the necessary and sufficient condition that ${A}^{T}A$ be regular. Since ${A}^{T}A$ is always positive semi-definite, its invertibility is equivalent to its positive definiteness, so that, taking into account the expression [eq 2.1-4] of the second derivative of the functional $J(.)$ , we recover the well-known theorem of uniqueness of the optimum of the problem [eq2.1-2] for a convex functional that is twice continuously differentiable ([bib1] th 7.4-3 and 7.4-4).

In all generality, nothing prevents the matrix ${A}^{T}A$ from being singular, so the solution of the system [eq1‑1] in the sense of [eq 2.1-2] is not always unique. We nevertheless have a criterion for detecting this situation. In section 2.2 we established that $\text{Im}{A}^{T}\subset \text{Im}{A}^{T}A$ and, since the converse inclusion is trivially true, we may conclude that the identity $\text{Im}{A}^{T}=\text{Im}{A}^{T}A$ holds. The introduction of the **rank** $\text{rg}(A)$ of the matrix $A$ , the dimension of its image space, then allows us to say that a necessary and sufficient condition for ${A}^{T}A$ to be invertible is that $\text{rg}({A}^{T}A)=n$ , which is equivalent to $\text{rg}(A)=n$ because $\text{rg}({A}^{T}A)=\text{rg}({A}^{T})=\text{rg}(A)$ .

The interest of this criterion lies in the fact that it limits the analysis to the matrix $A$ alone, without it being necessary to form ${A}^{T}A$ explicitly. This criterion also shows us that the normal equations associated with a strictly under-constrained linear system always admit an infinity of solutions. Indeed, the rank of a matrix is also equal to the number of independent columns that it possesses; thus, for this rank to reach the value $n$ it is necessary that the columns of the matrix be of order at least $n$ .

### 2.4 Solution in the least-squares sense

We have just observed that the set of points which minimise the residual of the system [eq 1-1] is not necessarily reduced to a single point. In order to restore uniqueness we refine the notion of solution of the system [eq 1-1] of section 2.1 by defining the **solution in the least-squares sense** as the element of minimal norm of the set of points which minimise the residual. This solution $x$ is then characterised by:

$x\in {S}^{\text{def}}=\left\lbrace y\in {ℝ}^{n};{A}^{T}\text{Ax}={A}^{T}b\right\rbrace \text{et}\parallel x\parallel =\underset{y\in S}{\text{Inf}}\parallel y\parallel$

This characterisation is not satisfactory from the practical point of view because it requires the solution of a constrained optimisation problem. We shall replace it by another characterisation which is better suited in the sense that it will lead (see section 4.2) to a distinctly simpler computational procedure.

The set $S$ above is the translate of the kernel of ${A}^{T}A$ by any one of the solution vectors of the normal equations [eq 2.1-5]. Thus, the additional condition of minimisation of the norm is interpreted as a simple projection: the solution in the least-squares sense of the system [eq 1-1] is nothing other than the projection of the origin of Än onto the set of solutions of the normal equations. Thus, we can characterise it as the point of intersection between the set $S$ and the orthogonal complement of the kernel of ${A}^{T}A$ .

The definition of a solution of the system [eq 1-1] may then be summarised as follows:

> $x\text{est solution de}\text{Ax}=b\iff \lbrace \begin{array}{}{A}^{T}\text{Ax}={A}^{T}b\\ x\in {(\text{Ker}{A}^{T}A)}^{\perp }\end{array}$                 eq 2.4-1

The first condition makes $x$ a vector of minimal residual, while the second selects, among the vectors of minimal residual, the one of minimal norm.

The definition [eq 2.4-1] is a classical generalisation of the notion of solution of a regular equally constrained system and confers on every system of type [eq 1-1] one and only one solution.

## 3 Singular values

In this paragraph we present some results useful for the design of an operational method for solving the system [eq 1-1]. These results derive from the notion of singular values (section 3.1) and make it possible to construct a basis of the kernel and a basis of the image of the matrix of the system (section 3.2), from which it is possible to give a meaning, suited to the computation of the solution in the least-squares sense, to the inverse of an arbitrary matrix (section 3.3).

### 3.1 Singular value decomposition

Let us begin by recalling the definition of the singular values. The **singular values** of a real matrix $A$ of order $m\times n$ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of the square matrix ${A}^{T}A$ of order $n$ which, we recall, is positive semi-definite.

The notion of diagonalisation of square matrices (when they are diagonalisable) is generalised to rectangular matrices (without restriction) by the concept of decomposition (or factorisation) into singular values.

For every real matrix $A$ of order $m\times n$ , there exist two unitary square matrices $Q$ and $P$ of respective orders $m$ and $n$ such that:

> ${A=Q\Sigma P}^{T}$                                     eq 3.1-1

where $\Sigma$ is a matrix of order $m\times n$ whose structure is shown schematically below:

$\Sigma =∣\begin{array}{cccc}{\mu}_{1}& & & \\ & {\mu}_{2}& & \\ & & \mathrm{...}& \\ & & & {\mu}_{n}\end{array}∣\begin{array}{cccc}& & & \\ & 0& & \\ & & & \\ & & & \end{array}\mid \text{si}m\le n$

$\Sigma =∣\begin{array}{c}\underline{\begin{array}{cccc}{\mu}_{1}& & & \\ & {\mu}_{2}& & \\ & & \mathrm{...}& \\ & & & {\mu}_{n}\end{array}}\\ 0\end{array}∣\text{si}m>n$

The ${\mu}_{i}$ are the singular values of $A$ , which we assume ordered in decreasing order:

${\mu}_{1}\ge {\mu}_{2}\ge \text{...}\ge {\mu}_{n}$

A proof of this result may be found in [bib1] p. 10 for the equally constrained case and in [bib3] p. 73 for the over-constrained case; the under-constrained case is then deduced from it by transposition.

The SVD factorisation [eq 3.1-1] of $A$ gives ${A}^{T}A={P\Sigma }^{T}{\mathrm{SP}}^{T}$ and ${\text{AA}}^{T}={Q\Sigma \Sigma }^{T}{Q}^{T}$ , so that, ${\Sigma}^{T}\Sigma$ and ${\Sigma \Sigma }^{T}$ being diagonal square matrices, the matrix $P$ consists of the orthonormalised eigenvectors of the matrix ${A}^{T}A$ , while the matrix $Q$ consists of the orthonormalised eigenvectors of the matrix ${\text{AA}}^{T}$ .

### 3.2 Rank, image and kernel

Paragraph [§2] has shown the fundamental role played by the rank of the matrix $A$ and the kernel of the matrix ${A}^{T}A$ for the solution of a non-regular linear system of type [eq 1-1]. We shall now see how the factorisation [eq 3.1-1] can be used to determine this rank as well as a basis of $\text{Ker}{A}^{T}A$ .

Let $r$ be the index of the smallest non-zero singular value. The factorisation [eq 3.1-1] may also be written ${Q}^{T}\text{AP}=\Sigma$ , where taking the zero singular values into account makes it possible to specify the block decomposition of $\Sigma$ :

![Figure](/figures/r6.03.01/1000141E000069D500005B0EBFD22D0D54A23753.svg)

where ${\Sigma}_{r}=\text{Diag}({\mu}_{1},{\mu}_{2},\text{...},{\mu}_{r})$ is the diagonal matrix of order $r$ of the non-zero singular values in increasing order.

Since the matrices $Q$ and $P$ are regular, the matrices $A$ and $\Sigma$ are equivalent, so that their respective kernel and image coincide. We therefore deduce that:

- The rank of $A$ coincides with the number of non-zero singular values:

$\text{rg}A=r$

- The column vectors of $P$ of index $r+1$ to $n$ form a basis of $\text{Ker}A$
- The column vectors of $Q$ corresponding to the non-zero singular values form a basis of $\text{Im}A$

On the other hand, in section 2.3 we saw that $\text{Im}{A}^{T}=\text{Im}{A}^{T}A$ . The identity $\text{Im}{M}^{T}={(\text{Ker}M)}^{\perp }$ then gives us $\text{Ker}A=\text{Ker}{A}^{T}A$ , so that the second condition of the definition [eq 2.4-1] is simply realised by any vector which is expressed as a linear combination of the column vectors of $P$ corresponding to the non-zero singular values.

### 3.3 Pseudo-inverse and solution in the least-squares sense

Another application of the singular value decomposition consists in the notion of **pseudo-inverse** (or Moore–Penrose inverse), which generalises the usual notion of inverse of a regular square matrix to rectangular matrices on the one hand, and to singular square matrices on the other hand.

First of all, the pseudo-inverse of a matrix $\Sigma$ of the singular value decomposition [eq3.1-1] is defined by:

![Figure](/figures/r6.03.01/1000159A000069BB000059B6B61D5AED8C698D04.svg)

where ${\Sigma}_{r}^{-1}=\text{Diag}(\frac{1}{{\mu}_{1}},\frac{1}{{\mu}_{2}},\text{...},\frac{1}{{\mu}_{r}})$ is the inverse, in the usual sense, of ${S}_{r}$ .

This being so, we use the decomposition [eq 3.1-1] of the matrix $A$ to define its pseudo-inverse ${A}^{+}$ by:

> ${A}^{+}=P{\Sigma}^{+}{Q}^{T}$                                     eq 3.3-1

Likewise, from the decomposition [eq 3.1-1] of the matrix $A$ we draw ${A}^{T}A=P{\Sigma}^{T}\Sigma {P}^{T}$ , so that the pseudo-inverse ${({A}^{T}A)}^{+}$ of the matrix ${A}^{T}A$ is defined by:

> ${({A}^{T}A)}^{+}=P{\Sigma}^{+}{({\Sigma}^{T})}^{+}{P}^{T}$                             eq 3.3-2

We are now in a position to provide a simple interpretation of the solution in the least-squares sense defined by [eq 2.4-1].

The restriction to ${(\text{Ker}{A}^{T}A)}^{\perp }$ of the linear mapping associated with the matrix ${A}^{T}A$ defines an isomorphism of ${(\text{Ker}{A}^{T}A)}^{\perp }$ onto $\text{Im}{A}^{T}A$ . Since, on the one hand ${(\text{Ker}{A}^{T}A)}^{\perp }={(\text{Ker}A)}^{\perp }=\text{Im}{A}^{T}$ , and, on the other hand, $\text{Im}{A}^{T}A=\text{Im}{A}^{T}$ , this restriction is in fact an automorphism of ${(\text{Ker}{A}^{T}A)}^{\perp }$ .

In the basis of ${(\text{Ker}{A}^{T}A)}^{\perp }$ constituted by the first $r$ columns of the matrix $P$ , this automorphism is represented by the matrix ${\Sigma}_{r}^{2}$ . Thus, its reciprocal automorphism $y$ is represented by the matrix ${\Sigma}_{r}^{-2}$ . The extension to ${ℝ}^{n}$ of this automorphism is then represented, in the basis associated with the matrix $P$ , by the matrix ${({\Sigma}^{T}\Sigma )}^{+}={\Sigma}^{T}{({\Sigma}^{T})}^{+}$ , and therefore, in the canonical basis, by the matrix ${({A}^{T}A)}^{+}$ .

It follows that:

- We recover the fact that, for every $b$ element of ${ℝ}^{n}$ , there exists a unique vector $x\in {(\text{Ker}{A}^{T}A)}^{\perp }$ solution of ${A}^{T}\text{Ax}={A}^{T}b$ , that is, the existence and uniqueness of the solution in the least-squares sense [eq 2.4-1) of the system $\text{Ax}=b$ ,
- This unique solution is given by:

> $x={({A}^{T}A)}^{+}{A}^{T}b$                                 eq 3.3-3

The pseudo-inverse of a matrix is defined from the SVD decomposition of that matrix. Since the SVD decomposition is not unique, the pseudo-inverse matrix is not unique. On the other hand, from the point of view of the linear mappings associated with the matrices, the pseudo-inverse mapping is unique. All the pseudo-inverse matrices associated with the different SVD decompositions of a given matrix are then only particular matrix representatives which express this pseudo-inverse mapping relative to the bases induced by the orthogonal matrices of the SVD decompositions. Thus, the expression [eq 3.3-3] has a meaning: it defines a vector of which $\mathrm{x}$ represents the components with respect to the arrival basis (matrix $P$ ) of the SVD decomposition.

## 4 Solution of a rectangular linear system

The two methods of solution of the system [eq 1-1] which we present in sections 4.1 (method of the normal equations) and 4.2 (singular value decomposition) aim at the solution of the normal equations [eq 2.1-5]. These two methods differ not only in the choice of the algorithms that they implement (inversion versus pseudo-inversion), but also in their degree of generality and in their numerical properties, which are compared in section 4.3

### 4.1 Method of the normal equations

The solution of the system $\text{Ax}=b$ by the method of the normal equations consists in computing the solution in the least-squares sense [eq 2.4-1] in a “direct” manner, that is, by using directly the relation $x={({A}^{T}A)}^{-1}{A}^{T}b$ . For this purpose, one first computes ${A}^{T}A$ and ${A}^{T}b$ , then solves the system obtained either by an iterative method or by a factorisation of ${A}^{T}A$ .

We may already remark that this method is limited to regular matrices ${A}^{T}A$ , which limits its domain of application to the system [eq 1-1] whose matrix is of full rank (see section 2.3). In particular, the method of the normal equations can treat neither strictly under-constrained systems, nor singular equally constrained systems (see section 2.4).

### 4.2 Method by singular value decomposition

We saw in section 3.3 that the solution of the system $\text{Ax}=b$ in the least-squares sense defined by [eq 2.4-1] can be characterised by the relation $\mathrm{x}={({\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{A})}^{+}{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{b}$ [eq 3.3-3]. The method of solution of the system based on this property **is called the method by singular value decomposition** because it constructs the pseudo-inverse [eq 3.3-2] of ${A}^{T}A$ via the SVD decomposition [eq3.1‑1] of the matrix $A$ .

Since every matrix can be decomposed into singular values, it follows that every system of type [eq1-1] can be solved in the sense of [eq 2.4-1] by this method, which therefore presents, at least, the advantage of generality with respect to the method of the normal equations.

That is not all. The method by singular value decomposition, unlike the method of the normal equations, does not require the explicit construction of the matrix ${A}^{T}A$ and of the vector ${A}^{T}b$ (we shall see in section 4.3 the interest of this property from the numerical point of view). Indeed, it is easy to verify that the matrix $\Sigma$ of the singular values of the factorisation [eq 3.1-1] satisfies the identity ${\Sigma}^{+}{({\Sigma}^{T})}^{+}{\Sigma}^{T}={\Sigma}^{+}$ , so that, premultiplying ${A}^{T}$ by the pseudo-inverse of ${A}^{T}A$ and taking the factorisation [eq 3.1-1] into account, we obtain ${({A}^{T}A)}^{+}{A}^{T}=P{\Sigma}^{+}{({\Sigma}^{T})}^{+}{\Sigma}^{T}{P}^{T}P{\Sigma}^{T}{Q}^{T}$ , which, by orthogonality of $P$ , gives us ${({A}^{T}A)}^{+}{A}^{T}={A}^{+}$ . From then on, the characterisations [eq 3.3-3] and [eq 2.4-1] of the sought solution are equivalent to the characterisation:

> $x\text{est solution de}\text{Ax}=b\iff x={A}^{+}b$                     eq 4.2-1

### 4.3 Comparison of the method of the normal equations with the singular value decomposition method

In the two preceding sections, we have just observed that, algebraically, the singular value decomposition method is more general and simpler than the method of the normal equations. We shall now observe, following [bib3] p. 336, that it is also superior to it from the numerical point of view. This superiority is expressed, on the one hand, in terms of stability not only of the solution, but also of the construction of the problem and, on the other hand, at a less critical level, in terms of adaptation to the treatment of sparse matrices.

#### 4.3.1 Conditioning

The condition number of a matrix $A$ of order $m\times n$ is defined as the ratio of its extreme non-zero singular values:

$\text{cond}(A)=\frac{{\mu}_{1}}{{\mu}_{r}}$

where $r$ is the rank of the matrix $A$ .

The results presented in [bib3] p. 184, using the normal equations as a tool of analysis and not as a tool of computation, show that the perturbation of the solution of the optimisation problem [eq 2.1-1] due to rounding errors may be proportional to $\text{cond}{(A)}^{2}$ . But the classical results of the stability analysis of the solution of a linear system with respect to these same errors show a proportionality to the condition number of the matrix. So that, in the case of a direct solution of the normal equations, we obtain an error always proportional to $\text{cond}({A}^{T}A)=\text{cond}{(A)}^{2}$ , which is worse than $\text{cond}(A)$ .

The method of solution by singular value decomposition uses only orthogonal transformations (see paragraph 4), so that it does not modify the initial conditioning of the problem [eq 1-1] and is therefore, from this point of view, more attractive than the method of the normal equations.

#### 4.3.2 Loss of precision

We have just seen that rounding errors lead to a degradation of the solution which is more sensitive when it is computed via the normal equations rather than by a singular value decomposition. The following example, taken from [bib 2], shows that the very construction of the system [eq2.1-5] of the normal equations is perturbed by rounding errors.

Consider therefore the following matrix:

$A=\left[\begin{array}{cc}1& 1\\ \varepsilon & 0\\ 0& \varepsilon \end{array}\right]$ leading to ${A}^{T}A=\left[\begin{array}{cc}1+{\varepsilon}^{2}& 1\\ 1& 1+{\varepsilon}^{2}\end{array}\right]$

whose singular values are ${\mu}_{1}=\sqrt{2+{\varepsilon}^{2}}$ and ${\mu}_{2}=\mid \varepsilon \mid$ , so that the rank of $A$ is $2$ as soon as $\varepsilon \ne 0$ . If $\varepsilon$ satisfies ${\varepsilon}^{2}<{\varepsilon}_{\text{mach}}<\varepsilon$ where ${\varepsilon}_{\text{mach}}$ is the machine precision, then all the coefficients of ${A}^{T}A$ will be computed with the value $1$ and the computed singular values will be, at best, ${\mu}_{1}=\sqrt{2}$ and ${\mu}_{2}=0$ . It follows that the numerical rank, computed by the normal equations, will be $1$ , whereas that computed by an SVD decomposition of the matrix $A$ would be equal to $2$

#### 4.3.3 Sparse structure

At a lesser level, the construction of the matrix of the normal equations induces a fill-in of the associated system which the method using the singular value decomposition avoids.

#### 4.3.4 Conclusion

The following table summarises the discussion of the preceding subsections:

|  | **Generality** | **Conditioning** | **Loss of precision in the construction of the problem** | **fill-in** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal equations | full-rank systems | $\text{cond}{(A)}^{2}$ | possible | yes |
| SVD | any system | $\text{cond}(A)$ | impossible | no |

## 5 SVD algorithm for the solution of an equally or under-constrained linear system

In this paragraph, we detail the method of solution of non-regular systems implemented in *Code_Aster*. This method applies to the under-constrained or singular equally constrained system and provides the solution in the least-squares sense [eq 2.4-1].

The computation of an SVD decomposition of $A$ is equivalent to the computation of the spectrum of the associated normal matrix ${A}^{T}A$ . Thus, it can be obtained only at the convergence of an iterative process.

**Section 5.1** sets out the **principle of the algorithm** and shows in particular how the application of two orthogonal transformations makes it possible to **reduce the problem** to the simple search for the SVD decomposition of an upper bidiagonal matrix. **Sections 5.2** and **5.3** are devoted to **the algorithms** of these **reductions**. **Section 5.4** presents the algorithm of the **SVD decomposition** of the bidiagonal matrix.

The algorithms will be described with the notational convention in which:

- $R(i,j,\theta )$ denotes the Givens rotation of the plane $(i,j)$ and of angle $\theta$ ,
- ${A}^{(k)}$ denotes the iterate of index $k$ of a matrix iteration and ${A}^{(k,l)}$ the iterate $l$ of an iteration internal to the iterate ${A}^{(k)}$ .

### 5.1 Reduction of the problem and principle of the algorithm

In this section, we present the algorithm for the solution of an equally or under-constrained linear system by the SVD method.

We reduce the problem to the search for the SVD decomposition of a bidiagonal matrix as in [bib2], but we carry out the reduction in a manner different from that proposed in [bib2]: we begin by reducing the matrix to an upper triangular form, then we reduce this triangular matrix to an upper bidiagonal form. These two reductions are carried out by orthogonal transformations.

The operations of **computation of the SVD decomposition** follow one another as follows:

#### 5.1.1 Reduction to upper triangular form

> $\begin{array}{ccc}A=[U\text{}0]{P}_{1}^{T}& & \text{si}m<n\\ A={\mathrm{UP}}_{1}^{T}& & \text{si}m=n\end{array}$ **eq 5.1-1**

where ${P}_{1}$ is an orthogonal matrix of order $n$ and $U$ an upper triangular matrix of order $m$ .

#### 5.1.2 Reduction to upper bidiagonal form

> $U={Q}_{2}{\text{BP}}_{2}^{T}$                                     eq 5.1-2

where ${Q}_{2}$ and ${P}_{2}$ are two *orthogonal matrices* of order $m$ and $B$ an upper *bidiagonal matrix* of order $m$ .

#### 5.1.3 SVD decomposition of the upper bidiagonal matrix

> $B={Q}_{3}\Sigma {P}_{3}^{T}$                                     eq 5.1-3

### 5.2 Reduction to upper triangular form

Starting from a matrix $A$ of order $m\times n$ for $m\le n$ one determines an upper triangular matrix $U$ of order $m$ and an orthogonal matrix $P$ of order $n$ such that:

$\begin{array}{ccc}A=\left[U\text{}0\right]{P}^{T}& & \text{si}m<n\\ & & \\ A={\mathrm{UP}}^{T}& & \text{si}m=n\end{array}$

Algorithmically, the factorisation uses an elimination method which is interpreted as the construction of a sequence of matrices ${A}^{(k)}$ by:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{A}^{(m)}=A& & \\ & & \\ {A}^{(k-1)}={A}^{(k)}{P}^{(k)}& & \text{pour}k=m,m-1,\text{...},1\end{array}$

where each current matrix ${A}^{(k)}$ presents the structure shown schematically below:

![Figure](/figures/r6.03.01/10001FAA000069D500003FAAAAD18EFEC0022B4C.svg)

The coefficients ${a}_{i,j}^{(k)}$ of the matrices ${A}^{(k)}$ of the iteration therefore satisfy:

> ${a}_{i,j}^{(k)}=0\text{si}\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}k+1\le i\le m& \text{et}m+1\le j\le n\\ k+1\le i\le m& \text{et}1\le j\le k\\ k+1\le j\le i\le m& \end{array}$                 eq 5.2-1

so that, at the end of the recurrence, we shall have:

$U={A}^{(1)}\text{et}P=\underset{k=m,m-1,\text{...},1}{\Pi {P}^{(k)}}$

The problem therefore reduces to the preservation of the structure [eq 5.2-1] in passing from ${A}^{(k)}$ to ${A}^{(k-1)}$ by a transformation ${P}^{(k)}$ which must be orthogonal. The problem of orthogonality is settled by choosing the transformation as a product of Givens rotations, and the problem of the preservation of the structure is solved by carrying out this product in an order which does not destroy the zeros created.

Taking into account the rectangular structure of the matrix ${A}^{(k)}$ , we construct the iterate in ${A}^{(k-1)}$ two phases:

- Phase $1$ successively annihilates the coefficients ${a}_{k,j}^{(k-1)}$ corresponding to the columns $j=k-1,k-2,\text{...},1,$ , which is expressed by:

$\begin{array}{cc}{A}^{(k-1,k-1)}={A}^{(k)}& \\ {A}^{(k-1,j-1)}={A}^{(k-1,j)}R{(k,j,{\theta}_{j}^{(k)})}^{T}& \text{pour}j=k-1,k-2,\text{...},1\end{array}$

- Phase $2$ successively annihilates the coefficients ${a}_{k,j}^{(k-1)}$ corresponding to the columns $j=n,n-1,\text{...},m+1,$ , which is expressed by the recurrence:

$\begin{array}{cc}{A}^{(k-1,n)}={A}^{(k-1,0)}& \\ {A}^{(k-1,j-1)}={A}^{(k-1,j)}R{(k,j,{\theta}_{j}^{(k)})}^{T}& \text{pour}j=n,n-1,\text{...},k+1\end{array}$

The angle ${\theta}_{j}^{(k)}$ of the Givens rotation of the plane $(k,j)$ is chosen so as to annihilate the coefficient in position $(k,j)$ of ${a}^{(k-1,j)}$ . The application of each rotation therefore modifies only the columns $k$ and $j$ , which does not destroy the zero coefficients produced by the preceding stages. We observe that the column $k$ plays a particular role (that of pivot) because it alone is systematically modified by each rotation, whereas the other columns are modified only by the rotation which annihilates their coefficient at row $k$ .

At the end of these recurrences, we have ${A}^{(k-1)}={A}^{(k-1,k)}$ . The matrix ${P}^{(k)}$ is then given by:

${P}^{(k)}=\underset{j=m+1}{\overset{j=n}{\Pi}}R(k,j,{\theta}_{j}^{(k)})\underset{j=1}{\overset{j=k-1}{\Pi}}R(k,j,{\theta}_{j}^{(k)})$

so that the matrix $P$ is equal to:

$P=\underset{k=1}{\overset{k=m}{\Pi}}(\underset{j=m+1}{\overset{j=n}{\Pi}}R(k,j,{\theta}_{j}^{(k)})\underset{j=1}{\overset{j=k-1}{\Pi}}R(k,j,{\theta}_{j}^{(k)}))$

### 5.3 Reduction to upper bidiagonal form

Reducing an upper triangular square matrix $A$ of order $m$ to upper bidiagonal form consists in finding two orthogonal matrices $P$ and $Q$ and an upper bidiagonal matrix $B$ , all three of order $m$ , such that:

$A={\text{QBP}}^{T}$

Algorithmically, the factorisation proceeds like that of the preceding section by using an elimination method which is interpreted algebraically as the construction of a sequence of matrices ${A}^{(k)}$ by:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}{A}^{(1)}=A& \\ {A}^{(k+1)}={Q}^{{(k)}^{T}}{A}^{(k)}{P}^{(k)}& \text{pour}k=1,2,\text{...},m-2\end{array}$

where each current matrix ${A}^{(k)}$ presents the following block-diagonal structure:

- The upper diagonal block (row and column indices varying from $1$ to $k-1$ ) is an upper bidiagonal matrix of order $k-1$ ,
- The lower diagonal block (row and column indices varying from $k$ to $m$ ) is an upper triangular matrix of order $m-k$ .

The coefficients ${a}_{i,j}^{(k)}$ of the matrices ${A}^{(k)}$ of the iteration therefore satisfy:

> ${a}_{i,j}^{(k)}=0\text{si}\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}1\le i\le k-1& \text{et}i+2\le j\le m\\ 1\le i\le k-1& \text{et}i<j\\ k\le i\le m& \text{et}1\le j<i\end{array}$                 eq 5.3-1

so that, at the end of the recurrence, we shall have:

$B={A}^{(m+1)}\text{},\text{}Q=\underset{k=1}{\overset{k=m}{\Pi}}{Q}^{(k)}\text{et}P=\underset{k=1}{\overset{k=m}{\Pi}}{P}^{(k)}$

As for the factorisation of the preceding section, the problem reduces to the preservation of the structure [eq 5.3-1] in passing from ${A}^{(k)}$ to ${A}^{(k+1)}$ . The orthogonality of the transformations ${Q}^{(k)}$ and ${P}^{(k)}$ is obtained by constructing them as a product of Givens rotations, and the problem of the preservation of the structure is solved by carrying out these products in an order which does not destroy the zeros created by the preceding stages.

The algorithm therefore successively annihilates the coefficients $(k,j+1)$ for $j=m-1,m-2,\text{...},k+2$ by the application on the right of a Givens rotation of the plane $(j,j+1)$ . This rotation modifies only the columns $j$ and $j+1$ , which creates a parasitic coefficient in position $(j+1,j)$ . This parasitic coefficient is then eliminated by the application on the left of the transpose of a Givens rotation in the plane $(j,j+1)$ .

The process of passing from ${A}^{(k)}$ to ${A}^{(k+1)}$ may then be formalised by:

$\begin{array}{}{A}^{(k+1,m-1)}={A}^{(k)}\\ \lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{A}^{(k+1,j=1/2)}={A}^{(k+1,j)}R(J,J+1,{\theta}_{j}^{(k)})& & \\ {A}^{(k+1,j=1)}=R{(J,J+1,{\theta}_{j=1/2}^{(k)})}^{T}{A}^{(k+1,j=1/2)}& & \text{pour}j=m-1,m-2,\text{...},k+1\end{array}\\ {A}^{(k+1)}={A}^{(k+1,k)}\end{array}$

where the angles ${\theta}_{j}^{(k)}$ and ${\theta}_{j=1/2}^{(k)}$ are chosen so as to annihilate respectively the coefficient in position $(k,j+1)$ of ${A}^{(k+1,j)}$ and the coefficient in position $(j+1,j)$ of ${A}^{(k+1,j=1/2)}$ .

The structure of the matrices ${A}^{(k+1,j)}$ is illustrated in the following figure:

![Figure](/figures/r6.03.01/10001D98000069D5000040646A50C96C78BCDF81.svg)

At the end of this recurrence, the matrices ${P}^{(k)}$ and ${Q}^{(k)}$ are given by:

${P}^{(k)}=\underset{j=m-1}{\overset{j=k+1}{\Pi}}R(j,j+1,{\theta}_{j}^{(k)})\text{et}{Q}^{(k)}=\underset{j=m-1}{\overset{j=k+1}{\Pi}}R(k,j,{\theta}_{j=1/2}^{(k)})$

so that the matrices $P$ and $Q$ are equal to:

$P=\underset{k=1}{\overset{k=m-2}{\Pi}}\underset{j=m-1}{\overset{j=k+1}{\Pi}}R(j,j+1,{\theta}_{j}^{(k)})\text{et}Q=\underset{k=1}{\overset{k=m-2}{\Pi}}\underset{j=m-1}{\overset{j=k+1}{\Pi}}R(j,j+1,{\theta}_{j=1/2}^{(k)})$

### 5.4 SVD decomposition of an upper bidiagonal matrix

We present an algorithm for the construction of the SVD decomposition of an upper bidiagonal matrix $A$ of order $m$ . The algorithm therefore constructs two orthogonal matrices $Q$ and $P$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ such that:

$A={\text{QDP}}^{T}$

The algorithm is taken from [bib2].

#### 5.4.1 Principle of the algorithm

The algorithm for the computation of the SVD decomposition diagonalises the matrix $A$ iteratively by means of the recurrence:

> $\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}{A}^{(1)}=A& \\ {A}^{(k+1)}={Q}^{{(k)}^{T}}{A}^{(k)}{P}^{(k)}& \text{pour}k=1,2,\text{...}\end{array}$                 eq 5.4.1-1

where the matrices ${Q}^{(k)}$ and ${P}^{(k)}$ are orthogonal and the matrices ${A}^{(k)}$ are upper bidiagonal.

At convergence, we shall have:

$D={A}^{(\infty )},P=\underset{k=1}{\overset{k=\infty }{\Pi}}{P}^{(k)}\text{et}Q=\underset{k=1}{\overset{k=\infty }{\Pi}}{Q}^{(k)}$

The idea of the iteration consists in:

- Choosing ${P}^{(k)}$ so as to make the algorithm $\text{QR}$ converge, applied to the diagonalisation of the (so-called normal) matrix ${A}^{T}A$ without forming it explicitly. Indeed, the matrix $P$ of the SVD decomposition of $A$ is nothing other than the matrix of the eigenvectors of

${A}^{T}A$ ,

- Choosing ${Q}^{(k)}$ so as to preserve the upper bidiagonal structure of the successive iterates.

As in the case of the factorisations presented in sections 5.2 and 5.3, the matrices ${Q}^{(k)}$ and ${P}^{(k)}$ are constructed as a product of Givens rotations. The passage from ${A}^{(k)}$ to ${A}^{(k+1)}$ is then realised by:

> ${A}^{(k+1)}={\left[{Q}^{(k,2)}{Q}^{(k,3)}\text{...}{Q}^{(k,m)}\right]}^{T}{A}^{(k)}=\left[{P}^{(k,2)}{P}^{(k,3)}\text{...}{P}^{(k,m)}\right]$             eq 5.4.1-2

where the ${Q}^{(k,i)}$ and ${P}^{(k,i)}$ are two rotations of the plane $(i-1,i)$ of respective angles ${\theta}_{j}$ and ${\phi }_{i}$ :

${Q}^{(k,i)}=R(i-1,i,{\theta}_{i}^{(k)})\text{et}{P}^{(k,i)}=R(i-1,i,{\phi }_{i}^{(k)})$

The rotations are applied alternately on the right and then on the left so that ${A}^{(k+1)}$ preserves the upper bidiagonal structure of ${A}^{(k)}$ . To this end:

The angle ${\phi }_{2}^{(k)}$ is, for the moment, chosen arbitrarily; the application of the rotation ${P}^{(k,2)}$ then creates a coefficient in position $(2,1)$ ,

The angle ${\theta}_{2}^{(k)}$ is chosen so that the application of the rotation ${Q}^{(k,2)}$ annihilates the coefficient in position $(2,1)$ , which creates a non-zero coefficient in position $(1,3)$ ,

The angle ${\phi }_{3}^{(k)}$ is chosen so that the application of the rotation ${P}^{(k,3)}$ annihilates the coefficient in position $(1,3)$ , which creates a non-zero coefficient in position $(3,2)$ ,

$\begin{array}{}.\\ .\\ .\end{array}$

The angle ${\theta}_{m-1}^{(k)}$ is chosen so that the application of the rotation ${Q}^{(k,m-1)}$ annihilates the coefficient in position $(m-1,m-2)$ , which creates a non-zero coefficient in position $(m-2,m)$ ,

The angle ${\phi }_{m}^{(k)}$ is chosen so that the application of the rotation ${P}^{(k,m)}$ annihilates the coefficient in position $(m-2,m)$ , which creates a non-zero coefficient in position $(m,m-1)$ ,

The angle ${\theta}_{m}^{(k)}$ is chosen so that the application of the rotation ${Q}^{(k,m)}$ annihilates the coefficient in position $(m,m-1)$ , and so that the matrix ${A}^{(k+1)}$ is upper bidiagonal.

For any value of the angle, this process ensures the maintenance of the upper bidiagonal structure of the iterates [eq 5.4.1-2]. We shall now see how it is possible to choose this angle so as to make the iteration [eq 5.4.1-1] converge.

#### 5.4.2 Implicit diagonalisation of the normal matrix

The algorithm of the preceding subsection leaves undetermined the angle ${\phi }_{2}^{(k)}$ of the first rotation of ${P}^{(k)}$ . We shall remove this indeterminacy so as to make the matrix ${P}^{(k)}$ the orthogonal matrix of one step $\text{QR}$ , with spectral shift, applied to the diagonalisation of the normal matrix $M={A}^{T}A$ .

To the SVD iteration [eq 5.4.1-1] of the matrix $A$ , we associate an iteration on the normal matrix $M={A}^{T}A$ :

${M}^{(k+1)}={A}^{{(k+1)}^{T}}{A}^{(k+1)}={P}^{{(k)}^{T}}{M}^{(k)}{P}^{(k)}$

**Iteration** $\text{QR}$ **for the diagonalisation of the normal matrix**

The transformation $\text{QR}$ , with spectral shift ${\sigma}_{k}$ , applied to ${M}^{(k)}$ is written:

Factorise ${M}^{(k)}-{\sigma}_{k}I$ in the form ${M}^{(k)}-{\sigma}_{k}I={P}_{\sigma}{R}_{\sigma}$

Construct ${M}_{\sigma}^{(k+1)}$ by ${M}_{\sigma}^{(k+1)}={P}_{\sigma}{R}_{\sigma}+{\sigma}_{k}I$

where ${P}_{\sigma}$ and ${R}_{s}$ are two matrices, respectively orthogonal and upper triangular. The matrices ${M}^{(k)}$ and ${M}_{\sigma}^{(k+1)}$ are therefore tridiagonal and similar:

${M}_{\sigma}^{(k+1)}={P}_{\sigma}^{T}{M}^{(k)}{P}_{\sigma}$

From the practical point of view, the matrix ${P}_{\sigma}$ appears as a product of Givens rotations:

${P}_{\sigma}^{T}=R(n-1,n,{\psi}_{n})R(n-2,n-1,{\psi}_{n-1})\text{...}R(1,2,{\psi}_{2})$

The angles ${\psi}_{k}$ are chosen so that the application on the left of $R(k-1,k,{\psi}_{k})$ to the matrix $\left[\underset{1=k-1,k-2,\text{...},2}{\Pi}R(1-1,1,{\psi}_{1})\right]({M}^{(k)}-{\sigma}_{k}I)$ annihilates the coefficient in position $(k,k-1)$ in the resulting matrix.

Francis showed that the passage from ${M}^{(k)}$ to ${M}_{\sigma}^{(k+1)}$ does not require the explicit formation of the matrix ${M}^{(k)}-{\sigma}_{k}I$ : the shift can be carried out implicitly. The theorem is stated as follows:

**Theorem** **(Francis)** : Let $X$ be an orthogonal matrix whose first column coincides with that of ${P}_{\sigma}$ . Under the hypotheses:

1. ${M}^{(k+1)}={X}^{T}{M}^{(k)}X$
2. ${M}^{(k+1)}$ is tridiagonal,
3. The subdiagonal elements of ${M}^{(k)}$ are all non-zero (irreducibility of ${M}^{(k)}$ ),

one has

${M}^{(k+1)}={\text{DM}}_{\sigma}^{(k+1)}D$

where $D$ is a diagonal matrix whose diagonal coefficients are all equal to $\pm 1$ .

**Application to the SVD algorithm**

From then on, the choice of the angle ${\phi }_{2}^{(k)}$ of the first rotation of the SVD iteration [eq 5.4.1-1] consists in ${\phi }_{2}^{(k)}\text{=-}{\psi}_{2}$ . Thus $R(1,2,{\phi }_{2}^{(k)})=R{(1,2,{\psi}_{2})}^{T}$ , so that the first column of ${P}^{(k)}$ coincides with the first column of ${P}_{\sigma}$ . Therefore, if all the subdiagonal elements of ${M}^{(k)}$ are non-zero, then the matrices ${P}^{(k)}$ and ${P}_{\sigma}$ are identified (up to a multiplicative factor $\pm 1$ of the columns) and the SVD iteration [eq 5.4.1-1] is equivalent to the application of the transformation $\text{QR}$ , with shift, to the matrix ${M}^{(k)}$ .

**Choice of the spectral shift**

The shift is usually chosen as the eigenvalue of the lower minor of order two of ${A}^{(k)}$ closest to ${a}_{m,m}^{(k)}$ . This choice ensures a global convergence which, generally, is cubic.

**Applicability of Francis's theorem and the splitting phenomenon**

The use of Francis's theorem assumes that all the subdiagonal coefficients of the matrices ${M}^{(k)}$ are non-zero, which is in no way guaranteed. Moreover, within the framework of the method $\text{QR}$ of diagonalisation of a symmetric tridiagonal matrix, the appearance of zero subdiagonal coefficients is:

- Desirable: the zero coefficients decouple the diagonal blocks which they bound, which reduces the diagonalisation of the complete matrix to the diagonalisation of its diagonal blocks (this phenomenon is often called “splitting”),
- Inevitable: the convergence of the algorithm towards an eigenvalue is interpreted algebraically as the appearance of a preceding diagonal block of order 1.

In the following subsection, we shall see what treatment it is appropriate to adopt in the presence of a splitting.

#### 5.4.3 Splitting analysis

The splitting analysis bears on each iterate taken independently of the others; thus, we shall not use the upper index $(k)$ .

Let ${d}_{1},{d}_{2},\text{...},{d}_{m}$ and ${e}_{2},{e}_{3},\text{...},{e}_{m}$ be the diagonal and superdiagonal elements, respectively, of $A$ . The subdiagonal elements of the normal matrix $M={A}^{T}A$ are then given by:

${m}_{i+1,i}={d}_{i}{e}_{l+1}\text{pour}i=1,2,\text{...},m-1$

Suppose, for simplicity, that only the coefficient ${m}_{l-1,l}$ is zero. The matrix $M$ then presents a structure of two diagonal blocks whose respective spectra, taken together, give the spectrum of $M$ . This splitting takes place either for ${e}_{1}=0$ or for ${e}_{1}\ne 0\text{et}{d}_{l-1}=0$ .

The case ${e}_{1}=0$ poses no difficulty. The matrix $A$ then possesses a diagonal structure of two blocks which each supply a complementary part of the SVD decomposition of $A$ . Each block being upper bidiagonal, with no zero superdiagonal coefficients, its SVD decomposition can be computed by the iteration [eq 5.4.1-1].

The case ${e}_{1}\ne 0\text{et}{d}_{l-1}=0$ is more delicate. Indeed, the iteration [eq 5.4.1-1] can be applied neither to the matrix $A$ , so as not to violate the hypotheses of Francis's theorem, nor to any submatrix of $A$ , so as to ensure the bidiagonal structure of the iterates. This problem is circumvented by the post-multiplication of $A$ by a series of Givens rotations in the successive planes $(l-1,l),(l-1,l+1),\text{...},(l-1,m)$ :

- The rotation of the plane $(l-1,l)$ annihilates the coefficient $(l-1,l)$ and creates a coefficient in position $(l-1,l+1)$ ,
- The rotation of the plane $(l-1,l+1)$ annihilates the coefficient $(l-1,l+1)$ and creates a coefficient in position $(l-1,l+2)$ ,
- The rotation of the plane $(l-1,l+1)$ annihilates the coefficient $(l-1,l+1)$ and creates a coefficient in position $(l-1,l+2)$ ,

$\begin{array}{}.\\ .\\ .\end{array}$

- The rotation of the plane $(l-1,m)$ annihilates the coefficient $(l-1,m)$ and does not create a coefficient.

So that the matrix produced by this process presents the same structure as that corresponding to the case ${e}_{1}=0$ .

#### 5.4.4 Organisation of the algorithm

The algorithm isolates each singular value successively; thus, there exists an index ${k}_{p}$ such that the iterate ${A}^{({k}_{p})}$ decomposes into two diagonal blocks:

![Figure](/figures/r6.03.01/10000C30000069BB000030E39465049A7D2849DF.svg)

where ${B}^{({k}_{p})}$ is an upper bidiagonal matrix of order $p$ and ${D}^{({k}_{p})}$ is a diagonal matrix of order $m-p+1$ gathering on its diagonal the singular values found.

Starting from this iterate, the algorithm applies the iteration of subsection 5.4.1 to the submatrix ${B}^{({k}_{p})}$ until the annihilation of the coefficient in position $(p-1,p)$ , the signal of the convergence of the ${p}^{\text{ième}}$ singular value. Each step of the internal iteration, thus defined, is organised as follows:

- Splitting analysis,
- In the case where the splitting found corresponds to a zero diagonal element, an additional series of rotations is applied in order to recover the splitting structure generated by a zero superdiagonal element. These rotations are constructed according to the method presented in subsection 5.4.3,
- If the coefficient in position $(p-1,p)$ does not produce a splitting, then the submatrix ${B}^{({k}_{p})}$ is the object of one step of the iteration [eq 5.4.1-1] where, in accordance with the analysis of subsection 5.4.2, the angle of the first rotation is chosen so that this step is equivalent to the application of a transformation $\text{QR}$ , with implicit spectral shift, to the associated normal matrix.

The complete convergence of the iteration is then obtained at the index ${k}_{m}$ for which the submatrix ${D}^{({k}_{p})}$ is of order $m$ .

Of course, in practice, a coefficient is considered to be zero as soon as it is smaller, in absolute value, than a certain tolerance. The tolerance generally used for singular-value problems is chosen as the product of the machine precision by ${\parallel A\parallel }_{1}$ . Note that, in the case of a splitting produced by a diagonal element which is zero at the chosen tolerance, the application of the additional series of rotations described in subsection 5.4.3 creates a sub-column of non-zero elements below this coefficient. These elements are not troublesome because they are all zero at the chosen tolerance.
