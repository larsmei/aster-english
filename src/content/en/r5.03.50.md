# r5.03.50 Discrete formulation of contact-friction

**Summary:**

This document describes the numerical methods used to treat contact/friction problems in large displacements in the operator STAT_NON_LINE or DYNA_NON_LINE by means of a discrete formulation. The available algorithms make it possible to treat the problem exactly (for contact) or approximately (for contact and for friction), without any restrictive choice on the underlying mechanical problem (kinematics or non-linear constitutive laws).

This document also contains the theoretical elements for describing the solution of general unilateral problems (without pairing, such as seepage for example) usable by the keyword FORMULATION='LIAISON_UNIL'.

## 1 Introduction

Two distinct solids are said to be in contact when they share a common surface which is part of their boundary. Treating unilateral contact consists in preventing one of the solids from “passing through” the other. Friction characterises the tangential sliding of one solid with respect to the other. In *code_aster*, only Coulomb dry friction is available.

Contact in the discrete formulation in *code_aster* has the following characteristics:

- The contact and friction laws are established from already discretised quantities: displacements and nodal forces on the meshed structures;
- The contact/friction problem is solved by decoupling it from the problem of the equilibrium of the structure.

In this document the various ingredients of the problem will be presented:

- Description of the contact and friction laws;
- Geometric pairing: this is the phase in which one links a node to another node, or a node to a mesh cell, in order to create a *potential* contact pair, that is to say one identifies the degrees of freedom for which discrete non-penetration relations will be written;
- Establishment of the contact/friction problem;
- Description of the algorithms available for solving the contact/friction problem.

This document is therefore limited to the methods known as GCP, CONTRAINTE and PENALISATION.

The formulation known as “continuous” (FORMULATION='CONTINUE' in DEFI_CONTACT) is the subject of the document [r5.03.52](doc:r5.03.52).

The formulation associated with the XFEM elements (FORMULATION='XFEM' in DEFI_CONTACT) is discussed in [r5.03.53](doc:r5.03.53).

This document also contains the theoretical elements for describing the solution of general unilateral problems (without pairing, such as seepage for example) usable by the keyword FORMULATION='LIAISON_UNIL'.

## 2 Mechanics of contact and of friction

### 2.1 Definition of the problem and notation

Consider two solids which may come into frictional contact; the contact zone is either a point, a line or a surface. Let $\left\lbrace n\right\rbrace$ be the outward normal to the surface of one of the solids in contact and $\left\lbrace u\right\rbrace$ the displacement vector between the two solids. Then $g=\langle u\rangle .\left\lbrace n\right\rbrace$ is the displacement projected onto this normal; it will be called the *gap*. From the Cauchy stress, one defines the pressure 1 $p=\langle n\rangle .\left[\sigma \right].\left\lbrace n\right\rbrace$ and the tangential shear stress $\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace =\left[\sigma \right].\left\lbrace n\right\rbrace -p.\left\lbrace n\right\rbrace$ as being exerted by one of the surfaces on the other.

### 2.2 Signorini contact conditions

The two variables defining contact have been introduced:

- The signed distance between the two surfaces, or “gap”, $g$ *;*
- The contact pressure $p$ ;

One then defines the three Hertz-Signorini-Moreau contact conditions

- Geometric condition: impenetrability of matter (Signorini-Hertz).

$$
g \ge 0 \iff    \left\{   \begin{array}{l}   g > 0 \quad \text{pas de contact} \\   g = 0 \quad \text{contact}   \end{array}   \right.
$$

- Mechanical condition: intensility (Signorini-Hertz)

$$
p\ge 0\mathrm{\iff} \left\{  \begin{array}{c} p>0 \quad \text{contact} \\ p=0 \quad \text{pas de contact}\end{array} \right.
$$

- Energetic condition: complementarity/exclusion (Moreau)

$$
p.g=0\iff \left\{  \begin{array}{c} p=0  \quad \text{décollement} \\ g=0  \quad \text{contact}\end{array} \right.
$$

The contact problem thus posed introduces a relation which is *not single-valued* ($p$ is not a *function* of $g$), *positive semi-definite* and *non-differentiable* at $p=g=0$. It is a problem which is mathematically difficult to treat. Contact is a reversible and conservative phenomenon for which an energy potential can be introduced and whose result does not depend on the loading path; it is similar to the Hencky elastoplastic model. The Signorini contact law is written:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{cc}g\ge 0& (a)\\ p\ge 0& (b)\\ p.g=0& (c)\end{array} \right.
$$

**Notes:**

- Contact is assumed to be without adhesion, by virtue of the second condition (intensility).
- The third condition allows the unilateral contact problem to be well-posed so as to be solvable by the classical techniques of constrained optimisation (introduction of the Kuhn & Tucker conditions), such as the active-constraint method.

### 2.3 Formulation of the friction problem

#### 2.3.1 Definitions

The friction criteria chosen are of the form:

$$
h(\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace )\le 0
$$

where $h(\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace )$ is a convex function. The non-sliding domain is defined by the interior of the convex set. Two friction criteria of the form $h(\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace )\le 0$ are particularly used: the Tresca criterion and the Coulomb criterion.

#### 2.3.2 The Tresca criterion

The Tresca criterion is defined by the following function $h(\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace )$:

$$
h(\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace )=\mathrm{\parallel }\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace \mathrm{\parallel }-k\le 0

\textrm{avec } k \textrm{ une constante donnée}
$$

One denotes by $C$ the convex disc of radius $k$ centred at the origin defined by:

$$
C=\left\lbrace \left\lbrace r\right\rbrace \text{tel que}\mathrm{\parallel }\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace \mathrm{\parallel }\le k\right\rbrace
$$

The non-sliding condition is then defined by the membership of $\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace$ in the interior of the disc $C$. In the case of sliding, for $\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace$ lying on the boundary of $C$, the sliding direction $\left\lbrace t\right\rbrace$ of $\left\lbrace \dot{u}\right\rbrace$ is given by the normal to the criterion at $\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace$, as indicated on the .

| ![Figure](images/Shape6.gif) |
| --- |
| **Figure** 2.3.2-a **: friction disc for the Tresca criterion.** |

This is expressed by an expression for the sliding velocity analogous to the plastic multiplier:

$$
{\dot{u}}_{t}=\lambda .r
$$

With $\lambda \ge 0$, that is to say that the sliding velocity is in the same direction as the tangential stress.

#### 2.3.3 The Coulomb criterion

The Coulomb criterion is defined by the following function $h(\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace )$:

$$
h(\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace ,\mu ,p)=\mathrm{\parallel }\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace \mathrm{\parallel }-k(\mu ,p)\le 0

\textrm{et}

k=\mu .\mid p\mid
$$

The value of $k$ depends on the contact pressure $p$ and on the Coulomb friction coefficient $\mu$. Defined in this way, $h$ is a cone. In the case of sliding, for $\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace$ lying on the boundary of $h$, the sliding direction $\left\lbrace t\right\rbrace$ of $\dot{u}$ is not given by the normal to the criterion at $\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace$, but by the normal to the convex disc $C$ of radius $k=\mu .\mid p\mid$. The Tresca criterion corresponds to a “slice” along the plane orthogonal to the Coulomb cone.

#### 2.3.4 Application to Coulomb friction

Let us write the system of equations and inequalities which must be satisfied by these quantities in the case of the Coulomb friction criterion:

$$
\left\{    \begin{array}{cc}g\ge 0& (a)\\ p\ge 0& (b)\\ p.g=0& (c)\\ \mathrm{\parallel }\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace \mathrm{\parallel }-\mu .\mid p\mid \le 0& (d)\\ \left\lbrace {\dot{u}}_{t}\right\rbrace =\lambda .\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace & (e)\\ \lambda .(\mathrm{\parallel }\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace \mathrm{\parallel }-\mu .\mid p\mid )=0& (f)\\ \lambda \ge 0& (g)\end{array} \right.
$$

The first set of equations and inequalities (a–c) corresponds to the treatment of contact. The second set (d–g) corresponds to the description of friction obeying the Coulomb criterion. It involves several fields and links them together: the normal pressure $p$, the shear stress $\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace$ and the tangential velocity $\left\lbrace {\dot{u}}_{t}\right\rbrace$. It may be understood as follows:

$$
\left\{    \begin{array}{cc}\text{Si}\mathrm{\parallel }\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace \mathrm{\parallel }<\mu .\mid p\mid \text{alors}\lambda =0\text{et}\left\lbrace {\dot{u}}_{t}\right\rbrace =0& (a)\\ \text{Si}\mathrm{\parallel }\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace \mathrm{\parallel }=\mu .\mid p\mid \text{alors}\lambda >0\text{et}\left\lbrace {\dot{u}}_{t}\right\rbrace =\lambda .\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace & (b)\end{array} \right.
$$

In the figure below the Coulomb cone has been represented. In stress space, the frictional-contact force can lie only inside the Coulomb cone: if it is strictly inside, the contact is sticking; if it is on the surface of the cone, the contact is sliding.

Friction introduces the notion of a threshold. The relation introduced by Coulomb friction is a relation which is not single-valued and is non-differentiable. Unlike contact, the relation is not associated 2 and cannot be derived directly from an energy dissipation potential. One will also note that friction links the relative *velocity* of the two surfaces and not the displacement. However, one can establish that in statics, if the problem is solved in incremental form (which is the case when one uses Newton as in *code_aster*), the relative velocity may be replaced by the tangential displacement increment.

### 2.4 Formulation by differential inclusions

The non-differentiable character of the friction relations leads us to introduce the notion of a differential sub-inclusion.

One denotes by $V$ the set of kinematically admissible displacements of the problem. The relation between the relative sliding velocity $\left\lbrace \dot{u}\right\rbrace$ and the shear stress $r$ expresses the two possible states of the system: no sliding, or relative sliding along the direction normal at $\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace$ to the convex disc $C$. For the three criteria presented, the function $\left\lbrace {\dot{u}}_{t}\right\rbrace (r)$ and its reciprocal $\left\lbrace r({\dot{u}}_{t})\right\rbrace$ both belong to the subdifferentials of two conjugate pseudo-potentials ${\Psi}_{c}(r)$ and ${\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}({\dot{u}}_{t})$, so that one can write:

$$
\left\lbrace \dot{u}\right\rbrace \in \partial {\Psi}_{c}(r)

\textrm{et}

\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace \in \partial {\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}(\dot{u})
$$

The appearance of the differential inclusions comes from the non-differentiable character of the contact-friction laws. Indeed, ${\Psi}_{c}$ denotes the indicator function of the convex disc $C$ of radius $k$, centred at the origin, defined previously. It is such that:

$$
{\Psi}_{c}(r)=\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}0\text{si}\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace \in C\\ +\infty \text{sinon}\end{array}
$$

The subdifferential $\partial {\Psi}_{c}(r)$ of the function ${\Psi}_{c}$ at $r$ coincides with the outward normal to $C$ at $\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace$. ${\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}(\dot{u})=k\mathrm{\parallel }\dot{u}\mathrm{\parallel }$, where $k$ is the friction resistance threshold; ${\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}$ is the Fenchel conjugate of the indicator function ${\Psi}_{c}$. ${\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}$ is positively homogeneous of degree one. This function is interpreted as the density of power dissipated in sliding. Using the notions of subdifferential, one can establish the following relations for associated $\left\lbrace \dot{u}\right\rbrace$ and $\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace$:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{c}\dot{u}\in \partial {\Psi}_{c}(r)\mathrm{\iff }\left\{  \begin{array}{c}{\Psi}_{c}(r)=0\\ \dot{u}.(r'-r)\le 0,\forall r'\in C\end{array}\right.\\ r\in \partial {\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}(\dot{u})\mathrm{\iff }\left\{  \begin{array}{c}\dot{u}\in V\\ r.(\dot{v}-\dot{u})\le {\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}(\dot{v})-{\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}(\dot{u}),\forall \dot{v}\in V\end{array}\right.\\ {\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}(\dot{u})+{\Psi}_{c}(r)=\dot{u}.r=k\mathrm{\parallel }\dot{u}\mathrm{\parallel }\end{array}\right.
$$

**Notes:**

- The two conjugate pseudo-potentials presented are non-differentiable.
- Once the normal reaction is known for the Coulomb criterion, one reduces locally to a Tresca friction criterion whose threshold is equal to $k=\mu .|p|$.
- The local criteria adopted having a circular form, one deduces that $\dot{u}\in \partial {\Psi}_{c}(r)$ implies that there exists a positive real $\lambda$ such that $\dot{u}=\lambda r$.
- The formulation of the problem in terms of velocity suggests an incremental numerical solution of the friction problem. The solution of the equilibrium problem will therefore be presented in incremental form.

### 2.5 Solution of the equilibrium problem

Consider two solids of total volume $\Omega ` dont la surface de contact est :math:`{\Gamma}_{c}$. To simplify, one will assume the existence of a differentiable strain energy in order to characterise the response of the two separate solids to external loads (in fact, one can show that the results given below are independent of this assumption). One denotes by $V$ the set of kinematically admissible displacement fields, constrained by compliance with the contact and friction conditions on the interface. The equilibrium of the two solids in the absence of friction is written:

*Find* U *a kinematically admissible displacement field such that*

$$
\left\lbrace U=\underset{v\in V}{\text{argmin}}\left[\Phi (\varepsilon (v))-W(v)\right]\right\rbrace \iff \left\lbrace \Phi (U)-W(U)\le \Phi (v)-W(v),\forall v\in V\right\rbrace
$$

In elasticity, $\Phi (v)={\int}_{\Omega}\phi (\epsilon (v)).d\Omega$ is the strain energy. The function $W(v)$ represents the work of the external forces. A necessary condition (which becomes sufficient if $\Phi$ is strictly convex) for this equilibrium to be satisfied is that:

$$
D\Phi (U)-\mathit{DW}\left(U\right)=D\Phi (U)-{L}^{\text{ext}}=0
$$

where $D$ is the Gâteaux derivative operator and ${L}^{\text{ext}}$ is the linear form associated with the external forces.

With the introduction of friction, the problem must be approached in incremental form. One is led (see   and ) to the following minimisation problem on the set $\overline{V}$ of the kinematically admissible fields constrained by compliance with the contact and friction conditions on the interface:

U *known, find* $\Delta U\in \overline{V}$ *such that*

$$
U+\Delta U=\underset{\Delta v\in \stackrel{ˉ}{V}}{\text{argmin}}\left[\Phi (\varepsilon (U+\Delta v))+{\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}(\Delta {v}_{t})-W(U+\Delta v)\right]
$$

$U+\Delta U$ is thus the solution of:

$$
\underset{\Delta v\in \stackrel{ˉ}{V}}{\min}\left[\underset{\Omega}{\int}\varphi (\varepsilon (U+\Delta v))d\Omega +\underset{{\Gamma}_{c}}{\int}k\mid \Delta {v}_{t}\mid d{\Gamma}_{c}-W(U+\Delta v)\right]
$$

where $\Delta {v}_{t}$ is the tangential component of the relative displacement increment 3 of solid $2$ with respect to solid $1$ along the contact surface, with the conventions adopted in r5.03.50-formulation-frottement.

Using the relations ${\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}(\Delta {v}_{t})=k\mid \Delta {v}_{t}\mid ` et :math:`{\Psi}_{c}^{\text{*}}(\Delta {v}_{t})\ge r\Delta v$ if $r\in C$ one deduces that $U+\Delta U$ is the solution of the following $\text{minmax}$ problem, on the space $V$ of the kinematically admissible fields:

$$
\underset{\Delta v\in V}{\text{Min}}\underset{r}{\text{Max}}\text{}J(U+\Delta v,r)
$$

where the functional $J$ is equal to:

$$
J(U+\Delta v,r)=\underset{\Omega}{\int}\phi (\varepsilon (U+\Delta v)).d\Omega +\underset{{\Gamma}_{c}}{\int}(r.\Delta {v}_{t}-{\Psi}_{c}(r)).d{\Gamma}_{c}-W(U+\Delta v)
$$

The presence of the indicator function in this expression indicates that the shear $r$ on the contact surface ${\Gamma}_{c}$ belongs to the convex friction disc $C$.

### 2.6 Variational formulation

If $\phi$ is convex, the $\text{minmax}$ problem to be solved is put in an equivalent way in the form:

*Find* $\Delta U\in V$ *and* $r\in C$ *, a set of independent variables such that*

$$
\delta J(U+\Delta U,r)\ni 0
$$

This amounts to solving the following system of equations at equilibrium:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{cc}\underset{\Omega}{\int}\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial \varepsilon }(\varepsilon (U+\Delta U)).\delta \varepsilon .d\Omega +\underset{{\Gamma}_{c}}{\int}r.\delta {v}_{t}.d{\Gamma}_{c}-{L}^{\text{ext}}.\delta v=0& (a)\\ \underset{{\Gamma}_{c}}{\int}(\delta r.\Delta {U}_{t}.d{\Gamma}_{c}-\partial {\Psi}_{c}(r))\ni 0& (b)\end{array}\right.
$$

or again, in an equivalent manner:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{cc}\underset{\Omega}{\int}\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial \varepsilon }(\varepsilon (U+\Delta U)).\delta \varepsilon .d\Omega +\underset{{\Gamma}_{c}}{\int}r.\delta {v}_{t}.d{\Gamma}_{c}-{L}^{\text{ext}}.\delta v=0& (a)\\ \Delta {U}_{t}\in \partial {\Psi}_{c}(r)& (b)\\ r=({\sigma}_{1}.n).t\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{c}& (c)\end{array}\right.
$$

As in the previous section, ${L}^{\text{ext}}$ is the linear form associated with the external forces. The linear form ${L}^{\text{frot}}$ is associated with the shear forces exerted by solid $2$ on the contact surface of solid $1$. One will also note that the variational formulation makes it possible to recover not only the equilibrium equations of the system but also the membership of $\Delta {U}_{t}$ in the subdifferential of ${\Psi}_{c}$.

### 2.7 Stages of the solution of the contact-friction problem

The discrete solution method implemented in *code_aster* is based on writing the interpenetration relations on the nodes of the meshes facing one another, which implies:

1. A discrete description of the contact surfaces (mesh);
2. The search for the minimum projection distance and for the position of this projection (the operation known as pairing);
3. The writing of the kinematic relations between the nodes;
4. Algorithms for the solution of the contact/friction problem;

Each of these aspects will be detailed in the remainder of the document.

For the formulations known as “discrete” (as opposed to the formulation known as “continuous”, see [r5.03.52](doc:r5.03.52)), *code_aster* solves the contact problem by methods which belong to the family of methods called the “status method” in the literature, with a decoupling of global-local type.

The contact/friction algorithms act in two stages:

1. Geometric pairing problem

- Identification: definition of the potential contact surfaces (cf. r5.03.50-definition-zones-potentielles) by the user 4.
- Pairing: determination of the potential contact pairs (cf. r5.03.50-algorithme-appariement) by a method of searching for the minimum gap between a node and a facet (for node/facet pairing) or for the node closest to another node (for nodal pairing).
- Kinematics: writing of the non-penetration relation by determination of the projection direction and evaluation of the coefficients (cf. r5.03.50-relations-cinematiques). The relation is written between the slave node and the master nodes.

1. Mechanical problem. Several types of algorithm are implemented in *code_aster*:

- An algorithm based on the active-constraint method, usable for contact without friction only. This algorithm rests on the assumptions that the tangent matrix of the problem is symmetric and positive definite, and guarantees convergence in a finite number of iterations in this case , , . It must not be used outside this framework, that is to say in particular for non-symmetric matrices, for example in THM modellings or in large strains. It is the one used by default and it corresponds to ALGO_CONT='CONTRAINTE'.
- A variant of the active-constraint method by means of an iterative solution by projected conjugate gradient. ALGO_CONT='GCP'. A method reserved exclusively for contact problems without friction.
- A solution algorithm by regularisation of the contact and/or friction conditions, which one activates with ALGO_CONT='PENALISATION' and ALGO_FROT='PENALISATION' by choosing judicious penalisation coefficients. This implies *a fortiori* a parametric study on the value of these coefficients: they are not homogeneous to $\left\lbrace u\right\rbrace$, but have the dimension of a ratio of a force to a displacement, and it is difficult to give a general rule for the choice of their values. The user is strongly advised to check *a posteriori* that the unilateral condition is correctly taken into account by viewing the results in the zone concerned. If this is not the case, it is necessary to increase the contact penalisation coefficient until it is. This regularisation algorithm may be chosen in the case of a modelling involving a non-symmetric matrix, which therefore cannot be treated by the active-constraint algorithm.

1. Unilateral conditions. More generally, it is possible to use the algorithms ALGO_CONT='CONTRAINTE' and ALGO_CONT='PENALISATION' in order to impose inequality boundary conditions on any degree of freedom. These are defined by FORMULATION='LIAISON_UNIL' in DEFI_CONTACT (see the documentation [u4.44.11](doc:u4.44.11)), and take the algebraic form $\left[A\right]\left\lbrace u\right\rbrace \le \left\lbrace d\right\rbrace$. Instead of being built by pairing, $\left[A\right]$ and $\left\lbrace d\right\rbrace$ are fixed and built from the information given by the user in DEFI_CONTACT, but the rest of the treatment is essentially identical.

The term “pressure” is an improper abbreviation of “contact-force density”

Tresca friction, on the other hand, is indeed an associated law.

$\Delta {v}_{t}$ is the tangential component of the relative displacement increment in the quasi-static formulation; it becomes the relative velocity of the two surfaces in the dynamic formulation.

There is no automatic mechanism for locating the surfaces in contact in code_aster

## 3 Geometric pairing problem

### 3.1 Introduction

The first problem to be treated in the case of contact is to define, in a reliable and robust manner, which parts of the two surfaces are likely to come into contact. This is the operation known as pairing, common with the mixed hybrid formulation known as the “continuous” method presented in the document [r5.03.52](doc:r5.03.52).

Two types of pairing are available in *code_aster*:

- Nodal pairing;
- Master-slave pairing (or node-facet).

Nodal pairing (APPARIEMENT='NODAL') imposes that the relative displacement between a slave node and the master node to which it is paired, projected onto the direction of the normal to the slave node, be less than the initial gap in this direction. The use of this formulation is not recommended because it requires compatible meshes (nodes “facing one another”) which remain so during the deformation (small-sliding assumption), and for which the master and slave normals are approximately collinear. Without these assumptions, the approximation made becomes hazardous and it is preferable to use the node-facet formulation.

Master-slave pairing, chosen by the keyword APPARIEMENT='MAIT_ESCL', does not grant an equivalent role to the two surfaces: the surface (${S}_{1}$) described under GROUP_MA_MAIT or MAILLE_MAIT is called the master surface and the surface (${S}_{2}$) is the slave surface. The non-interpenetration conditions express that the nodes of the slave surface (stars in the figure below) do not penetrate the mesh cells of the master surface. One can see, on the other hand, that it is possible for the master nodes (circles) to penetrate the slave surface.

### 3.2 Definition of the potential contact zones

*code_aster* having no mechanism for the automatic detection of the potential contact zones, it is therefore up to the user to define *a priori* the zones which he or she predicts will come into contact. These zones must be wide enough for no interpenetration to be observed. It should be noted that the pairing phase is in general an inexpensive operation (much less expensive than the solution of the mechanical contact problem) and that one can therefore define sufficiently extended zones without risking a penalty on performance, subject to observing certain precautions in order to ensure, in particular, the uniqueness of the projections.

Each contact zone is defined in the operator DEFI_CONTACT, factor keyword ZONE. - The choice of the surfaces which will be master or slave is important. Information on this subject is available in the documentation [u2.04.04](doc:u2.04.04).

#### 3.2.1 Particular case of contact for a cable or a beam in 3D

It is possible in 3D to treat the contact between a SEG2 or SEG3 mesh cell (modelling a cable or a beam) and a surface. In this case, one must imperatively use the pairing method 'MAIT_ESCL' and give the segments under the keyword GROUP_MA_ESCL (slave mesh cells). The section of the beam can then be taken into account by the use of the keyword DIST_ESCL (cf. r5.03.50-introduction-jeu-fictif).

#### 3.2.2 Case of nodal pairing

One must choose as the slave surface the one which has the fewer nodes (an error message will stop you if your master surface contains fewer nodes than your slave surface), in order to maximise the chances of having an injective pairing (a master node is paired with only one slave node). The master node paired with each slave node is determined by a nearest-neighbour computation explained in paragraph r5.03.50-recherche-proche-voisin. One uses the normal to the master node in order to write the non-interpenetration relation.

Even in the case of nodal pairing, the contact surfaces are defined in terms of mesh cells. The slave and master nodes are then the nodes of the mesh cells thus defined.

### 3.3 Orientation of the normals

It is imperative that the contact mesh cells be defined so that the normal is outward: the connectivity of the segments must be defined in the order $\mathrm{AB}$, that of the triangles in the order $\mathrm{ABC}$, and that of the quadrangles in the order $\mathrm{ABCD}$, as indicated in the figure. ### 3.4 Pairing algorithm

#### 3.4.1 Principle

The pairing algorithm proceeds in two stages:

1. For each slave node one searches for the nearest master node;
2. One then searches for the master mesh cell attached to the master node previously determined which is the closest to the slave node;

In the case of nodal pairing, one obviously carries out only the first stage.

#### 3.4.2 Search for the nearest neighbour of a node

This phase is common to both formulations: node/facet and node/node. The method used to search for the master node closest to a slave node is very simple: it is sufficient to compute the distance (in the updated geometry, cf. r5.03.50-reactualisation-geometrique) between the slave node and the candidate master nodes.

#### 3.4.3 Search for the paired master mesh cell

The algorithm for searching for the master mesh cell which is paired with the slave node is the following:

- Knowing the master node closest to the slave node $P$ (see r5.03.50-recherche-proche-voisin), one examines in succession the master mesh cells containing this node.
- For each mesh cell thus identified, one searches for the orthogonal projection $M$ of the slave node $P$ onto the master mesh cell.
- The mesh cell minimising the distance $\mathit{PM}$ is chosen to be paired with the slave node.

**Notes:**

- If the slave node projects outside a master mesh cell, one folds its projection back onto this mesh cell under certain conditions (see r5.03.50-traitement-projections).
- If at least one projection takes place inside a master mesh cell, then this one will be preferred to a projection leading to a fold-back (whatever the distance measured)
- If all the projections of the slave node take place outside their respective master mesh cell, the slave node is considered as not paired and will therefore be excluded from the solution phase

#### 3.4.4 Computation of the projection of the slave node onto the master mesh cell

##### 3.4.4.1 Notation

The parts of the surfaces $\partial {\Omega}^{i}$ liable to come into contact during the deformation of the two solids are denoted ${\gamma}_{c}^{i}$. One assumes the existence of regular charts denoted ${\Theta}^{i}$ describing the surfaces ${\gamma}_{c}^{i}$. These charts are defined as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{\Theta}^{i}:& {\omega}^{i}\to \overline{{\Omega}^{i}}\\ & ({\xi}_{1}^{i},{\xi}_{2}^{i})\to {p}^{i}={\Theta}^{i}({\xi}_{1}^{i},{\xi}_{2}^{i})\end{array}
$$

where ${\omega}^{i}$ is a bounded domain (of reference) contained in ${ℝ}^{2}$ (it is the reference parametric space of the finite element). Furthermore, one denotes by ${\varphi}^{i}$ the transformation of the solid ${B}^{i}$, defined by:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}{\varphi}^{i}:& \overline{{\Omega}^{i}}\to \overline{{\Omega}_{t}^{i}}\\ & \left\lbrace {p}^{i}\right\rbrace \to \left\lbrace {x}^{i}\right\rbrace \end{array}
$$

One always places oneself at a fixed time $t$. The master surface is denoted ${\gamma}_{c}^{1}$ and the slave surface is denoted ${\gamma}_{c}^{2}$. Pairing is carried out by searching, for every point $\left\lbrace {x}^{i}\right\rbrace ={\varphi}^{i}({p}^{i},t)$ of the boundary ${\gamma}_{c}^{1}$, for the point $\left\lbrace \tilde{x}\right\rbrace$ of ${\gamma}_{c}^{2}$ which is the closest. This amounts to solving the following constrained optimisation problem. For every point $\left\lbrace {x}^{1}\right\rbrace ={\varphi}^{1}({\Theta}^{1}({\xi}_{1}^{1},{\xi}_{2}^{1}),t)$ with $({\xi}_{1}^{1},{\xi}_{2}^{1})\in {\omega}^{1}$, and for every $t\ge 0$, find $\tilde{{\zeta}_{t}}=({\xi}_{1}^{2},{\xi}_{2}^{2})\in {\omega}^{2}$ such that:

$$
{\tilde{\zeta}}_{t}=\underset{({\xi}_{1}^{2},{\xi}_{2}^{2})\in {\omega}^{2}}{\text{argmin}}\left\lbrace \frac{1}{2}.∥{\varphi}^{1}({\Theta}^{1}({\xi}_{1}^{1},{\xi}_{2}^{1}),t)-{\varphi}^{2}({\Theta}^{2}({\xi}_{1}^{2},{\xi}_{2}^{2}),t)∥{2}^{}\right\rbrace
$$

The solution $\tilde{{\zeta}_{t}}$ is the position in the parametric reference space of the projection $M$ of the slave node $P$ onto the master mesh cell.

##### 3.4.4.2 Formulation of the minimisation problem

In order to solve the non-linear problem, one proposes to use a Newton minimisation algorithm. The problem is reformulated in the following manner: for a given slave node one searches for the point (in parametric coordinates) which minimises the distance to a given master mesh cell (the one defined in paragraph r5.03.50-recherche-maille-maitre).

One denotes by $f$ the ${L}_{2}$ norm of the distance between a slave node located at $\left\lbrace {x}_{e}\right\rbrace$ and a point $\left\lbrace {x}_{m}\right\rbrace$ belonging to the facing master mesh cell:

$$
f(\left\lbrace {x}_{m}\right\rbrace )={∥\left\lbrace {x}_{e}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {x}_{m}\right\rbrace ∥}^{2}
$$

One writes the functional in the parametric space, $\left\lbrace {x}_{m}\right\rbrace$ being expressed from the ${\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}$ nodes $\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace$ of the paired master mesh cell:

$$
f({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})=f(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace )={∥\left\lbrace {x}_{e}\right\rbrace -\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}}{\phi }_{i}(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace ).\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace ∥}^{2}
$$

It is this quantity which must be minimised. One applies the Newton algorithm to the stationarity conditions (Euler-Lagrange) of the minimisation problem thus stated, that is to say to the vector functional $\left\lbrace \nabla f(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace )\right\rbrace$, the expression of the first-order Taylor expansion, about the point $\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{0}\right\rbrace$:

$$
\left\lbrace \nabla f(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace )\right\rbrace \underset{\text{linéarisation}}{=}\left\lbrace \nabla f(\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{0}\right\rbrace )\right\rbrace +\left[H({\zeta}_{0})\right].(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{0}\right\rbrace )
$$

Where $\left[H\right]$ is the Hessian matrix of the second derivatives of $f$ and $\left\lbrace \nabla f(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace )\right\rbrace$ is its gradient. A minimum of $f$ occurs when its gradient is zero.

The iterative algorithm is then written:

1. Start from the initial point $\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i=0}\right\rbrace$ on the master mesh cell. This starting point is simply chosen at $\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i=0}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace 0\right\rbrace$;
2. Evaluate the gradient $\left\lbrace \nabla f\left(\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i}\right\rbrace \right)\right\rbrace$ and the Hessian matrix $\left[H\left({\zeta}_{i}\right)\right]$ at this point;
3. Compute the descent direction $\left\lbrace {d\zeta }_{i}\right\rbrace =-{\left[H\left({\zeta}_{i}\right)\right]}^{-1}.\left\lbrace \nabla f\left(\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i}\right\rbrace \right)\right\rbrace$
4. Compute the linear-search parameter $\alpha$ (see r5.03.50-recherche-linéaire-algorithme-projection)
5. Compute the next point such that $\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i+1}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i}\right\rbrace +\alpha \left\lbrace {d\zeta }_{i}\right\rbrace$;
6. If the process has converged, one stops, otherwise one loops to 2.

**Notes:**

- This problem is written without constraints;
- One cannot guarantee the uniqueness of the solution; the Newton process will find the first point satisfying the stationarity conditions.
- The existence of the gradient and of the Hessian matrix is ensured if the mesh cell is sufficiently regular, which is always the case on a finite-element mesh which is not too distorted.
- The iterative process stops by a criterion on the displacement increment in the parametric space $\epsilon =\frac{\sqrt{\langle {\zeta}_{i+1}\rangle \left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i+1}\right\rbrace -\langle {\zeta}_{i}\rangle \left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i}\right\rbrace }}{\sqrt{\langle {\zeta}_{i+1}\rangle \left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i+1}\right\rbrace }}$ with $\epsilon \le {10}^{-4}$ (a value which cannot be modified by the user). This value being estimated with respect to the parametric coordinates, there are no problems with the size of the mesh cells and the units used.
- The maximum number of Newton iterations is fixed at $200$ (a value which cannot be modified by the user).
- If the maximum number of iterations is reached, one selects as the projection the iterate which has minimised the distance between the slave node and the master mesh cell (it is therefore not perfectly the orthogonal projection).
- There exists a variant in which the search direction is not estimated by the Newton algorithm but is a fixed direction given by the user, which can be useful in certain difficult cases (a perfectly convex mesh cell which does not give a unique projection, for example). One activates it by the keyword TYPE_APPA='FIXE' and the vector is given by DIRE_APPA.
- The projection onto the segment elements in 3D (the case of beams or cables) requires a definition of the normal by the user via the keywords VECT_MAIT/VECT_ESCL.

##### 3.4.4.3 Expression of the tangent matrix and of the residual

The expressions of the terms involved in the writing of the Newton algorithm described above are given below. One recalls the functional:

$$
f\left({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2}\right)=f\left(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace \right)={∥\left\lbrace {x}_{e}\right\rbrace -\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}}{\varphi}_{i}\left(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace \right).\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace ∥}^{2}
$$

The gradient of this functional:

$$
\left\lbrace \nabla f\left(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace \right)\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial f}{\partial {\xi}_{1}}\\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial {\xi}_{2}}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

The terms are written explicitly:

$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial {\xi}_{j}}({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})=2(\left\lbrace {x}_{e}\right\rbrace -\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}}{\phi }_{i}(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace ).\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace ).(-\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}}\frac{\partial {\phi }_{i}}{\partial {\xi}_{j}}(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace ).\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace )
$$

The Hessian of this functional:

$$
\left[H(\zeta )\right]=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{\partial {\xi}_{1}^{2}}& \frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{\partial {\xi}_{1}.\partial {\xi}_{2}}\\ \frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{\partial {\xi}_{1}.\partial {\xi}_{2}}& \frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{\partial {\xi}_{2}^{2}}\end{array}\right]
$$

The terms are written explicitly:

$$
\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{\partial {\xi}_{1}^{2}}({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})={∥\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}}\frac{\partial {\phi }_{i}}{\partial {\xi}_{j}}(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace ).\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace ∥}^{2}+2(\left\lbrace {x}_{e}\right\rbrace -\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}}{\phi }_{i}(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace ).\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace ).(-\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}}\frac{{\partial}^{2}{\phi }_{i}}{\partial {\xi}_{j}^{2}}(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace ).\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace )
$$

And for the cross terms:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}\frac{{\partial}^{2}f}{\partial {\xi}_{j}\partial {\xi}_{k}}({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})& =(\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}}\frac{\partial {\phi }_{i}}{\partial {\xi}_{j}}(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace ).\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace ).(\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}}\frac{\partial {\phi }_{i}}{\partial {\xi}_{k}}(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace ).\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace )\\ & +2(\left\lbrace {x}_{e}\right\rbrace -\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}}{\phi }_{i}(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace ).\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace ).(-\sum_{i=1}^{{\mathit{nb}}_{\mathit{no}}}\frac{{\partial}^{2}{\phi }_{i}}{\partial {\xi}_{j}.\partial {\xi}_{k}}(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace ).\left\lbrace {x}_{i,m}\right\rbrace )\end{array}
$$

##### 3.4.4.4 Linear search in the projection algorithm

In order to improve the robustness of the projection algorithm, a linear-search phase is added in the Newton algorithm (cf. r5.03.50-formulation-problème-minimisation). The aim, given a descent direction $\left\lbrace {d\zeta }_{i}\right\rbrace$, is to determine an advance parameter $\alpha$ which minimises a functional $F$ associated with $f$.

The linear-search algorithm used (linear search with backtracking and the Armijo rule) is the same as for the implicit integration of the constitutive laws [r5.03.14](doc:r5.03.14).

The functional $F$, associated with the distance functional $f$ defined in , is the following:

$$
F(\alpha )=\frac{1}{2}{∥\nabla f\left(\left\lbrace \zeta \right\rbrace +\alpha \left\lbrace {d\zeta }_{i}\right\rbrace \right)∥}^{2}
$$

In order to choose $\alpha$, one will in fact not seek to minimise the functional $F$ exactly but an approximation of this latter (quadratic, cubic). In order to know which approximation to use, one relies on the Armijo rule. For more details, one refers to [r5.03.14](doc:r5.03.14) and   which detail the implementation of such an algorithm.

For the linear search implemented in the projection algorithm, the parameters chosen (hard-coded) are the following:

| Parameter of the Armijo rule | $\omega =0,0001$ |
| --- | --- |
| Minimum bound for the fold-back | ${\alpha}_{min}=0,1$ |
| Maximum bound for the fold-back | ${\alpha}_{max}=0,5$ |
| Maximum number of cubic interpolations | ${\mathit{it}}_{max}=2$ |

These parameters imply in particular that if the default step ($\alpha =1$) does not satisfy the Armijo rule, that is to say that the descent direction chosen does not bring one closer to the orthogonal projection, then $\alpha$ will be at most equal to $0,5$. This choice makes it possible to favour robustness (avoid following an erroneous search direction) rather than performance (values of $\alpha$ greater than $1$).

For meshes of poor quality, with distorted mesh cells, the linear search has shown its usefulness, finding the correct orthogonal projection on difficult cases where an algorithm without linear search failed.

Under certain conditions, one can detect contact between two surfaces when there is none. The limit value of this re-projection is fixed by the keyword TOLE_PROJ_EXT, which takes as its argument the value (referred to the reference element) of the extension of the master mesh cell within which one authorises the re-projection. #### 3.4.6 Case of the projection in a given direction

When one uses the option DIRE_APPA, the user can force the projection algorithm not to search for the minimum distance between a slave node and its orthogonal projection onto the master surface, but simply to find the point of the master surface linked to the slave node by a given vector. In this case the algorithm is the following:

1. Start from the initial point $\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i=0}\right\rbrace$ on the master mesh cell. This starting point is simply chosen at $\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i=0}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace 0\right\rbrace$;
2. Build the Newton matrix $\left[H({\zeta}_{i})\right]$ by the use of the direction given by DIRE_APPA;
3. Build the Newton right-hand side $\left\lbrace R\left(\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i}\right\rbrace \right)\right\rbrace$ by the difference between the slave node and its projection onto the master mesh cell;
4. Compute the descent direction $\left\lbrace {d\zeta }_{i}\right\rbrace =-{\left[H\left({\zeta}_{i}\right)\right]}^{-1}.\left\lbrace R\left(\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i}\right\rbrace \right)\right\rbrace$
5. Compute the next point such that $\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i+1}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {\zeta}_{i}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {d\zeta }_{i}\right\rbrace$;
6. If the process has converged, one stops, otherwise one loops to 2.

There is no linear-search algorithm, unlike the previous case.

### 3.5 Kinematic relations

#### 3.5.1 Definition of the contact matrix

One carries out an idealised modelling of the contact phenomenon, in the sense that it assumes the boundaries of the bodies to be perfectly defined by a line or a surface: one then writes a discrete and linearised non-interpenetration condition. Let $P$ be a slave node, $M$ its projection onto the master mesh cell which was determined during pairing. In 2D, this master mesh cell has two nodes (SEG2) or three nodes (SEG3). In 3D, it may have three, four, six, eight or nine (TRIA3, QUAD4, TRIA6, QUAD8, QUAD9). The displacement of the point $M$ is a linear combination of the displacements of the nodes of the finite element, with as coefficients the values of the shape functions $\Phi$ at $M$. Let us place ourselves in the case where the master mesh cell is a SEG2 in order to simplify the presentation. One then has:

$$
\left\lbrace {u}_{M}\right\rbrace =\left[{\Phi}_{A}\left(M\right)\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{A}\right\rbrace +\left[{\Phi}_{B}\left(M\right)\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{B}\right\rbrace
$$

As a first step, one chooses to take as the direction $\left\lbrace N\right\rbrace$ the outward normal of the master mesh cell (cf. ).

| ![Figure](images/Shape15.gif) |
| --- |
| **Figure** 3.5.1-a **: projection of a slave node onto a SEG2 mesh cell.** |

The normal gap is written as the difference between the slave node $P$ and its projection $M$ onto the master mesh cell:

$$
\langle N\rangle .\left[\left\lbrace {u}_{P}\right\rbrace -\sum_{j=1}^{{n}_{m}}\left[{\Phi}_{{B}_{j}}(M)\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{{B}_{j}}\right\rbrace \right]=d
$$

If one writes such a relation for all the contact pairs, one obtains the geometric non-penetration conditions in matrix form:

$$
\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace u\right\rbrace =d
$$

The matrix $\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]$, called the contact matrix, contains one row per contact pair, and as many columns as there are physical degrees of freedom of the problem. Suppose that one has two contact mesh cells of type SEG2, according to the diagram of the .

| ![Figure](images/Shape16.gif) |
| --- |
| **Figure** 3.5.1-b **: writing of the contact matrix A on an example.** |

If one denotes for example ${u}_{B}$ the displacement of the node $B$ along the direction $x$, ${v}_{B}$ the displacement of the node $B$ along the direction $y$, and ${d}^{1}$ and ${d}^{2}$ the current gaps for the two pairs:

$$
\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{u}_{P}\\ {v}_{P}\\ {u}_{Q}\\ {v}_{Q}\\ {u}_{B}\\ {v}_{B}\\ {u}_{C}\\ {v}_{C}\\ {u}_{D}\\ {v}_{D}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{d}^{1}\\ {d}^{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

With the contact matrix $\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]$:

$$
\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{cccccccccc}{N}_{x}^{1}& {N}_{y}^{1}& 0& 0& -\frac{1}{2}.{N}_{x}^{1}& -\frac{1}{2}.{N}_{y}^{1}& -\frac{1}{2}.{N}_{x}^{1}& -\frac{1}{2}.{N}_{y}^{1}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& {N}_{x}^{2}& {N}_{y}^{2}& 0& 0& -\frac{3}{4}.{N}_{x}^{2}& -\frac{3}{4}.{N}_{y}^{2}& -\frac{1}{4}.{N}_{x}^{2}& -\frac{1}{4}.{N}_{y}^{2}\end{array}\right]
$$

One has here considered only the degrees of freedom of the nodes involved in the contact; the matrix $\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]$ should be sparser. But in practice, one always reduces the contact matrix onto the active degrees of freedom. One therefore stores only the non-zero coefficients.

#### 3.5.2 Definition of the friction matrix

The notion of a contact matrix extends to the case of tangential sliding, on the tangent plane. It is the matrix of the kinematic friction relations $\left[{A}^{\text{f}}\right]$.

#### 3.5.3 Choice of the normal

In the previous paragraph, one chose to take as the direction $\left\lbrace N\right\rbrace$ the outward normal of the master mesh cell. This is the default behaviour in *code_aster*. However, it is possible to choose other normals:

- The slave normal NORMALE='ESCL';
- An average between the master normal and the slave normal NORMALE='MAIT_ESCL';

It is also possible to request the use of smoothed normals (LISSAGE='OUI'), that is to say that instead of using the master normal at the projection point, one can take a normal coming from the linear interpolation between the normals at the nodes of the master mesh cell.

Likewise, the computation of the normals is always done via the shape functions of the element; this is what one defines as the “true” normal or the “automatic” normal. But it is possible to impose a normal on the master mesh cell, the slave mesh cell or on both in a different manner:

- Directly (VECT_MAIT or VECT_ESCL='FIXE')
- Indirectly by the use of a triad (VECT_MAIT or VECT_ESCL='VECT_Y'). In this latter case, the normal used will be the vector coming from the vector product between the tangent to the mesh cell and the given vector VECT_Y.

**Notes:**

- Since this is a pairing of node-facet type, the slave normal is itself computed by smoothing; the LISSAGE option therefore has no effect if one chooses NORMALE='ESCL';
- The use of predefined normals is mandatory in the case of beams;
- The choice of a normal other than the master mesh cell must be limited to exceptional cases, such as when the mesh or the problems of compatibility of the contact with the boundary conditions have forced the user to take as master a coarsely meshed surface;
- The use of TYPE_APPA='FIXE' for the search for the closest master mesh cell (see r5.03.50-calcul-projection-esclave-maitre) does not prejudge the choice of the normal in the writing of the non-penetration relation, which remains at the user's choice. But it is more consistent to choose a fixed normal (VECT_MAIT='FIXE').

#### 3.5.4 Coefficients of the contact matrix

##### 3.5.4.1 Standard elements

The values of the shape functions ${\Phi}_{{B}_{j}}(M)$ of the master nodes at the point $M$ for the various contact mesh cells are standard (see [r1.01.01](doc:r1.01.01)).

##### 3.5.4.2 QUAD8 mesh cells

The mesh cells of quadrilateral type with eight nodes present a defect. Indeed, the classical shape functions are not positive over the whole domain and lead to aberrant results when they are used in contact. The principal symptom linked to the use of the classical shape functions is the appearance of non-physical negative contact forces which cause oscillations (between the vertex nodes and the mid-side nodes).

In order to avoid this phenomenon, *code_aster* proceeds to modify the QUAD8 element by imposing linear relations between mid-side nodes and vertex nodes and by finally using the shape functions of the QUAD4.

In general, it is preferable to avoid using this kind of element and to prefer to it complete quadratic elements such as the QUAD9, because one considers the QUAD8 as linear elements and one furthermore introduces linear relations which can be a nuisance.

##### 3.5.4.3 COQUE_3D elements

The elements of type COQUE_3D are mixed non-isoparametric finite elements. They are based on quadratic mesh cells of type QUAD9 (respectively TRIA7) but, the mid-side node carrying no translation degree of freedom, one proceeds to a projection onto a QUAD8 (respectively TRIA6) and one therefore falls back on the defects mentioned in the previous paragraph. These finite elements are therefore not recommended in contact problems.

### 3.6 Introduction of a fictitious gap

One may wish to model the contact between structures with certain particular features (“hole” or “bump”) which one does not wish to mesh (see figure).

| --- | --- |
| **Figure** 3.6-a **: holes and bumps.** |  |

One solution consists in meshing the surface without these defects and in adding to it a distance given by the user (see figure).

| ![Figure](images/Shape19.gif) |
| --- |
| **Figure** 3.6-b **: meshed surface without defect.** |

One corrects the value of the gap:

$$
\langle N\rangle .(\left\lbrace {u}_{P}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {u}_{M}\right\rbrace ).=d-({d}_{\text{e}}+{d}_{\text{m}})
$$

where ${d}_{\text{e}}$ and ${d}_{\text{m}}$ are given by the user respectively under the keywords DIST_MAIT and DIST_ESCL for each contact zone. These distances are signed: they represent the translation to be applied to the node of the mesh in the direction of the outward normal $\left\lbrace n\right\rbrace$ in order to obtain the point of the real structure. These keywords also make it possible to account for the contact between shells of which only the mean surfaces are meshed: ${d}_{\text{e}}$ and ${d}_{\text{m}}$ are then equal to half the thickness of the shells (positive values).

**Notes:**

- If one uses DIST_MAIT and DIST_ESCL, one must take care with the visual interpretation of the results. If $({d}_{\text{e}}+{d}_{\text{m}})>0$, the code may announce contact whereas the visualisation will show a spacing of the two meshes. If $({d}_{\text{e}}+{d}_{\text{m}})<0$, the code may announce contact whereas the visualisation will show two interpenetrated meshes;
- In order to remember the signs, think of:
- $({d}_{\text{e}}+{d}_{\text{m}})>0$: addition of material with respect to the mesh
- $({d}_{\text{e}}+{d}_{\text{m}})<0$: removal of material with respect to the mesh

The options DIST_POUTRE and DIST_COQUE rely on the elementary characteristics defined in the operator AFFE_CARA_ELEM in order to add the fictitious gap corresponding to the thickness (in the case of shells) or to the radius (in the case of beams of circular section).

### 3.7 Geometric update

In the framework of the modelling of contact in large displacements, the evolution of the geometry of the surfaces plays a fundamental role. Indeed, it is this which conditions the computation of the normals to the faces potentially in contact and therefore which conditions the pairing carried out.

The geometric update is defined by the keyword REAC_GEOM of the command DEFI_CONTACT. Its operation is the following:

- If REAC_GEOM='SANS': there is no geometric update. The whole computation is carried out on the initial configuration with the initial pairing.
- If REAC_GEOM='CONTROLE': one must supply NB_ITER_GEOM. Within the **same** load step, one carries out NB_ITER_GEOM times the cycle *iteration until convergence, geometric update, pairing*.
- If REAC_GEOM='AUTOMATIQUE': the decision to carry out a geometric pairing again is made automatically by the software. The criterion is the following:

$$
\frac{{\mathrm{\parallel }\Delta {u}^{+}-\Delta {u}^{-}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{\infty}}{{\mathrm{\parallel }\Delta {u}^{-}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{\infty}}<\text{RESI\_GEOM}
$$

RESI_GEOM is equal to $1\text{\%}$ by default. If the infinity norm of the displacement between two time instants, divided by the infinity norm of the displacement obtained since equilibrium, is greater than RESI_GEOM, then one updates. The infinity norm is defined by:

$$
{\parallel \Delta u\parallel }_{\infty}=\max\sqrt{{u}_{x}^{2}+{u}_{y}^{2}+{u}_{z}^{2}}
$$

**Notes:**

- At the first iteration or in the case of rigid-body motions (in dynamics), ${\mathrm{\parallel }\Delta {u}^{-}\mathrm{\parallel }}_{\infty}=0$. In order to avoid dividing by zero, one forces the update;
- The infinity norm is evaluated on all the nodes of the mesh (and not only on the nodes in contact) carrying displacement degrees of freedom.

One can first of all note that the pairing is subject to the geometric-update phase. Furthermore, carrying out several times within the same load step the cycle *iteration until convergence, geometric update, pairing* makes it possible to follow the evolution of the geometry of the structure. It must indeed be emphasised that this geometric evolution is one of the non-linear components of a contact computation in large displacements.

In practice, one can recommend the following choices for the keyword REAC_GEOM:

- For a computation in small displacements, REAC_GEOM='SANS'. One works on the initial configuration.
- For computations in large displacements, either use REAC_GEOM='AUTOMATIQUE' (default value), or use REAC_GEOM='CONTROLE' and a value for NB_ITER_GEOM depending on the importance of the geometric evolution of the surfaces.

In the case where the user does not leave code_aster the possibility of managing the geometric update automatically, the code will warn him or her by an alarm if the automatic criterion (equal to 1%) is not ensured because of the user's choice.

## 4 Mechanical contact/friction problem

Taking contact-friction into account in a mechanical problem has two consequences:

- The modification of the equilibrium equation in order to take into account the contact-friction reactions at the interfaces;
- The application of additional laws governing contact and friction (see r5.03.50-mecanique-contact-frottement) in order to compute these reactions but also to impose conditions on the kinematics.

For contact without friction, the Signorini conditions reduce to a classical constrained-optimisation problem (Kuhn & Tucker). On the other hand, for Coulomb friction, one cannot write an equivalent optimisation problem without making additional assumption(s).

In *code_aster*, the *discrete* methods for the solution of the contact/friction problem are based on a *decoupled* approach between equilibrium and the contact/friction law. After the solution of the mechanical problem *without* contact-friction, one corrects the solution (kinematics and reactions) by applying the Signorini-Coulomb law. This strategy makes it possible to make no other assumption on the nature of the mechanical problem, whether the kinematics or the constitutive relations. However, it is indispensable that the stiffness matrix of the problem without contact be *symmetric*.

**Notes:**

- The expression “carry out a computation with contact” means that one writes the non-penetration relations, but does not imply that there is effective contact for the loading considered. Now it is the solution of the contact-friction problem which is the most expensive.
- Contact acting as a correction on the results coming from a classical mechanical computation, it is indispensable that the problem without contact be mechanically well-posed and numerically solvable. In particular, any rigid-body motions must be assumed to have been eliminated outside the solution of the contact problem.

### 4.1 Mechanical problem without contact/friction

The solution of a non-linear problem in the operator STAT_NON_LINE (or DYNA_NON_LINE) is described in detail in the document [r5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01). At each time step $i$, one seeks to verify the global equilibrium of the structure:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace +{\left[B\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{i}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace \\ \left[B\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{d}\right\rbrace \end{array}
$$

In order to avoid overloading the equations, one makes the assumption that the boundary conditions are eliminated and where one therefore has no matrix $\left[B\right]$; one therefore seeks to solve:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace
$$

This non-linear problem is solved by an iterative method of Newton-Raphson type which has the following characteristics:

- An *a priori* subdivision of the loading into “time steps” (denoted by the index $i$);
- A linearisation of the equilibrium problem by the Newton method (the iterations being denoted by the index $n$).

The unknowns are computed in an incremental manner. From $\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace$, a solution satisfying equilibrium at ${t}_{i-1}$, one determines $\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}\right\rbrace$ which makes it possible to obtain the solution at ${t}_{i}$:

$$
\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}\right\rbrace
$$

The increment $\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}\right\rbrace$ is first estimated by linearising the problem with respect to time about $(\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace ,{t}_{i-1})$ (prediction or Euler phase). Then one uses a Newton method or one of its variants in order to solve equation  iteratively: one computes a sequence $\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace$ where the exponent $n$ is the iteration number. To simplify, one will not distinguish between the prediction phase and the Newton correction phase. One finally writes:

$$
\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace
$$

One places oneself at the time ${t}_{i}$ and at the Newton iteration $n$. The following notation has been used:

- $\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace$: displacements at the time instant ${t}_{i}$ and at the Newton iteration $n$;
- $\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace$: displacement at the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$. This solution satisfies the equilibrium condition of the structure;
- $\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace$: increment of the displacements for the Newton iteration $n$;
- $\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace$: increment of the displacements accumulated since the beginning of the time step, before the Newton iteration $n$;

After linearisation of , for the Newton iteration $n$, one introduces 1 the mechanical tangent matrix $\left[{K}^{\text{m},n-1}\right]$ and one must then find $\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace$ such that:

$$
\left[{K}^{\text{m},n-1}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{méca},n-1}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int,n-1}\right\rbrace
$$

### 4.2 Modification of the equilibrium equation with contact/friction

In order to ensure the equilibrium of the structure, contact/friction forces must be introduced. In the most general manner possible, one writes that the external mechanical loading is modified by the contact/friction forces:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{f}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace
$$

Where:

- $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace$ is the vector of the external forces (Neumann conditions);
- $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace$ is the vector of the contact forces;
- $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{f}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace$ is the vector of the friction forces;

A priori, all these quantities are non-linear because they depend on the displacement vector $\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right\rbrace$ (one speaks of “follower” loadings). Injecting  into , one therefore seeks to solve:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{f}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace
$$

At each Newton iteration $n$, one linearises equation  with respect to $\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace$. This process introduces the tangent matrix $\left[{K}^{\text{m},n-1}\right]$ which will contain the contributions coming from the linearisation of the internal and external forces and the matrix $\left[{K}^{\text{cf},n-1}\right]$ which will contain the contributions coming from the linearisation of the contact/friction forces; one must then find $\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace$ such that:

$$
(\left[{K}^{\text{m},n-1}\right]+\left[{K}^{\text{cf},n-1}\right]).\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{ext},n-1}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int,n-1}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{f},n-1}\right\rbrace
$$

### 4.3 Contact/friction laws

The Signorini and Coulomb laws imply inequalities and equalities. The discrete formulation available in *code_aster* consists in modifying the inequality relations into equality relations. In order to do this, there are two possible methods:

1. Dualise the contact/friction laws;
2. Regularise the contact/friction laws.

#### 4.3.1 Kinematics

##### 4.3.1.1 Contact/friction matrices

In paragraph r5.03.50-definition-matrice-contact, we have seen how to write the kinematic contact conditions by introducing the contact matrix $\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]$. In the same manner, but considering the kinematic friction conditions (on the tangent plane), one will evaluate the sliding matrices $\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right]$ and sticking matrices $\left[{A}^{\text{a}}\right]$. These almost-full rectangular matrices are computed by the evaluation of the kinematic relations between the slave node and the paired master mesh cell. The construction of the kinematic relations is done during the geometric pairing procedure which occurs at the beginning of each time step and at each geometric update. For performance reasons, one uses and builds only the sub-part of these matrices corresponding to the activation of the various thresholds (contact or sliding), within the contact/friction solution algorithms.

These matrices depend on the geometric update.

At the global level, the matrices do not depend on the solution without contact of the mechanical problem.

##### 4.3.1.2 Unilateral contact conditions

The non-penetration relation consists in saying that the relative displacement along a given direction cannot exceed the initial gap $\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$, measured on the mesh, in this direction. The unilateral contact condition is written (see r5.03.50-definition-matrice-contact):

$$
\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right\rbrace \le \left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace
$$

This equation expresses the fact that every motion of the structure must take place with compliance with the non-penetration condition, or again that the displacement of the nodes of the contact surface is less than the initial gap $\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$. After solution of the mechanical problem by the Newton method, the non-penetration condition becomes:

$$
\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace )\le \left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace
$$

This condition can be written in an iterative manner:

$$
\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace \le \left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace
$$

With $\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace$ the gap evaluated before the current Newton iteration $n$:

$$
\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace -\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace )
$$

##### 4.3.1.3 Sticking conditions

In sticking, the nodes do not move over the time step, that is to say:

$$
\left[{A}^{\text{a}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace )=0
$$

$\left[{A}^{\text{a}}\right]$ is the matrix of the nodes in sticking contact, that is to say the sub-part of the friction matrix $\left[{A}^{\text{f}}\right]$ (see r5.03.50-definition-matrice-contact) applied to the nodes in sticking contact.

##### 4.3.1.4 Sliding conditions

The shear stress $\left\lbrace {r}_{i}\right\rbrace$ is collinear with the tangential sliding direction, that is:

$$
\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace )=\lambda .\left\lbrace {r}_{i}\right\rbrace
$$

$\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right]$ is the matrix of the nodes in sliding contact, that is to say the sub-part of the friction matrix $\left[{A}^{\text{f}}\right]$ (see r5.03.50-definition-matrice-contact) applied to the nodes in sliding contact. One has:

$$
\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right].(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace )=\lambda .\left\lbrace {r}_{i}(\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right\rbrace )\right\rbrace
$$

Equation ( ) depends on the final displacement $\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right\rbrace$. The sliding conditions are introduced with a Lagrange multiplier $\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g}}\right\rbrace$ such that:

$$
\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace =\mu .\left[∣{\mu}^{\text{c}}({u}_{i})∣\right].\left\lbrace {t}^{n}\right\rbrace
$$

With ${t}^{n}$ the unit vector of the sliding direction, which in 3D is equal to:

$$
\left\lbrace t\right\rbrace =\frac{\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right].(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace )}{∥\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right].(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace )∥}
$$

Or again:

$$
\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace =\left[{k}^{\text{g}}({u}_{i})\right].\left\lbrace {t}^{n}\right\rbrace

\textrm{avec}

\left[{k}^{\text{g}}({u}_{i})\right]=\mu .\left[∣{\mu}^{\text{c}}({u}_{i})∣\right]
$$

The Lagrange multiplier is non-linear and depends on the solution $\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right\rbrace$. In 2D, it is also non-linear but the multiplier does not depend on the sliding direction; one therefore has:

$$
{\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace }_{\text{2D}}=\mu .\left[∣{\mu}^{\text{c}}({u}_{i})∣\right]=\left[{k}^{\text{g}}({u}_{i})\right]
$$

#### 4.3.2 Dualisation

In the case of dualisation, one uses a trial-and-error algorithm which postulates a priori the state of a link and which checks its state after application of the Signorini-Coulomb law. In order to take into account the constraints (one speaks of constraints in the sense of conditions of “restriction” and not in the mechanical sense of the term) bearing on the displacement field or on the forces, one makes them intervene in the equations through Lagrange multipliers (as can be done in *code_aster* for the kinematic boundary conditions). One introduces two sets of Lagrange multipliers:

- $\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$ bearing on the contact conditions;
- $\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g}}\right\rbrace$ bearing on the sliding conditions.

By writing the equilibrium of the structure, one gives the following interpretation of the Lagrange multipliers:

- $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace =[{A}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$ represents the nodal contact forces;
- $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{g}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace =[{A}^{\text{g}}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{g}}\right\rbrace$ represents the nodal sliding forces.

Even if the friction algorithm uses regularisation, one will see that the introduction of the “Lagrange multipliers” is necessary in order to linearise the nodal sliding forces.

#### 4.3.3 Regularisation

The principle of regularisation is to modify the contact-friction laws in order to obtain ones which are easier to handle, for example so that the relations become single-valued and differentiable (in order to be able to apply the Newton method, for example). One therefore introduces assumptions which make the model inexact with respect to the Signorini-Coulomb laws.

##### 4.3.3.1 Regularisation of the contact conditions

The principle of regularisation is to modify the graph of the contact law in order to remove the non-single-valued character of the contact relation.

| **Figure** 4.3.3.1-a **: regularisation of the contact condition** (continuous form) **.** |

One recalls that the non-interpenetration condition is written in iterative form:

$$
\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace \le \left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace
$$

The idea of regularisation is to penalise the situation for which there is interpenetration. The interpenetration $\left\lbrace {h}^{n}\right\rbrace$ is equal to:

$$
\left\lbrace {h}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace >0
$$

In other words, one writes that the contact force is all the larger as the interpenetration $\left\lbrace {h}^{n}\right\rbrace$ is significant, whence the regularised form of the contact force:

$$
{\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace }_{\text{regu}}={E}_{N}.[{A}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.{\left\lbrace {h}^{n}\right\rbrace }^{+}

\textrm{avec}

{E}_{N}>0
$$

${E}_{N}$ is the regularisation coefficient (or penalisation coefficient, of the dimension of a ratio of a force to a displacement) of the contact and one denotes by ${\square}^{+}$ the positive part of a quantity. The contact penalisation coefficient is interpreted as the spring of stiffness ${E}_{N}$ which opposes the penetration of the slave node into the master surface by applying to it a corresponding nodal force. The larger it is, the less interpenetration there is and the higher the restoring force.

##### 4.3.3.2 Regularisation of the sticking condition

The principle of the regularisation of sticking is to modify the graph of Coulomb's law. First of all, one removes the non-single-valued character of the law in the sticking part.

| ![Figure](images/Shape21.gif) |
| --- |
| **Figure** 4.3.3.2-a **: regularisation of the sticking condition – Tresca model** (continuous form) **.** |

One establishes the regularised form of the sticking force:

$$
{\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{a},n}\right\rbrace }_{\text{regu}}={E}_{T}.{\left[{A}^{\text{a}}\right]}^{T}.\left[{A}^{\text{a}}\right].(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace )

\textrm{avec}

{E}_{T}>0
$$

${E}_{T}$ is the regularisation coefficient (or penalisation coefficient, of the dimension of a ratio of a force to a displacement) of the friction. The notion of sticking proper will therefore disappear; all the nodes slide. One sees in the figure above that this regularisation is not satisfactory because one has only transformed the Coulomb problem into a Tresca problem: the force is proportional to the relative sliding. In order to approach the Coulomb model, an additional inequality must be added:

$$
\Vert {E}_{T}.\left[{A}^{\text{a}}\right].\left(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace \right)\Vert \le \mu .\left|p\left(u\right)\right|
$$

This additional inequality modifies the graph (see figure ( )). The result is an approximate form of the Coulomb model, in which one will define the sticking nodes with respect to the contact threshold, the product of the Coulomb coefficient $\mu$ and of the contact pressure $p\left(u\right)$. The approximate character of Coulomb's law is expressed by the fact that the nodes said to be “sticking” will slide all the more as the regularisation coefficient is small. The physical interpretation is therefore less direct than in the case of contact, which explains in large part the difficulty of finding in practice a satisfactory value of this coefficient. However, the error committed (“false” detection of the sliding threshold) is often of little importance compared with the modelling assumptions. There remains a non-linearity which concerns the “sliding” part of the law. The treatment of this non-linearity is deferred to paragraph r5.03.50-modelisation-glissement.

#### 4.3.4 Modelling of sliding

The sliding conditions are always introduced with a Lagrange multiplier $\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g}}\right\rbrace$ which is expressed (in 3D) from:

$$
\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace =\left[{k}^{\text{g}}({u}_{i})\right].\left\lbrace {t}^{n}\right\rbrace
$$

This brings out the two unknowns which both depend on the solution: the contact pressure in the matrix of the sliding thresholds $\left[{k}^{\text{g}}\right]$ and the sliding direction $\left\lbrace {t}^{\text{n}}\right\rbrace$. In practice, however, one considers that knowledge of the sliding threshold $\left[{k}^{\text{g},n-1}\right]$ is acquired at the previous iteration $(n-1)$, which amounts to reducing to a Tresca criterion for each iteration. At convergence the threshold is obviously fixed: there are therefore no longer any differences between the thresholds in the course of the iterations. $\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g},n}\right\rbrace$ is therefore approximated with respect to the state of the previous iteration $(n-1)$:

$$
\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g},n}\right\rbrace \approx \left[{k}^{\text{g},n-1}\right].\frac{\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right].(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace )}{∥\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right].(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace )∥}=\left[{k}^{\text{g},n-1}\right].\left\lbrace {t}^{n}\right\rbrace
$$

One also has the sliding threshold at the previous iteration:

$$
\left[{k}^{\text{g},n-1}\right]=\mu .\left[∣{\mu}^{\text{c},n-1}∣\right]
$$

#### 4.3.5 Linearisations

In order to solve the non-linear problem, it is necessary to linearise the quantities which depend on $\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace$ in the equilibrium equation. The writing of the contact/friction forces introduces non-linear quantities which it is therefore appropriate to linearise in order to be able to apply the Newton algorithm. In the following table, one lists the cases where linearisation is necessary according to the type of algorithm.

|  | Dualisation | Regularisation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace$ | No linearisation | Linearisation |
| $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{a},n}\right\rbrace$ |  | Linearisation |
| $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{g},n}\right\rbrace$ |  | Linearisation |

##### 4.3.5.1 Linearisation of the contact forces

One regularises the contact condition from the expression of the contact force, when one is in the interpenetration phase, that is to say by assuming $\left\lbrace {h}^{n}\right\rbrace >0$:

$$
{\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace }_{\text{regu}}={E}_{N}.[{A}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.(\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace )
$$

By linearising, one obtains:

$$
{\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace }_{\text{regu}}\underset{\text{linéarisation}}{\to }{\left\lbrace {\stackrel{ˆ}{L}}_{i}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace }_{\text{regu}}=[{K}_{i}^{\text{c},n-1}].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace
$$

$[{K}_{i}^{\text{c},n-1}]$ brings a new contribution to the tangent matrix of the problem; it is the “contact” tangent matrix. It is obviously equal to:

$$
[{K}_{i}^{\text{c},n-1}]={E}_{N}.[{A}^{\text{c},n-1}{]}^{T}.[{A}^{\text{c},n-1}]
$$

And $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace$ contributes to the right-hand side; it is equal to:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace =-{E}_{N}.[{A}^{\text{c},n-1}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace
$$

One recalls that the contact penalisation coefficient is interpreted as the spring of stiffness ${E}_{N}$ which opposes the penetration of the slave node into the master surface. The larger it is, the less interpenetration there is and the higher the restoring force, but this coefficient also intervenes in the tangent matrix, which modifies its conditioning and makes the solution of the linear system more difficult. When contact is not activated (that is to say if $\left\lbrace {h}^{n}\right\rbrace \le 0$), the matrix and the right-hand-side vector are zero.

##### 4.3.5.2 Linearisation of the sticking forces

One regularises the sticking condition from the expression of the sticking force, when one is in the sticking phase (in the sense defined in paragraph r5.03.50-regularisation-condition-adherence), that is to say by assuming that the inequality is strictly satisfied:

$$
{\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{a},n}\right\rbrace }_{\text{regu}}={E}_{T}.{\left[{A}^{\text{a}}\right]}^{T}.\left[{A}^{\text{a}}\right].(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace )
$$

By linearising, one obtains:

$$
{\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{a},n}\right\rbrace }_{\text{regu}}\underset{\text{linéarisation}}{\to }{\left\lbrace {\stackrel{ˆ}{L}}_{i}^{\text{a},n}\right\rbrace }_{\text{regu}}=[{K}_{i}^{\text{a},n-1}].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{a},n-1}\right\rbrace
$$

$[{K}_{i}^{\text{a},n-1}]$ brings a new contribution to the tangent matrix of the problem; it is the “sticking” tangent matrix. It is obviously equal to:

$$
[{K}_{i}^{\text{a},n-1}]={E}_{T.}[{A}^{\text{a},n-1}{]}^{T.}[{A}^{\text{a},n-1}]
$$

And $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{a},n-1}\right\rbrace$ contributes to the right-hand side; it is equal to:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{a},n-1}\right\rbrace ={E}_{T.}[{A}_{\text{a}}^{n-1}{]}^{T.}\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace
$$

One recalls that the contact penalisation coefficient is interpreted as the spring of stiffness ${E}_{T}$ (of the dimension of a ratio of a force to a displacement) which opposes the relative sliding of the two surfaces. The larger it is, the higher the restoring force, but this coefficient also intervenes in the tangent matrix, which modifies its conditioning and makes the solution of the linear system more difficult.

##### 4.3.5.3 Linearisation of the sliding forces

We have seen in paragraph r5.03.50-modelisation-glissement that the sliding forces depended on the final displacement $\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right\rbrace$ through the Tresca threshold, but that we made the assumption that the information at iteration $(n-1)$ was sufficient to solve Coulomb's law 2 by writing:

$$
\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g},n}\right\rbrace =\left[{k}^{\text{g},n-1}\right].\left\lbrace {t}^{n}\right\rbrace
$$

Nevertheless, one must prepare what will happen at the following iteration $(n+1)$ if the threshold is not correct and will require a new iteration. At the next Newton iteration, the solution displacement will be:

$$
\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{n+1}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n+1}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n+1}\right\rbrace
$$

One must therefore linearise the quantity at $(n+1)$:

$$
\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g},n+1}\right\rbrace =\left[{k}^{\text{g},n}\right].\left\lbrace t\right\rbrace
$$

Equation  is of the form $\frac{\left\lbrace h({x}^{n+1})\right\rbrace }{∥\left\lbrace h({x}^{n+1})\right\rbrace ∥}$ with $\left\lbrace {x}^{n+1}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {x}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {\delta x}^{n+1}\right\rbrace$. The linearisation is written:

$$
\left[k\right].\frac{\left\lbrace h({x}^{n+1})\right\rbrace }{∥\left\lbrace h({x}^{n+1})\right\rbrace ∥}\underset{\text{linéarisation}}{\to }\left[k\right].(\frac{\left\lbrace h({x}^{n})\right\rbrace }{∥\left\lbrace h({x}^{n})\right\rbrace ∥}+\frac{1}{∥\left\lbrace h({x}^{n})\right\rbrace ∥}.(\left[I\right]-\frac{\left\lbrace h({x}^{n})\right\rbrace \langle \left\lbrace h({x}^{n})\right\rbrace \rangle }{{∥\left\lbrace h({x}^{n})\right\rbrace ∥}^{2}}).\left\lbrace \delta {x}^{n+1}\right\rbrace )
$$

One applies  to the expression  with:

$$
\left\lbrace {x}^{n}\right\rbrace \leftarrow \left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace

\textrm{et}

\left\lbrace {x}^{n+1}\right\rbrace \leftarrow \left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n+1}\right\rbrace

\left\lbrace {\delta x}^{n+1}\right\rbrace \leftarrow \left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n+1}\right\rbrace

\left\lbrace h({x}^{n})\right\rbrace \leftarrow \left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right].(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace )

\textrm{et}

\left\lbrace h({x}^{n+1})\right\rbrace \leftarrow \left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right].(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n+1}\right\rbrace )

\left[k\right]\leftarrow \left[{k}^{\text{g},n}\right]
$$

One linearises :

$$
\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g},n+1}\right\rbrace \underset{\text{linéarisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {\stackrel{ˆ}{\mu}}^{\text{g},n+1}\right\rbrace
$$

One begins by denoting $\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace$ the relative tangential sliding at iteration $n$:

$$
\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace =\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right].(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace )
$$

One obtains:

$$
\left\lbrace {\stackrel{ˆ}{\mu}}^{\text{g},n+1}\right\rbrace =\left[{k}^{\text{g},n}\right].(\frac{\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace }{∥\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace ∥}+\frac{1}{∥\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace ∥}.(\left[I\right]-\frac{\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace \langle {g}^{\text{t},n}\rangle }{{∥\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace ∥}^{2}}).\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n+1}\right\rbrace )
$$

If one denotes:

$$
\left\lbrace {\rho}^{\text{g},n}\right\rbrace =\left[{k}^{\text{g},n}\right].\frac{\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace }{∥\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace ∥}
$$

And:

$$
\left[{B}^{\text{g},n}\right]=\frac{\left[{k}^{\text{g},n}\right]}{∥\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace ∥}.(\left[I\right]-\frac{\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace \langle {g}^{\text{t},n}\rangle }{{∥\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace ∥}^{2}})
$$

One can write  in the following condensed form:

$$
\left\lbrace {\stackrel{ˆ}{\mu}}^{\text{g},n+1}\right\rbrace =\left[{B}^{\text{g},n}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n+1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {\rho}^{\text{g},n}\right\rbrace
$$

The sliding force is expressed as a function of the Lagrange multiplier:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{g},n+1}\right\rbrace ={\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g},n+1}\right\rbrace
$$

The matrix $\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right]$ not depending on the displacement, the linearised sliding force can be written:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{g},n+1}\right\rbrace \underset{\text{linéarisation}}{\to }\left\lbrace {\stackrel{ˆ}{L}}_{i}^{\text{g},n+1}\right\rbrace ={\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\stackrel{ˆ}{\mu}}^{\text{g},n+1}\right\rbrace =\left[{K}^{\text{g},n}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n+1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {L}^{\text{g},n}\right\rbrace
$$

With the sliding matrix $\left[{K}^{\text{g},n}\right]$ such that:

$$
\left[{K}^{\text{g},n}\right]={\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right]}^{T}.\left[{B}^{\text{g},n}\right]
$$

And the vector:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}^{\text{g},n}\right\rbrace ={\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\rho}^{\text{g},n}\right\rbrace
$$

$\left[{K}^{\text{g},n}\right]$ brings a new contribution to the tangent matrix of the problem; it is the “sliding” tangent matrix. It is obviously equal to:

$$
\left[{K}^{\text{g},n}\right]=\frac{{\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right]}^{T}.\left[{k}^{\text{g},n}\right]}{∥\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace ∥}.(\left[I\right]-\frac{\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace \langle {g}^{\text{t},n}\rangle }{{∥\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace ∥}^{2}})
$$

The second part of the expression is preceded by the sign $-$; the effect of this contribution is particularly destabilising for the global behaviour of the tangent matrix of the system, more particularly when one is far from equilibrium and therefore at the beginning of the solution at each new time step. One therefore decides to take it into account only partially by affecting it with a coefficient $\theta \in [0,1]$ which one can modify via the keyword COEF_MATR_FROT:

$$
\left[{\tilde{K}}_{\theta}^{\text{g},n}\right]=\frac{{\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right]}^{T}.\left[{k}^{\text{g},n}\right]}{∥\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace ∥}.(\left[I\right]-\theta .\frac{\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace \langle {g}^{\text{t},n}\rangle }{{∥\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace ∥}^{2}})
$$

One advises using an initial value of $0.5$ for this coefficient and decreasing it if convergence is not obtained. In the case where $\theta =0$ convergence seems always to be obtained but is particularly slow. When one is close to the solution, it is on the other hand very useful to have a value of this coefficient equal to $1$ so as to accelerate convergence. This is done automatically in the code when the residual RESI_GLOB_RELA is less than ${10}^{-3}$. One replaces $\left[{K}^{\text{g},n}\right]$ by $\left[{\tilde{K}}_{\theta}^{\text{g},n}\right]$.

The right-hand side is finally equal to:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}^{\text{g},n}\right\rbrace ={\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right]}^{T}.\left[{k}^{\text{g},n}\right].\frac{\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace }{∥\left\lbrace {g}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace ∥}
$$

In 2D, the Lagrange multiplier for sliding is approximated by:

$$
{\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{g}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace }_{\text{2D}}=\mu .\left[∣{\mu}^{\text{c}}({u}_{i})∣\right]=\left[{k}^{\text{g}}({u}_{i})\right]
$$

One can show ${\left[{K}^{\text{g},n}\right]}_{\text{2D}}=0$. And therefore:

$$
{\left\lbrace {\stackrel{ˆ}{L}}_{i}^{\text{g},n+1}\right\rbrace }_{\text{2D}}={\left[{A}^{\text{g}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\rho}^{\text{g},n}\right\rbrace
$$

#### 4.3.6 General algorithms

The discrete methods for the solution of the contact/friction problem are based on a *decoupled* approach between equilibrium and contact/friction. Contact/friction (denoted $C$ in the second column) is treated after each Newton iteration of the global problem (denoted $G$ in the second column).

Without taking contact into account, one will denote the solution vectors with a $\text{~}$, for example:

$$
\left\lbrace {\tilde{u}}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace
$$

##### 4.3.6.1 Case of dualisation

The general procedure for the dualised case is the following:

| At the Newton iteration $n$ |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | G | Solution of the equilibrium problem without contact, equation | $\to \left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace$ |
| 2 | G | Update of the displacements without contact | $\left\lbrace {\tilde{u}}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace$ |
| 3 | C | Modification of the displacements in order to satisfy the contact conditions | $\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace \to \left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace$ |
| 4 | C | Update of the displacements with contact taken into account | $\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace$ |
| 5 | C | Computation of the contact forces | $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace$ and $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{f},n}\right\rbrace$ |
| 6 | C | Possible computation of matrices (case of sliding) | $\left[{K}^{\text{g},n+1}\right]$ |
| 7 | G | Computation of the internal and external forces with the modified displacements | $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{ext},n}\right\rbrace$ and $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int,n}\right\rbrace$ |
| 8 | G | Check of equilibrium |  |

The matrix $\left[{K}^{\text{cf},n+1}\right]$ is computed only in the case of sliding. It will not necessarily be used; everything will depend on the convergence of Newton and on the contact/friction algorithm (Tresca threshold converged on the Coulomb problem).

##### 4.3.6.2 Case of regularisation

The general procedure for the regularised case (penalisation) is the following:

| At the Newton iteration $n$ |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | G | Solution of the equilibrium problem without contact, equation | $\to \left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace$ |
| 2 | G | Update of the displacements without contact | $\left\lbrace {\tilde{u}}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace$ |
| 3 | C | Computation of the contact/friction forces | $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace$ and $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{f},n}\right\rbrace$ |
| 4 | C | Computation of the modified matrices | $\left[{K}^{\text{cf},n+1}\right]$ |
| 5 | G | Computation of the internal and external forces | $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{ext},n}\right\rbrace$ and $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int,n}\right\rbrace$ |
| 6 | G | Check of equilibrium |  |

The contact/friction algorithm does not modify the displacements; it is the global system which will modify them. This implies that for the penalised methods, one will always need at least two Newton iterations, even in the elastic case: one iteration to solve the equilibrium problem without contact/friction and a second iteration to integrate the contact-friction conditions which will modify the global system.

There exist several ways of choosing and computing this matrix. For more details see [r5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01)

The idea of transforming the Coulomb problem into a succession of Tresca problems is very widely justified in the literature (see   for example) and is used in the majority of commercial codes. It is also the strategy adopted in the “fixed-point” version of the algorithm in the continuous formulation (see [r5.03.52](doc:r5.03.52)).

## 5 Algorithmic solution – Contact without friction

### 5.1 Contact links

Each slave node *potentially* in contact has a status whose nature the algorithms will have to determine. A slave node is called a “potential contact link” or more simply a “link”. The term link alludes to the fact that the contact condition is the result of the imposition of a kinematic relation on the displacement *degrees of freedom* of the slave node. These links are gathered in various sets:

- :math:`` is the set of the possible links (active and inactive);
- ${\Xi}^{\text{nc}}$ is the set of the slave nodes which are not in contact (inactive links);
- ${\Xi}^{\text{c}}$ is the set of the nodes effectively in contact (active links).

One therefore has the following relations between the sets:

- ${\Xi}^{\text{c}}\cap {\Xi}^{\text{nc}}=\mathrm{\varnothing }$ because the nodes are in contact or not;
- ${\Xi}^{l}={\Xi}^{\text{c}}\oplus {\Xi}^{\text{nc}}$ because the nodes potentially in contact are so or not;

### 5.2 The dualised methods in contact

#### 5.2.1 Equilibrium of the structure in the presence of contact

One recalls that the equilibrium equation in the presence of contact is written:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace
$$

In the case of dualised contact, the contact force is written as a function of the contact Lagrange multiplier:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace ={\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace }_{\text{dual}}=[{A}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace
$$

After linearisation of the equilibrium equation, one introduces the tangent matrix $\left[{K}^{\text{m},n-1}\right]$ which will contain the contributions coming from the linearisation of the internal and external forces and the matrix $\left[{K}^{\text{c},n-1}\right]$ for the contact forces; one finds $\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace$, the solution increment of the equilibrium problem at iteration $n$ but without application of the contact law:

$$
(\left[{K}^{\text{m},n-1}\right]+\left[{K}^{\text{c},n-1}\right]).\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int,n-1}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{ext},n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace
$$

We have seen that the contact forces in the dualised case do not depend on the displacement (see r5.03.50-linearisation). One therefore has $\left[{K}^{\text{c},n-1}\right]=0$. This allows us to express the equilibrium of the structure with the contact forces taken into account (denoting $\left[K\right]=\left[{K}^{\text{m},n-1}\right]$ and $\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{ext},n-1}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int,n-1}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace$ in order to lighten the notation):

$$
\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace
$$

The solution $\left\lbrace {\tilde{u}}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace$ is obtained after equilibrium and before application of the contact conditions. It is written:

$$
\left\lbrace {\tilde{u}}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace
$$

In order to obtain the solution $\left\lbrace {\tilde{u}}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace$, we have not applied the contact conditions (Signorini's law) for the current Newton iteration $n$. On the other hand, the contact reactions computed at the previous Newton iteration $n-1$ are indeed taken into account in $\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace$. In order to solve completely the equilibrium problem with contact/friction, at iteration $n$, one must apply Signorini's law, which is expressed by the following system:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace \le \left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace & (a)\\ \left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \ge 0& (b)\\ \langle {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\rangle .(\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace )=0& (c)\end{array}
$$

One recalls the interpretation of the Signorini conditions:

- Equation (a) represents the geometric non-interpenetration conditions, the inequality being understood component by component (each row relates to a potential contact pair).
- Equation (b) expresses the absence of opposition to separation (the contact surfaces can experience only compressions); this is the condition known as intensility.
- Equation (c) is the compatibility condition. When, for a given link, the Lagrange multiplier is non-zero, one has contact and therefore the gap is zero. When the gap is non-zero (the two surfaces are not in contact), the associated multiplier must be zero (no compression).

The application of Signorini's law will modify the displacement increment $\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace$ (which becomes $\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace$), whence the solution obtained after equilibrium and *after* application of the contact law, which is written:

$$
\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace
$$

#### 5.2.2 System reduced to the active links

We are going to write the system making it possible to solve completely the equilibrium problem with Signorini's law taken into account. The idea is to transform the inequalities of the system into equalities. One begins by evaluating the gap given by the displacement computed before the contact correction $\left\lbrace {\tilde{d}}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace$, for all the links 1:

$$
{⌊\left\lbrace {\tilde{d}}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{l}}={⌊\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace -\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace )⌋}_{{\Xi}^{l}}
$$

One says that a link $J$ is active if its gap ${⌊\left\lbrace {\tilde{d}}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{\text{J}\in {\Xi}^{l}}$ is negative, which indicates an interpenetration (the contact condition is not satisfied); this link therefore becomes a contact link and thus makes it possible to define the *initially* postulated set of the contact links ${\Xi}^{\text{c}}$:

$$
{\Xi}^{\text{c}}=\left\lbrace J\in {\Xi}^{l}\mid {⌊\left\lbrace {\tilde{d}}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{J}<0\right\rbrace
$$

One postulates that, for these active links, the effective gap will be zero, and that therefore the inequality ${⌊\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{l}}\le {⌊\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{l}}$ becomes an equality for the set of the active links:

$$
{⌊\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{\text{c}}}
$$

If one uses $\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace$, the gap evaluated before the current Newton iteration:

$$
\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace -\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace )
$$

With:

$$
\left\lbrace {\tilde{d}}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace -\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace
$$

Equality  is finally written:

$$
{⌊\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{\text{c}}}
$$

The “mixed” inequality/equality system (containing the equilibrium equation and the Signorini conditions) is finally transformed into a simple system which treats only equalities, on the basis of the postulated contact links ${\Xi}^{\text{c}}$:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace +[{A}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace \\ {⌊\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{\text{c}}}\end{array}
$$

One seeks the unknowns $\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace$ and $\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$ which are solutions of the following system:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc}[K]& [{A}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}\\ [{A}^{\text{c}}]& [0]\end{array}\right].\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace \\ \left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace \\ \left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace \end{array}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\iff }\left[{K}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace \\ \left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace \\ \left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace \end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

One will note that the dualised formulation of the non-interpenetration condition expresses the stationarity of the Lagrangian $L$ thus defined:

$$
L(\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace ,\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace )=\frac{1}{2}.\langle \delta {u}^{n}\rangle .\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace -\langle \delta {u}^{n}\rangle .\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace +\langle {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\rangle .(\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace )
$$

This stationarity is expressed in the form of the saddle-point problem:

$$
\underset{\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace }{\min}\underset{\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \ge 0}{\max}L(\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace ,\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace )
$$

#### 5.2.3 The active-constraint method – ALGO_CONT='CONTRAINTE'

The aim is to generalise the approach of the previous paragraph by adopting an iterative algorithm which works in three phases:

1. Make assumptions on the state of the links (transformations inequalities → equality);
2. Solve the new system thus created;
3. Check the initial assumptions and loop (possibly) to 1.

A complete description of the method with the necessary theoretical justifications can be found in  and . The principle is the following: one postulates a set of constraints said to be active, which correspond to a zero gap (the inequality relation becomes an equality); one solves the system of equations obtained in this subspace, and one looks at whether the starting postulate was justified. If the set chosen was too small (one had forgotten active links), one adds to the set the link violating the non-interpenetration condition the most; if the set chosen was too large (links assumed active are in fact not), one removes from the set the most improbable link, *i.e.* the one whose Lagrange multiplier violates the intensility condition the most. Removing or adding only one link at each iteration of the method guarantees convergence in a finite number of iterations less than or equal to twice the maximum number of links, provided that the matrix $\left[K\right]$ is symmetric and positive definite , , .

In elasticity, at the end of the active-constraint iterations, one has a result converged in the Newton sense. In plasticity or if one updates the geometry, this is not the case because several Newton iterations are necessary in order to obtain equilibrium. After each Newton iteration, one launches the active-constraint algorithm in order to satisfy the contact conditions. Thus, in elasticity, one will necessarily converge for each step in one iteration if REAC_GEOM='SANS'.

##### 5.2.3.1 Writing of the iterative problem

One starts from the increment obtained without treating contact $\left\lbrace \delta {\stackrel{~}{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace$ and one carries out the active-constraint iterations $k$ until convergence proper of this algorithm. Convergence in the sense of the active constraints is obtained when no link violates the kinematic condition (inequality (a)) and when the associated Lagrange multipliers are all positive (inequality (b)).

One denotes by $k$ the active-constraint iterations. The starting solution without contact correction is $\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace$ and the increment added by the new contact iteration is $\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace$. One denotes:

$$
\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{0}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace

\left\lbrace {\delta}_{0}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace 0\right\rbrace

\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k-1}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace
$$

One places oneself at the contact iteration $k$. One seeks to solve the system:

$$
\left\{   \begin{array}{c}\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k}^{n}\right\rbrace +[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace \\ {⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}}\end{array} \right.
$$

One notes that the contact Lagrange multiplier $\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$ is not solved in an incremental manner but in a total manner (over the time step). Injecting  into this system, one obtains:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{c}\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k-1}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left[K\right].\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace +[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace \\ {⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k-1}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace )=\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}}\end{array} \right.
$$

The first equation can be rewritten:

$$
\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace ={\left[K\right]}^{-1}.\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k-1}^{n}\right\rbrace -{\left[K\right]}^{-1}.[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace
$$

Taking into account that $\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace ={\left[K\right]}^{-1}.\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace$, one simplifies:

$$
{\delta}_{k}=\delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}-\delta {u}_{k-1}^{n}-{K}^{-1}.{[{A}_{k}^{c}]}^{T}.{\mu}_{i}^{c}
$$

From the postulated state of the active links:

$$
{⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k-1}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace )=\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}}
$$

And taking up again the expression for $\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace$ given by :

$$
{⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k-1}^{n}\right\rbrace +\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}_{C}^{k}}={⌊\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace -\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace )⌋}_{{\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}}
$$

One uses the expression for $\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace$ given by :

$$
{⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace -\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].{\left[K\right]}^{-1}.[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}}={⌊\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace -\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace )⌋}_{{\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}}
$$

Finally, the Lagrange multipliers $\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$ for the contact links are solutions of the following system:

$$
{⌊-\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].{\left[K\right]}^{-1}.[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {\tilde{d}}_{\text{c},n}^{}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}}
$$

One notes that  is the Schur complement ${\left[K\right]}_{\text{schur}}=\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].{\left[K\right]}^{-1}.{\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}$ of the matrix $\left[{K}^{\text{c}}\right]$. One can finally compute the displacement increments $\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace$ with:

$$
\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace =\sum_{l=0}^{k-1}\left\lbrace {\delta}_{l}\right\rbrace -{\left[K\right]}^{-1}.{[{A}_{k}^{c}]}^{T}.{\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}\right\rbrace }^{c}
$$

The solution of  is the most expensive part, in computation time, of the algorithm. The whole efficiency of the strategy consists in using the fact that one solves this system only on the set of the active links ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$; one therefore uses two properties.

- The computation of the Schur complement ${\left[K\right]}_{\text{schur}}$ always calls upon a factorisation of type ${\mathit{LDL}}^{T}$, which makes it possible to save time, because such a factorisation possesses the remarkable property of being incremental, that is to say that the addition of a link does not force one to rebuild the factorised matrix from the beginning but only the part which one modifies.
- The solution (forward/back substitution) is also done only on the set of the active links ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$.

##### 5.2.3.2 Validity of the set of active links chosen

At the end of each active-constraint iteration, it is appropriate to check whether the set ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$ is indeed correct. Let the link be $J\in {\Xi}^{l}$; three situations are possible:

1. The relative displacement compensates the initial gap ${⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k}^{n}\right\rbrace )⌋}_{J\in {\Xi}^{l}}={⌊\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{J\in {\Xi}^{l}}$;
2. The relative displacement is less than the initial gap ${⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k}^{n}\right\rbrace )⌋}_{J\in {\Xi}^{l}}<{⌊\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{J\in {\Xi}^{l}}$;
3. The relative displacement is greater than the initial gap ${⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k}^{n}\right\rbrace )⌋}_{J\in {\Xi}^{l}}>{⌊\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{J\in {\Xi}^{l}}$.

Situation $(3)$ is forbidden because it corresponds to a violation of the non-interpenetration condition. Situation $(1)$ corresponds to a link said to be active, situation $(2)$ to an inactive link.

At the beginning of iteration $k$ of the algorithm, one had postulated a set of active links ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$. One has found a possible increment $\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace$ of the unknowns under these assumptions and one will now check that this increment is compatible with the assumptions. In practice, this consists in making two checks:

1. Is the set ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$ too small? One checks that the links assumed inactive do not violate the non-interpenetration condition, otherwise one activates one of them, the one which violates the condition the most.
2. Is the set ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$ too large? One checks that the links assumed active are associated with contact multipliers $\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$ which are positive or zero, otherwise one deactivates the one which violates the condition the most.

Is the set ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$ too small?

In order to check that the set ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$ is not too small, one is going to compute, for all the links assumed inactive, the following quantity:

$$
{\rho}_{J}={⌊\frac{\left\lbrace {d}_{\text{ini}}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace -\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].(\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k}^{n}\right\rbrace )}{\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace }⌋}_{J\in {\Xi}_{k}^{\text{nc}}}={⌊\frac{\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace -\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k}^{n}\right\rbrace }{\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace }⌋}_{J\in {\Xi}_{k}^{\text{nc}}}
$$

There are two cases:

1. If ${⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{J\in {\Xi}_{k}^{\text{nc}}}<0$, the gap for the link $J$ is going to increase, and therefore the link assumed inactive remains in this state; ${\rho}_{J}$ is strictly greater than $1$;
2. If ${⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{J\in {\Xi}_{k}^{\text{nc}}}>0$, the gap for the link $J$ is going to decrease, and therefore the link assumed inactive is going to be activated; ${\rho}_{J}$ is less than or equal to $1$;

One therefore examines $\stackrel{ˉ}{\rho}=\underset{J}{\text{Min}}{\rho}_{j}$ over the set of the links $J$ declared inactive. If $\stackrel{ˉ}{\rho}<1$, this indicates that at least one link is violated (situation $(3)$): one then adds to the list of active links the number of the link whose interpenetration is the greatest, that is to say the one which realises the minimum of ${\rho}_{J}$, and one writes $\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k+1}^{n}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k}^{n}\right\rbrace +\stackrel{ˉ}{\rho}.\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace$ (which corresponds to a zero gap for the link added).

One presents the algorithm used:

> If ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}={\Xi}^{l}$

> $\stackrel{ˉ}{\rho}=1$

> Else

> $\stackrel{ˉ}{\rho}=1$
> Loop over the links $J\in {\Xi}_{k}^{\text{nc}}$

> Computation of ${\alpha}_{J}={⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {\delta}_{k}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{J}$
> If ${\alpha}_{J}<0$

> ${\rho}_{J}=\frac{{⌊\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace -\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k}^{n}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{J}}{{\alpha}_{J}}$
> $\stackrel{ˉ}{\rho}=\min({\rho}_{J},\stackrel{ˉ}{\rho})$
> ${J}_{min}=J$

> End Loop

On exit from this algorithm, one will have the number of the link which violates the non-penetration condition the most, ${J}_{min}$, and the value $\stackrel{ˉ}{\rho}$: $\left\lbrace {J}_{min},\stackrel{ˉ}{\rho}\right\rbrace =\mathit{IsPetit}({\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}})$

Is the set ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$ too large?

The second check consists in asking whether the set of active links is too large. One now places oneself in the case where $\stackrel{ˉ}{\rho}\ge 1$, that is to say that one knows that ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$ is not too small. Then:

- If no link is active, the method has converged towards a state without contact;
- If there are links assumed active:
- If all the Lagrange multipliers $\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$ are positive or zero, one has likewise converged towards a state with effective contact;
- If there exist negative Lagrange multipliers $\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$, the corresponding links should not be active: one removes from the set of active links the link whose negative multiplier is the largest in absolute value.

On exit from this algorithm, one will have the number of the link which violates the intensility condition the most: $\left\lbrace {J}_{max}\right\rbrace =\mathit{IsGrand}({\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}})$.

#### 5.2.4 The Projected Conjugate Gradient method – ALGO_CONT='GCP'

The solution method presented in this part is an application of the Projected Conjugate Gradient algorithm. It is very precisely the iterative version of the Active Constraint method presented in the previous part.

##### 5.2.4.1 Reformulation of the contact problem

One recalls that the system to be solved at each Newton iteration $n$ is the following:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace +[{A}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace \\ \left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace \le \left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace \end{array}
$$

It comes from the dualisation of the contact conditions and it expresses the stationarity of the Lagrangian $L$ thus defined:

$$
L(\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace ,\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace )=\frac{1}{2}.\langle \delta {u}^{n}\rangle .\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace -\langle \delta {u}^{n}\rangle .\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace +\langle {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\rangle .(\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace )
$$

This stationarity can be expressed in the form of the saddle-point problem:

$$
\underset{\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace }{\min}\underset{\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \ge 0}{\max}L(\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace ,\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace )
$$

Now the minimum of $L$ with respect to $\delta {u}_{i}$ is known; it has the expression:

$$
\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace ={\left[K\right]}^{-1}.(\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace -{\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace )

\textrm{et}

\langle \delta {u}^{n}\rangle =(\langle F\rangle -\langle {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\rangle .\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]).{\left[K\right]}^{-1}
$$

One therefore only has to carry out the maximisation of $L$ with respect to $\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \ge 0$. Knowing that maximising a functional $J$ is equivalent to minimising $-J$, one reduces to the following minimisation problem:

$$
\underset{\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \ge 0}{\min}H(\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace )
$$

Where the functional $H$ is written:

$$
H(\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace )=\frac{1}{2}.\langle {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\rangle .\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].{\left[K\right]}^{-1}.{\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace +\langle {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\rangle .(\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace -\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace )+\frac{1}{2}.\langle F\rangle .{\left[K\right]}^{-1}.\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace
$$

This expression is the dual form of the contact problem: it involves the field of Lagrange multipliers $\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$ and no longer at all the displacement field $\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace$. The problem to be solved is now a minimisation under a positivity constraint on the unknown. The Projected Conjugate Gradient method is a simple and effective method for this type of problem. One will denote:

- $\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace$ the search direction
- $\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace$ the subgradient and $\left\lbrace {r}^{p}\right\rbrace$ its preconditioned version

##### 5.2.4.2 Linear search

In a conjugate-gradient algorithm, it is necessary to estimate an advance step. Two variants of linear search are available, admissible or not. They are represented graphically in the figure. The admissible variant, which imposes remaining in the admissible convex domain, induces a single solution per iteration; it leads to a fairly regular convergence. The non-admissible variant, which authorises leaving the admissible domain in order to project back into it afterwards, induces two solutions per iteration; it leads to a fairly erratic convergence but generally faster than the admissible method.

Figure 5.2.4.2-a: variants of the linear search.

Here is the algorithm of the linear search:

> $\mathit{RechLine}$
> $\mathit{Ini}$        Contact state $\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]\leftarrow \left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right]$ and ${\Xi}^{\text{c}}\leftarrow {\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$

> Computation of the right-hand side $\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace ={\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace$
> Solution of $\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace \to \left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace$
> Computation of $\alpha =\frac{\langle r\rangle .\left\lbrace {r}^{p}\right\rbrace }{\langle \delta a\rangle .\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace }$

**If ADMISSIBLE**

$\forall J\in {\Xi}^{l}:\text{Si}{\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace }_{J}<0\text{alors}\alpha =\underset{J\in {\Xi}^{l}}{\min}(\alpha ,-\frac{{\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace }_{J}}{{\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace }_{J}})$

$\left\lbrace {\mu}_{k+1}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {\mu}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace +\alpha .\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace$

$\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k+1}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k}\right\rbrace -\alpha .\left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace$

**Else**

$\left\lbrace {\mu}_{k+1}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {\mu}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace +\alpha .\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace$

$\left\lbrace {\mu}_{k+1}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \leftarrow \max(\left\lbrace {\mu}_{k+1}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace ,0)$

Computation of the right-hand side $\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace ={\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$

Solution of $\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace \to \left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace$

$\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k+1}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace$

> $F$                   END

On exit from this algorithm, one will have the displacement and the Lagrange multipliers after computation of the advance step: $\left\lbrace \left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k+1}\right\rbrace ,\left\lbrace {\mu}_{k+1}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \right\rbrace =\mathit{RechLine}(\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace ,\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace ,\left\lbrace {r}^{p}\right\rbrace )$. This algorithm comprises two solutions but, by using the matrix $\left[K\right]$ already factorised (it is the global matrix of the equilibrium problem), one therefore only does a forward-back substitution, which costs little.

The non-admissible variant is obliged to recompute a displacement increment compatible with the positivity constraint on the contact multipliers.

##### 5.2.4.3 Preconditioning

In the GCP algorithm, mention is made of an optional call to a preconditioner. The aim of this preconditioner is to accelerate the convergence of the method. Its definition comes from the following considerations of functional analysis: in the update phase $\left\lbrace {\mu}_{k+1}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {\mu}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace +\alpha .\left\lbrace {Z}_{k}\right\rbrace$ and if one has not called upon the preconditioner, the field $\left\lbrace {\mu}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$ belongs to ${H}^{-1/2}({\Gamma}_{\text{c}})$ whereas $\left\lbrace {Z}_{k}\right\rbrace$ belongs to ${H}^{1/2}({\Gamma}_{\text{c}})$. This sum therefore has no mathematical meaning. In order to give it one, one must send $\left\lbrace {Z}_{k}\right\rbrace$ into ${H}^{-1/2}({\Gamma}_{\text{c}})$; this is the preconditioning operation. Knowing that $\left\lbrace {Z}_{k}\right\rbrace$ is obtained by the expression $\left\lbrace {Z}_{k}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {r}_{k}^{p}\right\rbrace +{\gamma}_{k}.\left\lbrace {Z}_{k-1}\right\rbrace$, it is on $\left\lbrace {r}_{k}^{p}\right\rbrace$ that one is going to operate. This is what is done by the preconditioner by solving the following auxiliary problem:

$$
\left\{   \begin{array}{c}{⌊\left[K\right].\left\lbrace a\right\rbrace +{\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {r}_{k}^{p}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}}=0\\ {⌊\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace a\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {r}_{k}^{p}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}}\end{array}\right.
$$

One therefore solves a problem of imposed displacement on the part where contact is effective and one recovers the reactions at the fixed support $\left\lbrace {r}_{k}^{p}\right\rbrace$ which do indeed belong to ${H}^{-1/2}({\Gamma}_{\text{c}})$. In the terminology of domain decomposition, this is a Dirichlet preconditioner.

In order to solve this auxiliary problem, one also uses a conjugate-gradient algorithm but without projection because no positivity constraint appears in it. This iterative approach authorises an approximate solution so as to save computation time.

- $\left\lbrace X\right\rbrace$ the search direction;
- $\left\lbrace s\right\rbrace$ the subgradient.

Here is the algorithm of the preconditioner:

> $\mathit{PreCond}$
> $\mathit{Ini}$        $\left\lbrace {a}_{0}\right\rbrace =0$

> Contact state $\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]\leftarrow \left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right]$ and ${\Xi}^{\text{c}}\leftarrow {\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}$
> If ${\Xi}^{\text{c}}=\mathrm{\varnothing }$

> Goto End $F$

> ${B}_{p}$             Loop over $p=1,{\mathit{Iter}}_{max}$

> Computation of the gradient $\left\lbrace {s}_{p}\right\rbrace =\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace {a}_{p-1}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace$
> Computation of the residual $\varepsilon =\underset{J\in {\Xi}^{\text{c}}}{max}\left\lbrace {s}_{p}\right\rbrace$
> If $\varepsilon <{\varepsilon}_{\mathit{precond}}$

> Goto End $F$

> If $p=1$ or update

> $\left\lbrace {X}_{p}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {s}_{p}\right\rbrace$

> Else, conjugation

> $\beta =\frac{\langle {s}_{p}\rangle .\left\lbrace {s}_{p}\right\rbrace }{\langle {s}_{p-1}\rangle .\left\lbrace {s}_{p-1}\right\rbrace }$
> $\left\lbrace {X}_{p}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {s}_{p}\right\rbrace +\beta .\left\lbrace {X}_{p-1}\right\rbrace$

> Computation of the right-hand side $\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace ={⌊{\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {X}_{p}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{\text{c}}}$
> Solution of $\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace F\right\rbrace \to \left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace$                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            | Computation of the Schur complement ${\left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace }_{\text{schur}}={⌊\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{\text{c}}}$
> Computation of the advance step $\alpha =\frac{\langle {s}_{p}\rangle .\left\lbrace {s}_{p}\right\rbrace }{\langle F\rangle .{\left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace }_{\text{schur}}}$
> New subgradient $\left\lbrace {s}_{p}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {s}_{p-1}\right\rbrace +\alpha .\left\lbrace {X}_{p}\right\rbrace$
> New displacement $\left\lbrace {a}_{p}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {a}_{p-1}\right\rbrace -\alpha .\left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace$

> ${B}_{k}$             End Loop $k=k+1$
> $F$                   END

> $\left\lbrace {r}_{k}^{p}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {s}_{p}\right\rbrace$
> Projection: $({max}_{J}({r}_{k}^{p},0)\mathit{si}{\mu}_{J}<0)$

On exit from this algorithm, one will have the preconditioned subgradient: $\left\lbrace {r}_{k}^{p}\right\rbrace =\mathit{PreCond}(\left\lbrace {r}_{k}\right\rbrace )$.

##### 5.2.4.4 Algorithm

Here is the global algorithm for the GCP method:

> $\mathit{Ini}$    If $\langle {\mu}_{i-1}^{\text{c}}\rangle \cdot \left\lbrace {\mu}_{i-1}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \mathrm{\ne }0$ then $[K].\left\lbrace v\right\rbrace =[{A}_{i-1}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.\left\lbrace {\mu}_{i-1}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \to \left\lbrace v\right\rbrace$

> If $\langle {\mu}_{i-1}^{\text{c}}\rangle \cdot \left\lbrace {\mu}_{i-1}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace =0$ then $\left\lbrace v\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace 0\right\rbrace$
> Computation of ${\mathit{\delta u}}_{0}^{n}=\delta {\stackrel{̃}{u}}^{n}-v$
> Evaluation of ${\Xi}_{0}^{\text{c}}=\left\lbrace J\in {\Xi}^{l}\mid {⌊\left\lbrace {\tilde{d}}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{J}<0\right\rbrace$

> ${B}_{k}$         Loop over $k=1,{\mathit{Iter}}_{max}$

> ${\Xi}_{k}^{\text{c}}\leftarrow {\Xi}_{k-1}^{\text{c}}$
> Computation of the subgradient $\left\lbrace {r}_{k}\right\rbrace =(\left[{A}_{k}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k-1}^{n}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace )$
> Projection of the subgradient $\left\lbrace {r}_{k}\right\rbrace =\underset{J\in {\Xi}^{l}}{max}(\left\lbrace {r}_{k}\right\rbrace ,0)$
> Computation of the residual $\varepsilon =\underset{J\in {\Xi}^{l}}{max}\left\lbrace {r}_{k}\right\rbrace$
> Preconditioning $\left\lbrace {r}_{k}^{p}\right\rbrace =\mathit{PreCond}(\left\lbrace {r}_{k}\right\rbrace )$
> Conjugation

> Computation of the coefficient ${\gamma}^{k}=\frac{\langle {r}_{k}\rangle .\left\lbrace {r}_{k}^{p}\right\rbrace -\langle {r}_{k}\rangle .\left\lbrace {r}_{k-1}^{p}\right\rbrace }{\langle {r}_{k-1}\rangle .\left\lbrace {r}_{k-1}^{p}\right\rbrace }$
> $\left\lbrace {Z}_{k}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {r}_{k}\right\rbrace +{\gamma}^{k}.\left\lbrace {Z}_{k-1}\right\rbrace$

> Linear search $\left\lbrace \left\lbrace \delta {u}_{k+1}\right\rbrace ,\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace \right\rbrace =\mathit{RechLine}(\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace ,\left\lbrace r\right\rbrace ,\left\lbrace {r}^{p}\right\rbrace )$

> ${B}_{k}$         Loop $k=k+1$
> $F$               END

One can make the following comments on the algorithm:

- During the linear search, the solution of the system $\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta a\right\rbrace ={\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace$ and the computation of the term $\langle \delta a\rangle .{\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace$, one perceives that the algorithm presented is the iterative version of the active-constraint method. Indeed, if one makes explicit the term $\langle \delta a\rangle .{\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace$, one obtains $\langle Z\rangle .(\left[{A}_{\text{c}}\right].{\left[K\right]}^{-1}.{\left[{A}_{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}).\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace$. One recovers the Schur complement ${\left[K\right]}_{\text{schur}}=\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].{\left[K\right]}^{-1}.{\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}$ which is explicitly built in the active-constraint method. As in all iterative methods, the Projected Conjugate Gradient does not build this operator but uses its effect on a vector (here on $\left\lbrace Z\right\rbrace$);
- In order to be truly effective, this algorithm must be used with a direct solver or an iterative solver preconditioned by the LDLT_SP method.

  - With a direct solver, the matrix of the system being already factorised, each solution reduces to a forward-back substitution:
  - With an iterative solver preconditioned by the LDLT_SP method, each solution reduces to a few iterations of the iterative solver.
- One conjugates at each iteration.

### 5.3 The penalised method in contact –ALGO_CONT='PENALISATION'

The penalised method is particularly appropriate when the matrix $\left[K\right]$ is non-symmetric. Nevertheless, it requires a sensitivity study with respect to the penalisation coefficient (of the dimension of a ratio of a force to a displacement) and, at a minimum, a check on the results that contact is correctly taken into account (the structures do not interpenetrate).

#### 5.3.1 Equilibrium of the structure in the presence of contact

One recalls that the equilibrium equation in the presence of contact is written:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}({u}_{i})\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace
$$

In the case of penalised contact, the contact force is written:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace ={\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace }_{\text{regu}}={E}_{N}.[{A}^{\text{c}}{]}^{T}.(\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace )
$$

After linearisation of the equilibrium equation, one introduces the tangent matrix $\left[{K}^{\text{m},n-1}\right]$ which will contain the contributions coming from the linearisation of the internal and external forces and the matrix $\left[{K}^{\text{c},n-1}\right]$ for the contact forces:

$$
[{K}_{i}^{\text{c},n-1}]={E}_{N}.[{A}^{\text{c},n-1}{]}^{T}.[{A}^{\text{c},n-1}]
$$

The right-hand side being equal to:

$$
\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace =-{E}_{N.}[{A}^{\text{c},n-1}{]}^{T.}\left\lbrace {d}^{\text{c},n-1}\right\rbrace
$$

Concerning the algorithm, we have seen in paragraph r5.03.50-cas-regularisation that it acts as a correction of the equilibrium problem without contact.

When we are in the contact algorithm, at the Newton iteration $n$, we evaluate $\left[{K}_{i}^{\text{c},n+1}\right]$ and $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace$, which will be used only at the following Newton iteration. There is a shift of one iteration: one necessarily needs at least two Newton iterations in order to solve a problem with penalised contact.

#### 5.3.2 Algorithm

| $\mathit{Ini}$ | Computation of ${⌊\left\lbrace {\tilde{d}}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{l}}$ |
| --- | --- |
|  | Evaluation of ${\Xi}^{\text{c}}=\left\lbrace J\in {\Xi}^{l}\mid {⌊\left\lbrace {\tilde{d}}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{J}<0\right\rbrace$ and $\left[{A}^{\text{c},n}\right]$ |
|  | Contact pressures ${⌊\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{\text{c}}}=-{E}_{N}.{⌊\left\lbrace {\tilde{d}}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{\text{c}}}$ |
|  | Right-hand side $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace ={\left[{A}^{\text{c},n}\right]}^{T}.{⌊\left\lbrace {\mu}^{\text{c},n}\right\rbrace ⌋}_{{\Xi}^{\text{c}}}$ |
|  | Matrix $\left[{K}^{\text{c},n+1}\right]={E}_{N}.{\left[{A}^{\text{c},n}\right]}^{T}.\left[{A}^{\text{c},n}\right]$ |

**Notes:**

- The quantities $\left[{K}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right]$ and $\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{c}}\right\rbrace$ are evaluated only for the active links. For the rest of the unknowns, one initialises to zero;
- The modification of the system does not introduce any new variables with respect to the problem without contact;
- There is no index $k$ in the various quantities (in particular the set of active links or the contact matrix) because this is not an iterative algorithm;
- For the computation of the matrix, one proceeds in two stages: evaluations of the elementary matrices, then assembly. But these two operations are specific to discrete contact; one does not use the generic elementary-computations/assembly mechanism of code_aster.
- The choice of the penalisation coefficient is crucial: too small, interpenetrations will be observed; too large, the conditioning of the tangent matrix deteriorates.

One denotes by ${⌊⌋}_{\square}$ the operations which one carries out on a subset of links such as defined in paragraph r5.03.50-liaisons-frottement-sans.

## 6 Algorithmic solution – Contact with friction

### 6.1 Friction links

One adds two sets with respect to those defined in paragraph r5.03.50-liaisons-frottement-sans in order to take into account the friction status of a slave node:

- ${\Xi}^{\text{a}}$ is the set of the sticking contact nodes;
- ${\Xi}^{\text{g}}$ is the set of the sliding contact nodes.

One therefore has the following relations between the sets:

- ${\Xi}^{\text{c}}={\Xi}^{\text{a}}\oplus {\Xi}^{\text{g}}$ because the nodes in contact are either sliding or sticking;
- ${\Xi}^{\text{a}}\mathrm{\subseteq }{\Xi}^{\text{c}}$ and ${\Xi}^{\text{g}}\mathrm{\subseteq }{\Xi}^{\text{c}}$ because only the nodes in contact can be sticking or sliding.

### 6.2 Kinematics and tangential gap

We have seen in paragraph r5.03.50-cinematique that friction uses kinematic quantities analogous to the case of contact alone, by introducing the notion of “tangential” gap $\left\lbrace {\tilde{g}}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace$ (relative sliding of the surfaces in the case of non-adhesion); this tangential gap is defined by:

$$
\left\lbrace {\tilde{g}}^{\text{t},n}\right\rbrace =\left[{A}^{\text{f}}\right].(\left\lbrace \Delta {u}_{i}^{n-1}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace )
$$

One uses the friction matrix $\left[{A}^{\text{f}}\right]$ (see r5.03.50-definition-matrice-frottement). The use of the quantity $\left\lbrace \delta {\tilde{u}}^{n}\right\rbrace$ in place of $\left\lbrace \delta {u}^{n}\right\rbrace$ is justified by the fact that one solves a Tresca problem, therefore without modifying the gap during the process of solution of the sliding problem (see r5.03.50-modelisation-glissement).

**Note:**

- In the algorithms presented in the remainder of the document, one generally does not distinguish between the sliding part and the sticking part of the nodes at the level of the writings, in order to lighten the notation.

## 7 Theoretical convergence results

For the problems without friction, one will find in  a proof of convergence for the active-constraint method.

For the problems with friction, convergence results with uniqueness of the solution for the discretised problem are established in  for small values of the Coulomb friction coefficient. The results are obtained by using a fixed-point algorithm associated with a Lagrange-multiplier method. For each contact problem solved, one studies the associated friction problem. Once the latter has been solved, one solves a new contact problem, and so on. These methods are however different from those presented here and one cannot therefore present theoretical convergence results for the latter.

### 7.1 Subdivision of the time step

On the theoretical level, the convergence of the active-constraint method is ensured in a finite number of iterations. In practice, certain numerical artefacts can make this convergence delicate. A strategy has therefore been developed in order to ensure the robustness of the algorithm.

During contact computations, in particular if the load steps carried out are too large, undesirable phenomena can appear:

- The contact matrix is singular,
- Oscillation of the active-constraint method: a node is detected alternately “stuck” then “separated”.

In order to overcome these difficulties, the following strategy has been adopted. If:

- The contact matrix is singular,
- The number of active-constraint iterations is greater than a limit which depends on the number of potential links. This number is fixed at twice the total number of slave nodes for the active-constraint method, and at ITER_CONT_MULT times the total number of slave nodes for the other methods.

Then one subdivides the time step, *i.e.* one returns to the previous load step and, instead of trying to reach the following loading level in one step as one has just done, one does several of them (For more details on this functionality of the operator STAT_NON_LINE, see the documentation [U4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03)).

### 7.2 Compatibility with the Dirichlet boundary conditions

In the case of the methods with Lagrange multipliers, one can observe incompatibilities with the fact of imposing boundary conditions of Dirichlet type. Indeed, the problem must make sense physically. One cannot treat a contact problem in the direction of the axis $z$ if all the points have a zero displacement along $z$. As we are going to see, treating such a problem leads to a singularity of the matrices of the type $\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].{\left[K\right]}^{-1}.{\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}$ with the treatment of the Dirichlet boundary conditions by double Lagrange multiplier of *code_aster*.

#### 7.2.1 Writing of the boundary conditions

Taking inspiration from the reference documentation [r5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01) of STAT_NON_LINE, the dualisation of the Dirichlet boundary conditions leads to the following system of equations to be solved:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\left[K\right].\left\lbrace \delta u\right\rbrace +{\left[B\right]}^{T}.\left\lbrace \delta \lambda \right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{int}\right\rbrace -\left\lbrace {L}_{i}^{\text{ext}}\right\rbrace \\ \left[B\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{i}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{d}\right\rbrace -\left[B\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{i-1}\right\rbrace \end{array}
$$

One then denotes $\left[C\right]$ the stiffness matrix of the system such that:

$$
\left[C\right]=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\left[K\right]& {\left[B\right]}^{T}\\ \left[B\right]& \left[0\right]\end{array}\right]
$$

This matrix possesses an inverse of the form:

$$
{\left[C\right]}^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\left[E\right]& \left[F\right]\\ {\left[F\right]}^{T}& \left[G\right]\end{array}\right]
$$

such that: $\left[E\right].{\left[B\right]}^{T}=\left[0\right]$. One thus checks that for each boundary condition $l$ one has the property $\left[E\right].{\left[{B}_{l}\right]}^{T}=\left[0\right]$.

#### 7.2.2 Return to the contact problem

The matrix $\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].{\left[K\right]}^{-1}.{\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}$ can also be written $\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left[E\right].{\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}$ since the link vectors $\left[A\right]$ involve only the displacement degrees of freedom.

- It then follows that if a link vector $J$ of the matrix $\left[A\right]$ is a linear combination of the boundary conditions of Dirichlet type it satisfies the following property: $\left[E\right].{\left[{A}_{J}\right]}^{T}=\left[0\right]$. The matrix $\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left[E\right].{\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}$ is then singular because it possesses a column of zeros. In practice, without particular treatment, one ends up in the code on a stop message of the type stop on a singular contact-friction matrix. The detection of these singular columns has been implemented in the code so as to eliminate from the contact-friction relations this type of relation and to avoid the stop described previously.
- It then follows that if a link vector $J$ of the matrix $\left[A\right]$ contains a linear combination of the boundary conditions of Dirichlet type and is written $\left[{A}_{J}\right]=\sum{\alpha}_{i}.\left[{B}_{i}\right]+\left[{\stackrel{ˉ}{A}}_{J}\right]$, it satisfies the following property: $\left[E\right].{\left[{A}_{J}\right]}^{T}=\left[E\right].{\left[\stackrel{ˉ}{{A}_{J}}\right]}^{T}$. One can then have a matrix $\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right].\left[E\right].{\left[{A}^{\text{c}}\right]}^{T}$ which is singular because it possesses two identical rows. This detection is for the moment not available in the code and one ends up in the code on a stop message of the type stop on a singular contact-friction matrix.

**Note:**

*This problem of compatibility between contact-friction and the boundary conditions does not appear with the regularised methods, inasmuch as one adds stiffness to the global stiffness and one does not carry out an elimination as in the computation of the Lagrange multipliers.*

## 8 Conclusion

Discrete modellings of contact-friction with 1D and 2D sliding surfaces have been implemented in *code_aster*. These modellings, usable with STAT_NON_LINE and DYNA_NON_LINE, are accessible under DEFI_CONTACT.

The modellings proposed rely on the meshes of the surfaces coming into contact and make it possible to transcribe, node to node, the frictional-contact conditions between the surfaces after discretisation of the corresponding variational formulation. The method then extends without difficulty from small displacements to the case of large displacements. Indeed, the absence of the use of finite elements, between the surfaces which may come into contact, avoids the large distortion of the latter, in the case of large displacements. One can then use either direct node-to-node link conditions for meshes which are initially compatible, or node-to-node link conditions weighted according to a projection approach of master-slave type for incompatible meshes.

In the case of 1D sliding surfaces one has been able to develop an algorithm using only Lagrange multipliers. The finite convergence of this type of algorithm is proved for unilateral contact without friction and, in the case with friction, for small values of the Coulomb friction coefficient. In the case of 2D sliding surfaces, frictional contact is treated by regularisation.
