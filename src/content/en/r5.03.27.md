# r5.03.27 Mechanical behaviours for numerical simulations

**Summary:**

The behaviour of the material is an essential input datum in most mechanical studies. In a numerical simulation this behaviour is taken into account by means of a model of greater or lesser complexity, assumed to reproduce sufficiently accurately the behaviour of the real material. The parameters controlling the model will have been adjusted beforehand from experimental data.

Since every model is a simplified and inexact representation of reality, it is essential to ensure that the choices of formulation, and the set of parameters chosen, are relevant for the whole domain of loadings characterising the study.

As soon as one leaves the simple cases in which a linear elastic behaviour or a plastic behaviour with isotropic hardening is sufficient, the choice of a constitutive model and its calibration for a given material is a long and delicate process, which requires relevant experimental data that are not always easy to collect.

This document is complementary to [U2.04.03](doc:u2.04.03), which gives advice to a user wishing to carry out analyses with non-linear behaviours of elastoplastic or elastoviscoplastic type, in order to choose a law suited to the modellings envisaged.

## 1 Some reminders on mechanical behaviour

In mechanics, the term “constitutive law” may be used to group the models which govern the relation between the local states of stress and of strain, possibly taking previous states into account.

Before discussing constitutive laws, it seems useful to recall briefly the main phenomena that may be encountered in terms of the behaviour of materials, with particular emphasis on the behaviour of metallic materials.

In order to go further into the subject, the reader may refer to various reference works in the field, notably [bib2] and [bib3].

### 1.1 Elasticity and plasticity

The simplest constitutive law is linear elasticity, which corresponds to the ability of the material to recover its original shape when the loading is removed. The constitutive law which represents linear elasticity corresponds to a relation of proportionality between the stresses and the strains. For a large number of materials, elasticity may be considered as isotropic (the coefficient of proportionality is the same whatever the direction of loading). Certain materials (single crystals, textured materials), however, present elastic characteristics which differ according to the direction of loading.

Certain materials, such as elastomers, possess a non-linear elastic behaviour. In this case, the stresses and the strains are no longer related by a relation of proportionality; the material nevertheless recovers its initial shape when it is not loaded.

For most materials (including metallic materials) a linear elastic behaviour is observed for moderate loadings. When the loading continues to be increased, an irreversible plastic strain is progressively created and the linear relation between stress and strain is lost.

### 1.2 Influence of temperature

When the same material is loaded mechanically at different temperature levels, an evolution of its response is observed both in the elastic domain and in the plastic domain. Generally, when the temperature increases, the total strain observed is larger for the same stress level.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.27/1000020100000147000000F1F352FE77393B55F2.png)

Figure 1.2-1: Influence of temperature

### 1.3 Viscosity

If the same material is loaded at different loading rates, different mechanical responses are generally obtained in the plastic domain. Sensitivity to the loading rate reflects the viscous character of the material.

Viscosity manifests itself in different ways according to the type of loading applied. With a monotonically increasing loading, one will be interested in the hardening modulus as a function of the loading rate. A strain step may also be applied to the material, and the progressive decrease of the stress at constant strain then observed. This is referred to as relaxation. A stress step may also be imposed, and a progressive increase of the strain at constant stress observed. This is referred to as creep.

It will be noted that viscosity is generally a thermally activated mechanism. It will be all the more significant when the material is at a high temperature. More complex temperature dependencies may sometimes be found. This is for example the case of austenitic stainless steels, which present a significant viscosity at room temperature. This viscosity decreases as the temperature rises and becomes practically zero around $350°C$, then it reappears and grows rapidly beyond $450°C$.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.27/100002010000037A000000F13BC7FD289B6FE75C.png)

Figure 1.3-1: Manifestation of viscosity according to the nature of the loading

### 1.4 Cyclic behaviour

The phenomena described so far are observable with monotonic loadings. When the applied loading is cyclic and the level of applied loading is sufficient to produce a plastic strain of the material, an evolution of the mechanical response is observed over the cycles. Depending on the material considered, this evolution may be of different kinds.

Under a cyclic loading with a constant stress amplitude, an accumulation of strain is observed during the first cycles. If the stress amplitude is sufficiently high, strain continues to be accumulated at each cycle. This is referred to as progressive deformation, which may lead to rapid failure of the material.

If the applied stress amplitude is more moderate, a stabilisation of the cyclic response is observed. Depending on the material (and the loading amplitude), the stabilisation may be of two types. If the stabilised cycle exhibits plasticity, one speaks of accommodation of the material. If the stabilised cycle is entirely elastic, one speaks of adaptation.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.27/10000200000002B800000127230E9D973310169E.png)

Figure 1.4-1: Stabilisation of the cyclic response of the material

Under cyclic loading, an evolution of the envelope of the loading path is generally observed over the cycles, as a result of an accumulation of hardening. Materials may be classified into two categories:

1. Softening materials, when the stress amplitude decreases for a loading at constant strain amplitude, or when the strain amplitude increases for a loading at constant stress amplitude.
2. Hardening materials, when the stress amplitude increases over the cycles for a loading at constant strain amplitude, or whose strain amplitude decreases for a loading at constant stress amplitude.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.27/10000201000003020000027B315A22C7F7635D89.png)

Figure 1.4-2: Cyclic hardening and softening

When the loading amplitude is constant, the phenomena of hardening or softening tend to die away with the accumulation of cycles, until a stabilisation of the response of the material is reached.

If the material exhibits viscosity, the response of the material to a cyclic loading will depend on the loading rate.

### 1.5 Static recovery

Certain materials also present a sensitivity to time in the case of a loading with plastification followed by an unloading and then a reloading. If the material is left at rest for some time before the second loading is applied, a recovery phenomenon is sometimes observed.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.27/10000201000001C0000000E7E988D3CDFDE12523.png)

Figure 1.5-1: The recovery phenomenon

If the reloading is carried out immediately after the unloading, an elastic behaviour is observed until the maximum stress level before unloading is reached, and the response curve of the material then continues the initial curve before unloading.

If the material is sensitive to recovery, and if the material is left at rest before being reloaded, a response of the material is observed which will be intermediate between the initial response of the material and the response obtained on immediate reloading. It is thus found that the material at rest progressively loses the memory of the hardening accumulated during the first loading.

### 1.6 Memory effect

On certain metallic materials, and more particularly alloys with an $\mathit{C.F.C.}$ (face-centred cubic) crystal structure, it is observed that the cyclic behaviour retains the memory of the maximum strain level reached during the life of the material.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.27/10000201000001800000013B573D61979082C108.png)

Figure 1.6-1: The hardening-memory effect

Figure 1.6-1 describes the evolution of the stress amplitude as a function of the strain amplitude for a large number of cyclic tests on the same material. When an initially virgin material is loaded, the cyclic consolidation curve marked on the diagram is obtained. If the material is first hardened up to a relatively high strain level and is then cycled with a lower loading, the response corresponds to a “harder” material, as shown by the two curves A and B corresponding to different levels of pre-hardening.

### 1.7 Additional hardening

The mechanical response of the material may sometimes be very different according to whether the loading is uniaxial or non-proportional multiaxial. One then speaks of multiaxial additional hardening. This phenomenon is illustrated by the following two figures. A 316L steel specimen is subjected to a combined loading of alternating tension and torsion (controlled in strain amplitude) with a phase shift of 90° between the two loadings.

The stress levels reached (represented by the red points on the right-hand part of the figure) are about twice as high as those which would be reached on a specimen loaded with a radial or uniaxial loading of equivalent strain amplitude.

### 1.8 Isotropic and kinematic hardening

When the loading exceeds the yield limit of the material, it has been seen above that plastification is accompanied by a hardening which modifies the subsequent response of the material in the event of unloading or of alternating loading.

If one places oneself in stress space, a threshold surface may be defined inside which the material remains elastic. When the loading path reaches this surface, plastic strain appears and the threshold surface adapts in such a way that the loading point remains on the surface as long as there is no unloading. Adaptation of the yield surface may take place in two distinct modes:

If the surface expands while remaining centred on the origin (which corresponds to a state of zero stress), one speaks of isotropic hardening (see Figure 1.8-1). Isotropic hardening therefore corresponds to a variation in the size of the elastic domain.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.27/10000201000002B30000014B1E8F37120626DCA7.png)

Figure 1.8-1: Isotropic hardening

For a tensile loading followed by an unloading and then a compression, the material will retain an elastic behaviour up to a compressive level equivalent to the maximum loading reached in tension.

If the yield surface moves so as to follow the loading, one speaks of kinematic hardening (see Figure 1.8-2). The elastic domain retains a constant size and translates so as to follow the loading. In the case of an alternating tension–compression loading, the elastic domain will correspond to twice the initial yield limit (in the absence of residual stress in the virgin material).

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.27/1000020100000345000001931800ECECB7C3DACA.png)

Figure 1.8-2: Kinematic hardening

The behaviour observed on real materials generally corresponds to a combination of kinematic and isotropic hardening.

## 2 Taking mechanical behaviour into account in numerical simulations

In many cases an isotropic elastic constitutive law is sufficient, for example for a sizing study in which one is generally content to check that the maximum stress does not exceed a threshold value (yield limit, fatigue endurance threshold, etc.). The only behaviour parameters to be defined are Young's modulus $E$ and Poisson's ratio $\nu$.

For anisothermal studies, it is sufficient to replace the scalar parameters $E$ and $\nu$ by functions tabulated in temperature.

When it is necessary to take the plastic behaviour of the material into account, the simplest solution is to use directly an experimental uniaxial tensile curve which will have been tabulated beforehand in the form of an evolution of the stress as a function of the strain. In the Code_Aster analysis code, formulations such as VMIS_ISOT_TRAC make it possible, during the solution, to generalise the uniaxial curve to multiaxial loading paths.

This type of model has the advantage of a very simple implementation, in so far as one is dispensed from any step of identification of the parameters of a constitutive law, since an experimental tensile curve is used directly. It must however be borne in mind that these models implicitly incorporate the assumption of a purely isotropic behaviour of the material.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.27/1000020100000132000000F4A4DEFC2459E2C68B.png)

Figure 2-1: Use of a tensile curve as a constitutive model

Although real materials generally present a share of kinematic hardening, the approximation by a purely isotropic behaviour is not necessarily penalising, provided that the loading path at every point of the structure under study satisfies certain conditions specified below.

In order to bring out the limits of the isotropic-behaviour assumption, the limiting case is considered of a material whose real hardening behaviour is purely kinematic, and a material point subjected to an increasing then decreasing uniaxial loading is considered. As long as the loading path at every point of the structure remains monotonic, the isotropy assumption has no effect. If a moderate local unloading is obtained, the model remains relevant. However, if the unloading becomes more pronounced, the model will predict an elastic response up to a compressive stress equivalent to the maximum tensile stress reached previously (the response of the model is represented by a solid curve on the figure). The real response of the material is represented by a dashed line. If the real material is purely kinematic, it is found that the onset of plasticity in compression occurs much earlier than predicted by the isotropic constitutive model, and that the discrepancy between the computed stress and the real stress may be significant.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.27/10000201000001C4000001DB47411927D0E44D63.png)

Figure 2-2: Discrepancy between isotropic behaviour and real behaviour

The illustration presented here corresponds to a very unfavourable situation, notably because a material is assumed whose real behaviour is purely kinematic, but it is important, in any study using this type of behaviour, to be vigilant with respect to this type of error. It must also be emphasised that a small decrease in the global loading may result in a much larger unloading at certain points of the structure. It is therefore desirable to carry out a check on the whole stress field throughout the simulation.

Anisothermal simulations are relatively simple with this type of data setup. Several tensile curves corresponding to different temperatures are supplied to the analysis code, and the code takes care of the interpolation in temperature between the various curves.

If the study to be carried out requires account to be taken of a behaviour more complex than those addressed at the beginning of this chapter, it is necessary to use a constitutive law based on a mathematical formulation (generally in the form of a differential equation).

Several steps are then necessary before carrying out the numerical simulation proper:

1. The choice of the formulation must be made consistently with the physical phenomena that one wishes to take into account.
2. The experimental data relevant to the calibration of the model are collected, with the aim of covering as well as possible the domain of loading undergone by the structure (supplementing them with additional tests if necessary).
3. The parameters of the model are then identified so that the numerical response is as close as possible to the experimental results.

It is common to avoid the whole identification process when one has a law and a set of parameters obtained in a comparable study on the same material, or taken from a thesis or a paper.

In that case, it is important to bear in mind that, whatever its complexity, a constitutive model offers only a simplified and therefore imperfect reproduction of the reaction of the material. It is usable only over a limited domain of mechanical loading, which generally corresponds to the range of loading covered by the experimental data used for its identification. The reuse of a pre-existing formulation and parameters may therefore prove risky if one does not also have precise information as to the domain of validity of the law that one wishes to use.

By way of illustration, figure 2-3 compares the experimental and simulated loading paths for cyclic loadings at different amplitude levels. The constitutive law used was identified over a range of total-strain amplitude lying between $\pm 0,2\text{\%}$ and $\pm 0,7\text{\%}$ (with tests at intermediate loadings of $\pm 0,3$ and $\pm 0,5\text{\%}$). This range of amplitude may therefore be considered *a priori* as the domain of validity of the law in terms of strain amplitude.

The least heavily loaded test was carried out at an amplitude of $\pm 0,15\text{\%}$. This level is close to the limit of the domain of validity, and the constitutive law appears relevant despite a slight extrapolation.

The test at $\pm 0,4\text{\%}$ lies within the domain of validity. The interpolation between the results at $\pm 0,3$ and $\pm 0,5\text{\%}$ used for the identification proves satisfactory.

For the two tests at $\pm 1\text{\%}$ and $\pm 1,2\text{\%}$, it is found that extrapolating the model towards loading amplitudes distinctly higher than the limit of the domain of validity leads to a significant underestimation of the simulated stress amplitude.

It will be retained that knowledge of (and compliance with) the domain of validity of the law implemented are essential ingredients for the quality of the result of a numerical simulation. In the absence of precise information on the domain of validity of a “recycled” law, it is strongly recommended to confront the model with a few well-chosen experimental results before using it in a simulation.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.27/1000020100000364000002757217BAEEBB29A65D.png)

Figure 2-4: Estimation of the robustness of a constitutive law in the event of extrapolation outside its domain of validity.

For anisothermal simulations, several strategies are possible.

The simplest consists in having an isothermal constitutive law for which sets of parameters are identified for several temperature levels. During the simulation, the analysis code will carry out, at each integration point, a linear interpolation in temperature of each parameter of the law, and will then integrate the behaviour on the basis of the interpolated parameters. This solution is entirely satisfactory, provided that one makes sure that the evolution of each parameter as a function of temperature is reasonably regular.

A more elegant alternative from the thermodynamic point of view consists in incorporating the effects of temperature directly into the formulation of the constitutive law. This approach may prove a little more complex, but it makes it possible in particular to propose a finer interpolation (and not necessarily a linear one) of the behaviour between the experimental data available at constant temperature.

In general, it is important, during an anisothermal simulation, to make sure that the temperature range covered does not include a change of deformation mechanism for the material considered (or a change of phase). If this proves to be the case, the change of mechanism must be taken into account in the constitutive model retained.

Other parameters may influence the mechanical behaviour of a material, such as the water content (for certain polymers or for concretes) or irradiation, whose effect on the behaviour at very high dose leads to swelling, an activation of creep and a hardening of the steel. The influence of these parameters is generally incorporated directly into the formulation of specific constitutive laws.
