# r3.07.04 Volumetric shell finite elements

**Summary:**

In order to complete the library of plane-plate finite elements [R3.07.03](doc:r3.07.03) currently available in *Code_Aster*  (DKT, DST, Q4G...), it is proposed to introduce two volumetric, or three-dimensional, shell finite elements [bib1]. This modelling COQUE_3D [U3.12.03](doc:u3.12.03) makes it possible to carry out analyses of shell structures of arbitrary shape with a better approximation of the geometry and of the kinematics.

One will restrict oneself to the framework of linear kinematics. One therefore remains in small displacements and small strains. No restriction is made on the type of constitutive behaviour in plane stress.

The two elements which are introduced are the 9-node quadratic Heterosis quadrilateral and its 7-node triangular counterpart. The formulation of the continuous problem is carried out in Cartesian coordinates, which makes it possible to avoid the explicit computation of the curvatures. These two elements have as their counterpart the 3-node line shell element presented in the document [R3.07.02](doc:r3.07.02).

These two new elements are validated on existing plate test cases, and on three new shell test cases developed in the validation documentation, the principal conclusions of which are presented briefly here.

This note also presents in an annex how to take the anisotropy of materials into account.

> **Contents**

## 1 Introduction

Two volumetric shell finite elements with transverse shear are introduced into *Code_Aster* (the 9-node quadrilateral MEC3QU9H and the 7-node triangle MEC3TR7H) for the analysis of shell structures of arbitrary shape. To represent this type of structure, one previously used with *Code_Aster* plane-facet plate elements, which induced parasitic bendings, and shells of revolution which were too restrictive as regards the type of structure [R3.07.02](doc:r3.07.02). The development was carried out for isotropic materials with linear kinematics, for small displacements and small strains. This formulation can be extended to anisotropic materials [Annex 1] and to non-linear kinematics (large displacements and large rotations) [R3.07.05](doc:r3.07.05).

For the solution of chained thermomechanical problems, one must beforehand use the 7- and 9-node thermal shell finite elements described in [R3.11.01](doc:r3.11.01).

The continuous mechanical problem is developed below by describing the shell kinematics of Hencky–Mindlin–Naghdi type (assumption of straight or plane sections) completed by a transverse distortion, and the thermo-elasto-plastic constitutive law. By means of a penalty parameter one can pass from a theory with shear to a theory without shear. The finite elements chosen are then presented; they are quadratic isoparametric elements making it possible to have a fine representation of a curved geometry and good estimates of the stresses. The interpolation and the integration method are also described.

The development is finally validated on a few benchmark cases.

The non-linear kinematics of these shells is treated in the reference documentation [R3.07.05](doc:r3.07.05).

## 2 Formulation

### 2.1 Geometry of the shell

For the volumetric shell elements $\Omega$ one defines a reference surface $\omega$ , or middle surface, which is skewed (with curvilinear coordinates ${\xi}_{1}{\xi}_{2}$ for example) and a thickness $h({\xi}_{1}{\xi}_{2})$ measured along the normal to the middle surface. This thickness must be small compared with the other dimensions (extents, radii of curvature) of the structure to be modelled. The figure [Figure 2.1-a] below illustrates the point.

![Figure](/figures/r3.07.04/Object_5.png)

**Figure 2.1-a**

The position of the points of the shell is given by the curvilinear coordinates $({\xi}_{1}{\xi}_{2})$ of the middle surface $\omega$ and the elevation ${\xi}_{3}$ with respect to this surface. $(O,{e}_{k})$ is the global Cartesian frame, with associated axes $({x}_{k})$ .

#### 2.1.1 Geometric description of the middle surface

**Local natural basis and local Cartesian basis**

Let $P$ be an arbitrary point of the reference middle surface $\omega$ ; one has:

$\text{OP}={x}_{k}^{0}({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2}){e}_{k}$

One defines the vectors ${a}_{\alpha}$ of the local natural basis of the plane tangent at $P$ to $\omega$ , attached to $P$ by:

${a}_{\alpha}=\frac{\partial \text{OP}}{\partial {\xi}_{\alpha}}={\text{OP}}_{,\alpha }$

and one defines the unit normal $n$ by:

$n=\frac{{a}_{1}\mathrm{\wedge }{a}_{2}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{a}_{1}\mathrm{\wedge }{a}_{2}\mathrm{\parallel }}$

${\xi}_{3}$ is the position variable through the thickness associated with $n$ .

$\left({a}_{1},{a}_{2},{a}_{3}\right)$ constitutes the natural basis attached to $P$ .

The system of curvilinear coordinates $({\xi}_{1}{\xi}_{2})$ not necessarily being orthogonal, the basis $({a}_{\alpha})$ is therefore not necessarily orthogonal (and even less orthonormal). One therefore defines a local orthonormal basis ${t}_{k}$ as follows:

${t}_{1}=\frac{{a}_{1}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{a}_{1}\mathrm{\parallel }},{t}_{2}=n\mathrm{\wedge }{t}_{1}{t}_{3}=n$

and one denotes by $\left({s}_{1},{s}_{2}\right)$ the coordinate system associated with $\left({t}_{1},{t}_{2}\right)$ .

**Intrinsic frame**

The local basis ${t}_{k}$ will serve to define the intrinsic frame of a shell element by choosing as point P the first vertex of the element and as vector ${a}_{1}$ a vector coinciding with the projection of the first side onto the plane tangent at the first vertex.

**Computation of the curvature tensor**

The curvature tensor is linked to the variation of the normal over $\omega$ . It is defined by its mixed components:

${n}_{,\beta }\text{=-}{C}_{\beta}^{\gamma}{a}_{\gamma}$

or again by its covariant components: ${C}_{\alpha \beta }\text{=-}{a}_{\alpha}.{n}_{,\beta }=n.{a}_{\alpha ,\beta }$ . This tensor is symmetric since ${a}_{\alpha ,\beta }={a}_{\beta ,\alpha }$ . Its trace $\text{tr}{C}_{\alpha \beta }$ is the mean curvature and its determinant the Gaussian curvature.

#### 2.1.2 Description of the geometry of the shell

Let $Q$ be an arbitrary point of $\Omega$ , the volume of the shell of thickness $h$ considered constant; one has:

$\text{OQ}+\text{OP}+\text{PQ}=\text{OP}+\frac{{\xi}_{3}}{2}hn$

where ${\xi}_{3}\in \left[-1,1\right]$ .

$({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2},\frac{{\xi}_{3}}{2}h)$ constitutes a system of curvilinear coordinates of $\Omega$ .

One can also write $\text{OQ}$ as a function of its components $({x}_{k})$ in the global basis $({e}_{k})$ :

$\text{OQ}={x}_{k}{e}_{k}$

**Local natural basis, local orthonormal basis and metric tensor**

As for $P$ , one defines the natural basis of the 3D space $({g}_{k})$ attached to $Q$ by:

${g}_{\alpha}=\frac{\partial \text{OQ}}{\partial {\xi}_{\alpha}}={a}_{\alpha}+{\xi}_{3}\frac{h}{2}{n}_{,\alpha },{g}_{3}=\frac{{g}_{1}\mathrm{\wedge }{g}_{2}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{g}_{1}\mathrm{\wedge }{g}_{2}\mathrm{\parallel }}=n$

As $({g}_{k})$ is not necessarily orthogonal, one defines a local orthonormal basis $({T}_{k})$ as follows:

${T}_{1}=\frac{{g}_{1}}{\mathrm{\parallel }{g}_{1}\mathrm{\parallel }},{T}_{2}=n\mathrm{\wedge }{T}_{1},{T}_{3}=n$

and one denotes by $({x}_{k})$ the coordinate system associated with $({T}_{k})$ .

One will call $({T}_{k})$ the local orthonormal basis, and $({x}_{k})$ the coordinates in this local orthonormal basis.

By definition, one has:

${T}_{k}=\frac{\partial \text{OQ}}{\partial {\tilde{x}}_{k}}=\frac{\partial {x}_{j}}{\partial {\tilde{x}}_{k}}{e}_{j}={T}_{k}^{j}{e}_{j}$

with $\frac{\partial {x}_{j}}{\partial {\tilde{x}}_{k}}={T}_{k}^{j}$ the components of $({T}_{k})$ in the global basis $({e}_{j})$ . (They are also the components of the transformation matrix from $({T}_{k})$ to $({e}_{j})$ since the transformation matrix is orthogonal. Thus if ${T}_{k}={T}_{k}^{j}{e}_{j}$ one also has ${e}_{k}={T}_{j}^{k}{T}_{j}$ ).

One defines the metric tensor $G$ associated with $Q$ by its components deduced from the scalar products of the vectors of the local orthonormal basis:

${G}_{ij}={T}_{i}.{T}_{j}$

This tensor $G$ is equal to the identity $\text{Id}$ .

### 2.2 Theory of plates and shells

These elements rest on the theory of plates and shells, according to which:

#### 2.2.1 Kinematics

##### 2.2.1.1 Displacement field

The straight sections, which are the sections perpendicular to the middle surface, remain straight; the material points situated on a normal to the undeformed middle surface remain on a straight line in the deformed configuration. It follows from this approach that **the displacement fields vary linearly through the thickness of the shell** .

If one denotes by $Q'$ the position of $Q$ after deformation, one has:

$\text{OQ}'=\text{OQ}+\text{QQ}'=\text{OQ}+U(Q)$

where the displacement field chosen, corresponding to the Hencky–Mindlin kinematics, is written:

$U(Q)=u(P)+\frac{{\xi}_{3}}{2}h\beta (P)$ with $\beta (P).n=0$

where $u(P)$ and $\beta (P)$ are respectively the displacement vector and the rotation vector of $P$ , the projection of $Q$ onto the middle surface of the shell. The fact that $\beta (P).n=0$ indicates that one does not take into account, in this kinematics, the rotations of the shell about its normal.

**Notation:**

One denotes by $\stackrel{}{˜}$ the quantities expressed in the local Cartesian bases $({t}_{k})$ or $({T}_{k})$ for the points $P$ and $Q$ respectively. It follows that:

- the three-dimensional displacement vector $U$ can be written $U={\tilde{U}}_{k}{T}_{k}$ or again $U={U}_{k}{e}_{k}$ , where it is expressed respectively in its local orthonormal basis or in the global Cartesian basis,
- the displacement vector of the middle surface $u$ can be written $u={\tilde{u}}_{k}{t}_{k}$ or else $u={u}_{k}{e}_{k}$ according as it is expressed in its local orthonormal basis or in the global Cartesian basis,
- the rotation vector of the middle surface is written $b={\tilde{\beta}}_{\alpha}{t}_{\alpha}$ in its local orthonormal basis. $\beta$ being the rotation of the normal $\mathrm{n}$ (to the middle surface), one also writes $\beta =\theta \mathrm{\wedge }n$ with $\theta$ , the rotation vector of the middle surface, such that $\theta ={\tilde{\theta}}_{\alpha}{t}_{\alpha}$ . The equivalence of the two formulations shows that ${\tilde{\beta}}_{1}={\tilde{\theta}}_{2},{\tilde{\beta}}_{2}=-{\tilde{\theta}}_{1}$ .

##### 2.2.1.2 Expression of the three-dimensional strains

The strain tensor is computed in the local orthonormal Cartesian basis $({T}_{k})$ . It is defined as half the difference of the metric tensors associated with the local orthonormal bases after and before deformation. The metric tensor associated with this basis in the undeformed state is simply the identity $\text{Id}$ , whereas the metric tensor of the deformed state is ${\overline{G}}_{ij}={T}_{i}'.T{'}_{j}$ with $T{'}_{k}=\frac{\partial \text{OQ}'}{\partial {\tilde{x}}_{k}}$ .

The components of the strain tensor in $({T}_{k})$ are thus given by:

$\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{e}}_{\text{αβ}}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\P {\tilde{U}}_{\alpha}}{\P {\tilde{x}}_{\beta}}+\frac{\P {\tilde{U}}_{\beta}}{\P {\tilde{x}}_{\alpha}})\\ {\tilde{e}}_{\mathit{\alpha 3}}=\frac{1}{2}(\frac{\P {\tilde{U}}_{\alpha}}{\P {\tilde{x}}_{3}}+\frac{\P {\tilde{U}}_{3}}{\P {\tilde{x}}_{\alpha}})\end{array}$

The equations above are linear strain–displacement relations. The displacement variables are the components ${\tilde{U}}_{k}$ .

The components ${\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{kl}$ of the tensor $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ can also be expressed as a function of the components in the global frame $\frac{\partial {U}_{p}}{\partial {x}_{m}}$ . Indeed, as in the global frame $\varepsilon ={\varepsilon}_{ij}{e}^{i}\times {e}^{j}={\varepsilon}_{ij}{T}_{k}^{i}{T}_{l}^{j}{T}^{k}\times {T}^{l}={\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{kl}{T}^{k}\times {T}^{l}$ one therefore deduces immediately that: ${\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{kl}={\varepsilon}_{ij}{T}_{k}^{i}{T}_{l}^{j}$ . $({e}^{k})$ and $({T}^{k})$ are the contravariant bases associated with $({e}_{k})$ and $({T}_{k})$ respectively, such that: ${e}^{i}.{e}_{j}={\delta}_{ij}$ and . ${T}^{i}.{T}_{j}={\delta}_{ij}$  As the bases $({e}_{k})$ and $({T}_{k})$ are orthonormal, their associated contravariant bases coincide with themselves. Thus, in the same way as one had ${T}_{k}={T}_{k}^{j}{e}_{j}$ one recovers ${T}^{k}={T}_{k}^{j}{e}^{j}$ .

If one denotes $T={T}_{k}^{i}{e}_{i}\times {T}^{k}$ then $T\times T:\varepsilon ={\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{kl}{T}^{k}\times {T}^{l}=\tilde{\varepsilon}$ . In what follows one denotes by $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ the expression of the strain tensor in the local orthonormal frame and by $\varepsilon$ the expression of the same tensor in the global frame. The transformation relation from one to the other is given above in terms of tensors.

**Notes:**

*The terms* ${T}_{k}^{j}$ *contain the curvature terms of the shell* $\Omega$ *.*

*One notes in the strain–displacement relations that the component* ${\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{33}$ *is not determined by the kinematics. This is to be associated with the assumption of vanishing of the transverse normal stresses* ${\tilde{\sigma}}_{33}=0$ *justified by the behaviour of shells.*

In the literature (see for example [bib3]), the modelling of shells by the approach based on the curvilinear components ${\tilde{u}}_{k}$ of the displacement makes the curvature quantities appear explicitly at the level of the expression of the strain tensor [bib5]. As, in general, the geometry of the shell is not known explicitly, one must therefore determine numerically the geometric characteristics that are the vectors ${a}_{\alpha},{g}_{\alpha}$ *, ... and the curvatures* ${C}_{\alpha \beta }$ . With the finite-element method it is necessary to differentiate the shape functions twice (see page 20 of [bib5] and [R3.07.02](doc:r3.07.02)) in order to compute the ${C}_{\alpha \beta }$ *. This can make their computation inaccurate depending on the family of shape functions chosen. The error committed depends on the latter (linear, quadratic, cubic polynomials....) and becomes independent of the refinement of the mesh. A formulation involving the first derivative of the shape functions (computation of slopes) does not present this drawback. Thus the error consequent upon the computations of the curvature terms in a formulation based on the curvilinear approach does not decrease with the refinement of the mesh, whereas for the formulation described above it becomes small as the number of finite elements is increased. In view of the preceding observations, the so-called curvilinear approach has not been followed.*

#### 2.2.2 Constitutive law

The constitutive behaviour of the shells is a 3D behaviour in “plane stress”. It links the components of the stresses and of the strains, in the form of vectors, in the local orthonormal basis. The transverse stress ${\tilde{\sigma}}_{33}$ is zero because it is considered negligible compared with the other components of the stress tensor (plane-stress assumption). The most general constitutive law is then written as follows:

$(\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{11}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{22}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{12}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{13}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{23}\end{array})=\tilde{C}(\varepsilon ,\mu )(\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{11}-{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{11}^{\text{th}}\\ {\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{22}-{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{22}^{\text{th}}\\ {\tilde{\gamma}}_{12}\\ {\tilde{\gamma}}_{1}\\ {\tilde{\gamma}}_{2}\end{array})$

where $\tilde{C}(\varepsilon ,\mu )$ is the local constitutive matrix in plane stress and $\mu$ represents the set of internal variables when the behaviour is non-linear.

For behaviours in which the transverse distortions are uncoupled from the membrane and bending strains, $\tilde{C}(\varepsilon ,\mu )$ takes the form:

$\tilde{C}=(\begin{array}{cc}\tilde{H}& 0\\ 0& {\tilde{H}}_{\gamma}\end{array})$

where $\tilde{H}(\varepsilon ,\mu )$ is a $3\times 3$ membrane–bending constitutive matrix and ${\tilde{H}}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon ,\mu )$ a $2\times 2$ transverse-distortion constitutive matrix. The two phenomena being uncoupled, one can also write the behaviour in the form:

$(\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{\text{mf}}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{g}\end{array})=\tilde{C}(\varepsilon ,\mu )(\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\text{mf}}\\ \tilde{\gamma}\end{array})$ with:

${\tilde{\sigma}}_{\text{mf}}=(\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{11}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{22}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{12}\end{array})=\tilde{H}(\varepsilon ,\mu )(\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{11}-{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{11}^{\text{th}}\\ {\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{22}-{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{22}^{\text{th}}\\ {\tilde{\gamma}}_{12}\end{array})=\tilde{H}(\varepsilon ,\mu ){\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\text{mf}}$ and ${\tilde{\sigma}}_{\gamma}=(\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{13}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{23}\end{array})={\tilde{H}}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon ,\mu )(\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{\gamma}}_{1}\\ {\tilde{\gamma}}_{2}\end{array})={\tilde{H}}_{\gamma}(\varepsilon ,\mu )\tilde{\gamma}$

One will henceforth remain within the framework of this assumption.

For a homogeneous isotropic linear elastic behaviour, one thus has:

$\tilde{C}=\frac{E}{1-{\nu}^{2}}(\begin{array}{ccccc}1& \nu & 0& 0& 0\\ \nu & 1& 0& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& \frac{1-\nu }{2}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0& \frac{k(1-\nu )}{2}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0& 0& \frac{k(1-\nu )}{2}\end{array})$

where $k$ is the transverse-shear correction factor, the meaning of which is given in the reference documentation of the plate elements [R3.07.03](doc:r3.07.03), and [bib4] for more details. This coefficient is equal to $5/6$ for a theory of Reissner type and to $1$ within the framework of the Hencky–Mindlin theory. Finally, if one chooses $k$ very large, one recovers a theory of Love–Kirchhoff type. One neutralises the transverse distortion by penalisation of the associated energy, taking $k={10}^{6}h/R$ ($h$ being the thickness of the shell and $R$ its mean radius of curvature).

Still in the isotropic case, the only two non-zero components of ${\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{\text{th}}$ are ${\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{ii}^{\text{th}}$ for $i=1,2$ , such that:

${\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{ii}^{\text{th}}=\alpha (T-{T}^{\mathit{réf}})$

where $\alpha$ is the coefficient of thermal expansion and $T-{T}^{\mathit{réf}}$ the temperature difference, assumed known.

**Note:**

*One does not describe the variation of the thickness nor that of the transverse strain* ${\tilde{e}}_{33}$ *, which one can nevertheless compute by using the preceding plane-stress assumption. Moreover, no restriction is made on the type of constitutive behaviour in plane stress that one can represent.*

*In the same way as* $T\times T:\varepsilon =\tilde{\varepsilon}$ *one can deduce* $(T\times T{)}_{\text{mf}}:\varepsilon ={T}_{\text{mf}}:\varepsilon ={\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\text{mf}}$ *and* $(T\times T{)}_{\gamma}:\varepsilon ={T}_{\gamma}:\varepsilon ={\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\gamma}$ *, which makes it possible to recover* ${\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\text{mf}}$ *and* ${\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\gamma}$ *from the strain tensor in the global frame.*

## 3 Principle of virtual work

### 3.1 Strain work

In 3D the expression of the strain work is written:

$\begin{array}{c}{W}_{\text{def}}=\underset{S}{\int}\underset{-h/2}{\overset{h/2}{\int}}({\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{ij}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{ij})\text{dV}=\underset{S}{\int}\underset{-h/2}{\overset{h/2}{\int}}({\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{ij}{\tilde{C}}_{ijkl}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{kl})\text{dV}=\underset{S}{\int}\underset{-h/2}{\overset{h/2}{\int}}({\varepsilon}_{\text{rs}}{P}_{i}^{r}{P}_{k}^{s}{\tilde{C}}_{ijkl}{P}_{k}^{p}{P}_{l}^{q}{\varepsilon}_{\text{pq}})\text{dV}\\ =\underset{S}{\int}\underset{-h/2}{\overset{h/2}{\int}}({\varepsilon}_{\text{rs}}{C}_{\text{rspq}}{\varepsilon}_{\text{pq}})\text{dV}=\underset{S}{\int}\underset{-h/2}{\overset{h/2}{\int}}({\varepsilon}_{ij}{\sigma}_{ij})\text{dV}\end{array}$

One verifies that this expression is invariant with respect to the basis in which the tensors are expressed. One chooses, for the remainder of this document, to express everything in the local basis $({T}_{k})$ , knowing that one passes from the local constitutive tensor to the global constitutive tensor by the relation ${C}_{\text{rspq}}={P}_{i}^{r}{P}_{k}^{s}{\tilde{C}}_{ijkl}{P}_{k}^{p}{P}_{l}^{q}$ .

The general expression of the 3D strain work for the shell element is:

${W}_{\text{def}}=\underset{S}{\int}\underset{-h/2}{\overset{h/2}{\int}}(\tilde{\varepsilon}\tilde{\sigma})\text{dV}=\underset{S}{\int}\underset{-h/2}{\overset{h/2}{\int}}(\tilde{\varepsilon}\tilde{C}\tilde{\varepsilon})\text{dV}=\underset{S}{\int}\underset{-h/2}{\overset{h/2}{\int}}({\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\text{mf}}\tilde{H}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\text{mf}})\text{dV}+\underset{S}{\int}\underset{-h/2}{\overset{h/2}{\int}}({\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\gamma}{\tilde{H}}_{\gamma}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\gamma})\text{dV}$

where $S$ is the middle surface and the position through the thickness of the shell varies between $–h/2$ and $+h/2$ . There appears in the expression of the strain work a contribution of membrane–bending strain and a contribution of transverse-shear strain.

#### 3.1.1 Elastic internal energy of the shell

It is expressed in the following way:

${\Phi}_{int}=\frac{1}{2}\underset{S}{\int}[\frac{E}{1-{\nu}^{2}}({\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{11}^{2}+{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{22}^{2}+2\nu {\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{11}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{22})+G({\tilde{\gamma}}_{12}^{2}+k({\tilde{\gamma}}_{1}^{2}+{\tilde{\gamma}}_{2}^{2}))]\text{dV}$

where k is the transverse-shear correction factor defined in section 2 and $G=\frac{E}{2(1+\nu )}$ .

#### 3.1.2 Expression of the resultant forces

One denotes:

$N=(\begin{array}{c}{N}_{11}\\ {N}_{22}\\ {N}_{12}\end{array})=\underset{-h/2}{\overset{+h/2}{\int}}(\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{11}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{22}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{12}\end{array})\text{dz}$ ; $M=(\begin{array}{c}{M}_{11}\\ {M}_{22}\\ {M}_{12}\end{array})=\underset{-h/2}{\overset{+h/2}{\int}}(\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{11}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{22}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{12}\end{array})z\text{dz}$ ; $T=(\begin{array}{c}{T}_{1}\\ {T}_{2}\end{array})=\underset{-h/2}{\overset{+h/2}{\int}}(\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{13}\\ {\tilde{\sigma}}_{23}\end{array})\text{dz}$ .

${N}_{11},{N}_{22},{N}_{12}$ are the generalised membrane forces (in $N/m$ );

${M}_{11},{M}_{22},{M}_{12}$ are the generalised bending forces, or moments (in $N$ );

${T}_{1},{T}_{2}$ , are the generalised shear forces, or shear forces (in $N/m$ );

The expression of the resultant forces given here is an approximate expression which does not take the curvature of the shell into account (cf. p.316 of [bib3]). The error committed on these forces is then of order ${h}^{2}/R$ where $1/R$ is the mean curvature. When the shell becomes plane, the expressions given above are exact and the meaning of the resultant forces can be found in [R3.07.03](doc:r3.07.03). We shall not develop this aspect further, which is moreover well documented in [bib3], because the shell theory used here does not rest on a generalised-strains / resultant-forces formulation but on a three-dimensional-strains / stresses formulation.

${W}_{\text{ext}}=\underset{S}{\int}\underset{-h/2}{\overset{+h/2}{\int}}{F}_{v}.U\text{dV}+\underset{S}{\int}{F}_{s}.U\text{dS}+\underset{C}{\int}\underset{-h/2}{\overset{+h/2}{\int}}{F}_{c}.U\text{dz}\text{ds}$

where ${F}_{v},{F}_{s},{F}_{c}$ are the volume, surface and contour forces acting on the shell, respectively. $C$ is the part of the contour of the shell on which the contour forces ${F}_{c}$ are applied.

### 3.3 Work of the inertia forces

The work due to the acceleration quantities is written:

${W}^{\text{ac}}={\int}_{\Omega}\rho \ddot{\mathit{OQ}}'\cdot \mathit{OQ}'\mathit{dv}$

where $\rho$ is the density.

One assumes that $\stackrel{..}{\text{OQ}}'$ , the acceleration vector of the point $Q'$ , is of the following form:

$\stackrel{..}{\text{OQ}}'={\ddot{U}}_{k}{e}_{k}+W\mathrm{\wedge }\left[W\mathrm{\wedge }{x}_{k}^{0}{e}_{k}\right]$

where one has neglected the Coriolis forces and the metric correction through the thickness.

One denotes ${\ddot{U}}_{k}=\frac{{d}^{2}{U}_{k}}{{\text{dt}}^{2}}$ , and $\Omega$ is the vector of uniform rotation of the global frame $(O,{e}_{k})$ (with respect to a Galilean frame which has the same origin $O$ as the global frame).

One expresses $\Omega$ in the global basis $({e}_{k})$ :

$\Omega ={\Omega}_{k}{e}_{k}$

For the virtual displacement $\text{OQ}'$ , one has:

$\text{OQ}'={U}_{k}{e}_{k}$

The work due to the acceleration quantities then becomes:

${W}^{\text{ac}}=\underset{\Omega}{\int}\rho {U}_{k}{e}_{k}\left[{\ddot{U}}_{k}{e}_{k}+\Omega \mathrm{\wedge }(\Omega \mathrm{\wedge }{x}_{k}^{0}{e}_{k})\right]\text{dv}={W}_{\text{mass}}^{\text{ac}}+{W}_{\text{cent}}^{\text{ac}}$

with:

${W}_{\text{masse}}^{\text{ac}}={\int}_{\Omega}\rho {U}_{k}{\ddot{U}}_{k}\text{dv}$

and:

${W}_{\text{cent}}^{\text{ac}}=\underset{\Omega}{\int}{\mathit{\rho U}}_{k}{e}_{k}\left[\Omega \mathrm{\wedge }(\Omega \mathrm{\wedge }{x}_{k}^{0}{e}_{k})\right]\text{dv}$

### 3.4 Principle of virtual work

For a static loading, it is written in the following way: $\delta {W}_{\text{ext}}=\delta {W}_{\text{def}}$ where ${W}_{\text{ext}}$ is the sum of the various elementary works, corresponding to the various loadings.

In harmonic dynamics (eigenmode analyses), the principle of virtual work gives: $\delta {W}_{\text{ext}}+\delta {W}_{\text{mass}}^{\text{ac}}=0$

## 4 Numerical discretisation of the variational formulation arising from the principle of virtual work

### 4.1 Introduction

This chapter is devoted to the discretisation of the various energy terms introduced in the preceding chapter. The choice of the HENCKY-MINDLIN-NAGHDI framework to describe the shell kinematics, presented in paragraph [§2], leads to expressions of the strains in which the derivatives are limited to order 1, unlike the LOVE-KIRCHHOFF model. One can therefore use a finite element of limited order while ensuring conformity (see p.110 of [bib7]).

The degrees of freedom are the 3 displacements in the global frame and the 2 rotations in the local frame.

The elements chosen are isoparametric quadrilaterals or triangles. The quadrilateral is represented below. The quadrilaterals give the best results (see p.202 of [bib8]). The best choice consists in taking, for these elements, quadratic interpolation functions (see p.224 of [bib8]) in order to model correctly the membrane, bending and shear effects. According to the results based on numerous test cases in the literature, the optimal choice is the quadratic isoparametric quadrilateral, which makes it possible to have a fine representation of a curved geometry and good estimates of the stresses. Among the elements with quadratic functions one chooses the heterosis element (Q9H), whose displacements are approximated by the interpolation functions of the Serendipity element and the rotations by the functions of the Lagrange element (cf. Annex 3). This choice is justified below.

Risks of membrane or shear locking appear when the thickness of the shell becomes small compared with its radius of curvature and the interpolation functions are of too low an order. To resolve them one uses a selective numerical integration [bib6]. For certain types of boundary conditions (clamping) with the Serendipity element the locking persists despite the selective integration. Moreover, for the Lagrange element, this type of integration leads to singularities in the stiffness matrix. The Heterosis element Q9H with selective integration does not encounter the problems mentioned and appears to be the most efficient for the modelling of very thin shells (see p.224 of [bib8]). It should be noted that this element possesses a deformation mode without associated energy if it is used alone. This mode disappears when one uses more than two elements [bib7].

For the triangular elements, the Heterosis element T7H is required for the same reasons but proves to be distinctly less efficient (see section 5 concerning the validation).

One decides to carry out all the discretisation computations in the global Cartesian basis.

### 4.2 Discretisation of the geometric terms

The coordinates ${x}_{k}^{0}$ of a point $P$ of the middle surface $\omega$ are interpolated by the shape functions in the following way:

${x}_{k}^{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{\text{Nb}1}{N}_{i}^{(1)}{x}_{\text{ik}}^{0}$

where the number $\text{Nb}1$ and the shape functions ${N}_{i}^{(1)}$ depend on the type of element chosen, and ${x}_{\text{ik}}^{0}$ are the coordinates at node $i$ of the element.

The covariant vectors ${a}_{\alpha}$ (attached to the point $P$ ) are then given by:

${a}_{\alpha}=\sum_{i=1}^{\text{Nb}1}\frac{\partial {N}_{i}^{(1)}}{\partial {\xi}_{\alpha}}{x}_{\text{ik}}^{0}{e}_{k}$

One avoids the computation of the vectors ${T}_{k}$ because the components ${T}_{k}^{j}$ contain the curvature quantities, the computation of which is often inaccurate, as was shown in paragraph [§2.2.1].

In order to avoid the presence of the curvature terms, one writes:

$n=\sum_{i=1}^{\text{Nb}1}{N}_{i}^{(1)}{n}_{i}$

where ${n}_{i}$ is the vector normal to the nodes of the element.

### 4.3 Discretisation of the displacement field

One adopts the following expression for the displacement at the point $Q$ :

$U=\sum_{i=1}^{\text{Nb}1}{N}_{i}^{(1)}{u}_{\text{ik}}{e}_{k}+\frac{{\xi}_{3}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\text{Nb}2}{N}_{i}^{(2)}{h}_{i}({\tilde{\theta}}_{\mathit{i2}}{t}_{\mathit{i1}}-{\tilde{\theta}}_{\mathit{i1}}{t}_{\mathit{i2}})$

where the ${t}_{\alpha}$ are evaluated at the nodes, and where one observes that the interpolation functions ${N}_{i}^{(2)}$ and their number $\text{Nb}2$ for the rotations ${\tilde{\theta}}_{\alpha}$ are a priori different from those used for the displacements ${u}_{k}$ .

By expressing the ${t}_{i\alpha }$ as a function of their components in the global Cartesian basis, one obtains:

$U=\sum_{i=1}^{\text{Nb}1}{N}_{i}^{(1)}{u}_{\text{ik}}{e}_{k}+\frac{{\xi}_{3}}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{\text{Nb}2}{N}_{i}^{(2)}{h}_{i}({\tilde{\theta}}_{\mathit{i2}}{t}_{\mathit{i1k}}+{\tilde{\theta}}_{\mathit{i1}}{t}_{\mathit{i2k}}){e}_{k}$

One then computes the various elementary terms, in order to obtain the complete discretised formulation. In what follows one uses Einstein’s summation convention, bearing in mind that the number of interpolations is $\text{Nb}1$ for ${x}_{k}^{0},n,{u}_{k}$ , and $\text{Nb}2$ for ${\tilde{\theta}}_{\alpha}$ , ${t}_{\alpha}$ .

#### 4.3.1 Heterosis element Q9H

With this element, the number of interpolations for the geometry $({x}_{k}^{0},n)$ and the displacements ${u}_{k}$ is $\mathit{Nb1}=8$ (vertex nodes and mid-side nodes), whereas the number of interpolations for the ${t}_{\alpha}$ and the rotations ${\tilde{\theta}}_{\alpha}$ is $\mathit{Nb2}=9$ (vertex nodes and mid-side nodes + barycentre). The total number of degrees of freedom of the element is therefore $\mathit{Nddle}=3\times 8+2\times 9=42$ .

The interpolation functions ${N}_{i}^{(1)}$ and ${N}_{i}^{(2)}$ respectively for the geometry and the displacements, and for the rotations, can be found for example in [bib2] and are recalled in annex 2.

The elementary displacement vector can be put in the following form:

With this element $\mathit{Nb1}=6$ (vertex nodes and mid-side nodes) and $\mathit{Nb2}=7$ (vertex nodes and mid-side nodes + barycentre). The total number of degrees of freedom of the element is $\mathit{Nddle}=3\times 6+2\times 7=32$ .

### 4.4 Discretisation of the strain field

The strain field is expressed as the symmetrised gradient of the displacement field:

$\varepsilon =S\nabla U=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla U+\nabla {U}^{T})$

As:

$U(x)=N\left[\xi (x)\right]{\tilde{q}}^{e}$

one therefore has:

$\nabla U=\nabla N(\xi )\frac{\partial \xi }{\partial x}{\tilde{q}}^{e}$

where $N$ groups the shape functions ${N}_{i}^{(1)}$ and ${N}_{i}^{(2)}$ and the transformation matrices ${t}_{i\alpha k}$ , $\frac{\partial \xi }{\partial x}$ is the inverse of the Jacobian $J$ and ${\tilde{q}}^{e}$ is the vector of the degrees of freedom at the nodes (translations ${u}_{k}$ and rotations ${\tilde{\theta}}_{\alpha}$ ).

Taking these relations and $\tilde{\varepsilon}=T\times T\varepsilon$ into account, one obtains the components of the strain tensor in the local frame:

$\tilde{\varepsilon}=\tilde{B}{\tilde{q}}^{e}$

where $\tilde{B}$ is the interpolation matrix of $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ , such that:

$\tilde{B}=T\times TS{J}^{-1}\nabla N(\xi )$

### 4.5 Stiffness matrix

The principle of virtual work is written in the following way: $\delta {W}_{\text{ext}}=\delta {W}_{\text{def}}$ or again $\delta {U}^{T}\text{KU}=\delta {U}^{T}F$ in matrix form, where $K$ is the stiffness matrix arising from the assembly, in the global frame, of the set of elementary stiffness matrices. At the elementary level the discretisation of the strain work is written, with the preceding notations:

$\delta {W}_{\text{def}}^{\text{el}}=\delta {\tilde{q}}^{{e}^{t}}\underset{-1}{\overset{1}{\int}}\underset{{A}_{r}}{\overset{}{\int}}{\tilde{B}}^{t}\tilde{C}\tilde{B}\detJd{\xi}_{1}d{\xi}_{2}d{\xi}_{3}{\tilde{q}}^{e}=\delta {\tilde{q}}^{{e}^{t}}{\tilde{K}}^{e}{\tilde{q}}^{e}$

where ${A}_{r}$ is the reference domain of the element.

#### 4.5.1 Decomposition of the elementary matrices

This stiffness matrix comprises three contributions due to the membrane, bending and transverse-distortion strains. One thus has: ${\tilde{K}}^{e}={\tilde{K}}_{m}^{e}+{\tilde{K}}_{f}^{e}+{\tilde{K}}_{\gamma}^{e}$ with:

$\begin{array}{c}{\tilde{K}}_{m}^{e}=\underset{-1}{\overset{1}{\int}}\underset{{A}_{r}}{\overset{}{\int}}{\tilde{B}}_{m}^{t}H{\tilde{B}}_{m}\detJd{\xi}_{1}d{\xi}_{2}d{\xi}_{3};\\ {\tilde{K}}_{f}^{e}=\underset{-1}{\overset{1}{\int}}\underset{{A}_{r}}{\overset{}{\int}}{\tilde{B}}_{f}^{t}H{\tilde{B}}_{f}\detJd{\xi}_{1}d{\xi}_{2}d{\xi}_{3};\\ {\tilde{K}}_{\gamma}^{e}=\underset{-1}{\overset{1}{\int}}\underset{{A}_{r}}{\overset{}{\int}}{\tilde{B}}_{\gamma}^{t}{H}_{\gamma}{\tilde{B}}_{\gamma}\detJd{\xi}_{1}d{\xi}_{2}d{\xi}_{3}.\end{array}$

#### 4.5.2 Assembly of the elementary matrices

The principle of virtual work for the set of elements is written:

$\delta {W}_{\text{def}}=\sum_{e=1}^{\text{nb}\text{elem}}\delta {W}_{\text{def}}^{e}=\delta {U}^{T}\text{KU}$ where $U$ is the set of degrees of freedom of the discretised structure and $K$ arises from the assembly of the elementary matrices.

##### 4.5.2.1 Degrees of freedom

The process of assembly of the elementary matrices implies that all the degrees of freedom are expressed in the global frame. In the global frame, the degrees of freedom are the three displacements with respect to the three axes of the global Cartesian frame and the three rotations with respect to these three axes. One therefore uses, for the rotation degrees of freedom, transformation matrices from the local orthonormal frame ${t}_{\alpha}$ to the global frame for each element.

##### 4.5.2.2 Fictitious rotations

The rotation with respect to the normal to the shell is not a true degree of freedom. In order to ensure compatibility in passing from the local frame to the global frame, one therefore adds a supplementary local rotation degree of freedom of the shell, which is that corresponding to the rotation with respect to the normal to the middle surface of the element. This implies an expansion of the blocks of dimension $(5,5)$ of the local stiffness matrix into blocks of dimension $(6,6)$ by adding a row and a column corresponding to this rotation. These supplementary rows and columns are a priori zero. One then carries out the passage from the enlarged local stiffness matrix to the global stiffness matrix.

In the preceding transformation, one has merely added the rotations with respect to the normals to the surface of the elements without modifying the strain energy. The contribution to the energy brought by these supplementary degrees of freedom is indeed zero and no stiffness is associated with them.

The global stiffness matrix thus obtained nevertheless presents the risk of being non-invertible. To avoid this inconvenience it is accepted practice to assign a small stiffness to these supplementary degrees of freedom at the level of the enlarged local stiffness matrix. In practice, one chooses it between 10–6 and 10–3 times the smallest diagonal term of the local rotation stiffness matrix. The user can choose this multiplicative coefficient COEF_RIGI_DRZ himself in AFFE_CARA_ELEM; by default it is equal to 10–5.

### 4.6 Mass matrix

The virtual work of the inertia effects can be expressed in the form:

$\delta {W}_{\text{mass}}^{\text{ac}}=\underset{\Omega}{\int}\rho \ddot{U}(Q).\delta U(Q)d\Omega$

One assumes that the strains and the displacements remain sufficiently small for the normal to the middle surface of the shell to remain unchanged.

With these assumptions, we can write the virtual displacement field:

$\delta U(Q)({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2},{\xi}_{3})=\delta u(P)({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})+{\xi}_{3}\frac{h}{2}\delta \theta ({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})\mathrm{\wedge }n({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})$

and the acceleration field:

$\ddot{U}(Q)({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2},{\xi}_{3})=\ddot{u}(P)({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})+{\xi}_{3}\frac{h}{2}\ddot{\theta}({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})\mathrm{\wedge }n({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})$

In this expression, we have neglected the gyroscopic terms.

#### 4.6.1 Discretisation of the displacement for the mass matrix

At the point $Q$ , one takes as interpolation of the displacement field:

$\delta U(Q)({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2},{\xi}_{3})=\sum_{I=1}^{\text{Nb}1}{N}_{I}^{1}({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})(\begin{array}{c}\delta {u}_{\mathit{I1}}\\ \delta {u}_{\mathit{I2}}\\ \delta {u}_{\mathit{I3}}\end{array})-{\xi}_{3}\frac{h}{2}\sum_{I=1}^{\text{Nb}2}{N}_{I}^{2}({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& -{n}_{\mathit{I3}}& {n}_{\mathit{I2}}\\ {n}_{\mathit{I3}}& 0& -{n}_{\mathit{I1}}\\ -{n}_{\mathit{I2}}& {n}_{\mathit{I1}}& 0\end{array}\right](\begin{array}{c}\delta {\theta}_{\mathit{I1}}\\ \delta {\theta}_{\mathit{I2}}\\ \delta {\theta}_{\mathit{I3}}\end{array})$

For the acceleration field, the interpolation is written:

$\ddot{U}(Q)({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2},{\xi}_{3})=\sum_{I=1}^{\text{Nb}1}{N}_{I}^{1}({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})(\begin{array}{c}{\ddot{u}}_{\mathit{I1}}\\ {\ddot{u}}_{\mathit{I2}}\\ {\ddot{u}}_{\mathit{I3}}\end{array})-{\xi}_{3}\frac{h}{2}\sum_{I=1}^{\text{Nb}2}{N}_{I}^{2}({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2})\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& -{n}_{\mathit{I3}}& {n}_{\mathit{I2}}\\ {n}_{\mathit{I3}}& 0& -{n}_{\mathit{I1}}\\ -{n}_{\mathit{I2}}& {n}_{\mathit{I1}}& 0\end{array}\right](\begin{array}{c}{\ddot{\theta}}_{\mathit{I1}}\\ {\ddot{\theta}}_{\mathit{I2}}\\ {\ddot{\theta}}_{\mathit{I3}}\end{array})$

We rewrite the two preceding equations in matrix form:

$\delta U(Q)({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2},{\xi}_{3})=N\delta {u}^{e}$

$\ddot{U}(Q)({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2},{\xi}_{3})=N{\ddot{u}}^{e}$

where $N$ is the interpolation matrix, whose expression is:

$N=\left[{\left[{N}_{I}^{1}(\begin{array}{ccc}1& 0& 0\\ 0& 1& 0\\ 0& 0& 1\end{array})-{\xi}_{3}\frac{h}{2}{N}_{I}^{2}(\begin{array}{ccc}0& -{n}_{\mathrm{I3}}& {n}_{\mathrm{I2}}\\ {n}_{\mathrm{I3}}& 0& -{n}_{\mathrm{I1}}\\ -{n}_{\mathrm{I2}}& {n}_{\mathrm{I1}}& 0\end{array})\right]}_{I=1,\text{Nb}1}-{\xi}_{3}\frac{h}{2}{N}_{\text{Nb}2}^{2}(\begin{array}{ccc}0& -{n}_{\text{Nb}23}& {n}_{\text{Nb}22}\\ {n}_{\text{Nb}23}& 0& -{n}_{\text{Nb}21}\\ -{n}_{\text{Nb}22}& {n}_{\text{Nb}21}& 0\end{array})\right]$

#### 4.6.2 Elementary mass matrix

With the preceding notations, the virtual work of the inertia effects takes the following matrix form:

$\delta {W}_{\text{mass}}^{\text{inertie}}=\delta {u}^{\text{eT}}{M}^{e}{\ddot{u}}^{e}$

with ${M}^{e}$ the consistent mass matrix, which can be expressed in the form:

${M}^{e}=\underset{\Omega e}{\int}\rho {N}^{T}N\det(J({\xi}_{3}))d{\xi}_{1}d{\xi}_{2}d{\xi}_{3}$

It is important to note that, because of the curvature, a coupling of the translation terms with those of rotation is possible (indeed, $\det(J({\xi}_{3}))$ is not constant through the thickness).

#### 4.6.3 Assembly of the elementary mass matrices

The assembly of the mass matrices follows the same logic as that of the stiffness matrices. The degrees of freedom are the same and one finds the treatment specific to the rotations normal to the surface of the shell. Although the consistent mass matrix is constructed in the global frame, it remains singular with respect to the rotation of the normal at each node. We need to supply this matrix starting from the variational form:

$\delta {W}_{n}^{\text{inertie}}=\sum_{I=1}^{\text{Nb}2}{m}_{e}\delta {\theta}_{I}({n}_{I}\times {n}_{I}){\ddot{\theta}}_{I}$

where ${m}_{e}$ is chosen constant per element and computed according to the formula:

${m}_{e}={\text{Cm}}_{\max}$

${m}_{\max}$ being the largest term due to the rotations (in the local frame of the element) on the diagonal of the matrix ${M}^{e}$ . It should therefore be noted that, in order to do this, it was necessary to bring the contribution of the rotations, initially expressed in the global frame of the element, back into the local frame of the element by a change of frame.

For modal analyses involving both the computation of the stiffness matrix and that of the mass matrix, one must take a mass on the degree of rotation normal to the surface of the shell equal to $C$ times the smallest diagonal term of the mass matrix for the rotation terms in the local frame, where $C$ lies between 10–6 and 10–3. One chooses to identify the values of this coefficient with those of COEF_RIGI_DRZ for the equivalent operation on the stiffness matrix. By default $C$ is therefore equal to 10–5. This makes it possible to inhibit, during a modal analysis, the modes which may appear on the supplementary degree of freedom of rotation about the normal to the surface of the shell.

### 4.8 Numerical integration for plasticity

The principle of the surface integration remains the same as in elasticity, but the initial thickness is divided into $N$ layers of identical thicknesses. There are three integration points per layer. The integration points are situated on the upper skin of the layer, at the middle of the layer and on the lower skin of the layer. For $N$ layers, the number of integration points is $\mathrm{2N}+1$ . One advises using from 3 to 5 layers through the thickness for a number of integration points equal to 7, 9 and 11 respectively.

For the stiffness, one computes for each layer, in plane stress, the contribution to the membrane, bending and transverse-distortion stiffness matrices. These contributions are added and assembled in order to obtain the total tangent stiffness matrix.

For each layer, one computes the stress state $({\sigma}_{11,}{\sigma}_{22,}{\sigma}_{12})$ and the set of internal variables, at the middle of the layer and on the upper and lower skins of the layer, from the local plastic behaviour and the local strain field $({\varepsilon}_{11,}{\varepsilon}_{22,}{\varepsilon}_{12})$ . The positioning of the integration points allows us to have the most accurate estimates, because they are not extrapolated, on the lower and upper skins of the layer, where one knows that the stresses are liable to be maximal. The plastic behaviour does not for the moment include the transverse-shear terms, which are treated in an elastic manner, because the transverse shear is uncoupled from the membrane behaviour in plane stress.

#### 4.9.6 Taking thermal expansion loadings into account

One treats only the case in which the thermoelastic characteristics $E$ , $\nu$ , $\alpha$ depend only on the mean temperature $\stackrel{ˉ}{T}$ through the thickness. Moreover, the material is homogeneous isotropic thermoelastic through the thickness.

The variational formulation of the work due to the thermal expansions is written:

The temperature is represented by the following three-field thermal model [R3.11.01](doc:r3.11.01):

$T({\xi}_{\alpha},{\xi}_{3})={T}^{m}({\xi}_{\alpha}).{P}_{1}({\xi}_{3})+{T}^{s}({\xi}_{\alpha}).{P}_{2}({\xi}_{3})+{T}^{i}({\xi}_{\alpha}).{P}_{3}({\xi}_{3})$ ,

with: ${P}_{j}({\xi}_{3})$ : the three LAGRANGE polynomials through the thickness: $]-1,+1[$ :

${P}_{1}({\xi}_{3})=1-{({\xi}_{3})}^{2};{P}_{2}({\xi}_{3})=\frac{{\xi}_{3}}{2}(1+{\xi}_{3});{P}_{3}({\xi}_{3})\text{=-}\frac{{\xi}_{3}}{2}(1-{\xi}_{3});$

from the representation of the temperature above, one obtains:

- the mean temperature through the thickness:

$\stackrel{ˉ}{T}({\xi}_{\alpha})=\frac{1}{2}{\int}_{-1}^{+1}T({\xi}_{\alpha},{\xi}_{3})d{\xi}_{3}=\frac{1}{6}({\mathrm{4T}}^{m}({\xi}_{\alpha})+{T}^{s}({\xi}_{\alpha})+{T}^{i}({\xi}_{\alpha}))$ ;

- the mean temperature gradient through the thickness:

$\stackrel{ˆ}{T}({\xi}_{\alpha})=3{\int}_{-1}^{+1}T({\xi}_{\alpha},{\xi}_{3}){\xi}_{3}d{\xi}_{3}={T}^{s}({\xi}_{\alpha})-{T}^{i}({\xi}_{\alpha})$ ;

Thus the temperature can be written in the following way:

$T({\xi}_{\alpha},{\xi}_{3})=\stackrel{ˉ}{T}({\xi}_{\alpha})+\stackrel{ˆ}{T}({\xi}_{\alpha}).{\xi}_{3}/2+\tilde{T}({\xi}_{\alpha},{\xi}_{3})$ such that:

${\int}_{-1}^{+1}\tilde{T}({\xi}_{\alpha},{\xi}_{3})=0;{\int}_{-1}^{+1}{\xi}_{3}\tilde{T}({\xi}_{\alpha},{\xi}_{3})=0$ .

If the temperature is effectively affine through the thickness one has, $\tilde{T}=0$ .

It is necessary to evaluate the three-dimensional thermal stresses, at each integration point through the thickness. These stresses of thermal origin, subtracted from the usual mechanical stresses, are computed at the integration points through the thickness by:

${\tilde{\sigma}}_{\beta \gamma }^{\text{ther}}=\frac{\alpha .E}{1-{\nu}^{2}}(\stackrel{ˉ}{T}-{T}^{\text{réf}}+\stackrel{ˆ}{T}.{\xi}_{3}/2){\delta}_{\beta \gamma }$

#### 4.9.7 Assembly

The variational formulation of the work of the external forces for the set of elements is then written:

$\delta {W}_{\text{ext}}=\sum_{e=1}^{\text{nb}\text{elem}}\delta {W}_{\text{ext}}^{e}=\delta {U}^{T}F$ where $U$ is the set of degrees of freedom of the discretised structure and $F$ arises from the assembly of the elementary force vectors.

As for the stiffness matrices, the process of assembly of the elementary force vectors implies that all the degrees of freedom are expressed in the global frame. In the global frame, the degrees of freedom are the three displacements with respect to the three axes of the global Cartesian frame and the three rotations with respect to these three axes. One therefore uses transformation matrices from the local frame to the global frame for the rotations of each element.

**Note:**

*The external forces can also be defined in the user frame. One then uses a transformation matrix from the user frame to the local frame of the element in order to have the expression of these forces in the local frame of the element and to deduce the corresponding local elementary force vector. For the assembly one then passes from the local frame of the element to the global frame.*

## 6 Thermomechanical chaining

### 6.1 Description

For the solution of chained thermomechanical problems, one must use, for the thermal analysis, thermal shell finite elements [R3.11.01](doc:r3.11.01) whose temperature field is recovered as input data of *Code_Aster*  for the mechanical analysis. There must therefore be compatibility between the thermal field given by the thermal shells and that recovered by the mechanical shells. The latter is defined by the knowledge of the 3 fields TEMP_SUP, TEMP_MIL and TEMP_INF given on the lower skin, at mid-thickness and on the upper skin of the shell.

Thermomechanical chaining is also possible if one knows, from experimental measurements, the variation of the temperature field through the thickness of the structure or of certain parts of the structure. One will note that it is not necessary, for the mechanical analysis, that the number of integration points through the thickness be equal to the number of discretisation points of the temperature field through the thickness. The temperature field is automatically interpolated at the integration points through the thickness of the shell elements.

## 8 Conclusion

The curved shell finite elements which we describe here are used in the analyses of thin curved structures whose ratio of thickness to characteristic length is less than 1/10. Two volumetric shell finite elements supported on quadrilateral and triangular mesh cells have been introduced into *Code_Aster* . They were chosen with a very particular aim: to be able to represent a complete behaviour of curved structures, whereas until now one could use only plane-facet elements, which induced parasitic bendings and required the meshes to be refined.

They are elements for which the strains and the stresses in the plane of the element vary linearly with the thickness of the shell. The kinematics chosen is a shell kinematics of Hencky–Mindlin–Naghdi type, making it possible to introduce the transverse-shear energy. The distortion associated with the transverse shear is constant through the thickness of the element. The variable correction on the transverse-shear coefficient $k$ offers a flexibility of use making it possible to pass from the theory of HENCKY-MINDLIN-NAGHDI for $k=1$ , to that of REISSNER for $k=5/6$ and to that of LOVE_KIRCHHOFF (for very thin structures) if one chooses a value of $k$ equal to ${10}^{6}\times h/L$ , $h$ being the thickness and $L$ a characteristic distance (mean radius of curvature, zone of application of the loads....). As in this last case one uses a penalty method in order to make the transverse-shear terms small, one can, if one takes a value of $k$ that is too large, make the numerical system singular. In that case, one must decrease the value of $k$ .

The default value of $k$ is $5/6$ . One generally uses it when the structure to be meshed has a ratio of thickness to characteristic length lying between $1/20$ and $1/10$ . For smaller thicknesses, where the transverse distortion becomes small, one may wish to use a value of $k={10}^{6}\times h/L$ (in order to be able to make comparisons with DKT plate elements, for example). When the transverse distortion is non-zero, the shell elements do not satisfy the 3D equilibrium conditions and the boundary conditions on the vanishing of the transverse-shear stresses on the upper and lower faces of the shell, which are compatible with a transverse distortion constant through the thickness of the shell. It thus follows that, at the level of the constitutive behaviour, a coefficient of 5/6 for a homogeneous shell corrects the usual relation between the stresses and the transverse distortion so as to ensure equality between the shear energies of the 3D model and of the shell model with constant distortion. In that case, the deflection ${\tilde{u}}_{3}$ has as its interpretation the mean transverse displacement through the thickness of the shell and not the displacement of the middle surface of the shell.

For structures of small thickness, in order to avoid locking phenomena, one uses reduced under-integration for the membrane and shear parts of the stiffness matrix. The choice of finite elements fell on the Heterosis quadrilateral elements Q9H and the triangular elements T7H. Indeed, among the finite elements with quadratic interpolation functions, the performance of the Heterosis element Q9H is known. It is in particular superior to that of the Serendipity elements Q9S or of the Lagrange elements Q9. This performance nevertheless rests on the selective integration of the element, with reduced integration of the membrane and shear terms on the one hand, and normal integration of the bending terms on the other hand. By analogy with Q9H, one has taken the finite element T7H as the element of triangular shape. However, as far as possible, one will use the Q9H rather than the T7H, which is distinctly less efficient.

The non-linear behaviours in plane stress are available for these elements. One points out, however, that the stresses generated by the transverse distortion are treated elastically, for want of a better approach. Indeed, the rigorous taking into account of a non-zero constant transverse shear through the thickness, and the determination of the associated correction on the shear stiffness relative to a model satisfying the equilibrium conditions and the boundary conditions, are not possible and therefore make the use of these elements, when the transverse shear is non-zero, rigorously impossible in plasticity. Rigorously, for non-linear behaviours, one should therefore use these elements within the framework of Love–Kirchhoff theory.

Elements corresponding to the mechanical elements exist in thermal analysis; the thermomechanical chainings are therefore available with 7- and 9-node thermal shell finite elements. Extensions of the preceding formulation presented in the annex also make it possible to take into account the anisotropy of the materials and the kinematic non-linearity. This second extension is operational in *Code_Aster* and is the subject of a reference documentation [R3.07.05](doc:r3.07.05).

Extension to anisotropic materials not programmed

One considers that the shell is constituted of an orthotropic material, with axes of orthotropy ${\tilde{\tilde{x}}}_{k}$ associated with the basis ${k}_{k}$ . The constitutive law in these axes is written:

$\underset{(6\times 1)}{\tilde{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}=\underset{(6\times 6)}{{\tilde{\tilde{S}}}_{k}}\underset{(6\times 1)}{\tilde{\tilde{\sigma}}}$

where $\tilde{S}$ is the compliance matrix of the constituent $k$ .

Let $\tilde{\varepsilon}$ and $\tilde{\sigma}$ be the strain and stress tensors in the axes ${\tilde{x}}_{k}$ ; one has:

$\begin{array}{c}\tilde{\sigma}={}^{t}\text{}Q\tilde{\tilde{\sigma}}Q\\ \tilde{\varepsilon}={}^{t}\text{}Q\tilde{\tilde{\varepsilon}}Q\end{array}$

where $Q={\left[{T}_{1},{T}_{2},{T}_{3}\right]}_{/{k}_{k}}$ (${Q}_{ij}={T}_{i}.{k}_{j}$ ) is the matrix of the direction cosines of ${T}_{k}$ in the basis ${k}_{k}$ .

In vector form, one has:

$\begin{array}{c}\tilde{\sigma}=\tilde{T}\tilde{\tilde{\sigma}}\\ \tilde{\varepsilon}=\tilde{T}\tilde{\tilde{\varepsilon}}\end{array}$

where the components of $\tilde{T}$ are defined as a function of those of $Q$ .

Conversely, one has:

$\begin{array}{c}\tilde{\tilde{\sigma}}={\tilde{T}}^{\text{-1}}\tilde{\sigma}\\ \tilde{\tilde{\varepsilon}}={\tilde{T}}^{\text{-1}}\tilde{\varepsilon}\end{array}$

therefore, one obtains:

$\tilde{\varepsilon}=\tilde{T}{\tilde{\tilde{S}}}_{k}{\tilde{T}}^{-1}\tilde{\sigma}$

which one writes:

$\tilde{\varepsilon}={\tilde{S}}_{k}\tilde{\sigma}$

In order to be consistent with the plane-stress assumption ${\tilde{\sigma}}_{33}=0$ , one writes:

$\underset{(5\times 1)}{{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{r}}=\underset{(5\times 5)}{{\tilde{S}}_{\text{kr}}}\underset{(5\times 1)}{{\tilde{\sigma}}_{r}}$

with the symbol r as in reduced, which gives:

${\tilde{\sigma}}_{r}={\tilde{C}}_{k}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{r},{\tilde{C}}_{k}={\tilde{S}}_{\text{kr}}^{-1}$

which one rewrites by omitting the symbol $r$ ,

$\tilde{\sigma}={\tilde{C}}_{k}\tilde{\varepsilon}$

The elastic strain energy ${W}^{\text{el}}$ is:

${W}^{\text{el}}=\frac{1}{2}{}^{t}\text{}{\tilde{q}}^{e}\underset{-1}{\overset{1}{\int}}\underset{\text{Ar}}{\overset{}{\int}}{}^{t}\text{}\tilde{B}{\tilde{C}}_{k}\tilde{B}\detJd{\xi}_{1}d{\xi}_{2}d{\xi}_{3}{\tilde{q}}^{e}$

If the shell is constituted of $\mathit{Nc}$ layers, each layer being considered as a constituent $k$ , then:

${W}^{\text{el}}=\frac{1}{2}{}^{t}\text{}{\tilde{q}}^{e}\sum_{k=1}^{\text{Nc}}\underset{2{e}_{k}^{-}/h}{\overset{2{e}_{k}^{+}/h}{\int}}\underset{\text{Ar}}{\overset{}{\int}}{}^{t}\text{}\tilde{B}\tilde{{C}_{k}}\stackrel{}{˜}B\detJd{\xi}_{1}d{\xi}_{2}d{\xi}_{3}{\stackrel{}{˜}q}^{e}$

where ${e}_{k}^{-}$ and ${e}_{k}^{+}$ are the abscissae of the lower and upper bounds of the layer $k$ of thickness

${e}_{k}={e}_{k}^{+}-{e}_{k}^{-}$ , with ${e}_{1}^{-}=-h/2$ and ${e}_{\text{Nc}}^{+}=h/2$ .

Setting:

${\xi}_{3}=\frac{{e}_{k}}{h}{\overline{\xi}}_{3}+\frac{{e}_{k}^{+}+{e}_{k}^{-}}{h},{\overline{\xi}}_{3}\in \left[-1,1\right]$

one has:

${W}^{\text{el}}=\frac{1}{2}{}^{t}\text{}{\tilde{q}}^{e}\sum_{k=1}^{\text{Nc}}\frac{{e}_{k}}{h}\underset{-1}{\overset{1}{\int}}\underset{\text{Ar}}{\overset{}{\int}}{}^{t}\text{}\tilde{B}{\tilde{C}}_{k}\tilde{B}\detJ({\xi}_{1},{\xi}_{2},{\overline{\xi}}_{3})d{\xi}_{1}d{\xi}_{2}d{\overline{\xi}}_{3}{\tilde{q}}^{e}$

Likewise, for the work due to the thermal expansions ${W}^{\text{th}}$ , one has:

${\tilde{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}_{\text{th}}^{k}=({\alpha}_{1}^{k}T,{\alpha}_{2}^{k}T,{\alpha}_{3}^{k}T,0,0,0)$

where the ${\alpha}_{i}^{k}$ are the coefficients of thermal expansion of the layer $k$ in the axes of orthotropy (${\tilde{\tilde{\xi}}}_{k}$ ).

With the relation:

${\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\text{th}}^{k}=\tilde{T}{\tilde{\tilde{\varepsilon}}}_{\text{th}}^{k}$

one obtains:

${W}^{\text{th}}\text{=-}{}^{t}\text{}{\tilde{q}}^{e}\underset{-1}{\overset{1}{\int}}\underset{\text{Ar}}{\overset{}{\int}}{}^{t}\text{}\tilde{B}(-{\tilde{C}}_{k}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\text{th}}^{k})\detJd{\xi}_{1}d{\xi}_{2}d{\xi}_{3}$

That is:

${W}^{\text{th}}={}^{t}\text{}{\tilde{q}}^{e}\sum_{h=1}^{\text{Nc}}\frac{{e}_{k}}{h}\underset{-1}{\overset{1}{\int}}\underset{\text{Ar}}{\overset{}{\int}}{}^{t}\text{}\tilde{B}{\tilde{C}}_{k}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{\text{th}}^{k}\detJd{\xi}_{1}d{\xi}_{2}d{\overline{\xi}}_{3}$

The 8 incomplete Lagrange shape functions of the quadrilateral element Q9H [Figure A2.2-a] for the interpolation of the displacements ${u}_{k}$ are:
