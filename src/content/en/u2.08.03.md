# u2.08.03 User's guide for the linear solvers

## 1 Purpose

The **linear solvers are in fact omnipresent in the course of the operators of** **Code_Aster**, because they are often buried in the deepest part of other numerical algorithms: non-linear scheme, integration in time, modal analysis, etc. They often consume the major part of the CPU time and of the memory. The choice and the complete parameter setting of the required linear solver are carried out *via* the factor keyword SOLVEUR. It is present in most of the analysis commands (STAT_NON_LINE, THER_LINEAIRE, CALC_MODES, etc.).

This **user's guide is complementary to the User documentation** of the keyword SOLVEUR[U4.50.01]. It aims to be an intermediate link between the "simple" keyword documentation and the theoretical documentation (each solver is backed by a Reference documentation).

The first part of this note gives an **overall view** of the different linear solvers available, of their scope of use and of their average performances in terms of robustness and of CPU/memory consumptions.

The following chapter groups together a few **pieces of advice** for making the best use of the parameters of SOLVEUR according to the case in hand: the size of the system, its numerical properties, the CPU and memory consumptions... To finish, certain parameters of SOLVEUR are detailed in order to help the user in an **advanced use** of this functionality of the code.

The **related issues** of improvement of the performances (RAM/CPU) of an analysis and of the use of parallelism are also briefly addressed. They are the subject of specific guides, respectively [U1.03.03](doc:u1.03.03) and [U2.08.06](doc:u2.08.06).

## 2 The different linear solvers available

These **linear solvers are in fact omnipresent in the course of the operators** of *Code_Aster*, because they are often buried in the deepest part of other numerical algorithms: non-linear scheme, integration in time, modal analysis, etc. They often consume the major part of the CPU time and of the memory. The choice and the complete parameter setting of the required linear solver are carried out *via* the factor keyword SOLVEUR. It is present in most of the analysis commands (STAT_NON_LINE, THER_LINEAIRE, CALC_MODES...).

This keyword makes it possible to choose between the two classes of solver: the direct ones and the iterative ones. Concerning the **direct** ones, one has the classical algorithm of "Gauss" (SOLVEUR/METHODE='LDLT'), a multifrontal factorisation ('MULT_FRONT') and an external one ('MUMPS'). For the **iterative** ones, it is possible to call a conjugate gradient ('GCPC') or certain tools of the public library PETSc ('PETSC').

Only MULT_FRONT, MUMPS and PETSC are **parallelised**. The first in OpenMP, the other two in MPI and in OpenMP (hybrid parallelism). But all the solvers are compatible with a parallel treatment (*via* MPI) of the elementary computations and of the assemblies. And this, whether these treatments are initiated just before the use of the linear solver proper, or in another operator (for example of pre/post-processing).

Let us detail a little the functioning of each of them:

| **Direct solvers** |  |
| --- | --- |
| **/'MUMPS'** | **Direct solver of multifrontal type** with pivoting. This solver is obtained by calling the **external product MUMPS**, developed by CERFACS/IRIT/INRIA/CNRS (cf. Copyright §4). The matrix storage outside the solver is MORSE. On entry to the solver, one carries out the conversion to the internal format of MUMPS: $i$, $j$, ${K}_{ij}$, centralised or distributed. For *Code_Aster*, **its principal interest lies in its capacity to pivot** rows and/or columns of the matrix during the factorisation in the event of a small pivot. This possibility is useful (indeed indispensable) for the models leading to matrices which are not positive definite (apart from the boundary conditions); for example, the 'mixed' elements having degrees of freedom of 'Lagrange' type (incompressible elements...). This **method is parallelised in distributed memory** (MPI) and in **shared memory** (OpenMP). It can be executed on several processors (*via* the Astk interface, menu Options/Options de lancement/ncpus&mpi_nbcpu&mpi_nbnoeud). **In MPI parallel** (mpi_nbcpu&mpi_nbnoeud)**, MUMPS naturally distributes its data** (matrix, factorised form...) among the cores of the different computation nodes allocated. This greatly accelerates the analyses and makes it possible to reduce the memory occupation necessary, per MPI process, in order to launch the analysis. This RAM consumption can be reduced still further *via* the parameters GESTION_MEMOIRE or RENUM of MUMPS. In terms of memory, the bottleneck may then lie at the level of the JEVEUX space. In order to reduce the latter, one can distribute the *Aster* matrix (MATR_ASSE) *via* the option MATR_DISTRIBUEE. MUMPS also relies on a **second level of nested parallelism** within the MPI parallelism (**hybrid parallelism**), based on OpenMP. This second level of parallelism is activated mainly in the calls to the underlying mathematical libraries (BLAS, LAPACK). Unlike MPI, it is a shared-memory parallelism and is therefore limited to the cores of a single computation node (ncpus). In order to accelerate large studies (N at least > 2.106 degrees of freedom) one can in addition activate acceleration/compression options of MUMPS (from v5.1.0 onwards) *via* the keywords ACCELERATION/LOW_RANK_SEUIL. |
|  |  |
| **/'MULT_FRONT'** | **Direct solver of multifrontal type developed internally at EDF R&D**. The matrix storage is MORSE (or 'CSC' for 'Compressed Sparse Column') and therefore rules out any pivoting. This method is parallelised in shared memory (OpenMP) and can be executed on several processors (*via* the Astk interface, menu Options/Options de lancement/ncpus). The initial matrix is stored in a single JEVEUX object and its factorised form is distributed over several, so it can be offloaded partially and automatically to disk. |
| **/'LDLT'** | **Direct solver with Crout factorisation** by blocks (without pivoting) **developed internally at EDF R&D**. The matrix storage outside the solver is MORSE. On entry to the solver, one carries out the conversion to the internal format of LDLT: 'skyline' ('SKYLINE'). One has a completely parameterisable paging of the memory (the matrix is decomposed into blocks managed independently in memory and offloaded to disk as one goes along), which makes it possible to treat large cases but which is paid for by costly disk accesses. Moreover, this solver makes it possible to factorise the matrix only partially. This possibility is 'historical'. It makes it possible to factorise the matrix in several 'goes' (several jobs), or even to modify on the fly the last rows of this factorised form. Today, one does not really see the interest of this functionality except for certain methods (said to be discrete) of frictional contact, in which one has deliberately placed, in the last rows of the matrix, the terms concerning the nodes liable to be in contact. Thus, as the pairing iterations proceed and the relations between these nodes change, one erases and then recomputes only these last contributions of the factorised form. It is a typical example in which the clever use of a fairly crude algorithm can bring major gains (in time). |
|  |  |
| **Iterative solvers** |  |
| /'GCPC' | **Iterative solver of conjugate-gradient type with preconditioning** $\mathit{ILU}(k)$ **or based on a single-precision factorised form (** **via** **MUMPS).** The storage of the matrix is then MORSE. With the incomplete Cholesky, the initial matrix and its incomplete factorised form are each stored in a single JEVEUX object. With the single-precision factorised form, the preconditioner is much more costly (in CPU/RAM), but it is carried out in single precision and its computation can be shared over several resolutions (problem of multiple right-hand sides type, e.g. STAT_NON_LINE). |
|  |  |
| /'PETSC' | **Iterative solvers coming from the external library PETSc** (Argonne Laboratory). The matrix storage outside the solver is MORSE. On entry to the solver, one carries out the conversion to the internal format of PETSc: 'CSR' for 'Compressed Sparse Row'. PETSc allocates blocks of contiguous rows per processor. This **method is parallelised in distributed memory** (MPI) and can be executed on several processors (*via* the Astk interface, menu Options/Options de lancement/mpi_nbcpu&mpi_nbnoeud). When PETSc uses a preconditioner based on MUMPS (PRECOND='LDLT_SP'), it also benefits from the potentially hybrid parallelism of the direct solver. But the best accelerations of PETSc occur rather by favouring the first level of parallelism, MPI. **Warning:** the solvers PETSC and MUMPS being incompatible in sequential mode, one generally favours MUMPS. In order to use PETSC, one must therefore often launch a parallel version of *Code_Aster* (even if one calls upon only a single processor). |

**Note:**

- *To be completely exhaustive, one may point out that a few (rare) numerical operations are carried out with a "frozen" solver parameter setting. The latter is therefore inaccessible to users (without a software overload). Among these, one finds certain resolutions of the discrete methods of frictional contact (reserved for* *LDLT), the searches for rigid-body modes in modal analysis, the computations of interface modes (reserved for* *MUMPS* *or for* *LDLT)... Each time, functional reasons or reasons of performance explain this not very transparent choice.*

OOC stands for 'Out-Of-Core'. That is to say that one is going to free RAM by offloading part of the objects to disk. This makes it possible to supplement the system swap and to treat much larger problems. Depending on the algorithmics, these additional disk accesses may be penalising. The opposite mode of management is "In-Core" (IC). All the computer objects remain in RAM. This limits the size of the problems accessible (to within the system swap) but favours speed.

## 3 A few pieces of advice on use

**If one wishes** 2
**to change linear solver or to adapt its parameter setting, several questions must be addressed:** What is the type of problem which one wishes to solve? What are the numerical properties of the linear systems encountered? etc.

One lists below, and in a non-exhaustive manner, several questions which it is interesting to ask oneself when one seeks to optimise the linear-solver aspects. Of course, certain questions (and answers) are cumulative and may therefore apply simultaneously.

**In brief:**

**The default method remains the internal multifrontal solver** **MULT_FRONT**. But in order to benefit fully from the CPU and RAM gains which **parallelism** provides, or in order to solve a **problem which is numerically often difficult** (X-FEM, incompressibility, THM), one recommends the use of the external product **MUMPS**. The larger the problem, the more one advises the use of MPI parallelism and of the acceleration/compression techniques (ACCELERATION/LOW_RANK_SEUIL).

If, in spite of everything, on a given computer platform, the problem does not fit into memory, recourse to the iterative solvers of PETSC may be a solution (if their functional scopes permit it). Let us note that by default they use MUMPS "under the bonnet" as a preconditioner (option PRE_COND='LDLT_SP').

In order to go further in the **savings of memory space**, one can also degrade the preconditioning (the analysis will probably be longer) by choosing one of the other preconditioners of PETSC ('LDLT_INC'...).

In **non-linear analysis, in order to gain time**, one can also play on several relaxation parameters (SYME in the non-symmetric case) or of interactions between "non-linear solver/linear solver" (REAC_PRECOND, NEWTON_KRYLOV..).

For **well-conditioned** non-linear problems (thermal...), recourse to a "relaxed" MUMPS (MIXER_PRECISION/FILTRAGE_MATRICE) can bring very significant memory gains. Likewise, in linear as in non-linear analysis, with PETSC without preconditioner (PRE_COND='SANS').

**For more details and more advice** on the use of the linear solvers, one may consult the user's guide [U4.50.01](doc:u4.50.01) and the associated reference documentations [R6...]. The related issues of improvement of the performances (RAM/CPU) of an analysis and of the use of parallelism are also the subject of detailed guides: [U1.03.03](doc:u1.03.03) and [U2.08.06](doc:u2.08.06).

**What is the type of problem to be solved?**

- **Resolution of numerous systems with the same matrix** (problem of multiple right-hand sides type 3) **⇒** solvers MULT_FRONT or MUMPS (if possible by deactivating RESI_RELA and with GESTION_MEMOIRE='IN_CORE').
- **Standard parametric analysis in linear** **⇒** a first trial with MUMPS, leaving the parameters at their default values, then all the other runs by switching off RESI_RELA or with POSTTRAITEMENTS='MINI'.
- **Parametric analysis in non-linear with a well-conditioned matrix** **⇒** a first trial with MUMPS, playing on the relaxation parameters (FILTRAGE_MATRICE/MIXER_PRECISION, or SYME if one is in the non-symmetric case). Then, if an optimised operating point (CPU/RAM consumptions) has been found, use it for all the other runs.

One can also try MUMPS but, this time, as a single-precision preconditioner of PETSC/GCPC (LDLT_SP). The interest is then to update it only periodically (REAC_PRECOND).

**What are its numerical properties?**

- **Well-conditioned linear system** 4 (<104) **⇒** solvers MUMPS+MIXER_PRECISION or GCPC/PETSC.
- **Difficult linear system** (poor conditioning, mixed finite elements, predominance of Lagranges...) **⇒** solver MUMPS.

**Does one need a very accurate solution?**

- One may be satisfied with a **very approximate solution** [5]_ **⇒** solvers GCPC or PETSC with a RESI_RELA=10-3, or MUMPS with LOW_RANK_SEUIL<10-9 and POSTTRAITEMENTS='SANS'.
- One wants an **accurate solution**, or at least a diagnosis of its quality and of the numerical difficulties of the system to be solved **⇒** solver MUMPS, activating NPREC and RESI_RELA (with INFO=2).

**How can one optimise the time/RAM consumptions of the linear solver?**

- **Time** ⇒ solver MUMPS, deactivating the OOC (GESTION_MEMORE='IN_CORE') or even RESI_RELA. Hybrid MPI/OpenMP parallel analysis (cf. doc. U2 on parallelism). On problems of large size, use of the low_rank compressions and of the accelerations *via* the keywords ACCELERATION/LOW_RANK_SEUIL.
- **Memory** ⇒ solver MUMPS, activating GESTION_MEMOIRE='OUT_OF_CORE'/MATR_DISTRIBUE and in distributed parallel mode. Or an iterative solver of Krylov type: GCPC/PETSC+LDLT_SP.

In non-linear analysis, if the matrix is well conditioned and/or non-symmetric, one can also play on the relaxation parameters of MUMPS (FILTRAGE_MATRICE, MIXER_PRECISION and SYME) or use an iterative solver by reducing the cost of the preconditioning. More details will be found in the discussion of § 7.2.6.

- **Memory** ⇒ solver PETSC, activating MATR_DISTRIBUE and in distributed parallel mode.

**Is it a frontier problem of very large size (> 5.10** 6 degrees of freedom **)?**

- **Robust solver** ⇒ solver MUMPS with the previous memory optimisations and the low-rank compressions (ACCELERATION/LOW_RANK_SEUIL).
- **Solvers of "last resort"** ⇒ iterative solvers (with a low level of preconditioning).

**How can one optimise the global performances of my analysis?**

- User's guide [U1.03.03](doc:u1.03.03).

**How can one carry out, calibrate and optimise a parallel analysis?**

- User's guide [U2.08.06](doc:u2.08.06).

Or if, quite simply, one must play on this parameter because the analysis does not fit on the software platform chosen, or does so with consumptions in time and in memory incompatible with the constraints of the study.

This is the case, for example, in a thermomechanical chaining when the material characteristics do not depend on the temperature or, in non-linear analysis, when one updates the tangent matrix only infrequently.

Either the typology of the analysis provides this information (one knows, for example, that this is often the case for thermal analysis), or one obtains it otherwise (for example, by carrying out a preliminary test with MUMPS and by activating the keyword RESI_RELA+INFO=2).

Convex non-linear analyses, a prospective analysis, or an analysis the quality of whose mechanical solutions of interest is mastered by other means.

## 5 Links with parallelism

**Note:** for further information one may consult the user's guide dedicated to parallelism [U2.08.06](doc:u2.08.06).

### 5.1 General points

Often a *Code_Aster* simulation can **benefit from significant gains in performance** by distributing its computations over several cores of a PC or over one or several nodes of a centralised machine.

![Figure](/figures/u2.08.03/100000000000031B000001DEEA0EBF441461F79C.png)

[U4.50.01](doc:u4.50.01)), of Code_Aster v13.1. Comparisons carried out on the performance test cases perf008d and perf009d and on the centralised machine Aster5.

One can **gain in time** (with MPI parallelism and with OpenMP parallelism) as well as in **memory** (only *via* MPI). These gains vary according to the functionalities called upon, their parameter settings, the data set and the software platform used: cf. figure 5.1.1.

In *Code_Aster,* by default, the analysis is sequential. But one can activate **different parallelisation strategies**. These depend on the computational step considered and on the parameter setting chosen. They are often cumulative, or can be chained.

  - One has three broad classes of problem which can be parallelised, the second being the most common:
- either the simulation can be organised as several **independent sub-analyses** (cf. §5.2),
- or this is not the case, but:

  - it remains **dominated by linear or non-linear analyses** (operators STAT/DYNA/THER_NON_LINE, MECA_STATIQUE... cf. §5.3),
  - it remains **dominated by modal analyses** which can be divided into frequency sub-bands (INFO_MODE/CALC_MODES+'BANDE', cf. §5.4).

In order to have an **estimate of the time spent by an operator**, and therefore of the predominant steps of an analysis, one can activate the keyword MESURE_TEMPS of the commands DEBUT/POURSUITE (cf. [U1.03.03](doc:u1.03.03)) on a typical study (possibly shortened or watered down).

In every case, one will advise **dividing the largest analyses into different steps**, in order to separate those which are purely **computational** [6]_
, from those concerning **displays**, **post-processings** and **manipulations of fields** [7]_
.

### 5.2 Independent analyses

**When the simulation can be organised as different independent** **Aster** **sub-analyses** (cf. figure 5.2.1)**,** the Astk tool [U1.04.00] offers a suitable functionality (cf. [U2.08.07](doc:u2.08.07)). The latter distributes these sub-analyses over different machine resources and retrieves their results. It is a completely "computer-science" parallel scheme.

The limit being, for the moment, that all these unit analyses must each be able to run sequentially on the machine chosen (which can sometimes pose problems of memory resources, cf. [U4.50.01](doc:u4.50.01)).

### 5.3 Parallelisation of linear systems

  - When this simulation cannot be decomposed into similar and independent *Aster* sub-analyses, but nevertheless remains **dominated by linear** or **non-linear analyses** (operators STAT/DYNA/THER_NON_LINE, MECA_STATIQUE... cf. §5.3), one can organise a specific parallel scheme.

It is founded on the **distribution of the tasks** and of the **data structures** involved in the manipulations of **linear systems**. For it is these steps of construction and of resolution of linear systems which are often the most demanding in computation time and in memory resources. They are present in most of the operators, because they are buried in the deepest part of other, "more application-oriented", algorithms: non-linear solver, modal and vibratory analysis, time-stepping scheme...

The first step of the parallel scheme concerns the distribution of the finite elements of the model over all the MPI processes. Each MPI process will therefore manage only the treatments and the data associated with the elements for which it is responsible. The construction of the linear systems in *Code_Aster* (elementary computations, assemblies) is then accelerated. One often speaks of "**parallelism in space**". It is a parallel scheme of a rather "computer-science" kind.

Once these portions of linear system have been built (cf. figure 5.3.1), two cases arise:

- either the **following treatment is naturally sequential**, and therefore all the MPI processes must have access to the global information. To this end one gathers these pieces of linear systems together, and the following step will therefore be neither accelerated nor see its memory consumptions fall. It is most often the end of an operator, a post-processing, or a linear solver which is not parallelised in MPI (MULT_FRONT, LDLT, GCPC).
- or the **following treatment accepts MPI parallelism**; this is then mainly the HPC linear solvers MUMPS and PETSC. The parallel flow of data built upstream is then transmitted to them (after a few adaptations). These packages of linear algebra then reorganise, internally, their own parallel schemes (with a more algebraic view). One then speaks of a parallel scheme of a rather "numerical" kind. This combination of "computer-science parallelism", at the level of the assembly of the linear system, and of "numerical parallelism", at the level of its resolution, the two of them *via* MPI, is the most common combination.

Moreover, one can **superimpose upon this MPI parallelism, or substitute for it,** (it works on all platforms) another level of parallelism, managed this time by the **OpenMP language**. The latter is however limited to the fractions of a machine which physically share the same memory (multi-core PC, or nodes of a computation server).

It does not make it possible to lower the memory consumptions, but on the other hand it accelerates certain types of computation, and does so with a finer granularity than that of MPI: it provides a better acceleration even if the flow of data/treatments is not very large. It is a parallel scheme of "computer-science" kind, which intervenes mainly in the basic operations of linear-algebra algorithms (*via*, for example, the BLAS library and certain steps of the MUMPS solver).

This parallelism may:

- either be cumulated with the MPI parallelism of MUMPS, by accelerating the computations within each MPI process. One then obtains a hybrid parallel scheme with 2 levels.
- or be substituted for the MPI parallelism, by accelerating the resolution of the linear system with MULT_FRONT.

### 7.2 MUMPS solver (METHODE='MUMPS')

#### 7.2.1 General points

The **MUMPS solver**, currently developed by **CNRS/INPT-IRIT/INRIA/CERFACS**, is a **direct solver of multifrontal type, parallelised** (in MPI) and robust, because it makes it possible to pivot the rows and columns of the matrix during the numerical factorisation.

MUMPS supplies an estimate of the quality of the solution $\text{u}$ (cf. keyword RESI_RELA) of the matrix problem $\text{K}\text{u}=\text{f}$ *via* the notions of *relative forward error* ('relative forward error') and of *backward* error ('backward error'). This 'backward error', $\eta (\text{K},\text{f})$, measures the behaviour of the resolution algorithm (when all goes well, this real number is close to machine precision, that is 10-15 in double precision). MUMPS also computes an estimate of the conditioning of the matrix, $\kappa (\text{K})$, which reflects the good behaviour of the problem to be solved (a real number lying between 104 for a well-conditioned problem and 1020 for a very poorly conditioned one). The product of the two is an upper bound on the relative error in the solution ('relative forward error'):

$\frac{\parallel \delta u\parallel }{\parallel u\parallel }<{C}^{\mathrm{st}}\cdot \kappa (\text{K})\cdot \eta (\text{K},\text{f})$

By specifying a strictly positive value for the keyword RESI_RELA (e.g. 10-6), the user indicates that he wishes to test the validity of the solution of each linear system solved by MUMPS against this value. If the product $\kappa (\text{K})\cdot \eta (\text{K},\text{f})$ is greater than RESI_RELA, the code stops in ERREUR_FATALE, stating the nature of the problem and the values incriminated. With the display INFO=2, one details each of the terms of the product: $\eta (\text{K},\text{f})$ and $\kappa (\text{K})$.

In order to continue the analysis, one can then:

- *Increase the tolerance of* *RESI_RELA*. For poorly conditioned problems, a tolerance of 10-3 is not rare. But it must be taken seriously, because this type of pathology can seriously disturb an analysis (cf. the following note on conditioning, and §3.4).
- *If* *it is the 'backward error'* which is too large: it is advised to modify the resolution algorithm. That is to say, in our case, to play on the launching parameters of MUMPS (TYPE_RESOL, PRETRAITEMENTS...).
- *If* *it is the conditioning of the operator* which is the cause, it is advised to balance the terms of the matrix, outside MUMPS or *via* MUMPS (PRETRAITEMENTS='OUI'), or to change the formulation of the problem.

**Note:**

- *Even within the very precise framework of the resolution of a linear system, there exist numerous ways of defining the sensitivity to rounding errors of the problem considered (that is to say, its conditioning). The one retained by MUMPS, and which is a reference in the field (cf. Arioli, Demmel and Duff 1989), is inseparable from the 'backward error' of the problem. The definition of the one has no meaning without that of the other. One must therefore not confuse this type of conditioning with the notion of classical matrix conditioning.*

*On the other hand, the conditioning supplied by MUMPS takes into account the RIGHT-HAND SIDE of the system as well as the SPARSE CHARACTER of the matrix. Indeed, there is no point in taking into account possible rounding errors on matrix terms which are zero and which are therefore not supplied to the solver! The corresponding degrees of freedom do not "speak to one another" (seen through the finite-element spyglass). Thus, this MUMPS conditioning respects the physics of the discretised problem. It does not plunge the problem back into the over-rich space of full matrices.*

**Thus, the conditioning figure displayed by MUMPS is much less pessimistic than the standard computation which another product may supply** *(Matlab, Python...). But let us insist that it is only its product with the 'backward error', called the 'forward error', which is of interest. And only within the framework of a resolution of a linear system via MUMPS.*

#### 7.2.2 Scope of use

It is a universal linear solver. It is deployed for all the functionalities of *Code_Aster.*

On the other hand, use of the solver suffers from small limitations, which are infrequent and which one can easily get round should the need arise.

- In POURSUITE mode one saves to file only the FORTRAN objects of code_aster, and therefore not the occurrences of external products (MUMPS, PETSc). So take care with the use of the exploded commands in this framework (NUME_DDL/FACTORISER/RESOUDRE). With MUMPS, it is not possible to FACTORISER or to RESOUDRE a linear system built during a previous Aster run (with NUME_DDL).
- Likewise, one limits the number of simultaneous occurrences of MUMPS to NMXINS=50. During the construction of his problem *via* exploded commands, the user must take care not to exceed this figure, otherwise the analysis stops in error. To this end, one can for example destroy the user concepts associated with the matrices (for example *via* the command del in the .comm, cf. test case mumps03a.comm).
- With PETSc, on the other hand, this figure is limited to 5, because the data structure is potentially richer and this library of iterative solvers is not commonly used in the exploded commands (only in the implicit operators which solve one or two linear systems simultaneously).

#### 7.2.3 Parameter RENUM

This keyword makes it possible to control the tool used to renumber the linear system [11]_
. The *Aster* user can choose different tools, divided into two families: the "crude" tools, dedicated to one use and supplied with MUMPS ('AMD', 'AMF', 'QAMD', 'PORD'), and the richer and more "sophisticated" libraries, which must be installed separately ('PARMETIS'/'METIS', 'PTSCOTCH'/'SCOTCH').

The **choice of the renumberer** is of great importance for the memory and time consumptions of the linear solver. If one seeks to **optimise/tune the numerical parameters** linked to the linear solver, this parameter must be **one of the first to be tried**.

The MUMPS product decomposes its computations into three steps (cf. [R6.02.03](doc:r6.02.03) §1.6): analysis phase, numerical factorisation, and forward-backward substitution. In certain cases, **the analysis step may prove predominant**. Either because the problem is numerically difficult (numerous blockings, links or contact zones, incompressible elements...), or because the other two steps have been greatly reduced thanks to parallelism (cf. [U2.08.06](doc:u2.08.06)). It may then be of interest to parallelise this analysis step (*via* MPI). This makes it possible to accelerate it and to lower its memory consumption. This is done by choosing one of the parallel renumberers proposed: 'PARMETIS' or 'PTSCOTCH'.

The choice of such a parallel renumberer, while it often improves the performances of the analysis step of MUMPS, may nevertheless degrade those of the two following MUMPS steps. Nevertheless, if the main problem was to reduce the memory consumption of this analysis step, or if the following steps of MUMPS benefit from sufficient parallelism (or from compression, cf. parameters ACCELERATION/LOW_RANK_SEUIL), the balance may be positive overall.

On the other hand, during **parallel modal analyses** (operator CALC_MODES), disappointing speed-ups have sometimes been observed because of an inappropriate choice of renumberer. In that case it was found that the choice of a "sophisticated" renumberer was counterproductive. It is better to impose on MUMPS a simple 'AMF' or 'QAMD', rather than 'METIS' or 'PARMETIS' (often taken automatically in 'AUTO' mode).

#### 7.2.4 Parameter ELIM_LAGR2

Historically, the direct linear solvers of *Code_Aster* ('MULT_FRONT' and 'LDLT') did not have a pivoting algorithm (which seeks to avoid the accumulation of rounding errors by division by very small terms). In order to get round this problem, the taking into account of the boundary conditions by Lagranges (AFFE_CHAR_MECA/THER…) was modified by introducing double Lagranges. Formally, one does not work with the initial matrix ${\mathrm{K}}_{0}$

> ${\mathrm{K}}_{0}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{K}& \mathrm{blocage}\\ \mathrm{blocage}& 0\end{array}\right]\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{u}\\ \mathrm{lagr}\end{array}$

but with its doubly dualised form ${\mathrm{K}}_{2}$

> ${K}_{2}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}K& \mathrm{blocage}& \mathrm{blocage}\\ \mathrm{blocage}& -1& 1\\ \mathrm{blocage}& 1& -1\end{array}\right]\begin{array}{}u\\ {\mathrm{lagr}}_{1}\\ {\mathrm{lagr}}_{2}\end{array}$

Whence an overhead in memory and in computation.

Since MUMPS has pivoting capabilities, this choice of dualisation of the boundary conditions can be called into question. By initialising this keyword to 'OUI', **one no longer takes into account more than one Lagrange, the other being a spectator** [12]_
. Whence a working matrix ${\mathrm{K}}_{1}$, simply dualised

> ${K}_{1}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}K& \mathrm{blocage}& 0\\ \mathrm{blocage}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& -1\end{array}\right]\begin{array}{}u\\ {\mathrm{lagr}}_{1}\\ {\mathrm{lagr}}_{2}\end{array}$

which is smaller, because the off-diagonal terms of the rows and of the columns associated with these spectator Lagranges are then initialised to zero. *Conversely*, with the value 'NON', MUMPS receives the usual dualised matrices.

For **problems comprising numerous Lagranges (up to 20% of the total number of unknowns)**, activating this parameter often pays (smaller matrix). But when **this number explodes (>20%)**, the process may be counterproductive. The gains achieved on the matrix are cancelled out by the size of the factorised form and, above all, by the number of delayed pivots which MUMPS must carry out. Imposing ELIM_LAGR2='NON' may then be very advantageous (for example: a gain of 40% in CPU time on the test case mac3c01).

One also **switches off** this parameter temporarily when one wishes to compute **the determinant of the matrix**, because otherwise its value is falsified by these modifications of the blocking terms. The user is warned of this automatic modification of the parameter setting by a dedicated message (visible only with INFO=2).

#### 7.2.5 Parameter RESI_RELA

Default value = -1.d0 in non-linear and in modal analysis, 1.d-6 in linear analysis.
This parameter is deactivated by a negative value.

By specifying a strictly positive value for this keyword (e.g. 10-6), the user indicates that he wishes to test the validity of the solution of each linear system solved by MUMPS against this value.

This cautious approach is advised when the solution is not itself corrected by another algorithmic process (Newton algorithm, detection of singularity…), in short in the linear operators THER_LINEAIRE and MECA_STATIQUE. In non-linear analysis, the singularity-detection criterion and the Newton correction are sufficient safeguards. One can therefore switch off this control process (this is what is done by default *via* the value -1). In modal analysis, this detection of singularity is an algorithmic tool for capturing the eigenmodes. This detection is the subject of a dedicated parameter setting, proper to each method.

If the relative error in the solution estimated by MUMPS is greater than resi, the code stops in ERREUR_FATALE, stating the nature of the problem and the values incriminated.

Activating this keyword also initiates an iterative-refinement process, whose objective is to improve the solution obtained. This post-processing benefits from a particular parameter setting (keyword POSTTRAITEMENTS). It is the solution resulting from this iterative improvement process which is tested by RESI_RELA.

**Note:**

- *This control process implies the estimation of the matrix conditioning and a few forward-backward substitutions of the iterative-refinement post-processing. It may therefore be fairly costly, notably in OOC, because of the RAM/disk I/O during the forward-backward substitutions (up to 40%). When sufficient safeguards have been put in place, one can switch it off by initialising* *resi to a negative value.*

#### 7.2.6 Parameters for optimising the memory management (MUMPS and/or JEVEUX)

In general, a large part of the computation times and of the RAM peaks of a *Code_Aster* simulation is attributable to the linear solvers. The linear solver MUMPS is no exception, but the richness of its internal parameter setting and its close coupling with *Code_Aster* leave the user a certain flexibility. In particular as regards the management of the RAM consumption.

In fact, **during a memory peak** occurring for the resolution of a linear system *via* MUMPS, the RAM may be decomposed into 5 parts:

- The JEVEUX objects other than the matrix,
- The JEVEUX objects associated with the matrix (generally the data structures MATR_ASSE and NUME_DDL),
- The MUMPS objects used to store the matrix,
- The MUMPS objects used to store the factorised form,
- The auxiliary MUMPS objects (pointers, vectors, communication buffers...).

Roughly speaking, part 4 is the most bulky. In particular, it is much larger than parts 2 and 3 (a factor of at least 30, due to the fill-in phenomenon, cf. [R6.02.03](doc:r6.02.03)). The latter are equivalent in size, but one of them occupies the space allotted to JEVEUX, while the other lives in the complementary space allocated to the job by the task manager. As for the other two parts, 1 and 5, they often play only a marginal role [13]_
.

In order to reduce these memory consumptions, the *Aster* **user has several levers** (cumulative, most of the time):

- **Centralised parallel analysis** on n cores (part 4 divided by $n$) or distributed (parts 3 and 4 divided by $n$).
- **Distributed parallel analysis** + **MATR_DISTRIBUEE** (limited scope of use): parts 3 and 4 divided by $n$, part 2 divided by a little less than $n$.
- Activation of the **OOC of** **MUMPS** (keyword GESTION_MEMOIRE='OUT-OF-CORE'): part 4 is reduced by 2/3.

It being understood that, before each call to the 'analysis + numerical factorisation' cycle of MUMPS, one systematically offloads to disk the largest JEVEUX objects of part 2.

**Advice:**

In view of the preceding points, an obvious tactic consists in running the analysis in distributed parallel mode (the default value) rather than sequentially. Centralised parallel mode bringing nothing from this point of view [14]_
, it is not to be considered. If, however, one remains tied to a particular mode, here is the advice associated with each:

- *In sequential mode:* the principal gains will come from activating GESTION_MEMOIRE='OUT_OF_CORE' (on problems of reasonable size).
- *In distributed parallel mode:* beyond about ten processors, OUT_OF_CORE no longer provides much gain. MATR_DISTRIBUEE can then help in certain situations.

If these strategies do not bring sufficient gains, one can also try, in non-linear analysis (at the price of possible losses of accuracy and/or of time), to relax the resolution of the linear system (FILTRAGE_MATRICE, MIXER_PRECISION), or even that of the encompassing process (elastic tangent matrix, reduction of the projection space in modal analysis...). If the matrix is well conditioned and if one does not need to detect the singularities of the problem (hence not a modal analysis, not buckling...), one can also try an iterative solver (GCPC/PETSC+LDLT_SP).

In short, on very large cases (several million degrees of* *freedom), even with this OOC, memory contingencies may prevent the analysis. One exits, in principle, with a documented* *ERREUR_FATALE.*
- *In MUMPS, so as to optimise the memory occupation, most of the integers are coded in* INTEGER*4 *. Only the integers corresponding to a memory address are transcribed into* INTEGER*8 *. This makes it possible to address problems of larger size, on 64-bit architectures. this coexistence of short and long integers, in order to optimise the memory space, has been extended to certain large* JEVEUX *objects.*
- *When Code_Aster has finished assembling the working matrix, before "handing over" to MUMPS, it offloads to disk the largest* JEVEUX *objects linked to the resolution of the linear system (* SMDI/HC, DEEQ, NUEAQ, VALM. *..). And this in order to leave as much RAM as possible for MUMPS. In* GESTION_MEMOIRE='AUTO' *mode, if this memory space is not sufficient for MUMPS to operate in IC, one completes this partial release of* JEVEUX *objects by a general release of all the releasable objects (that is to say, those not open for reading/writing). This operation can provide large gains when one is "dragging" in memory many peripheral* JEVEUX *objects (projection of fields, a long transient...). On the other hand, this massive offloading can waste time. In particular in parallel mode, because of congestion of the accesses between cores and RAM.*

*Keyword* *MATR_DISTRIBUEE*

**This parameter can be used in the operators** MECA_STATIQUE, STAT_NON_LINE, DYNA_NON_LINE, THER_LINEAIRE and THER_NON_LINE **with** AFFE_CHAR_MECA **or** AFFE_CHAR_CINE**. It is active only in distributed parallel (** AFFE_MODELE/ DISTRIBUTION/ METHODE equal to something other than CENTRALISE **)**. This functionality can of course be cumulated with GESTION_MEMOIRE='OUT_OF_CORE', whence a greater variety of modes of operation, so as to adapt to the contingencies of execution.

In parallel mode, when one distributes the JEVEUX data upstream of MUMPS, one does not necessarily recut the data structures concerned. With the option MATR_DISTRIBUEE='NON', all the distributed objects are allocated and initialised to the same size (the same value as in sequential mode). On the other hand, each processor will modify only the parts of the JEVEUX objects for which it is responsible. This scenario is particularly well suited to the distributed parallel mode of MUMPS (the default mode), because this product gathers these incomplete data flows together internally. Parallelism then makes it possible, besides the gains in computation time, to reduce the memory space required by the MUMPS resolution, but not that necessary for the construction of the problem in JEVEUX.

This is not a problem so long as the RAM space for JEVEUX remains very much smaller than that required by MUMPS. Since JEVEUX stores mainly the matrix, and MUMPS its factorised form (generally tens of times larger), the RAM bottleneck of the analysis is theoretically on MUMPS. But as soon as one uses a few tens of processors in MPI and/or one activates OOC, since MUMPS distributes this factorised form per processor and offloads these pieces to disk, the "ball is back in JEVEUX's court".

Whence the option MATR_DISTRIBUEE, which resizes the matrix down to just the non-zero terms for which the processor is responsible. The JEVEUX space required then decreases with the number of processors, and falls below the RAM necessary for MUMPS. The results of figure 7.2-1 illustrate this gain in parallel on two studies: an RIS pump and the Epicure vessel.

#### 7.2.7 Parameters for reducing the run time via different acceleration/compression techniques

```text
Mots-clés ACCELERATION et LOW_RANK_SEUIL
```

**These two keywords activate and drive acceleration/compression techniques of MUMPS**. These can significantly reduce the run time of large studies, and this without restriction of the scope of use, and with potentially little or no impact on the accuracy, the robustness and the global behaviour of the simulation.

**They are generally of interest only on problems of large size (N at least > 2.106 degrees of freedom)**. The **gains observed** on a few performance test cases and Aster studies vary from **20% to 80%** (examples in figures 7.2-2/7.2-3). They increase with the size of the problem and with its massive character, and they are complementary to those provided by parallelism and by the renumberer.

One completes here the description of these functionalities given in the document [U4.50.01](doc:u4.50.01).

When one has chosen an acceleration based on 'low-rank' compressions (values 'LR' and 'LR+' of the keyword ACCELERATION), one must define the rate of these compressions. This rate is given by the keyword LOW_RANK_SEUIL. It drives the truncation criterion of the numerical compression algorithm [17]_
. *Roughly*, the larger this figure is, for example 10-12 or 10-9, the greater the compression will be, and therefore the more interesting the gains in time can be.

However, if this value is too large (for example >10-9), the factorised matrix may be too approximate, and the solution vector may thus prove too inaccurate! In a non-linear process this is not always so serious, because the encompassing Newton algorithm can correct the aim!

On the other hand, in linear analysis, or in order to treat numerically difficult problems (incompressible finite elements, X-FEM...), one must then make sure that the resolution does indeed include the corrective post-processing procedure (cf. iterative refinement, keyword POSTTRAITEMENTS). As it happens, in this situation, or when one seeks to strike a compromise between performance and accuracy, the value POSTTRAITEMENTS='MINI' (+ RESI_RELA<0) is often to be preferred to the default choice (POSTTRAITEMENTS='AUTO' + RESI_RELA>0).

To be exhaustive, let us note that the real value of this keyword may also be zero — the compression is then carried out to within machine precision — or become negative — the compression then uses the relative threshold

> $∥\mathrm{K}∥\times \mid \text{lr\_seuil}\mid$

The first value makes it possible to benefit from a little compression with no impact on the accuracy (for functional tests or for expert assessments).

In summary, the approximation criterion of the compression algorithms of MUMPS is fixed according to the following rule:

> If LOW_RANK_SEUIL=0.D0:

> truncation to within machine precision.

> If LOW_RANK_SEUIL>0.D0:

> the truncation of the compression algorithms uses lr_seuil directly.

> If LOW_RANK_SEUIL<0.D0:

> the truncation of the compression algorithms is based on the relative threshold recalled above.
