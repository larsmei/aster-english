# r5.05.04 Modelling of damping in linear dynamics

**Summary:**

The linear dynamic analyses of structures subjected to imposed forces or motions require mechanical damping characteristics to be added to the stiffness and mass characteristics of the model.

Several classical modellings are available, applicable to all the types of finite elements available:

- the viscous damping model,
- the hysteretic damping model (also called “structural damping”)

for the harmonic analysis of viscoelastic materials.

For the analyses using dynamic response methods by modal recombination, with a modal basis of real eigenmodes, it is possible to introduce modal damping coefficients.

## 1 Notion of mechanical damping

### 1.1 Damping models

The motion of structures subjected to imposed forces or motions, variable with time, depends in particular on the damping properties, that is to say on the dissipation of energy in the constitutive materials of the structure and in the connections of the various structural elements with one another and with the surrounding medium.

The physical phenomena involved in this dissipation of energy are numerous: friction, shocks, viscosity and plasticity, vibratory radiation at the supports.

The constitutive models representing these phenomena are often poorly known and it is difficult to describe them explicitly at the elementary level. This is why the models most used are the simple models which make it possible to reproduce at the macroscopic scale the main effects on the structures [bib1] [bib2]. Those currently available are:

- viscous damping: energy dissipated proportional to the velocity of the motion,
- hysteretic damping (also called “structural damping”): energy dissipated proportional to the displacement such that the damping force is of opposite sign to that of the velocity.

Note that Coulomb damping, which corresponds to a friction damping for which the energy dissipated is proportional to the reaction force normal to the direction of displacement, requires the contact to be modelled, which is outside the strictly linear framework. The non-linear operators can take it into account in all its generality [R5.03.50 & R5.03.52] whereas the operator for the transient solution in modal form can model Coulomb friction in the framework of point contacts [R5.06.03](doc:r5.06.03).

The values of the parameters of these models are deduced, when they are available, from experimental results. At the design stage, one limits oneself to the use of regulatory values.

### 1.2 General definitions for characterising the damping [bib1]

#### 1.2.1 Loss coefficient

The loss coefficient $\eta$ is a dimensionless coefficient characteristic of the damping effect defined as the ratio of the energy dissipated ${E}_{d}^{\text{cycle}}$ during a cycle to the maximum potential energy ${E}_{p}^{\max}$ multiplied by $2\pi$ :

$$
\eta =\frac{{E}_{d\text{par}\text{cycle}}}{2\pi {E}_{p\max}}
$$

#### 1.2.2 Reduced damping

By definition the reduced damping $\xi$ is equal to half the loss coefficient observed for an excitation angular frequency equal to the natural angular frequency of the undamped system:

$$
\xi =\frac{\eta}{2}
$$

## 2 Viscous damping model

### 2.1 Physical definition of viscous damping

Classical damper devices (for example, by laminating a viscous fluid through the orifices of a piston driven by the vibratory motion) deliver forces proportional to the velocity of the motion and of opposite sign. Over a cycle, the work of these forces is positive: this is viscous damping.

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.04/1000067C0000078B00000894872BA668D3E83E70.svg)

For a simple oscillator of stiffness $k$ , of mass $m$ and of viscous damping $c$ , the applied external force balances the three components: the elastic restoring force $\mathit{ku}$ , the damping force $c\dot{u}$ and the inertia force $m\ddot{u}$ , whence the dynamic equation in absolute motion:

$$
m\ddot{u}+c\dot{u}+\mathrm{ku}=f
$$

For this viscous damping model the energy dissipated during a cycle of angular frequency $\omega$ is proportional to the vibratory velocity $-\omega {u}_{0}\sin(\omegat )$ associated with the displacement ${u}_{0}\cos(\omegat )$ :

$$
{E}_{d}^{\text{cycle}}={\int}_{0}^{2\pi }-c\omega {u}_{0}\sin\omega \mathit{td}\left({u}_{0}\cos\omega t\right)=\pi c\omega {u}_{0}^{2}
$$

and the potential energy for a sinusoidal displacement ${u}_{0}\cos\omega t$ is:

$$
{E}_{p}^{\max}={\int}_{\pi /2}^{0}{\mathit{ku}}_{0}\cos\omega td\left({u}_{0}\cos\omega t\right)=\frac{1}{2}{\mathit{ku}}_{0}^{2}
$$

For a cycle of angular frequency $\omega$ and of sinusoidal displacement ${u}_{0}\cos\omega t$ , the loss coefficient is proportional to the frequency of the motion:

$$
\eta =\frac{c\omega }{k}
$$

### 2.2 Harmonic oscillator with viscous damping

The classical analysis of the undamped model associated with equation (), put in the form $\left(k-m{\omega}^{2}\right)u=0$ gives us ${\omega}_{0}=\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}$ the natural angular frequency. The critical damping from which the differential equation () no longer has an oscillating solution is given by the formulae ${c}_{\text{critique}}=2\sqrt{\text{km}}=2m{\omega}_{0}=\frac{2k}{{\omega}_{0}}$ which makes it possible to give a numerical interpretation of the reduced damping, which is often expressed as a percentage of the critical damping:

$$
\xi =\frac{\eta}{2}=\frac{c}{{c}_{\text{critique}}}=\frac{c}{2m{\omega}_{0}}
$$

#### 2.2.1 Response to a release from excitation

Starting from a static deflection ${u}_{\text{st}}=\frac{{f}_{0}}{k}$ , a release (freeing of the system) produces a free oscillatory motion ${u}_{l}(t)={u}_{0}{e}^{-\xi {\omega}_{0}t}\cos{\omega}_{0}^{'}t$ which brings out the natural angular frequency of the damped system $\omega {'}_{0}={\omega}_{0}\sqrt{(1-{\xi}^{2})}$ .

Over time, the extreme amplitude $({u}_{1},{u}_{2})$ decreases at each period by ${e}^{-\xi {\omega}_{0}T}={e}^{-2\pi \xi }={e}^{-\delta }$ where $\delta$ is the logarithmic decrement such that $\delta =2\pi \xi$ .

#### 2.2.2 Response to a harmonic excitation

The response to a harmonic excitation of the form $f(t)={f}_{0}{e}^{j\omega t}$ is written with a forced response, a permanent particular solution $u(t)={u}_{0}{e}^{\left(j\omega t-\phi \right)}$ which is written with the reduced angular frequency $\lambda =\frac{\omega}{{\omega}_{0}}$ $\frac{{\mathit{ku}}_{0}}{{f}_{0}}=\frac{1}{1-{\lambda}^{2}+j2\xi \lambda }={H}_{v}\left(j\omega \right)$ where ${H}_{v}(j\omega )$ is the complex transfer function of a simple oscillator with viscous damping.

The modulus of the response $\frac{{u}_{0}}{{u}_{\text{st}}}=\frac{{\mathit{ku}}_{0}}{{f}_{0}}=|{H}_{v}\left(j\omega \right)|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\left(1-{\lambda}^{2}\right)}^{2}+{\left(2\xi \lambda \right)}^{2}}}$ brings out a dynamic amplification with respect to the static response ${u}_{\text{st}}$ .

This amplification is maximum for $\lambda =\frac{{\omega}_{0}^{'}}{{\omega}_{0}}=\sqrt{\left(1-{\xi}^{2}\right)}$ and gives the value of the maximum displacement $\frac{{u}_{0\max}}{{u}_{\text{st}}}=\frac{1}{2\xi \sqrt{\left(1-{\xi}^{2}\right)}}$ . If one observes the vibratory velocity $\dot{u}(t)=j\omega u(t)$ , the amplification of the vibratory velocity is maximum for $\lambda =\frac{{\omega}_{0}}{{\omega}_{0}}=1$ and the maximum amplitude of the velocity is ${\dot{u}}_{0\max}=\frac{1}{2\xi }=Q$ , where $Q$ is the mechanical analogy of the quality factor of electrical engineers. These properties are at the origin of the methods for measuring the damping characteristics of mechanical structures.

## 3 Hysteretic damping model

### 3.1 Physical definition of hysteretic damping

For a sinusoidal excitation applied to an elastoplastic structure or to an elastic structure with friction, the force–displacement curve brings out a positive work of the external force which corresponds to an energy dissipated in the structure, which one can as a first approximation represent as below:

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.04/10000948000015CF00000A21FD70657F91F6C280.svg)

In both cases the loss coefficient increases, in general, with the amplitude of the cycle. For small values of the loss coefficient (less than $0,2$ ), the shape of the cycle has no appreciable influence on the motion and one can liken it to an ellipse [bib1].

In the particular case of a force–displacement relation whose cycle is elliptical in shape, the expression of the loss coefficient is simple. For an applied force $F$ and a displacement $u={u}_{0}\cosq$ the restoring force is ${\mathit{ku}}_{0}\cos\theta$ , $k$ being the “classical” stiffness of the mechanical system, and the damping force $-{\mathit{hu}}_{0}\sin\theta$ , $h$ being the stiffness phase-shifted by $90°$ , which leads to the constitutive relation $F={\mathit{ku}}_{0}\cos\theta -{\mathit{hu}}_{0}\sin\theta$ .

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.04/10000E5800001192000013896BBFF5948F838E3D.svg)

The energies, dissipated during a cycle and maximum potential, are:

$$
{E}_{d}^{\text{cycle}}={\int}_{0}^{2\pi }-{\mathit{hu}}_{0}\sin\theta d\left({u}_{0}\cos\theta \right)=\pi {\mathit{hu}}_{0}^{2}
$$

and:

$$
{E}_{p}^{\max}={\int}_{\pi /2}^{0}{\mathit{ku}}_{0}\cos\theta d\left({u}_{0}\cos\theta \right)=\frac{{\mathit{ku}}_{0}^{2}}{2}
$$

whence the loss coefficient:

$$
\eta =\frac{\pi {\mathit{hu}}_{0}^{2}}{2\pi \frac{{\mathit{ku}}_{0}^{2}}{2}}=\frac{h}{k}
$$

For a sinusoidal cycle $\theta =\omegat$ , the hysteretic damping coefficient $\eta =\frac{h}{k}$ is independent of $\omega$ . It can be determined from a test under harmonic cyclic loading.

### 3.2 Harmonic oscillator with hysteretic damping

The hysteretic damping model can be used to treat the harmonic responses of structures with viscoelastic materials.

The energy dissipated per cycle in the form ${E}_{d}^{\text{cycle}}={\int}_{0}^{2\pi }\sigma d\epsilon$ makes it possible to bring out a complex Young's modulus ${E}^{\text{*}}$ from the stress–strain relation of a viscoelastic material $\sigma ={\sigma}_{0}{e}^{j\omega t}$ and $\epsilon ={\epsilon}_{0}{e}^{j\left(\omega t-\phi \right)}$ where ${\sigma}_{0}$ and ${\epsilon}_{0}$ are the amplitudes and $\varphi$ the phase:

$$
{E}^{\text{*}}=\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon}=\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{0}}{{\epsilon}_{0}}\right){e}^{j\phi }=\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{0}}{{\epsilon}_{0}}\right)\left(\cos\phi +j\sin\phi \right)
$$

Denoting by ${E}_{1}=\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{0}}{{\epsilon}_{0}}\right)\cos\phi$ the real part and by ${E}_{2}=\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{0}}{{\epsilon}_{0}}\right)\sin\mathrm{\phi }$ the imaginary part, one obtains:

$$
{E}^{\text{*}}={E}_{1}+{\mathit{jE}}_{2}={E}_{1}\left(1+j\eta \right)
$$

with $\eta =\frac{{E}_{1}}{{E}_{2}}=\text{tg}\phi$ where $\phi$ is also called the loss angle.

The classical analysis of equation () only makes sense, with a hysteretic damping model, for a harmonic excitation $f(t)={f}_{0}{e}^{j\omegat }$ which leads to the equation:

$$
m\ddot{u}+k\left(1+j\eta \right)u=m\ddot{u}+\left(k+\mathit{jh}\right)u={f}_{0}{e}^{j\omega t}
$$

where the real part of the displacement $u$ represents the displacement of the mass and $h=k\eta$ .

As previously [§ 2.2], the harmonic response can be written, with the reduced angular frequency $\lambda =\frac{\omega}{{\omega}_{0}}$ , in the following form:

$$
\frac{{\mathit{ku}}_{0}}{{f}_{0}}=\frac{1}{1-{\lambda}^{2}+j\eta }={H}_{h}\left(j\omega \right)
$$

where ${H}_{h}(j\omega )$ is the complex transfer function of a simple oscillator with hysteretic damping.

The modulus of the response $\frac{{u}_{0}}{{u}_{\text{st}}}=\frac{{\mathit{ku}}_{0}}{{f}_{0}}=|{H}_{h}\left(j\omega \right)|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\left(1-{\lambda}^{2}\right)}^{2}+{\eta}^{2}}}$ brings out a dynamic amplification with respect to the static response, an amplification which is maximum for $\lambda =1$ and gives the value of the maximum displacement $\frac{{u}_{0\max}}{{u}_{\text{st}}}=\frac{1}{\eta}=\frac{1}{2\xi }$ .

In conclusion, the reduced damping associated with hysteretic damping is:

$$
\xi =\frac{\eta}{2}=\frac{h}{2k}=\frac{h}{2m{\omega}_{0}^{2}}
$$

### 3.3 A note on linear viscoelasticity

Linear viscoelastic materials have constitutive laws of the type:

$$
L\sigma =Mϵ
$$

where $M$ and $L$ are linear differential operators in the time variable, that is to say of the type $L=\sum{l}_{k}{\partial}_{t}^{{\alpha}_{k}}$ where ${l}_{k}$ are real tensors and ${\alpha}_{k}$ are the orders of differentiation, potentially non-integer.

In order to compute the motion at a given instant $t$ , one must know the dynamics of the previous instants: in this sense, the motion at the instant $t$ depends on its history. If models with integer time derivatives require knowledge of the velocity, of the acceleration or of higher orders of the motion in order to compute the forces at a given instant, fractional-order models, for their part, need the whole history of the motion. Indeed, fractional derivatives have the characteristic of being non-local: for example the Grunwald–Letnikov derivative of $f$ at $x$ of order $\alpha$ is defined as the limit when $h\to 0$ of the finite difference:

$$
\frac{1}{{h}^{\alpha}}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{(-1)}^{k}\left(\begin{array}{c}\alpha \\ k\end{array}\right)f(x+(\alpha -k)h)
$$

thus each point $y<x$ enters into the computation of this derivative.

The time-domain analysis of a viscoelastic material, although conceptually simple, is complicated to implement on industrial application cases; the evaluation of the constitutive law requires one to be able to access rapidly a large number of subsequent analysis instants.

The harmonic analysis is for its part very simple to carry out; indeed the constitutive law $L\sigma =M\epsilon$ becomes:

$$
\sigma ={H}_{c}(\omega )\epsilon
$$

where ${H}_{c}(\omega )$ is a Hooke tensor with complex values dependent on the frequency. Two families of rheological models compete for the modelling of the elasticity moduli of the Hooke tensor:

- Compound models: the polymers forming the viscoelastic material are modelled as a series or parallel arrangement of an elementary motif composed of dampers and pistons.
- Fractional-type models: the polymer chains are modelled as a whole by an elementary motif comprising springs, pistons and “pistors” of order alpha (a pistor is an element which, with a strain $u$, associates ${\partial}_{t}^{\alpha}u$ )

Examples of models:

| Model | Maxwell chain | Fractional Zener |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Variation with $\omega$ of the moduli | ${E}^{\text{*}}(\omega )=\sum_{k=1}^{N}{E}_{k}\frac{j{\omega}_{k}{\eta}_{k}}{{E}_{k}+j{\omega}_{k}{\eta}_{k}}$ | ${E}^{\text{*}}(\omega )=\frac{{E}_{0}+{E}_{\infty}{(j\omega \tau )}^{\alpha}}{1+{(j\omega \tau )}^{\alpha}}$ |
| Diagram | ![Figure](images/10000201000002FD0000025A111AD6535F9898BD.png) | ![Figure](images/1000020100000262000002815A77A1954650C33A.png) |

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.04/10000201000002FD0000025A111AD6535F9898BD.png)

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.04/1000020100000262000002815A77A1954650C33A.png)

Compound models generally need a number of terms $N$ greater than $15$ ; the number of coefficients to be determined by tests can be large. Whereas a fractional Zener model needs only four numbers to fit the experimental curves.

A viscoelastic material is therefore a material which in the frequency domain follows a Hooke law whose elasticity moduli are complex and dependent on the frequency. The coefficients of the model of each elasticity modulus ($E$ , $G$ , $\nu$ , $K$ , ...) are measured by specific tests at different frequencies.

For example, an isotropic viscoelastic material modelled by a fractional Zener law has as constitutive law:

$$
\sigma =2\frac{{G}_{0}+{G}_{\infty}{(j\omega {\tau}_{G})}^{{\alpha}_{G}}}{1+{(j\omega {\tau}_{G})}^{{\alpha}_{G}}}(\epsilon -\frac{1}{3}\mathit{Tr}(ϵ)\mathit{Id})+\frac{{K}_{0}+{K}_{\infty}{(j\omega {\tau}_{K})}^{{\alpha}_{K}}}{1+{(j\omega {\tau}_{K})}^{{\alpha}_{K}}}\mathit{Tr}(\epsilon )\mathit{Id}
$$

This material must therefore be characterised in frequency by determining the two quadruplets $({G}_{0,}{G}_{\infty},{\alpha}_{G},{\tau}_{G})$ and $({K}_{0,}{K}_{\infty},{\alpha}_{K},{\tau}_{K})$ .

## 4 Other damping models

The models representing the damping “added” by confined fluids at rest or by fluids in motion are not treated here. One will refer to the documents [R4.07.02-R4.07.04], dealing with fluid–structure coupling.

## 5 Structural analysis with damping

The modellings presented are not easily generalisable to the various structural analyses.

**Note**: *The two modellings do not have the same domain of linear analysis. Viscous damping can be used in transient or harmonic analysis and* *hysteretic damping can be used only in harmonic analysis.*

The modelling options allow the definition of a global damping for the structure and of dampings localised on mesh cells or mesh-cell groups.

### 5.1 Global damping of the structure

In the absence of sufficient information on the components and connections creating a dissipation of energy, a current modelling consists in building a global damping matrix.

#### 5.1.1 Global proportional viscous damping (Rayleigh)

One places oneself within the framework of the classical equations of the dynamics of linear structures:

$$
M\ddot{U}+C\dot{U}+\mathrm{KU}=F(t)
$$

The notion of Rayleigh damping makes it possible to define the damping matrix $C$ as a linear combination of the stiffness and mass matrices:

$$
C=\alpha K+\beta M
$$

**Advantages:**

- easy to implement using the operators DEFI_MATERIAU [U4.43.01](doc:u4.43.01) and ASSEMBLAGE(OPTION='AMOR_MECA'). One can also use the operator COMB_MATR_ASSE [U4.53.01](doc:u4.53.01), after having assembled the stiffness and mass matrices with real coefficients;
- useful for the validation of solution algorithms;
- historically, its success is attached to the methods of transient analysis by modal recombination from a basis of real eigenmodes. The orthogonality properties of the real eigenmodes solutions of the eigenvalue problem $\left(K-{\omega}^{2}M\right)\phi =0$ result in the simultaneous diagonalisation, in the passage to generalised modal coordinates, of ${\phi }^{T}K\phi$ and ${\phi }^{T}M\phi$ . Rayleigh damping is a sufficient condition for diagonalising ${\phi }^{T}C\phi$ . The system of modal equations $\ddot{q}+\frac{{\phi }^{T}C\phi }{{\phi }^{T}M\phi }\dot{q}+{\omega}^{2}q=\frac{{\phi }^{T}}{{\phi }^{T}M\phi }F(t)$ then becomes diagonal:

$$
\ddot{q}+2\xi \omega \dot{q}+{\omega}^{2}q=\frac{{\phi }^{T}}{{\phi }^{T}M\phi }F(t)
$$

**Drawbacks:**

- This modelling does not make it possible to represent the heterogeneity of the structure with respect to damping.
- The damping actually introduced into the model depends strongly on the identification of the coefficients $\alpha$ and $\beta$ cf. [§ 5.1.2].

#### 5.1.2 Influence of the proportional damping coefficients

Three simple identification cases are presented here to illustrate the effects induced by this modelling:

- damping proportional to the inertia characteristics: $\alpha =0$ and $\beta$

This case has been widely used in direct transient solution: if the mass matrix is diagonal, the damping matrix still is and the saving in memory space is obvious. The coefficient $\beta$ can be identified with the experimental reduced damping ${\xi}_{1}$ of the eigenmode $\left({\phi }_{1},{\omega}_{1}\right)$ which participates the most in the response, whence $\beta =2{\xi}_{1}{\omega}_{1}$ . For any other angular frequency one obtains a reduced modal damping $\xi =\beta \frac{{\omega}_{1}}{\omega}$ . The high modes $\omega \text{>>}{\omega}_{i}$ will be very little damped and the low-frequency modes $\omega <{\omega}_{1}$ too damped.

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.04/1000063E0000124B000008E39859E46CDB501970.svg)

- damping proportional to the stiffness characteristics: $\alpha$ and :math:$beta =0$

The coefficient $\alpha$ can be identified, as previously, from ${\xi}_{2}$ associated with the mode $\left({\mathrm{\phi }}_{2},{\omega}_{2}\right)$ whence $\alpha =2{\xi}_{2}{\omega}_{2}$ . For any other angular frequency one obtains a reduced modal damping $\xi =\alpha \frac{\omega}{{\omega}_{2}}$ . The high modes $\omega \text{>>}{\omega}_{2}$ are very damped.

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.04/1000064C00001216000008FEBE94065E6A60A4F7.svg)

- complete proportional damping: $\alpha$ and :math:$beta$

From an identification on two independent modes $({\varphi}_{1},{\omega}_{1})$ and $({\varphi}_{2},{\omega}_{2})$ , one obtains for any other angular frequency a reduced modal damping $\xi =\frac{1}{2}\left(\alpha \omega +\frac{\beta}{\omega}\right)={\xi}_{1}\frac{\omega}{{\omega}_{1}}+{\xi}_{2}\frac{{\omega}_{2}}{\omega}$ .

In the interval $\left[{\omega}_{1},{\omega}_{2}\right]$ , the variation of the reduced damping is small and outside it one finds the addition of the previous drawbacks: the modes outside the interval are too damped.

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.04/100000000000020D0000010EB4DE6DA33E30A1F8.png)

In none of the previous cases will one be able to reproduce an assumption of modal damping equal for all the modes. Methods have been devised in order to tend towards this objective [bib1].

#### 5.1.3 Global hysteretic damping

The generalisation of the equation of the simple oscillator with hysteretic damping leads to the system of complex equations where $F\left(\Omega \right)$ is a harmonic excitation.

$$
M\ddot{U}+K\left(1+j\eta \right)U=F\left(\Omega \right)
$$

Knowing the real stiffness matrix, it is possible to build a hysteretic damping matrix ${K}_{h}=j\eta K$ , with a global loss coefficient $\eta$ .

As previously in solution by modal recombination, from a basis of real eigenmodes, one obtains ${\phi }^{T}M\phi \ddot{q}+j{\phi }^{T}{K}_{h}\phi q+{\phi }^{T}K\phi q={\phi }^{T}F(t)$ where the generalised hysteretic damping matrix is diagonal ${\phi }^{T}{K}_{h}\phi =\left[\text{diag}\eta {\gamma}_{i}\right]$ , like the generalised stiffness matrix ${\phi }^{T}K\phi =\left[\text{diag}{\gamma}_{i}\right]$ .

According to the definition of the reduced damping, the modal damping is constant for all the modes, whence $\xi =\frac{\eta}{2}$ .

**Advantages:**

- easy to implement using the operators DEFI_MATERIAU [U4.43.01](doc:u4.43.01) and ASSEMBLAGE(OPTION='RIGI_MECA_HYST') [U4.61.21](doc:u4.61.21). One can also proceed by using the operator COMB_MATR_ASSE [U4.53.01](doc:u4.53.01), after having assembled the stiffness matrix;
- very useful for the validation of solution algorithms;
- the damping actually introduced into the model is constant for all the modes of the structure, as required by construction regulations.

**Drawbacks:**

- this modelling is ill-suited to industrial studies, because it does not make it possible to represent the heterogeneity of the structure with respect to damping;
- only the harmonic analysis (in complex variables) is possible.

#### 5.1.4 Viscoelastic hysteretic damping

The structure is damped by a viscoelastic material; the harmonic analysis then aims to solve the equation:

$$
\left(-{\omega}^{2}M+{K}_{r}(\omega )+j{K}_{i}(\omega )\right)U=F\left(\Omega \right)
$$

The determination of the complex modes is for the moment possible only by use of DYNA_VISCO [U4.53.04](doc:u4.53.04), which makes it possible to compute the modes in air of an isotropic viscoelastic material whose Poisson's ratio is real.

**Advantages:**

- easy to implement using the operators DEFI_MATERIAU [U4.43.01](doc:u4.43.01) and ASSEMBLAGE(OPTION='RIGI_MECA_HYST') [U4.61.21](doc:u4.61.21);
- the damping is physical.

**Drawbacks:**

- only the harmonic analysis (in complex variables) is possible.

### 5.2 Localised damping

For the analyses requiring a modelling representing the heterogeneity of the structure, it is possible to assign damping characteristics localised on the mesh cells of the structure, in fact on elements of the model.

#### 5.2.1 Damper elements

It is possible to apply discrete damper elements:

- on POI1 mesh cells: the damping is linked to the displacement (respectively the velocity) of the support node,
- on SEG2 mesh cells: the damping is linked to the relative displacement (respectively the relative velocity) of the two connected nodes.

The operator AFFE_CARA_ELEM [U4.24.01](doc:u4.24.01) makes it possible to define for each discrete element:

- a damping matrix of viscous type ${a}_{\text{discret}}$ whose terms are assigned to the various degrees of freedom of the nodes concerned; several modes of description of the matrix are available.
- a hysteretic loss coefficient ${\eta}_{\text{discret}}$ multiplying the stiffness matrix of the discrete element assigned to the support mesh cell.

#### 5.2.2 Damping assigned to any type of finite element

The elastic material assigned to any finite element can be defined with damping parameters by the operator DEFI_MATERIAU [U4.23.01](doc:u4.23.01).

- Proportional viscous damping with two Rayleigh parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$ .

```text
AMOR_ALPHA    =    :math:`\alpha`
AMOR_BETA    =    :math:`\beta`
```

For all types of finite elements (of continuous media, structural or discrete), it is possible to compute the real elementary matrices corresponding to the computation option 'AMOR_MECA', after having computed the elementary matrices corresponding to the computation options 'RIGI_MECA' and 'MASS_MECA'.

The elementary matrix of the element $i$ assigned the material ${\alpha}_{j},{\beta}_{j}$ is then of the form:

- for a finite element:

$$
{c}_{\text{elem,}i}={\alpha}_{j}{k}_{\text{elem,}i}+{\beta}_{j}{m}_{\text{elem,}i}
$$

- for a discrete element:

$$
{c}_{\text{elem},i}={a}_{\text{discret},i}
$$

- Hysteretic damping with a coefficient $\eta$

```text
AMOR_HYST = coef
```

For all types of finite elements (of continuous media, structural or discrete), it is possible to compute the complex elementary matrices corresponding to the computation option 'RIGI_MECA_HYST', after having computed the elementary matrices corresponding to the computation options 'RIGI_MECA'.

The elementary matrix of the element $i$ assigned the material ${\alpha}_{j},{\beta}_{j}$ is then of the form:

- for a finite element:

$$
{k}_{\text{elem},i}^{\text{*}}={k}_{\text{elem},i}\left(1+j{\eta}_{j}\right)
$$

- for a discrete element:

$$
{k}_{\text{elem},i}^{\text{*}}={k}_{\text{elem},i}\left(1+j{\eta}_{\text{discret},i}\right)
$$

#### 5.2.3 Construction of the damping matrix

The assembly of the elementary damping matrices is obtained with the usual operator ASSE_MATRICE [U4.42.02](doc:u4.42.02) or by the command ASSEMBLAGE [U4.31.02](doc:u4.31.02). One must use the same numberings and the same storage mode as for the stiffness and mass matrices (operator NUME_DDL [U4.42.01](doc:u4.42.01)).

**Note**: the *damping matrix obtained is non-proportional* $C\ne \alpha K+\beta M$ or ${K}_{h}\ne j\eta K$ .

## 6 Use of the damping matrix

### 6.1 Use of the viscous damping matrix

#### 6.1.1 Direct linear dynamic analysis

The viscous damping matrix $C$ , whatever its mode of construction and its proportional or non-proportional character, can be used for the direct linear dynamic analysis (keyword MATR_AMOR) with the operator DYNA_VIBRA.

#### 6.1.2 Dynamic analysis by modal recombination

For the analyses by modal recombination, one must project this matrix into the subspace defined by a set $\phi$ of real eigenmodes, obtained on the associated undamped problem $(K-{\omega}^{2}M)\phi =0$ .

This operation is possible with the command PROJ_BASE [U4.55.11](doc:u4.55.11) or with the operator PROJ_MATR_BASE [U4.55.01](doc:u4.55.01).

For the computation of the dynamic response in imposed force or motion in the modal space, the following possibilities are available:

- use of the generalised damping matrix ${\phi }^{T}C\phi$ :

  - in transient analysis with the operator DYNA_VIBRA [R5.06.04](doc:r5.06.04) and [U4.54.03](doc:u4.54.03) and the keyword AMOR_GENE,
  - in seismic analysis by the spectral method with the operator COMB_SISM_MODAL [R4.05.03](doc:r4.05.03) and [U4.54.04](doc:u4.54.04) and the keyword AMOR_GENE,
  - in harmonic analysis with the operator DYNA_VIBRA [R5.05.03](doc:r5.05.03) and [U4.54.03](doc:u4.54.03) and the keyword MATR_AMOR.

It is recalled that in the case of heterogeneous damping (use of the localised damping options), the matrix ${\phi }^{T}C\phi$ is not diagonal.

- use of the viscous modal damping by supplying a reduced modal damping constant for all the modes $\xi$ or a list of values ${\xi}_{i}$ .

Several methods of identification of these coefficients are possible but there is no command for the systematic construction of the list of values. One can nevertheless mention the use of Basile's assumption $(2{\xi}_{i}{\omega}_{i}=\text{diag}\frac{{\phi }^{T}C\phi }{{\phi }^{T}M\phi })$ , the regulatory practice RCC-G (or ETC-C) for seismic analysis with soil damping, exploitation of experimental results, ...

  - in transient and harmonic analysis with the operator DYNA_VIBRA [R5.06.04](doc:r5.06.04) [U4.54.03](doc:u4.54.03) and the keyword AMOR_REDUIT.
  - in seismic analysis by the spectral modal method with the operator COMB_SISM_MODAL [R4.05.03](doc:r4.05.03) [U4.54.04](doc:u4.54.04) and the keywords AMOR_GENE or LIST_AMOR, or also with the keyword AMOR_REDUIT.

For the analyses by dynamic substructuring, with the use of a modal basis (Ritz basis) one will refer to [R4.06.03](doc:r4.06.03) and [R4.06.04](doc:r4.06.04).

### 6.2 Use of the complex stiffness matrix

The complex stiffness matrix ${K}^{\text{*}}=K+{K}_{h}$ , where ${K}_{h}$ is an imaginary matrix (in the sense of complex numbers!), can be used for the direct harmonic analysis with the operator DYNA_VIBRA [R5.05.03](doc:r5.05.03) and [U4.54.03](doc:u4.54.03) and the keyword MATR_RIGI.

For the analyses by modal recombination, no functionality is currently available for the use of the hysteretic damping model.

### 6.3 Complex modal analysis

The viscous damping matrix $C$ is indispensable for the complex modal analysis with the operator treating the quadratic eigenvalue problem [R5.01.02](doc:r5.01.02) and CALC_MODES [U4.52.02](doc:u4.52.02).

It is recalled that the complex eigenmodes allow an approach better suited to the dynamic study of strongly damped structures (reduced damping $\xi$ > 20%). No tool for dynamic response by modal recombination using a basis of complex eigenmodes is available.
