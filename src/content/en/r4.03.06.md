# 1 **r4.03.06** Recalibration algorithms

**Summary:**

This document presents the recalibration algorithms implemented in MACR_RECAL. These are, first, a bounded Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm and, second, an evolutionary algorithm.

For the first, the general method is described before certain elements are specified. The calculation of the functional and of the Jacobian matrix, the determination of the initial regularisation parameter and its evolution, the handling of the bounds and the convergence criterion are detailed.

The general principles of genetic algorithms, and in particular of the evolutionary algorithm, as well as the implementation in *Code_Aster*, are presented in the second part of the document.

## 1 Introduction

Before addressing the recalibration problem proper, it is useful to recall a few points about parameter identification. Suppose that one wishes to identify $n$ parameters from a given mechanical test. Within this identification, the following quantities are defined:

- $\mathrm{c}$ , the vector of the $n$ parameters to be identified, belonging to $O$ , a closed convex set of ${ℝ}^{n}$ .
- $\mathrm{d}$ , the vector of the quantities computed during a simulation of the test using the parameters $c$ , as opposed to ${d}^{\exp}$ , the vector of the quantities measured during an experimental test. Both belong to the space $L$ of observable quantities. The simulation of the experimental test, parameterised by the vector $c$ , may be carried out by various methods: finite differences, finite elements, boundary elements, .... This is what will be called the direct problem.

The aim of the identification is to determine the set of parameters $c$ that reduces the difference between measured and experimental quantities (in the strong hope that the reduction of this discrepancy will be sufficient to obtain the desired set of parameters ...). A cost functional denoted $J$ is therefore introduced, depending on $c$ and measuring the distance between $d$ and ${d}^{\exp}$ .

$J(c)=\parallel d-{d}^{\exp}\parallel$                                         eq 1-1

where $\parallel .\parallel$ denotes a norm on $L$ .

The identification is therefore expressed as the following minimisation problem:

Determine ${c}^{\text{*}}\in O$ such that $J({c}^{\text{*}})=\underset{c\in O}{\text{Min}}J(c)$

Finally, recalibration is defined as the minimisation of a particular type of functional, known as “least squares”, which is expressed in the form:

$J(c)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{j}_{n}^{2}(c)$                                         eq 1-2

where ${j}_{n}(c)$ represents component $n$ of the discrepancy between the vector of computed quantities and the vector of experimental quantities.

It is commonly accepted that, among deterministic minimisation algorithms, the most efficient for this type of functional is the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm. This is the algorithm that was historically the first to be implemented in the command MACR_RECAL of *Code_Aster*, and it is presented below.

## 2 Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm

### 2.1 Statement of the problem

There are several families of minimisation algorithms [bib1]. For relatively regular problems, the most widely used are descent methods. Their principle is to generate iteratively a sequence ${({c}^{k})}_{k\in N}$ defined by:

$({c}^{k+1})={c}^{k}+{\alpha}^{k}{g}^{k}$                                     eq 2.1-1

such that, for $f(x)=J({c}^{k}+x{g}^{k}),x\in {R}_{+}^{\text{*}}$

- $f(x)$ is decreasing in the neighbourhood of ${0}^{+}$
- $f({\alpha}^{k})=\underset{x>0}{\text{Min}}f(x)$

${g}^{k}$ is the descent direction at step $k$ . It is the method used to determine ${g}^{k}$ that governs the nature and hence the efficiency of the algorithm used, these techniques being based mainly on approximations of $J$ to order 1 or to order 2. For the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm, an approximation of the functional to order 2 is used.

### 2.2 Solution

In the context of recalibration, least-squares cost functionals of the following type are used:

$J(c)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}{j}_{n}^{2}(c)$                                         eq 2.2-1

where, for example, ${j}_{n}(c)=({F}_{n}^{\text{calc}}(c)-{F}_{n}^{\exp})$ , with obvious notation.

The particular feature of these cost functionals is that the form of their first and second derivatives is known:

$({\nabla}_{c}J(c)){}_{i}\text{}=2\sum_{n=1}^{N}{j}_{n}(c)\frac{\partial {j}_{n}}{\partial {c}_{i}}$                                 eq 2.2-2

$(H(c)){}_{ij}\text{}=2\sum_{n=1}^{N}(\frac{\partial {j}_{n}}{\partial {c}_{i}}.\frac{\partial {j}_{n}}{\partial {c}_{j}}+{j}_{n}(c)\frac{{\partial}^{2}{j}_{n}}{\partial {c}_{i}\partial {c}_{j}})$                     eq 2.2-3

Then, assuming that the second term of the previous equation is negligible compared with the first (which is true when the ${j}_{k}$ are linear in $c$ : this term is zero), one may rewrite:

$(H(c)){}_{ij}\text{}\approx 2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\partial {j}_{n}}{\partial {c}_{i}}.\frac{\partial {j}_{n}}{\partial {c}_{j}}$                                 eq 2.2-4

It is useful at this point to introduce the sensitivity matrix, or Jacobian matrix, defined by:

$A=\left[\begin{array}{cccc}\frac{\partial {j}_{1}}{\partial {c}_{1}}& \frac{\partial {j}_{1}}{\partial {c}_{2}}& ...& \frac{\partial {j}_{1}}{\partial {c}_{n}}\\ \frac{\partial {j}_{2}}{\partial {c}_{1}}& \frac{\partial {j}_{2}}{\partial {c}_{2}}& ...& ...\\ ...& ...& ...& ...\\ ...& ...& ...& ...\\ \frac{\partial {j}_{N}}{\partial {c}_{1}}& \frac{\partial {j}_{N}}{\partial {c}_{2}}& ...& \frac{\partial {j}_{N}}{\partial {c}_{n}}\end{array}\right]$                             eq 2.2-5

The gradient and the Hessian can thus be expressed as:

${\nabla}_{c}J({c}^{k})=2{A}^{T}j$                                     eq 2.2-6

$H({c}^{k})\approx 2{A}^{T}A$                                         eq 2.2-7

with $j={\left[{j}_{1},...,{j}_{N}\right]}^{T}$ .

The expansion of $J$ limited to order 2 is then written:

$J(c)\approx J({c}^{k})+{(c-{c}^{k})}^{T}.{\nabla}_{c}J({c}^{k})+\frac{1}{2}{(c-{c}^{k})}^{T}H({c}^{k}).(c-{c}^{k})$         eq 2.2-8

Let ${g}^{k}=c-{c}^{k}$ be the descent step at the point ${c}^{k}$ ; it must satisfy the stationarity condition of the quadratic approximation:

${\nabla}_{c}J({c}^{k})+H({c}^{k}){g}^{k}=0$                                 eq 2.2-9

From the expression of the gradient and of the Hessian of $J$ , one may write:

$({A}^{T}A){g}^{k}=-{A}^{T}j$                                 eq 2.2-10

The solution of this equation leads to an algorithm known as Gauss–Newton, which is very efficient but nevertheless has a few drawbacks:

- $({A}^{T}A)$ may be nearly singular and cause the solution not to exist.
- There is no control over ${g}^{k}$ , which may be too large and therefore take the parameters out of the admissible space.

To overcome these drawbacks, the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm is preferred; it proposes a regularisation of the Gauss–Newton algorithm:

$({A}^{T}A+\lambda I){g}^{k}=-{A}^{T}j$                         eq 2.2-11

where $\lambda$ is a scalar and $I$ the identity matrix.

It is observed that if $\lambda =0$ , the direction given by Gauss–Newton is recovered, and if $\lambda \to +\infty$ , the direction given by the opposite of the gradient of $J$ is recovered, *i.e.* the steepest descent.

The Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm therefore consists in starting from a “fairly high” value of $\lambda$ and reducing it by a factor of 10, for example, at each decrease of $J$ . One thus passes gradually from a steepest-descent algorithm to the Gauss–Newton algorithm. This procedure may therefore be presented as follows:

- Choice of a starting point ${c}^{0}$ and of an initial value of $\lambda$
- At iteration $k$ , solve

$\begin{array}{}({A}^{T}A+\lambda I){g}^{k}=-{A}^{T}j\\ {c}^{k+1}={c}^{k}+{g}^{k}\end{array}$

- If $J({c}^{k+1})<J({c}^{k})$ , then $\lambda =\lambda /10$ otherwise $\lambda =\lambda \ast 10$
- Convergence test

**Note:**

The Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm has been considered above from the point of view of the regularisation of the Gauss–Newton algorithm. It is possible to give this algorithm a different interpretation by considering it as a trust-region algorithm [bib *2*]. Indeed, it can easily be shown that the system [eq 2.2-11] is the stationarity condition of the minimisation problem:

*Determine* ${g}^{k}$ *such that* ${g}^{k}=\text{ArgMin}({g}^{kT}.{A}^{T}j+\frac{1}{2}{g}^{kT}{A}^{T}A{g}^{k})$ *subject to* $\parallel {g}^{k}\parallel \le {D}^{k}$ **.**

*Where* ${D}^{k}=-{({A}^{T}A+\lambda I)}^{-1}{A}^{T}j\text{et}\lambda \ge 0$ .

This is a very simple implementation of the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm, within which various questions are not addressed:

- How should the functional $J$ be defined when several tests are available?
- How should the initial value of $\lambda$ be chosen?
- How should $\lambda$ be made to evolve in a finer way?
- How should the domain of evolution of each parameter be defined?

These various points are specified below.

### 2.3 Practical implementation

#### 2.3.1 Definition of the functional

During a recalibration, the user often has several different measurements available; these are discrete physical quantities, possibly of different natures, measured during one or more tests. They are functions of a given parameter denoted $t$ (time, abscissa, …) which may therefore be represented by:

${F}_{1}^{\exp}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{t}_{1}& {f}_{1}^{\exp}({t}_{1})\\ ⋮& ⋮\\ {t}_{N}& {f}_{1}^{\exp}({t}_{N})\end{array}\right]{F}_{2}^{\exp}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{t}_{1}& {f}_{2}^{\exp}({t}_{1})\\ ⋮& ⋮\\ {t}_{M}& {f}_{2}^{\exp}({t}_{M})\end{array}\right]{F}_{L}^{\exp}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{t}_{1}& {f}_{L}^{\exp}({t}_{1})\\ ⋮& ⋮\\ {t}_{P}& {f}_{L}^{\exp}({t}_{P})\end{array}\right]$     eq 3.1-1

Each of these experimental measurements has its counterpart

${F}_{1}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k})=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\tilde{t}}_{1}& {f}_{1}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{\tilde{t}}_{1})\\ ⋮& ⋮\\ {\tilde{t}}_{I}& {f}_{1}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{\tilde{t}}_{I})\end{array}\right]{F}_{2}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k})=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\tilde{t}}_{1}& {f}_{2}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{\tilde{t}}_{1})\\ ⋮& ⋮\\ {\tilde{t}}_{J}& {f}_{2}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{\tilde{t}}_{J})\end{array}\right]{F}_{L}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k})=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\tilde{t}}_{1}& {f}_{L}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{\tilde{t}}_{1})\\ ⋮& ⋮\\ {\tilde{t}}_{K}& {f}_{L}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{\tilde{t}}_{K})\end{array}\right]$ eq 3.1-2

computed for a given set of parameters ${c}^{k}$ . Note that the computed quantities are not necessarily the same in number as the measured quantities, nor evaluated for the same value of the parameter $t$ . The least-squares functional to be minimised may then be defined by:

$J({c}^{k})=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{(\frac{{f}_{1}^{\exp}({t}_{i})-{f}_{1}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{t}_{i})}{{f}_{1}^{\exp}({t}_{i})})}^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}{(\frac{{f}_{2}^{\exp}({t}_{i})-{f}_{2}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{t}_{i})}{{f}_{2}^{\exp}({t}_{i})})}^{2}+...+\sum_{i=1}^{P}{(\frac{{f}_{L}^{\exp}({t}_{i})-{f}_{L}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{t}_{i})}{{f}_{L}^{\exp}({t}_{i})})}^{2}}{J({c}^{0})}$     eq 3.1-3

It is important to note that:

- if a computed measurement ${f}_{j}^{\text{calc}}$ is not defined at a time instant ${t}_{i}$ , its value is linearly interpolated
- if an experimental measurement ${f}_{j}^{\exp}$ is zero, the quantity ${f}_{j}^{\exp}({t}_{i})-{f}_{j}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{t}_{i})$ is not divided, and it appears as it stands in the expression of the functional
- the functional $J$ is normalised so as to be equal to 1. at the start of the recalibration iterations

#### 2.3.2 Expression of the Jacobian matrix

For the calculation of the Jacobian matrix, the error vector $j$ is defined by:

$j=\left[\begin{array}{c}\frac{{f}_{1}^{\exp}({t}_{1})-{f}_{1}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{t}_{1})}{{f}_{1}^{\exp}({t}_{1})}\\ ⋮\\ \frac{{f}_{1}^{\exp}({t}_{N})-{f}_{1}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{t}_{N})}{{f}_{1}^{\exp}({t}_{N})}\\ \frac{{f}_{2}^{\exp}({t}_{1})-{f}_{2}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{t}_{1})}{{f}_{2}^{\exp}({t}_{1})}\\ ⋮\\ ⋮\\ \frac{{f}_{P}^{\exp}({t}_{L})-{f}_{P}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{t}_{L})}{{f}_{P}^{\exp}({t}_{L})}\end{array}\right]$ **eq 3.2-1**

That is:

${j}_{i}^{K}=\frac{{f}_{K}^{\exp}({t}_{i})-{f}_{K}^{\text{calc}}({c}^{k},{t}_{i})}{{f}_{K}^{\exp}({t}_{i})}$ **eq 3.2-2**

The expression of the Jacobian matrix of [eq 2.2-5] is then recovered:

${\rm A}=\left[\begin{array}{cccc}\frac{\partial {j}_{1}^{1}}{\partial {c}_{1}}& \frac{\partial {j}_{1}^{1}}{\partial {c}_{2}}& \cdots & \frac{\partial {j}_{1}^{1}}{\partial {c}_{n}}\\ \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots \\ \frac{\partial {j}_{N}^{1}}{\partial {c}_{1}}& \frac{\partial {j}_{N}^{1}}{\partial {c}_{2}}& \cdots & \frac{\partial {j}_{N}^{1}}{\partial {c}_{n}}\\ \frac{\partial {j}_{1}^{2}}{\partial {c}_{1}}& \frac{\partial {j}_{1}^{2}}{\partial {c}_{2}}& \cdots & \cdots \\ \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots \\ \cdots & \cdots & \cdots & \cdots \\ \frac{\partial {j}_{L}^{P}}{\partial {c}_{1}}& \frac{\partial {j}_{L}^{P}}{\partial {c}_{2}}& \cdots & \frac{\partial {j}_{L}^{P}}{\partial {c}_{n}}\end{array}\right]$                                 eq 3.2-3

where the terms are computed by forward finite differences:

$\frac{\partial {j}_{1}^{1}}{\partial {c}_{i}}({c}_{1},...,{c}_{i},...,{c}_{n})\approx \frac{{j}_{1}^{1}({c}_{1},...,{c}_{i}+{\mathrm{\alpha c}}_{i},...,{c}_{n})-{j}_{1}^{1}({c}_{1},...,{c}_{i},...,{c}_{n})}{{\mathrm{\alpha c}}_{i}}$ **eq 3.2-4**

#### 2.3.3 Regularisation of the linear system

The problem of determining and evolving the regularisation parameter $\lambda$ is addressed here. To this end, the following are defined:

- ${\lambda}_{\max}=\text{Max}(\text{Valeurs propres de}{A}^{T}A)$ , ${\lambda}_{\min}=\text{Min}(\text{Valeurs propres de}{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{A})$ , $\mathrm{cond}={\lambda}_{\max}/{\lambda}_{\min}$  if ${\lambda}_{\min}\ne 0$
- $Q(c)=J({c}^{k})+{(c-{c}^{k})}^{T}.{A}^{T}j+\frac{1}{2}{(c-{c}^{k})}^{T}.({A}^{T}A+\lambda I).(c-{c}^{k})$

##### 2.3.3.1 Initial value of $\lambda$

Knowing the quantities above, the following algorithm is defined:

- If ${\lambda}_{\min}=0$ , then $\lambda =1.E-3{\lambda}_{\max}$
- Otherwise
- If $\mathrm{cond}<1.E5$ , then $\lambda =1.E-16{\lambda}_{\max}$
- Otherwise $\lambda =∣(1.E5{\lambda}_{\min}-{\lambda}_{\max})∣/10001$

**Note:**

*In the last case, the value assigned to* $\lambda$ *has the effect of bringing the condition number of* ${A}^{T}A$ *back to* $1.E5$

##### 2.3.3.2 Evolution of the value of $\lambda$

To evolve $\lambda$ , the ratio ${R}^{k}=\frac{J({c}^{k})-J({c}^{k+1})}{Q({c}^{k})-Q({c}^{k+1})}$ is defined, which makes it possible to assess the validity of the quadratic approximation of $J$ : the closer it is to 1, the more valid this approximation is. The following algorithm is deduced from it [bib2]:

- If ${R}^{k}<0.25$ , then $\lambda =\lambda \times 10$
- If ${R}^{k}>0.75$ , then $\lambda =\lambda /15$

#### 2.3.4 Limitations of the domain of evolution of the parameters

For various reasons, such as to guarantee the physical validity of the parameters (Young’s modulus strictly positive, Poisson’s ratio between $0$ and $0.5$ , …), it is necessary to limit their domain of evolution. It is therefore imposed that $c$ remain in an admissible domain $O$ , a closed convex set of ${R}^{n}$ . This therefore imposes constraints on the parameters:

${c}_{\sup}>{c}^{k}+{g}^{k}>{c}_{\inf}$

After dualisation of these conditions by introduction of the Lagrange multipliers ${\mu}_{\inf}$ and ${\mu}_{\sup}$ , the system is solved:

*Find* ${g}^{k}$ ${\mu}_{\inf}$ ${\mu}_{\sup}$ *such that*

$\begin{array}{c}({A}^{T}A+\lambda I){g}^{k}+{\mu}_{\inf}+{\mu}_{\sup}=-{A}^{T}j\\ \lbrace \begin{array}{}{c}^{k}+{g}^{k}>{c}_{\inf}\\ {\mu}_{\inf}>0\\ {({c}^{k}+{g}^{k}-{c}_{\inf})}_{i}{({\mu}_{\inf})}_{i}=0\forall i=[1,n]\end{array}\\ \lbrace \begin{array}{}{c}^{k}+{g}^{k}<{c}_{\sup}\\ {\mu}_{\sup}<0\\ {({c}^{k}+{g}^{k}-{c}_{\sup})}_{i}{({\mu}_{\sup})}_{i}=0\forall i=[1,n]\end{array}\end{array}$

This solution is carried out by means of an active-set algorithm. For any detail of this algorithm, refer to [bib3] or [bib4].

#### 2.3.5 Non-dimensionalisation

One is often led to identify parameters of various physical natures. The orders of magnitude of these parameters may be extremely different. This can generate very large differences in the orders of magnitude of the components of the gradient and of the Hessian of the cost functional, and compromise the solution.

To overcome this difficulty, it is essential to non-dimensionalise the unknowns before starting the solution. Here is a simple and efficient procedure.

Let ${c}^{0}={}^{T}\text{}\left[{c}_{1}^{0},{c}_{2}^{0},...,{c}_{n}^{0}\right]$ be the initial vector of the quantities to be reconstructed. The non-dimensionalisation matrix $D$ is defined:

$D=\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{c}_{1}^{0}& & & & \\ & {c}_{2}^{0}& & & \\ & & & & \\ & & & {c}_{n-1}^{0}& \\ & & & & {c}_{n}^{0}\end{array}\right]$                             eq 3.5-1

Then, if the ${c}_{i}^{0}$ are all non-zero, the non-dimensional unknowns may be defined by:

${\tilde{c}}^{0}={D}^{-1}.{c}^{0}$                                 eq 3.5-2

Likewise, a non-dimensional cost functional is introduced:

$\tilde{\mathrm{J}}(\tilde{\mathrm{c}})=\mathrm{J}(\mathrm{D}.\tilde{\mathrm{c}})=\mathrm{J}(\mathrm{c})$                             eq 3.5-3

As well as its gradient:

${\nabla}_{\tilde{\mathrm{c}}}\tilde{\mathrm{J}}(\tilde{\mathrm{c}})=\frac{\partial \tilde{\mathrm{J}}(\tilde{\mathrm{c}})}{\partial \tilde{\mathrm{c}}}=\frac{\partial \mathrm{J}(\mathrm{D}.\tilde{\mathrm{c}})}{\partial \tilde{\mathrm{c}}}=\frac{\partial \mathrm{J}(\mathrm{c})}{\partial \mathrm{c}}.\frac{\partial \mathrm{c}}{\partial \tilde{\mathrm{c}}}=\mathrm{D}.{\nabla}_{c}\mathrm{J}(\mathrm{c})$             eq 3.5-4

And its Jacobian matrix:

${\tilde{A}}_{ij}={A}_{ij}\times {c}_{j}^{0}$                                 eq 3.5-5

From an algorithmic point of view, the Jacobian matrix is computed in the usual way with the functional $J$ , then it is non-dimensionalised, as are the current parameters $c$ , before being passed to the minimisation algorithm. On leaving the latter, the parameters $\tilde{c}$ are re-dimensionalised to allow the functional $J$ to be computed.

#### 2.3.6 Convergence criterion

The convergence criterion used in MACR_RECAL consists in testing the decrease of the gradient of the functional. It is recalled that the use of this criterion is naturally justified by the fact that the objective of the recalibration algorithm is to cancel this gradient.

$\frac{\mid \mid {\nabla}_{c}J({c}^{k})\mid \mid }{\mid \mid {\nabla}_{c}J({c}^{0})\mid \mid }<\mathrm{Prec.}$                             eq 3.6-1

where $\mathrm{Prec}$ is taken equal to 1.E-3 by default.

### 2.4 Overall algorithm

In order to make explicit the sequence of the various operations described above, the recalibration algorithm is presented formally:

- Initialisations
- $k=0$
- computation of $A$ , non-dimensionalisation of $A$
- Computation of the initial $\lambda$
- **Global iterations**
- Non-dimensionalisation of ${c}^{k}$
- Solution of the Levenberg–Marquardt equation
- Enforcement of the bounds
- Re-dimensionalisation of ${c}^{k+1}$
- Computation of $J({c}^{k+1})$
- Update of $\lambda$
- Computation of $A$ , non-dimensionalisation of $A$
- Convergence test
- $k=k+1$
- End

## 3 Evolutionary algorithm

### 3.1 General principles

Evolutionary algorithms belong to the stochastic methods of global optimisation based on the principles of Darwinian evolution of biological populations, methods commonly called genetic algorithms. The main phases of a simple genetic algorithm are first recalled:

  - *coding*: each parameter to be recalibrated is coded in binary; the population is created;
  - *evaluation*: each individual of the population is assigned a measure of its fitness, computed from the cost function to be minimised;
  - *selection*: the individuals that will produce the next generation of the population are selected; the best in the sense of fitness are naturally retained;
  - *crossover*: new individuals are created by the parents designated at the previous phase. In practice this consists in exchanging part of the binary string between two parents;
  - *mutation*: a random mechanism that perturbs one or more bits of the children’s binary string in order to maintain a certain level of diversity in the population;
  - *replacement*: a new population, of the same size, is formed by replacing the parents with the children

Even if, by misuse of language, the term genetic is often used in place of evolutionary, the few differences that distinguish evolutionary algorithms should be recalled [5]:

- the individuals of the population are represented by vectors of real numbers and no longer in binary coding;
- the selection process is different: whereas genetic algorithms select $n$ parents to create $n$ children who replace them entirely in the population, evolutionary programs generate *m* children from $n$ parents and then select the new population by keeping the $n$ best among the set $n+m$ ;
- the probabilities of crossover and of mutation may vary over the generations.

The interest of introducing an evolutionary algorithm into *Code_Aster* lies in its ability to explore the topological space of the parameters in several directions, while avoiding local minima of the functional to be minimised as far as possible.

The implementation of this algorithm in Code_Aster is the result of the partnership between the AMA department and the Politecnico di Milano.

It is also noted that the most criticised shortcoming of evolutionary algorithms, that of being greedy in CPU time, can easily be eliminated by the many possible variants of parallelisation.

### 3.2 Operation of the algorithm

The algorithm is launched by choosing METHODE= 'GENETIQUE' or 'HYBRIDE' in the options of the command MACR_RECAL. In the second case, the evolutionary algorithm carries out a coarse search followed by an optimisation by the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm.

Figure 3.2-a Logical diagram of the operation of the evolutionary algorithm

The overall operation of the algorithm is illustrated in the logical diagram of Figure 3.2-a, and the steps are described below:

1. **Initialisation**: a population of sets of parameters is generated and all the individuals are initialised with the initial values of the parameters to be recalibrated. The size of this population is given by the value of the parameter NB_PARENTS imposed by the user. This value depends on several factors, such as the uncertainty on the solution: the greater this uncertainty, the larger the population must be. It is therefore advisable to start a recalibration study by taking 10 for this value and to refine it according to the results.
2. **Evaluation of the functional**: this is the functional presented in equation 3.1-3. Initially, after the initialisation step, a single evaluation is made because all the individuals of the population are identical. Then, in the recalibration loop, as many evaluations of the functional are made during one iteration as the number of children (NB_FILS) imposed by the user. The larger this parameter, which governs the renewal rate of the population, the more greedy the algorithm is in CPU time at this step. By default the number of children is set to 5 (half the population size, which is also imposed by default).
3. **Stopping criterion**: once the population of $n$ parents has been formed and each individual has its value of the functional, one checks:

  - the best value of the functional;

- the number of iterations already performed by the algorithm

If the best value of the functional is lower than the relative residual imposed by the user (1.E-3 by default), or if the maximum number of iterations is reached, the best individual of the population is supplied as the solution.

    1. **Selection**: the best individual of the population is selected and it is **the only one** (in this implementation) that is allowed to reproduce and to generate the $m$ children. This generation is quasi-random, about the best individual, with a standard deviation imposed by the user (keyword ECART_TYPE). During this step, the algorithm handles the bounds imposed on the parameters by the user. The individuals thus generated are accepted as children only if they lie inside the bounds.

1. **Replacement**: after the *m* children have been generated, the overall population at this stage of the iteration is $n+m$ (NB_PARENTS + NB_FILS). The replacement operator here ranks the individuals according to the associated values of the functional and replaces the individuals of the PARENTS population with the $n$ best among the $n+m$ .

One particular feature of this implementation of the evolutionary algorithm in *Code_Aster* is the absence, for the time being, of the mutation and crossover mechanisms.

### 3.3 Hybrid recalibration technique

By choosing METHODE='HYBRIDE' in MACR_RECAL, the user has the possibility of combining the advantages of stochastic algorithms (the evolutionary one here) with those of deterministic algorithms (Levenberg–Marquardt in the present case). This consists in carrying out a first “coarse” search in the topological space of the parameters, which will allow the Levenberg–Marquardt algorithm to start the optimisation from a starting point closer to the **global** solution of the functional.

The balance between stochastic and deterministic is to be set by the user in the light of his or her expertise. A large uncertainty on the optimal values of the parameters to be recalibrated must impose:

- more iterations of the evolutionary algorithm;
- a larger standard deviation for the random draws;
- a larger population size.

These are the three levers available to the user in order to carry out a high-quality recalibration with a satisfactory level of performance (CPU time and memory used).
