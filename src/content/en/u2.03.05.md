# u2.03.05 User's guide for the numerical simulation of welding

> **Summary.**

The aim of this document is to give the information needed for a user to carry out multipass welding analyses with *Code_Aster*. It constitutes a methodological guide to the tools available and to the good practice to put in place in order to conduct a quality study in the numerical simulation of welding.

The various pieces of advice given to the user come essentially from the feedback of three studies:

- A bevelled 316L pipe welded by the TIG process, with filler metal, in thirteen passes (CEA/EDF sheet 3488); the first 2 passes of this example constitute a *Code_Aster* test case [V7.42.100](doc:v7.42.100);
- A bevelled 316L plate welded by the TIG process, with filler metal, in two welding passes (CEA/EDF sheet 2425); the various command files attached to this study are stored in the *Code_Aster* study database (SERVICE/BDD Etudes);
- An unbevelled 316L plate, with creation of a fusion line by the TIG process, but without filler metal (INSA Lyon thesis of L. Depradeux, defended in 2004).

## 2 General points on welding

### 2.1 What is welding?

The welding of two metal parts is the obtaining of continuity by local fusion or diffusion, thanks to a supply of heat and possibly of a supply of metal. Many **welding processes can therefore be described physically as a brief passage to very high temperature of a small quantity of metal, followed by free or forced cooling**, most often by conduction in the bulk of the welded parts and in the elements of the workstation. Over time, this temperature evolution determines the final metallurgical structure of the welded joint, sometimes different from the base metal.

### 2.2 Phenomena involved during welding

From a mechanical point of view, the highly localised thermal gradients involved during the welding of two materials generate local expansions and contractions, with the consequence that incompatible strain and stress fields appear, which remain after return to ambient temperature.

For certain materials, the thermal history associated with the welding operation generates changes of microstructure in the solid state which influence the residual stress state and the distortions of the welded joint. These changes of microstructure occur when the temperature reaches a threshold called the transformation start temperature. These transformations stop when the temperature exceeds the transformation finish temperature.

One can then distinguish two types of stress: the stresses of thermal origin generated throughout the duration of the welding process, and the stresses of metallurgical origin (possibly) generated during the duration of the transformations.

**Consequently, a numerical simulation of welding must take into account the phenomena coupled with one another: thermal, possibly metallurgical, and finally mechanical.**

### 2.3 Review of the various interactions

#### 2.3.1 Thermal–metallurgical interaction

- *Thermal => Metallurgical direction*: the thermophysical properties (conductivity, heat capacity, diffusivity…) depend on the phases present.
- *Metallurgical => Thermal direction*: the metallurgical transformations are accompanied by latent-heat effects which modify the temperature distributions.

#### 2.3.2 Thermal–mechanical interaction

- *Thermal => Mechanical direction*: this influence results, on the one hand, from the variations of the mechanical characteristics with temperature and, on the other hand, from the expansions and contractions of thermal origin.
- *Mechanical => Thermal direction*: the evolution of the irreversible strain, as well as of the internal hardening variables, leads to a dissipation of energy in the form of heat. However, this temperature rise of mechanical origin is often negligible in comparison with that coming from the heat input delivered by the welding process, given the relatively small strains and strain rates which are involved. This assumption is no longer valid in the case of processes involving strong dissipations, for example friction welding.

#### 2.3.3 Metallurgical–mechanical interaction

- *Metallurgical => Mechanical direction*: the influence of the metallurgical history on the mechanical history results mainly from four factors. The first lies in the expansions and contractions caused by the metallurgical transformations. The second factor is the transformation-plasticity phenomenon. The third is the hardening-recovery phenomenon. Finally, the last factor corresponds to the particular behaviour linked to the multiphase nature of the material.
- *Mechanical => Metallurgical direction*: the application of a stress modifies the energy stored in the material and the atomic structure of the lattice. The presence of a mechanical loading can therefore play a role in the metallurgical transformations.

### 2.4 What is meant by fusion zone, HAZ, tempered zone?

When one speaks of welding, the study engineer will often hear of fusion zone, of HAZ and of tempered zone, which correspond to three distinct zones in a welded component. That is why we give below a very general definition of these zones.

- **The fusion zone (F.Z.)**: this is the zone where the **temperature is higher than the solidus of the steel considered**, or of the filler metal. The composition of the molten metal results from the chemistry of the filler metal, from the chemistry of the base metal and from the interactions with the environment. This zone is separated from the heat-affected zone (HAZ) by the fusion line, which characterises the set of points that have reached the solidus temperature.
- **The heat-affected zone (HAZ)**: in a very general way, this is **the solid zone** of the welded assembly where the **constitutive behaviour of the material is strongly influenced by the temperature field** due to welding. The term HAZ has a very particular meaning when the material of the base metal or of the filler metal undergoes structural transformations: it is the zone where the transformation takes place. This H.A.Z. itself breaks down into three main parts: the zone where the transformation is complete, the zone where the transformation is partial, and finally the **tempered zone (T.Z.)**, where the maximum temperature reached is slightly lower than the transformation start temperature but where the constitutive behaviour of the material is slightly modified relative to that of the base metal.
- **The zone not affected thermally**: in a very general way, this is **the solid zone** of the welded assembly where the **constitutive behaviour of the material is little influenced by the temperature field**. For materials undergoing structural transformations, this is the part of the welded assembly where the maximum temperature reached is well below the transformation start temperature and where the welding operation does not generate any modification of the metallurgical and mechanical characteristics of the base metal.

### 2.5 Why is it important to simulate the welding process?

The evolution of the welding processes applied to steels has made it possible today to achieve good reproducibility of the operation and good quality of the welded assemblies. **Welds nevertheless constitute the weak points of structures.** The strain and stress fields induced by the welding operation have a determining role in the quality and the mechanical strength of the welded component; the residual stresses induced by welding accentuate all the risks of damage by fatigue, by creep, by corrosion and by brittle fracture…

That is why it is important to have robust and reliable tools in order to simulate this process.

## 3 Numerical simulation of welding

Everything that is written hereafter can be implemented in two study situations:

- When one simulates, obviously, the welding of two parts;
- But also when one simulates the deposition of a cladding on a part. One can apply the same methodology as that adopted for a welding operation.

### 3.1 Visual representation of a welding operation

- **Reality**: one has two parts A and B which one wishes to weld in order to obtain a part AB, using a suitable welding process (for example by heat input). The two parts are never welded in a single operation, but in successive passes, the first pass being called the root pass.
- **The numerical representation**: the first root pass is never modelled. One therefore assumes that, initially, the two parts A and B form a single block. Later, we shall see that several methods exist for activating the passes. Most often (this is the configuration presented in the drawing below), one chooses to represent all the passes in the initial model and one activates them progressively.

### 3.2 What does one seek to compute?

The final aim of the simulation is to determine the residual stress and strain fields. To do this, it is necessary to couple or to chain thermal, possibly metallurgical, and finally mechanical analyses.

### 3.3 What are the main difficulties of the simulation?

The main difficulties, questions and methodological choices which the engineer will face can be summarised in six points:

- 2D or 3D representation of the structure studied?
- Which mesh to adopt?
- How to take the heat input into account?
- How to handle the addition of material, in the thermal analysis and in the mechanical analysis?
- Which mechanical constitutive model (for the metallurgical transformations, a single model exists in *Code_Aster*)?
- Which physical data?

**Note**: The choices which will finally be adopted for the simulation depend greatly on the experimental data in the possession of the engineer. These data include:

- Material data. As in any simulation, it is important to have material data in order to identify as cleanly as possible the constitutive laws (thermal, metallurgical and mechanical). Welding involving very high temperatures (up to melting of the material), one must have available, if possible, on the one hand parameters between 20°C and the melting temperature, and on the other hand tests making it possible to characterise the viscous phenomena.
- Data on the welding conditions. Among the numerous uncertainties on the input data which come into play during the numerical simulation of a welding operation or of the deposition of a cladding, the indeterminacy of the heat input is the most penalising. Indeed, even when the welding is automated and the welding parameters are well known, the modelling of the heat input remains difficult in any case, and a calibration is almost always necessary (on temperature measurements or on macrographs of fusion zones) if a **predictive numerical simulation is aimed at**.

In section 3, and for each of these points, we shall present the various possible methodologies and, sometimes, the recommended choices.

### 3.4 The specific features of Code_Aster

#### 3.4.1 Which couplings are considered?

In *Code_Aster*, it is currently possible to take into account the essential phenomena, but in a decoupled way, by means of **chained thermal, then metallurgical (possibly), and finally mechanical analyses.**

The possible metallurgical analysis is thus carried out as post-processing of the thermal analysis, without taking into account the influence of the metallurgy on the thermal analysis (difference in thermophysical properties according to the phases, and latent heats of transformation).

Likewise, the mechanical analysis is decoupled from the thermal and metallurgical analyses: the influence of the intrinsic dissipation on the thermal fields is neglected, as is the influence of the stress state on the metallurgical transformations.

To summarise, here are the interactions taken (OUI) or not (NON) into consideration:

- Influence of the thermal analysis on the metallurgy: OUI
- Influence of the metallurgy on the thermal analysis: NON
- Influence of the thermal analysis on the mechanical analysis: OUI
- Influence of the mechanical analysis on the thermal analysis: NON
- Influence of the metallurgy on the mechanical analysis: OUI
- Influence of the mechanical analysis on the metallurgy: NON

#### 3.4.2 Typical layout of a Code_Aster command file

To summarise, here are the main stages of an analysis of a welding operation in *Code_Aster*:

| 1 | A thermal analysis is carried out, which makes it possible to obtain the temperature field at each node of the mesh. |
| --- | --- |
| 2 | If the material considered undergoes metallurgical transformations: one carries out, as post-processing of the thermal analysis, the metallurgical analysis, which makes it possible to obtain the proportion of the various metallurgical phases (and possibly the associated hardness) at each node of the mesh. |
| 3 | From the temperature field and possibly from the metallurgical phases, one carries out the mechanical analysis by choosing a constitutive model which possibly takes into account the various possible effects of the metallurgical transformations. One thus obtains the stress, strain and internal-variable fields at each Gauss point. |

#### 3.4.3 Restrictions of Code_Aster [5]

- **Thermal analysis**

  - The solution of the heat equation in a moving frame by the command THER_NON_LINE_MO [U4.53.03](doc:u4.53.03) (useful for calibrating the heat source of an unsteady 3D thermal analysis) is available only in the case of a rectilinear path of the heat source, and not in the case of an axisymmetric path, as during the welding of cylindrical pipes for example.
  - It is currently impossible to define a function of more than two variables with *Code_Aster*, whereas the "classical" heat sources (Gaussian, double ellipsoid, CIN source) are volume or surface flux densities, functions of space and of time. If the source is a function of time (at a minimum, except if the analysis is carried out in a moving frame), only a single space parameter remains available.
- **Metallurgical analysis**

  - The tempering phenomenon (which has in particular, as a consequence, a decrease in the yield stress) of the as-quenched phases is not taken into account. One can possibly work around this problem. If the number of phases formed on cooling makes it possible to leave metallurgical internal variables "free", one can then assign to these internal variables proportions of tempered phases and model the tempering kinetics by introducing a fictitious TRC.
- **Mechanical analysis**

  - It would be of interest to develop, for a material comprising only a single metallurgical phase, the models equivalent to META_XXX_XXX (several phases). This currently forces one to carry out a dummy metallurgical analysis in order to benefit from these laws. This can pose, in 3D, significant problems of analysis time and of memory capacity, if for example one uses a model with kinematic hardening (one stores a tensor as an internal variable for each computation step and for each phase).

## 4 Methodology for the simulation

The feedback cited in this part comes essentially from the following three studies:

- A bevelled 316L pipe welded by the TIG process [2], with filler metal, in thirteen passes (CEA/EDF/FRAMATOME sheet 3488); the first 2 passes of this example constitute a *Code_Aster* test case [V7.42.100](doc:v7.42.100);
- A bevelled 316L plate welded by the TIG process [4], with filler metal, in two welding passes (CEA/EDF sheet 2425); the various command files attached to this study are stored in the *Code_Aster* study database (SERVICE/BDD Etudes);
- An unbevelled 316L plate, with creation of a fusion line by the TIG process [3], but without filler metal (INSA Lyon thesis of L. Depradeux, defended in 2004).

For simplicity, we shall subsequently name them sheet 3488, sheet 2425 and thesis L.D. 2004.

### 4.1 Type of modelling: 2D or 3D?

In all rigour, the welding process is strictly three-dimensional, the input of heat and possibly of material being mobile and often of constant speed. The temperature and mechanical fields generated are therefore three-dimensional and transient.

It is therefore preferable to carry out a three-dimensional transient analysis.

However, this choice not always being possible because of the complexity, the analysis time and the memory capacity required by 3D analyses, one often resorts to a 2D modelling in the numerical simulation of welding (in particular for multipass welding with a large number of passes).

**Case of a 2D modelling**

- In the majority of cases, the 2D simulations consider a transverse section, perpendicular to the advance of the welding torch. These simulations are carried out under the plane-strain assumption;
- 2D axisymmetric analyses are also adopted for the case of the welding of cylindrical pipes.

  - **The weak point of the two preceding modellings is that one neglects the effect of the welding speed and that one assumes that the bead (or the heat) is deposited simultaneously over the whole length of the part to be welded.**
- Other, less classical, choices are also possible: one can choose, for example, to mesh a longitudinal section (in the welding direction) or else, if one considers that the temperatures are constant through the thickness, one can choose to mesh the mid-plane of the plate. These simulations are then carried out under the plane-stress assumption.

**Feedback**

- Sheet 3488: The analysis of the bevelled tube is carried out as axisymmetric. Comparisons between this 2D axisymmetric approach and a 3D approach have been carried out in [1] and show the good representativeness of the 2D approach, even if the latter does not take speed effects into account.

  - Thesis L.D. 2004: On the unbevelled plate, several modellings are tested:
  - Full 3D thermomechanical analysis;
  - 2D plane-strain analysis, in which one models a section perpendicular to the advance of the torch;
- 2D plane-stress analysis, in which one models the mid-plane of the plate.

One observes, **in this case**, that the 2D plane-strain analysis does not reproduce well the results obtained on the 3D analysis, in terms of displacements and of residual stresses, except for the latter in the central zone of the plate. As for the 2D plane-stress analysis, it gives residual-stress results very close to those of the 3D analysis.

### 4.2 The mesh

**General advice**

- As with any numerical study, it is always difficult to give precise values for the mesh density; this depends greatly on the physical problem considered. In the case of welding, the mesh must be sufficiently fine around the heat source (weld beads and fusion zone) in order to capture correctly the strong thermal and mechanical gradients in this zone.
- In the case of a quasi-steady thermal modelling in a moving frame (cf. § 3.3.2), the speed of the analyses allows a higher mesh density, in particular around the source.
- In the case of welding on steels with metallurgical phase transformations, it is necessary to mesh the HAZ sufficiently finely. This is all the more important as the stress maximum is generally reached at the periphery of the HAZ, in the partially austenitised zone. The mesh density in this zone is therefore essential. Welding simulations on a steel which presents phase transformations therefore require, a priori, meshes that are larger in number of elements than welding simulations on steels without transformations (for an identical study).
- For 3D mechanical analyses, it is very important to coarsen the mechanical mesh as much as possible as one moves away from the welded zone. Indeed, the strong non-linearity of the problem (and the analysis times that go with it) and the transient aspect (requiring numerous time steps) limit in practice the number of nodes of the model.
- As for any problem, it is necessary to plan, from the meshing stage, the groups intended to receive the thermal and mechanical boundary conditions (path of the source, exchange surface…), as well as the zones intended for post-processing. This is all the more true for the thermal mesh, where the loading (heat source) and the boundary conditions (convection and radiation) move as the beads are deposited.
- For the fusion zone, it is often difficult to represent it because, in the absence of a macrograph, its shape is unknown.

**Feedback**

- Sheet 3488: The weld beads can be represented geometrically and meshed in a more or less complex way. There are 3 possible choices, going from the most complicated to the simplest:

  - One can choose to respect both the volume and the shape of the pass. The shape of the beads being curved, one will have to mesh surfaces with curved edges, and therefore use finite elements of degree at least 2;
  - One respects only the volume of the pass;
  - One respects the volume of the pass as well as possible.

Comparisons have been made in [2] with these three possibilities. It turns out that the results of the mechanical analyses differ very little from one mesh to another (for an equivalent thermal analysis). In conclusion, a curved mesh brings nothing significant in terms of results compared with a polygonal mesh. It is important, on the other hand, to respect approximately the volume of the deposited beads.

### 4.3 Thermal analysis

**The critical point for the thermal part concerns the modelling of the heat input.**

#### 4.3.1 Introduction

In *Code_Aster*, the computation of the thermal evolution is carried out by the non-linear solution of the heat equation (operator THER_NON_LINE [R5.02.02](doc:r5.02.02)) in the volume of the part, given an initial condition and thermal boundary conditions on the boundaries. The solution is transient; the heat source moves over the mesh.

The diffusion of heat is treated by an enthalpic formulation, which is the integral of the specific heat with respect to temperature $beta (T)=underset{0}{overset{T}{int}}rho {C}_{p}(u)mathrm{du}$. One can therefore supply either the conductivity and the specific heat $rho {C}_{p}$ as a function of temperature, or, and this is preferable (cf. the note below), the conductivity and the enthalpy as a function of temperature.

**Note on the choice of an enthalpic formulation**

- At the melting temperature, the heat capacity (which is the energy to be supplied in order to raise the temperature of the body) undergoes a discontinuity which is expressed by the latent heat of fusion, which represents the energy to be supplied in order to pass the phase-change temperature. In the case of a mixture of constituents (this is the case of alloys), melting is spread between the liquidus and solidus temperatures $mathrm{Tl}$ and $mathrm{Ts}$. An "enthalpic" formulation of the heat equation is useful for taking into account the latent-heat phenomena of phase changes. Indeed, this formulation avoids "missing" the transformation, which risks happening for the capacity formulation if the time steps are not sufficiently small.

#### 4.3.2 Handling of the addition of material (the weld beads)

**Description of the various methods**

Several methods exist for taking into account, in the transient thermal model, the successive addition of material during the various welding passes:

- The first method consists in considering only a single model (in the *Code_Aster* sense, defined by the command AFFE_MODELE) containing all the passes, and in artificially "deactivating" the beads not yet deposited by imposing on them a zero thermal conductivity (${10}^{-5}W/m°C$ in practice).
- The second method consists in nesting the thermal models (in the *Code_Aster* sense): a global mesh of all the beads is used, but the thermal model is assigned only on the part of the mesh corresponding to the metal already deposited (during the AFFE_MODELE stage). The beads not yet deposited are not included in the model. In this case, it is necessary to have as many thermal models as there are deposited beads. The various models are nested in one another, that is to say that thermal model I contains the passes from $1$ to $I$ and thermal model $I+1$ contains the passes from $1$ to $I$ and pass $I+1$. This method is cleaner than the previous one, in so far as the elements not deposited do not disturb the analysis of the pass in progress, since they are not included in the model. On the other hand, this poses a problem when chaining the thermal analyses, the temperature fields of model $I$ not being defined at all the nodes of the model corresponding to the next pass $I+1$. One must therefore carry out an extension of the computed fields from one model to the other. To do this, one proceeds in 4 stages:

  - One begins by creating a temperature field at ambient temperature ($mathrm{T20}$) over the whole mesh by the command CREA_CHAMP (operation 'AFFE'),
  - One extends the first temperature field computed at pass I (sequence number 1) by completing it with $mathrm{T20}$ on the new mesh cells (CREA_CHAMP, operations 'EXTR' then 'ASSE'),
  - One stores this field in a new data structure of type evol_ther by the command CREA_RESU,

⇒ one loops over the remaining sequence numbers and one repeats operations 2 and 3 for each sequence number, enriching the data structure created at stage 3 (keyword reuse of CREA_RESU).

- Finally, the third method consists, on a single thermal model (in the *Code_Aster* sense), in duplicating the nodes at the interface of each bead, which must be planned for at the mesh-construction stage. When assigning the thermal loading (AFFE_CHAR_THER), one assigns only the part of the model which corresponds to the metal already present. Thus, the beads not deposited do not see the loading, and the duplication of the nodes prevents heat transfer at the interface of the beads not deposited (a temperature equal to ambient is applied in the beads not yet deposited). When a new bead is added, one reconnects the corresponding nodes by applying equality of the temperatures at the duplicated nodes, by the command LIAISON_GROUP. This method is certainly the most reliable, but construction of the mesh becomes tedious if the number of passes is large.

**Feedback**

- The advantage of the first method (a single model and an almost zero conductivity for the bead not yet deposited) is its simplicity of implementation. On the other hand, it can lead to numerical oscillations of the temperature, due to the discontinuity of the conductivity at the interfaces between the beads, as observed in sheet 3488 [2]. Nevertheless, this method is often chosen because of its simplicity.
- The three methods have been tested on plate 2425 [4] and compared with experimental temperature measurements. The three analyses lead to results that are essentially identical at the measurement points.

#### 4.3.3 Modelling of the heat source

A truly complete modelling of the thermal problem would require taking into account the electro-thermo-fluid heat transfers in the electric arc, with account taken of the electromagnetic phenomena, and the modelling of the convective movements in the weld pool, in interaction with the shielding plasma, and the heat transfers in the solid part. Although an abundant literature exists concerning the taking into account of the modelling of the arc and of the weld pool in the simulation, we shall subsequently place ourselves in the perspective of modelling with an industrial analysis code, which does not make it possible to model the phenomena present in the arc and in the weld pool. These are replaced by the definition of a heat source of suitable shape. Only heat conduction is therefore modelled, which is why no further allusion will be made to the modelling of the arc and of the pool.

**Description of the two possible methods**

- The first method consists in **imposing temperature cycles** on the material which one deposits. These imposed temperatures can be applied either on the deposited bead alone, or on the whole formed by the deposited bead plus the fusion zone.

  - For simplicity, the imposed-temperature function ${T}^{mathrm{imp}}$ is chosen, most often, constant in space but variable in time, that is ${T}^{mathrm{imp}}={T}^{mathrm{imp}}(x,y,z;t)={T}^{mathrm{imp}}(t)$;
  - Moreover, one often imposes this temperature only on the deposited bead: indeed, the shapes and sizes of the fusion zones are rarely known, and even if they are, this makes construction of the mesh tedious, especially if the number of beads is high;
  - The temperature method is recommended in the case of 2D modellings, or when no data are available on the welding parameters. The function ${T}^{mathrm{imp}}(t)$ often has the following form:

${T}^{mathrm{imp}}(t)=lbrace begin{array}{cc}frac{{T}^{max}}{{t}_{1}}t+{T}^{mathrm{ini}}& text{si}tle {t}_{1}\ {T}^{max}& text{si}{t}_{1}le tle {t}_{2}\ text{éventuellement,}frac{-({T}^{max}-{T}^{min})}{{t}_{3}-{t}_{2}}(t-{t}_{2})+{T}^{max}& text{si}{t}_{1}le tle {t}_{3}end{array}$ One prescribes a rise from the initial temperature ${T}^{mathrm{ini}}$ to the melting temperature, or to a possibly higher temperature ${T}^{max}$, of duration ${t}_{1}$ (the prescribed temperature rise can be linear or non-linear, exponential for example), then a hold at this temperature for a duration (${t}_{2}-{t}_{1}$), and finally, possibly, one can prescribe the start of cooling down to the temperature ${T}^{min}$. The parameters $({t}_{1,}{t}_{2,}{t}_{3,}{T}^{min})$ can be identified if temperature measurements exist;

- The remainder of the cooling takes place with exchanges by convection and radiation.

- The second method consists in **imposing a surface (** $J/{mathrm{sm}}^{2}$ **) or volume (** $J/{mathrm{sm}}^{3}$ **) heat flux** on the modelled weld bead.

  - It is used preferentially when the welding energy delivered is known: this is the case for arc welding, for which one can estimate the power delivered, UI (with $U$ the voltage and $I$ the current). Of this delivered power, only a fraction $eta mathrm{UI}$ actually penetrates the part and contributes to the heating and the melting of the materials. For the TIG process, for example, the parameter $eta$ is of the order of 0.6 to 0.9;
  - It is then possible to distribute this power, over the surface or through the volume (or both), on the mesh. The distribution of this heat flux can be extremely simple (constant in space in the deposited metal), or more elaborate (double-ellipsoid model with a Gaussian distribution, developed by J. Goldak). Calibration of the parameters of the heat source adopted requires temperature measurements.

Subsequently, we detail the procedure to follow in the case of a modelling by heat flux, distinguishing the 3D and 2D simulations. One assumes that temperature data are available.

**Case of a 3D modelling with heat flux**

- We give below a few possible forms for the spatial representation of the volume or surface fluxes:

  - Example of a volume flux, constant along $y$ and $z$ and variable in the welding direction, here $x$. ${Q}^{V}={Q}^{V}(x,y,z;t)={Q}^{V}(x;t)=lbrace begin{array}{cc}frac{{Q}^{max}}{L/2}x& text{si}0le xle frac{L}{2}\ frac{-{Q}^{max}}{L/2}(x-L)& text{si}frac{L}{2}le xle Lend{array}$
  - Example of a cylindrical surface flux ${Q}^{S}={Q}^{S}(x,y;t)$, with circular base of radius $R$ and maximum intensity ${Q}^{max}$.
- When temperature measurements are available, the parameters of this heat input (sizes, values of the maximum power injected) can be calibrated so as to reproduce as well as possible, by analysis, the measured thermal cycles.
- This calibration stage can be carried out easily and with short analysis times by means of a quasi-steady analysis in a moving frame (THER_NON_LINE_MO). This assumes that the path of the source is rectilinear and uniform, at constant speed. In this case, one assumes that a steady regime is established, and the heat equation written in this frame becomes independent of time. One therefore obtains the temperature field in this frame (a function of space only). One can possibly recover the time dimension by a change of variable ($X=x–mathrm{V.t}$). One can therefore rapidly (a single time step) identify the parameters of the source, which are then reused in the case of the transient analysis.
- Once the identification has been carried out, and if the power of the welding process is known, one will be able to deduce from it the value of the parameter $eta$ in the net power (in order to check the consistency of the value obtained with respect to the process). Indeed, the integral of the volume (respectively surface) density of heat flux over the chosen volume (respectively the chosen surface) must be equal to the net power, that is

$underset{V}{int}{Q}^{V}(x,y,z;t)mathrm{dv}=eta mathrm{UI}text{et}underset{S}{int}{Q}^{S}(x,y,z;t)mathrm{ds}=eta mathrm{UI}$

In the two examples presented above, this gives:

  - Example of the volume flux: $Sunderset{0}{overset{L}{int}}{Q}^{V}(x;t)mathrm{dx}=Sast frac{L}{2}ast {Q}^{max}=eta mathrm{UI}$ where $S$ is the surface of the deposited bead perpendicular to the welding direction.
  - Example of the surface flux: $underset{S}{int}{Q}^{S}(x,y,z;t)mathrm{ds}=pi ast {R}^{2}ast {Q}^{max}=eta mathrm{UI}$.
- The heat flux having been identified spatially, one must now implement the displacement of this source over the mesh for the transient thermal analysis. One thus defines a function of the welding direction, here $x$, and of time, by the operator DEFI_NAPPE: a flux as a function of time is applied successively on the mesh cells located under the path of the welding torch, considering a rise to the maximum value, followed by a descent to zero. The descent of the flux on one mesh cell corresponds to the rise of the flux on the next adjacent mesh cell. Thus the sources are "switched on" successively along the welding axis during the passage of the torch, which corresponds to a heat source of constant intensity travelling in space along the welding axis.

**Case of a 2D modelling with heat flux**

- In the case of a 2D modelling, the approach is different, because one must choose a flux (volume or surface) which varies in time, in order to take into account, in the 2D analysis, the approach and the moving away of the torch. For the spatial distribution of this flux, one often chooses a flux that is constant in space. An example of a surface flux is given below:

${Q}_{r}={Q}_{r}(x,y,z;t)={Q}_{r}(x;t)=lbrace begin{array}{cc}frac{{Q}^{max}}{{t}_{1}}t& text{si}tle {t}_{1}\ {Q}^{max}& text{si}{t}_{1}le tle {t}_{2}\ -{Q}^{max}frac{(t-{t}_{2})}{({t}_{3}-{t}_{2})}& text{si}{t}_{2}le tle {t}_{3}end{array}$

- Here again, one will have to identify, from temperature measurements, the parameters of the chosen flux (in our example, the time instants ${t}_{1}$, ${t}_{2}$ and ${t}_{3}$, as well as the value ${Q}^{max}$).
- If one knows the power delivered by the process, one can also deduce from it the parameter $\eta$. This gives, for our example:

$underset{t}{int}leftlbrace underset{S}{int}{Q}_{r}(t)mathrm{ds}rightrbrace =Sunderset{t}{int}{Q}_{r}(t)mathrm{dt}=frac{eta mathrm{UI}}{V}iff underset{t}{int}{Q}_{r}(t)mathrm{dt}=frac{eta mathrm{UI}}{mathrm{SV}}$

which gives:

$underset{t}{int}{Q}_{r}(t)mathrm{dt}={Q}^{max}ast frac{1}{2}({t}_{3}+{t}_{2}-{t}_{1})=frac{eta mathrm{UI}}{mathrm{SV}}$

where $V$ is the known welding speed and $S$ the known surface of the deposited bead. In order to refine the identification of the parameters of the source, one can use the parameter $eta$ found by a quasi-steady 3D analysis in a moving frame.

  - For the 2D modelling (plane strain), three representations were tested: imposed temperature, surface flux and volume flux, with identification of the parameters on measurements. #### 4.3.4 Thermal boundary conditions with the environment

The thermal boundary conditions are modelled by convective and radiative exchanges of the welded part with the environment, which are written:

$-lambda frac{partial T}{partial n}=h(T-{T}_{mathrm{air}})+varepsilon sigma ({T}^{4}-{T}_{mathrm{air}}^{4})$

where $n$ is the outward normal, $h$ the convective exchange coefficient, $sigma$ the Stefan–Boltzmann constant and $varepsilon$ the emissivity of the material.

**Notes**

- In the case of the welding of relatively thick parts, the values taken for the exchange coefficient and the emissivity often have only a secondary influence on the thermal analyses: indeed, the heat flux exchanged with the ambient air during heating and on cooling is very small compared with the heat flux by conduction in the part coming from the heat input.
- In the immediate vicinity of the source, the flux emitted by radiation is not negligible in reality, but the corresponding unknowns can be integrated into the definition of the size and of the distribution of the heat source.
- The thermal problem therefore reduces, most often, to a purely conductive problem, and not taking the radiative and convective exchanges with the environment into account is not essential.

#### 4.3.5 Thermophysical characteristics

The thermophysical properties appearing in the heat equation are functions of temperature. They are the thermal conductivity $lambda (T)$ and the specific heat $rho {C}_{p}$, or the enthalpy.

**Notes**

- As regards the thermal conductivity, it is sometimes customary to increase it artificially for temperatures higher than the melting temperature, so as to "take into account" the convective phenomena inside the pool and to homogenise the temperature of the latter. In the literature, the conductivity is thus increased by a factor of 2, 10 or 100 according to the authors (this is what is done for tube 3488, with a factor of 100). In practice, it is often reasonable to take a conductivity that is constant from the melting temperature onwards, and one considers that it is not necessary to increase it artificially, given that the unknowns concerning the weld pool (convective phenomena, and so on) are included in the definition of an ad hoc heat source.
- During welding, the latent heat of fusion (cf. the note of paragraph 3.3.1) is small compared with the heat input coming from the process, and it is not necessarily useful to take it into account. Moreover, this latent heat of fusion can also be included implicitly in the definition of the heat input.

### 4.4 Metallurgical analysis

**The critical point for the metallurgical part concerns, essentially, the knowledge of experimental data, in order to identify the metallurgical constitutive models correctly.**

#### 4.4.1 Introduction

In this part, one is interested only in steel-type material, in particular 16MND5, which can comprise five different metallurgical phases: ferrite, pearlite, bainite and martensite, called the cold phase or $alpha$, and austenite, called the hot phase or $gamma$.

When one heats a material, the $alpha$ phases transform into the $gamma$ phase. When one cools the material, the austenite transforms, according to the cooling rate, into ferrite and/or pearlite and/or bainite and/or martensite. One must therefore define, for heating, the kinetics of the transformation $alpha to gamma$ and, for cooling, the nature and the kinetics of the possible transformations $gamma to alpha$.

The metallurgical phases present therefore depend on the temperature and on the temperature rate. The computation of these phases is carried out as post-processing of a mechanical analysis, by the command CALC_META. One must specify in this command the initial metallurgical state.

#### 4.4.2 Constitutive models on heating and on cooling

Currently in *Code_Aster*, for steel-type materials, there exists only a single constitutive model making it possible to compute, at each time step, the proportions of the various phases. Nevertheless, this model is different on heating and on cooling. The detailed expression of the kinetics on heating and on cooling will be found in [R4.04.01](doc:r4.04.01).

#### 4.4.3 Thermal => Metallurgical interaction

As we have already specified previously, there is no coupling between the thermal and metallurgical analyses. Now, the thermophysical properties (conductivity, specific heat or enthalpy) of the material point depend on the proportions of the various phases present: if this is in general not too detrimental with respect to the mechanical predictions, it can be so for a fine prediction of the final metallurgy (but this is not, in general, the objective of a numerical simulation of welding).

### 4.5 Mechanical analysis

**The critical points for the mechanical part concern the choice of the constitutive law and the identification of the parameters of this law.**

#### 4.5.1 Principle of the mechanical analysis

The mechanical analysis is carried out by solution of the equilibrium equations (operator STAT_NON_LINE), taking into account the dependence of the mechanical properties with respect to temperature and, possibly, to the proportions of the metallurgical phases.

#### 4.5.2 Handling of the addition of material (the weld beads)

In mechanics, two methods exist for handling the addition of material, equivalent in principle to those of the thermal analysis.

- One can consider a single mechanical model comprising all the beads, in which the nodes at the interface between beads are duplicated. When a new bead is added, one assigns interface conditions bearing on the displacement increment (LIAISON_DDL). Just as in the thermal analysis, this method is certainly the most reliable, but construction of the mesh becomes tedious if the number of passes is high. There is no **recent** feedback on this method.
- The method most commonly used in mechanics is that in which one has a single mechanical model for all the passes, the elements not yet present being artificially deactivated by assignment of a "quasi-zero" Young's modulus ($E={10}^{-11}.E(20°C)$ in practice). The value of Young's modulus to assign in the metal not deposited results from a compromise: if the value is too high, stresses will be generated in the metal not yet deposited, but if it is too low, this can lead to convergence problems.

#### 4.5.3 Constitutive law

In *Code_Aster*, various constitutive laws exist for describing the non-linear behaviour of a material (isotropic, kinematic or mixed hardening, viscous effect, …). The user will find in document [U4.51.11](doc:u4.51.11) the inventory of these models. Given that these models are not specific to welding simulations alone, we shall not describe them.

**Note**

Nevertheless, we can cite the mechanical models with the effect of the structural transformations, which have been developed for steels such as 16MND5, and especially within the framework of welding activities.

These models, which are described in detail in documents [R4.04.02](doc:r4.04.02) and [R4.04.03](doc:r4.04.03), make it possible to model the following phenomena: plastic behaviour or viscous behaviour, linear or non-linear isotropic hardening or linear kinematic hardening, transformation plasticity, recovery of hardening of metallurgical origin, recovery of hardening of viscous origin. One can carry out an analysis in small strains but also in large strains, either with the option PETIT_REAC or with the option SIMO_MIEHE (the models with kinematic hardening do not exist with SIMO_MIEHE).

The two important questions concerning the choice of the constitutive law are:

- Must one take into account the viscous effects, because of the high temperatures generated during welding?
- Which type of hardening must one consider (isotropic, kinematic or mixed)? This question is important in welding, because this process involves tension and compression cycles.

The viscous effects are often neglected, essentially because of the lack of experimental data. A plastic model can then be sufficient if its identification is carried out from tests in which the loading rate is close to that encountered in the structure studied [6]. Otherwise, it is preferable to take a viscous model, especially if one wishes to simulate a loading of stress-relief type.

As regards the type of hardening, it has been observed in [3], [4] and [6] that a model with isotropic hardening generally leads to a very high final stress level (compared with measurements). The final maximum stress level will be all the higher as the tension–compression plastic cycles are numerous. That is why, the higher the number of passes modelled, the higher will be the residual stresses predicted by a modelling with isotropic hardening. One can attenuate this result by using isotropic hardening with recovery of viscous origin, which compensates the effect of the isotropic hardening. Conversely, a model with kinematic hardening tends to underestimate the residual stresses. The real behaviour of the material is often a combination of the two hardenings.

#### 4.5.4 Note on large strains

Taking large strains into account is in general not indispensable for a welding simulation on thick structures. On the other hand, if the structures are thin (large distortions), it is necessary to take them into account.

In *Code_Aster*, two possibilities exist for taking large strains into account in the operator STAT_NON_LINE:

- The option PETIT_REAC, suited when the rotations are small;
- The option SIMO_MIEHE, for an exact formulation of large strains, but restricted to models with isotropic hardening.

#### 4.5.5 Boundary conditions

**Feedback**

- Sheet 3488: The axisymmetric modelling of welding on the tube implicitly, and wrongly, assumes that welding takes place simultaneously over the whole circumference of the tube, and therefore that the temperature rises everywhere in the bevel. In reality, the heat source advances towards a part of the structure which has remained cold, which necessarily restrains the welded zone. The part, at the location of the heat source, cannot therefore expand freely. This self-restraint effect must fade when the heat diffuses, and disappear during the cooling phase. To remedy this problem, one can impose an axial restraint on the tube, only during the heating phase. One thus prevents the tube from expanding freely on heating; on the other hand, it is free to deform on cooling.

### 4.6 Analysis time

One observes (in version STA9), through the various welding studies carried out in recent years, that most of the analysis time is consumed in the mechanical analysis. To give an order of magnitude, we give two examples below:

- Test case HTNA100A, which considers a bevelled tube welded in two passes by the TIG process (the so-called sheet 3488):

  - Number of nodes = 3632
  - Modelling: axisymmetric
  - CPU time of the thermal analysis = 307 s
  - CPU time of the metallurgical analysis = 25 s
  - CPU time of the mechanical analysis = 5169 s
- Study on a bevelled plate welded in two passes by the TIG process (the so-called sheet 2425)

  - Number of nodes = 11600
  - Modelling: 3D
  - CPU time of the thermal analysis = 951 s
  - CPU time of the mechanical analysis = 64601 s

**Notes**

- The possible metallurgical analysis costs nothing in analysis time, because it consists only in integrating the constitutive models; there are no equilibrium equations to solve.
- The mechanical time could certainly be improved if one considered (during the solution of the mechanical equilibrium equations by the operator STAT_NON_LINE), in the prediction phase, the dependence of Young's modulus, and perhaps of the coefficient of thermal expansion, with respect to temperature. Currently, only the thermal strain (in fact its derivative) is taken into account in the right-hand side.
- The mechanical part being a strongly non-linear problem, it is often necessary to use line search in the STAT_NON_LINE module in order to facilitate convergence. Moreover, a time-step subdivision adapted to the evolution of the temperatures makes it possible to facilitate convergence.
