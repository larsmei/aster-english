# r6.02.02 Linear solver by the multifrontal method MULT_FRONT

**Summary:**

The multifrontal method MULT_FRONT is a direct method particularly well suited to the solution of linear systems whose matrix is sparse. This method comprises a preliminary renumbering phase intended to minimise the fill-in of the matrix during the factorisation.

This phase also makes it possible to group the variables into "super-variables" or "super-nodes". The factorisation itself is carried out as a sequence of eliminations of super-nodes, in dense matrices. These dense matrices allow the use of optimised routines such as the BLAS, which obtain the best performance on vector or scalar machines.

Once the factorised matrix is available, each solution of a system requires only an inexpensive "forward/back substitution".

> **Contents**

## 1 Description of the method

The multifrontal method is a **direct** method for the solution of linear systems, which is particularly well suited to systems having a sparse matrix. It factorises symmetric or non-symmetric matrices, not necessarily positive definite. In the most general case, the multifrontal method calls on the Gauss method with a search for pivots.

The method implemented in *Code_Aster* factorises real or complex matrices, symmetric or not (a non-symmetric matrix is accepted provided that its structure remains symmetric), and uses the algorithm known as ${\mathit{LDL}}^{T}$ or $\mathit{L.U}$ , without a search for pivots.

The solution of a linear system is carried out in three stages:

- renumbering of the unknowns,
- factorisation of the matrix,
- forward/back substitution.

In the case where several linear systems, with the same matrix, are to be solved, only the forward/back substitutions are to be performed. Likewise, if several matrices of the same structure are to be factorised, the renumbering of the unknowns will not have to be redone. This preliminary phase, which will ensure the performance of the factorisation, has a non-negligible cost; however, its relative weight (in computation time) decreases with the size of the matrices to be factorised.

We shall follow the following plan:

1. presentation of the classical ${\mathrm{LDL}}^{T}$ method adapted to dense symmetric matrices. Notion of elimination,
2. extension of the method to sparse matrices, notion of fill-in,
3. presentation of the multifrontal method.

1. the operations [eq 1.1-7] are called **the elimination** of the unknown $j$ . Indeed, after [eq 1.1-7], one will **never again** call on the terms of column $j$ in the remainder of the algorithm. By contrast, the methods by rows are called "looking backward methods"; one fetches the previously computed terms at each new computation,
2. the operation [eq 1.1-7] is an operation of "saxpy" type: one subtracts from the vector $({L}_{\text{ki}},k=i+1,n)$ the product of the constant ${L}_{ij}\cdot {D}_{jj}$ and of the vector $({L}_{\text{kj}},k=i+1,n)$ ,

- ${D}_{\text{j+1,j+1}}$ is known (one checks this easily),
- it is sufficient to divide the column $({L}_{\text{k,j+1}},k=j+2,n)$ by ${D}_{\text{j+1,j+1}}$ in order to have its definitive value.

(Above, the definitive terms ${L}_{\text{ki}}$ have been abusively confused with their programming name, which contains the values of ${L}_{\text{ki}}$ modified in the course of the eliminations).

### 1.2 Sparse matrix and fill-in

It is recalled that if the initial matrix contains zero terms, the successive eliminations cause fill-in, that is to say that certain terms ${L}_{\text{ki}}$ are different from zero whereas the initial term ${A}_{\text{ki}}$ is zero.

Suppose that before the elimination of the unknown $j$ , the term ${L}_{\text{ki}}$ is zero; if ${L}_{\text{kj}}$ and ${L}_{ij}$ are **both** non-zero, [eq 1.1-9] shows that ${L}_{\text{ki}}$ will itself become non-zero. This fill-in has a graphical interpretation. Suppose in this case that all the unknowns are represented by the nodes of a graph: one will connect the nodes $k$ and $i$ if and only if the term ${A}_{\text{ki}}$ of the initial matrix is non-zero. If ${A}_{\text{ki}}$ is zero with $i$ connected to $j$ and $k$ connected to $j$ , the elimination of $j$ from the graphical point of view will then consist in connecting $i$ and $k$ .

Figure [Figure 1.2-a] illustrates this interpretation. The dashed edges are those created by the elimination of $j$ . They correspond to new non-zero terms of the matrix $L$ .

### 1.3 Multifrontal method

The multifrontal method is a direct method, of Gauss type, which aims to exploit to the maximum the sparsity of the matrix to be factorised. It seeks, on the one hand, to minimise the fill-in by using an optimal renumbering and, on the other hand, it extracts from the sparse structure of the matrix the information allowing it **to eliminate** (cf. page 5, remark (1)) unknowns independently of one another.

One cannot build the elimination tree solely from the initial sparse matrix: one must also know the fill-in terms, as already mentioned previously. The numerical factorisation of the multifrontal method is preceded by an important phase: the simulation of the eliminations and thus of the creation of the non-zero terms. This simulation is also called the logical or symbolic elimination. This simulation takes place during* the first phase: the renumbering. The four phases of the multifrontal method will be seen, the most important being the first and the fourth.

**First phase:** The "minimum degree" renumbering.

This renumbering has as its **first aim** to minimise the number of operations to be performed during the factorisation. To this end one simulates the elimination of the nodes and one chooses as candidate for elimination the node of the graph having the smallest number of neighbours. One uses here the notion of graph seen in figure [Figure 1.2-a]. The initial sparse matrix defines the graph on which one works. The latter is then updated at each elimination of nodes (creation of links). The simulation of the fill-in makes it possible to achieve the **second aim**, which is the construction of the elimination tree seen in the previous paragraph. The **third aim** achieved is the creation of the **super-nodes**. This is an important notion which we shall develop.

A super-node (SN) is formed of the set of nodes which, in the course of the elimination, have the same neighbours in the sense of the elimination graph. In the course of the simulation of the elimination, one detects that, for example, the nodes

In summary, the first phase of the multifrontal method (the renumbering by the "minimum degree") consists of the following three actions:

- a renumbering of the unknowns to be eliminated, in order to minimise the fill-in. One simulates the eliminations by updating each time the lists of neighbours of the nodes,
- jointly with the previous action, one builds the elimination tree, and,
- one detects the "super-nodes", which may be described as an equivalence class for the relation "have the same neighbours".

**Note:**

*The elimination tree supplied by the renumbering is expressed in terms of super-nodes, because the numerical elimination to follow will be done by super-nodes.*

**Second phase:** The symbolic factorisation

This phase is not as fundamental as the previous one. It is a technical phase intended to build certain pointers. These are in particular the arrays of global and local indices which establish the correspondences between the unknowns during the assembly of the frontal matrices.

**Third phase:** The execution sequence

This is also a technical phase. It was seen in the previous paragraph that the method consisted in traversing the elimination tree, performing an elimination at each node of the tree. The result of this elimination is a **frontal matrix**. The order of this matrix is the number of neighbours of the eliminated SN. The storage of the frontal matrices is costly in terms of memory occupation.

The frontal matrix

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.02/Object_113.svg)

(result of the elimination of the

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.02/Object_114.svg)

There are several ways of traversing the tree while respecting the constraint: "the child must be eliminated before the father". The purpose of the third phase is to find the traversal order which minimises the memory space required for the storage of the frontal matrices ([bib2], page 2.12).

**Fourth phase:** The numerical factorisation

This phase is the actual factorisation, that is the computation of the matrices $L$ and $D$ . In what follows, these two matrices will be confused and, from the computer point of view, $D$ will be seen as the diagonal terms of $L$ .

The numerical factorisation consists in traversing the elimination tree; for each "super-node", one performs:

- the assembly, into the "mother" frontal matrix, of the "daughter" frontal matrices,
- the elimination of the columns of the super-node.

### 1.4 Forward substitution – back substitution

The columns of $L$ being stored in compacted form, the forward substitution is of "saxpy" type and the back substitution of scalar-product type, these two operations both being indexed. That is to say that the forward-substitution algorithm is roughly the following (having previously initialised

![Figure](/figures/r6.02.02/Object_129.svg)

by the right-hand side of the system):

> $\begin{array}{}\mid \begin{array}{}\text{pour}i=1\text{à}n\text{faire}\\ \text{}\mid \begin{array}{}\text{pour}k\in \text{colonne}i\text{faire}\\ x(\mathrm{global}(k))=x(\mathrm{global}(k))-{L}_{\text{ki}}\times x(i)\text{}(\mathrm{saxpy}\mathrm{indexé})\end{array}\end{array}\\ \mid \begin{array}{}\text{pour}i=1\text{à}n\text{faire}\\ \text{}x(i)=x(i)/{D}_{ii}\end{array}\end{array}$

## 2 Implementation and use in Code_Aster

The use of the multifrontal method is accessible through the operator NUME_DDL, in the following way:

```text
nu = NUME_DDL (matr_rigi = matr, stockage = 'MORSE',

renum = 'MD'

ou renum= 'MDA',

ou renum= 'METIS') ;
```

Two other renumbering methods are available: approximate minimum degree (MDA), which is a variant of the minimum-degree method, and a nested-dissection method (METIS). They are described briefly in annexes 1 and 2. It is sufficient to replace the value of renum by MDA, or METIS.

This method is also directly available under the keyword SOLVEUR in MECA_STATIQUE, STAT_NON_LINE, DYNA_NON_LINE, THER_LINEAIRE, THER_NON_LINE with the same logic.

Then, one will use the operator FACTORISER, the call being the same as in the case of a matrix stored in profile mode.

In NUME_DDL, one indicates that the matrix to be factorised is stored in MORSE mode and that one requests one of the two "minimum degree" renumberings, or a renumbering by nested dissection (METIS).

In this case, NUME_DDL performs the first three phases seen previously:

1. renumbering,
2. symbolic factorisation,
3. execution sequence.

Since a recent development, the renumbering is performed on the nodes (in the sense of the interpolation), and no longer on the degrees of freedom. This saves computation time and ensures that the initial order of the degrees of freedom inside the nodes is respected (this is sometimes necessary in the incompressible case, where the pressure must be numbered after the displacements). The numbering constraint on the "double Lagrange" multipliers bracketing the degrees of freedom affected by the boundary condition is also respected.

In addition, NUME_DDL prepares the splitting into blocks of the factorised matrix. Indeed, it was seen that at each elimination, one stored in an array the columns of the factorised matrix. These columns will be used only during the forward/back substitution. They are never used for the computation of other columns. There is therefore no point in having them all in memory. They are stored, in *Code_Aster*, in the form of a collection of JEVEUX objects of variable length. These objects, blocks of columns, must nevertheless satisfy the following constraint: each block corresponds to an integer number of "super-nodes". One cannot therefore have the columns of the same "super-node" on several blocks.

Since one does not know the memory space available during the numerical factorisation, one decides in NUME_DDL that the maximum length of each block will be the max. (over all the SNs) of the sum of the lengths of the columns of the $\mathit{SN}$ .

$L{b}_{\max}=\underset{\mathrm{SNi}}{\mathrm{Max}}(\sum_{k\in \mathrm{SNi}}\mathrm{longueur}({\mathrm{col.}}_{k}))\text{},\text{}\text{(en abrégé)}$

The operator FACTORISER uses the pointers created by NUME_DDL and the initial MORSE matrix. It creates the factorised matrix in the form of a collection of JEVEUX objects, as was seen previously. A **provisional** data structure is also necessary. It concerns the frontal matrices. Two cases arise:

- the stack of frontal matrices can fit entirely in memory (in a one-dimensional array); in this case, one allocates a JEVEUX object and the multifrontal algorithm then manages this space itself, by storing the "mother" frontal matrix in place of the "daughter" matrices,
- it does not fit and, in this case, it is created in the form of a collection of JEVEUX objects, each frontal matrix being an object. For the elimination of "super-node" $i$ one must have simultaneously in memory:
- the block of the factorised matrix to which the $\mathrm{SNi}$ belongs
- the frontal-matrix object $i$ as well as all the "daughter" frontal matrices of $i$ which will be **destroyed** after their use.

The frontal matrix $i$ will itself be kept in memory until its use and its destruction. One could, as was done previously, release in the JEVEUX sense each frontal matrix after its creation. This can then lead, in the event of little memory being available, to a storage on disk which is prohibitive in volume. Indeed, a destruction of a JEVEUX object does not entail the destruction of its image on disk, and the sum of the lengths of the frontal matrices is enormous.

In summary, one must be able to store simultaneously in memory:

- a block of the factorised matrix,
- the stack of frontal matrices, in a single object if it fits, in several objects otherwise.

One therefore needs **"enough"** memory to use the multifrontal method. The second way of storing the stack allows execution when the memory is sufficient, but fragmented.

When the memory is insufficient, one must relaunch the execution of FACTORISER with a larger memory.

**Reference documentation of the "Approximate Minimum degree" method**

The "minimum degree" algorithm consists in numbering the nodes of a graph in increasing order of the number of their neighbours. Here is a simplified but essential description of it.

1. one forms the initial graph associated with the sparse matrix to be factorised,
2. one computes the number of neighbours of the nodes (their degree),
3. one numbers first (then eliminates from the graph) the node having the fewest neighbours (minimum degree),
4. one updates the graph of step 1,
5. one returns to step 2.

The computation of the degree of the nodes is an operation costly in computation time. The "Approximate Minimum degree" method [bib3] proposes to reduce this cost. To this end, instead of computing the true degree of a node one contents oneself with an upper bound (often equal, according to the authors, to the true degree), of easier computation. Indeed, in the course of the elimination the true degree of node $i$ is equal to ${d}_{i}$ , the cardinal of the following set: ${A}_{i}\cup \lbrace {L}_{e}\rbrace$ , where the union is taken over the set of the neighbours $e$ *,* previously eliminated, of $i$ (fill-in terms), and where ${A}_{i}$ is the set of the initial neighbours. In the approximate method $i$ is replaced by ${d}_{i}=\text{card}({A}_{i})+\sum\text{card}\lbrace {L}_{e}\rbrace$ , that is to say that one neglects the intersection of the ${L}_{e}$ .

This method is described in [bib3] and the various variants of the "minimum degree" algorithm are set out in [bib4].

**Reference documentation** METIS

The implementation of the renumbering module for sparse matrices METIS is described in the note [bib5], supplied in the METIS-4.0/Doc directory of the software. The algorithm used is described in [bib6]

It is also possible to consult the following internet address: [http://www.cs.umn.edu/~karypis](http://www.cs.umn.edu/~karypis)

METIS is principally a tool for partitioning graphs (meshes), aiming at the following 2 goals:

- to partition a given graph into $p$ subgraphs of sizes as close as possible,
- to minimise the size of the boundaries between the subgraphs.

The first goal aims at a good balance of parallelisation on $p$ processors and the second aims at minimising the communications.

METIS uses a multilevel graph-partitioning algorithm, proceeding in the following way: at each level one seeks separators (sets of edges), of minimal size, cutting the graph into equal parts. METIS applies this principle to the renumbering of the nodes of a graph by seeking at each step a separator dividing the graph into 2 subgraphs. One numbers first the nodes of the first subgraph, then those of the second, then those of the separator. One then applies the same algorithm to the 2 subgraphs in a recursive manner.
