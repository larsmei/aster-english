# u2.07.07 Setting up an analysis by dynamic substructuring in the presence of IFS

**Summary:**

The purpose of this document is to help users and to give them advice for carrying out a study involving the dynamic substructuring technique in the presence of fluid–structure interaction (IFS). The latter must be modelled in a displacement–pressure formulation (called U_P in code_aster) and in 3D. See the documents U3.14.02 and R4.02.02 for any further detail.

One first presents the general approach for setting up the technique.

One then describes the sequences of operations required in order to obtain usable results. The presentation is illustrated by an example treating a modal analysis.

Reading the document U2.07.05 «Setting up an analysis by dynamic substructuring» is a prerequisite to reading the present document, which takes up many of its elements.

## 1 What does dynamic substructuring consist of?

Dynamic substructuring consists in partitioning a part into several substructures in order to reduce the size of the numerical model and to be able to study each substructure independently. The overall model is then obtained by assembling the models of the different substructures.

Each substructure is projected onto a judiciously chosen basis of vectors. This projection makes it possible to reduce the size of the model of each substructure and consequently the size of the model of the assembled structure.

## 2 Why use dynamic substructuring?

The growing complexity of mechanical structures makes it difficult to estimate their dynamic behaviour under an imposed loading. The numerical modelling of the complete structure would require a significant cost, both in terms of memory space and of analysis cost.

In addition to the reduction in the size of the model, the dynamic substructuring technique makes the modelling of the global structure much more modular.

## 3 Presentation of the methodology

### 3.1 General principle

For a complete presentation of dynamic substructuring, the reader is invited to consult the reference document [R4.06.02](doc:r4.06.02).

By way of preamble, it is necessary to recall that projecting the substructure onto a reduced basis necessarily introduces an approximation into the results obtained. The role of the modeller is to choose a basis rich enough to be able to capture the dynamic behaviour of the substructure. The quality of the results depends on the choice of this projection basis.

We concentrate here on the use of interface modes. This consists in carrying out a condensation of the behaviour of the structure onto the interface degrees of freedom and in solving the associated eigenvalue problem. The methodology proposed here differs from the general approach (see U2.07.05) by the fact that the interface modes mentioned here are defined in advance. **This constitutes a limitation of the approach**: the interface modes have been defined on the assumption that the methodology would be applied to piping, or at least to structures of shapes close to that of a pipe.

The principle of the methodology is the following:

- one computes, for each substructure, a set of modes with fixed interfaces;
- for each interface, one gives oneself a well-defined set of modes, which describe its kinematics. These modes have been chosen in advance so as to be able to represent well the strains of a section of a pipe filled with fluid.
- for each substructure, one computes its response under the effect of each of the interface modes. These modes come to complete the modes with fixed interfaces.

One then determines the response of the global structure with the aid of the modes thus constructed for each of the substructures. A mathematical description is proposed in section 5.

### 3.3 Extension to the case of IFS

The preceding example illustrates the methodology on a purely structural case. The extension to taking the interaction with a fluid into account is natural in this methodology. It proceeds essentially by recourse to the U_P formulation of the problem and by the definition of additional interface modes. Thus, the steps are modified as follows:

- the local modes become modes with the interface fixed in displacement **and** in pressure, and they naturally comprise a structural part and a fluid part;
- the interface modes are composed of structural modes identical to those presented in the preceding section, to which are added the fluid modes: 1 mode with constant pressure on the interface, 2 modes with a linear pressure variation along the X axis and along the Y axis of the interface, respectively.

These are axes local to the interface.

## 4 Practical implementation

### 4.1 Prerequisites in terms of modelling and mesh

We now turn to the implementation in code_aster of a modal analysis by dynamic substructuring with IFS taken into account. To do so, one defines the model problem of a lyre shown. It is a metal pipe in which water flows. The lyre is clamped at each of its ends, i.e. the displacement of the structure and the pressure of the fluid are fixed at 0. One seeks to determine the eigenmodes of this component.

![Figure](/figures/u2.07.07/100002010000048E00000392560CE67CF73A0853.png)

Figure 4.1-1: Model problem of a lyre

A section of the lyre is shown: one sees in yellow the structural elements and in blue the fluid elements. The figure recalls the components of the degrees of freedom of each of the parts of the model. One observes that the nodes shared by the structure and by the fluid carry displacement and pressure degrees of freedom. One recalls that the mesh must be composed of 3D finite elements and that a fluid–structure modelling U_P must be assigned to it.

![Figure](/figures/u2.07.07/100002000000022C000001ED402DB056DB53F849.png)

Figure 4.1-2: Section of the lyre

We are going to use dynamic substructuring to carry this simulation through. To do so, one must begin by cutting the global structure into 2 substructures. We must therefore produce 2 meshes, shown in the figure. The gap between the 2 substructures is purely artificial in this visualisation and is intended only to make the separation into 2 parts visible. For the simulation to be able to be carried out, it is **obligatory** that an interface surface be created. That is to say that, on the cut between the 2 substructures, a surface must be created and it must **bear the same name for each of them**. This is a means of identifying the interfaces in the case where one has more than 2 substructures.

On the other hand, it is interesting to note that **the meshes of 2 interface surfaces do not need to be conforming**, i.e. their nodes do not need to be coincident.

### 4.2 Course of the study

At the beginning of the command file, import the necessary objects from the dynamic_substructuring module:

| from code_aster.Applications.dynamic_substructuring import SubStructure, Structure, Interface |
| --- |

Once the meshes have been read into code_aster and the models assigned, the solution steps are the following. For each substructure: determination of the fixed-interface modes

  - Make sure that the displacements and the pressure are fixed at 0 on the interface (AFFE_CHAR_MECA – do **not** use AFFE_CHAR_CINE)
  - Computation and assembly of the mass and stiffness matrices of the substructure (ASSEMBLAGE): for example MASS1, MASS2, RAID1, RAID2
  - Computation of the fixed-interface modes (CALC_MODES): for example MODES1, MODES2

Next, the definition of the substructures, of the interface between them and the computation of the global response is carried out as follows, with the aid of Python commands:

| *# definition of the substructures* subS1 = SubStructure(RAID1, MASS1, MODES1) subS2 = SubStructure(RAID2, MASS2, MODES2) *# definition of the interface from its name (here 'Interface')* interface = Interface(subS1, subS2, 'Interface') *# computation of the values of the degrees of freedom at the interface* interface.computeInterfaceDofs('IFS') *# computation of the interface modes* ResuS_Tuyau1 = subS1.computeInterfaceModes() ResuS_Tuyau2 = subS2.computeInterfaceModes() *# construction of the global structure* myStructure = Structure([subS1, subS2],[interface]) *# computation of the* *global* *modes* *(here 6)* omega, resuSub = myStructure.computeGlobalModes(nmodes=6) |
| --- |

On exit from the call to the method computeGlobalModes of the object myStructure, the user obtains 2 outputs:

- omega: this is a list which contains the natural frequencies of the global structure
- resuSub: this is a list of code_aster result data structures of type MODE_MECA. These are the modes of the global structure projected respectively onto the first and the second substructure (in the order of the definition of the global structure, here [subS1, subS2]).

### 4.3 Practical advice

- It is advisable to place the interface between 2 substructures in zones of moderate strain. In the case of piping, this means keeping away from the elbows, which are the zones where the strains will be the largest and the least regular.
- It is advisable to compute the fixed-interface modes up to a frequency of at least 2 to 3 times the frequency sought on the global structure.
- If one wishes to model 3 substructures SD1, SD2 and SD3 separated respectively by the interfaces Int12 and Int23 as in the figure, the global structure will be defined as follows:

| *# construction of the global structure* myStructure = Structure([SD1, SD2, SD3],[Int12, Int23]) |

## 5 Theoretical elements

One presents in this section the theoretical foundations of the approach.

### 5.1 Preliminary

#### 5.1.1 General expression of the problem

One begins by recalling the formulation of a linear dynamics problem in the form of the minimisation of the Hamiltonian:

$$
\underset{\mathit{𝑥}}{min}\underset{{\mathit{𝑡}}_{1}}{\overset{{\mathit{𝑡}}_{2}}{\int}}\left({\mathit{𝑥}}^{\mathit{𝑇}}\mathit{𝐾𝑥}-{\dot{\mathit{𝑥}}}^{\mathit{𝑇}}\mathit{𝑀}\dot{\mathit{𝑥}}\right)\mathit{𝑑𝑡}
$$

With a few algebraic manipulations, one can obtain the expression of the modal analysis which matters to us:

$$
\mathit{𝐾𝑥}-{\mathit{𝜔}}^{2}\mathit{𝑀𝑥}=0
$$

#### 5.1.2 Modes and generalised unknowns

One also recalls that one denotes as follows the rectangular matrix of the ${n}_{m}$ modes:

$$
\Phi =\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{\Phi}_{1}& \text{...}& {\Phi}_{{n}_{m}}\end{array}\right]
$$

Each vector ${\Phi}_{i}$ being of length ${n}_{\text{eq}}$, the matrix $\Phi$ is of dimension ${n}_{\text{eq}}\times {n}_{m}$. One finally recalls the relation between a vector of unknowns in physical space $x$ and a vector of generalised unknowns $\eta$:

$$
x=\Phi \eta ={\eta}_{1}{\Phi}_{1}+{\eta}_{2}{\Phi}_{2}+\text{...}+{\eta}_{{n}_{m}}{\Phi}_{{n}_{m}}
$$

#### 5.1.3 Constrained problem

The last point presented concerns the solution of a dynamics problem under constraints. The latter is expressed in the general form:

$$
\mathit{Bx}=0
$$

In the present case, the matrix $B$ is a rectangular matrix whose number of rows is equal to the number of constraints applied to the unknowns. The constrained problem is expressed as follows:

$$
\underset{\mathit{𝐵𝑥}=0}{min}\underset{{\mathit{𝑡}}_{1}}{\overset{{\mathit{𝑡}}_{2}}{\int}}\left({\mathit{𝑥}}^{\mathit{𝑇}}\mathit{𝐾𝑥}-{\dot{\mathit{𝑥}}}^{\mathit{𝑇}}\mathit{𝑀}\dot{\mathit{𝑥}}\right)\mathit{𝑑𝑡}
$$

One assumes that one knows how to construct a basis of dimension ${n}_{\mathit{ker}}$ of the kernel of the constraint matrix. One denotes it:

$$
\Psi =\left[{\Psi}_{1}\mathrm{...}{\Psi}_{{n}_{\mathit{ker}}}\right]
$$

Since the solutions sought satisfy the constraints $\mathit{Bx}=0$, they do indeed belong to the kernel of $B$. One can therefore write them on this basis:

$$
x=\Psi \eta
$$

By injecting this expression into the minimisation problem, one observes that one can then relax the constraint and write the problem in the form:

$$
\underset{\eta}{min}\underset{{\mathit{𝑡}}_{1}}{\overset{{\mathit{𝑡}}_{2}}{\int}}\left({\eta}^{\mathit{𝑇}}\left[{\Psi}^{\mathit{𝑇}}\mathit{𝐾}\Psi \right]\eta -{\dot{\eta}}^{\mathit{𝑇}}\left[{\Psi}^{\mathit{𝑇}}\mathit{𝑀}\Psi \right]\dot{\eta}\right)\mathit{𝑑𝑡}
$$

The problem is therefore solvable with the aid of the classical techniques (minimisation without constraints).

### 5.2 Implementation

One considers a structure cut into 2 substructures. For each of them, the following have been determined:

- the mass matrices ${M}_{1}$ ${M}_{2}$ and the stiffness matrices ${K}_{1}$ ${K}_{2}$, of respective dimensions ${n}_{{\text{eq}}_{1}}\times {n}_{{\text{eq}}_{1}}$ and ${n}_{{\text{eq}}_{2}}\times {n}_{{\text{eq}}_{2}}$
- the matrices of the fixed-interface modes ${\Phi}_{1}$ and ${\Phi}_{2}$ of respective dimensions ${n}_{{\mathit{eq}}_{1}}\times {n}_{{m}_{1}}$ and ${n}_{{\mathit{eq}}_{2}}\times {n}_{{m}_{2}}$. They are linked respectively to the generalised unknowns ${\eta}_{1}$ and ${\eta}_{2}$.
- the matrices of the interface modes $\overline{{\Phi}_{1}}$ and $\overline{{\Phi}_{2}}$ of respective dimensions ${n}_{{\mathit{eq}}_{1}}\times {n}_{i}$ and ${n}_{{\mathit{eq}}_{2}}\times {n}_{i}$. They are linked respectively to the generalised unknowns $\overline{{\eta}_{1}}$ and $\overline{{\eta}_{2}}$.

On the basis of these definitions, one can write the link between the physical and generalised degrees of freedom of each of the substructures:

$$
{\mathit{𝑥}}_{1}=\left[{\Phi}_{1}{\overline{\Phi}}_{1}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}\\ {\overline{\eta}}_{1}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

Denoting $\stackrel{~}{{\Phi}_{i}}=\left[{\Phi}_{i}{\overline{\Phi}}_{i}\right]$ and $\stackrel{~}{{\eta}_{i}}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{i}\\ {\overline{\eta}}_{i}\end{array}\right\rbrace$, one can write the preceding relation in the form:

$$
{\mathit{𝑥}}_{1}={\stackrel{~}{\Phi}}_{1}{\stackrel{~}{\eta}}_{1}
$$

For each substructure, one can then compute the mass and stiffness matrices projected onto the fixed-interface modes and the interface modes:

$$
{\stackrel{~}{\mathit{𝐾}}}_{1}={\stackrel{~}{\Phi}}_{1}^{T}{\mathit{𝐾}}_{1}{\stackrel{~}{\Phi}}_{1}
$$

One next constructs the global matrices which bear on the 2 substructures:

$$
{\mathit{𝐾}}_{\mathit{𝑔}}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\stackrel{~}{\mathit{𝐾}}}_{1}& 0\\ 0& {\stackrel{~}{\mathit{𝐾}}}_{2}\end{array}\right]
$$

The generalised degrees of freedom are constructed in the same way:

$$
{\eta}_{g}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\stackrel{~}{\eta}}_{1}\\ {\stackrel{~}{\eta}}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}\\ {\overline{\eta}}_{1}\end{array}\right\rbrace \\ \left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{2}\\ {\overline{\eta}}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace \end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

One recalls that the ${\eta}_{i}$ are the generalised unknowns associated with the fixed-interface modes and the $\overline{{\eta}_{i}}$ those associated with the interface modes. Therefore, to ensure the continuity of the displacements in physical space across the interface, one must impose:

$$
\overline{{\eta}_{1}}=\overline{{\eta}_{2}}
$$

This can be expressed simply with the aid of a constraint matrix in the form:

$$
B{\eta}_{g}=0
$$

$$

$$
