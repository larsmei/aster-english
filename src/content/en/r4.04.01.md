# r4.04.01 Metallurgical constitutive models of steels

**Summary:**

This document presents the metallurgical constitutive models on heating and on cooling
making it possible to describe the structural transformations of steels during thermal cycles exceeding
temperatures of the order of $800°C$.

For the two types of transformation (on heating and on cooling) a detailed
description of the available models is given and identification methods are supplied.

Finally, one presents the hardness-computation model associated with the metallurgy.

## 1 Introduction

The assembly and thermomechanical-treatment operations undergone by the metallic components
of PWR plants can generate, in the materials of which they are made, stress fields,
called residual, which exist before any service loading. The metallurgical transformations and
principally the structural transformations are an important cause of these stress fields
because they modify the behaviour (by modifying the physical characteristics) and generate
thermo-mechanical loadings within the materials which undergo them (latent heats,
strains due to the differences in density of the different metallurgical phases).

These structural transformations are due essentially to the succession of a heating (often
beyond $800°C$) and of a more or less rapid cooling of the parts during their manufacture.
These thermal “cycles” may be intended (the case of heat treatments) or “undergone” (the case of welding).
In all cases, they are very variable from one point to another of the part.

The present document concerns the modelling of these structural transformations on heating and on
cooling for low-alloy steels, and this at a scale which, while remaining
“reasonable” for the metallurgist, is easily usable by the mechanical engineer.

This type of modelling can be carried out for all the elements (PLAN, AXIS, 3D) of the
`PHENOMENE= "THERMIQUE"` by the use of the operator [CALC_META](doc:u4.85.01) in “post-processing”
of a thermal-evolution analysis.
The constitutive relation dedicated to the steel, by difference with that dedicated to the zircaloys, to be used
under the keyword COMPORTEMENT of the operator is the relation `RELATION='ACIER'`.
For the definition of the metallurgical behaviour of the steel the supplying of the command
[DEFI_TRC](doc:u4.43.04) and of the factor keyword META_ACIER under the command
[DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01) is necessary.
Finally, the definition of the initial metallurgical state can be carried out with the aid of the command
[CREA_CHAMP](doc:u4.72.04), under the factor keyword ETAT_INIT of the operator [CALC_META](doc:u4.85.01).
The metallurgy analysis is necessary for carrying out mechanical analyses which take into account the
mechanical consequences of these metallurgical phenomena
[Elasto(visco)plastic modelling taking metallurgical transformations into account](doc:r4.04.02).

The models presented (on heating and on cooling) are formulated within the framework of constitutive
relations with internal (or memory) variables, and allow a simple and
rapid identification based on the experimental diagrams. The choice of the variables and the forms of the
evolution laws adopted are given and the description of the implementation of the models (identification
method) is also presented.

Finally, one presents the hardness-computation model which can, if necessary, complete the metallurgical
analyses.

## 2 Metallurgical constitutive model on cooling

### 2.1 Introduction

On the basis of the dilatometry test [r4.04.01-Dilato], the mere knowledge,
at a given time instant, of the temperature of a steel undergoing structural transformations does not
make it possible to know its strain state. On the other hand, the behaviour of such a steel seems
able to be described within the framework of constitutive models with internal variables.
Indeed, if one introduces:

- $Z=\left\lbrace {Z}_{i}; \; i=1,p \right\rbrace$ the $p$-tuple of the proportions of the
possible metallurgical constituents present at a point $M$ and at a given time instant $t$
(here, ${Z}_{1}+{Z}_{2}+{Z}_{3}+{Z}_{4}$ will be the proportions of ferrite, pearlite,
bainite and martensite and the proportion of austenite at $M$ will be equal to ${Z}_{\gamma} = 1-\left ({Z}_{1}+{Z}_{2}+{Z}_{3}+{Z}_{4}\right )$ );
- The thermal strains of the austenite ${\varepsilon}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{th}}\left (T\right )={\alpha}_{\gamma}\left (T-{T}^{\gamma}\right )$
and of the ferritic, pearlitic, bainitic and martensitic phases
${\varepsilon}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{th}}\left (T\right )={\alpha}_{\alpha}\left (T-{T}^{\gamma}\right )+\Delta {\varepsilon}_{\alpha\gamma }\left ({T}^{\gamma}\right )$ noting:

  - The mean thermal-expansion coefficient of the austenite ${\alpha}_{\gamma}$;
  - The reference temperature ${T}^{\gamma}$ at which one considers
${\varepsilon}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{th}}$ zero;
  - The mean thermal-expansion coefficient ${\alpha}_{\alpha}$ assumed
identical for the ferrite, the pearlite, the bainite and the martensite;
  - The strain $\Delta {\varepsilon}_{\alpha \gamma }^{\gamma}$, at the
temperature ${T}^{\gamma}$, of the ferritic, pearlitic, bainitic and
martensitic phases with respect to the austenite (taking the latter as the reference phase);

If one considers, in addition, that the strain of a multiphase mixture can be obtained from
the strains of each phase by a linear mixture law, one can then describe the evolution
of the strain state during a dilatometric test by:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{th}}\left (Z,T\right ) = \left (1-\sum_{i=1}^{i=4}{Z}_{i}\right ){\varepsilon}_{\gamma}^{\mathrm{th}}\left (T\right )+\left (\sum_{i=1}^{i=4}{Z}_{i}\right ){\varepsilon}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{th}}\left (T\right )
$$

that is

$$
{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{th}}\left (Z,T\right ) = \left (1-\sum_{i=1}^{i=4}{Z}_{i}\right )\left[{\alpha}_{\gamma}\left (T-{T}^{\gamma}\right )\right]+\left (\sum_{i=1}^{i=4}{Z}_{i}\right )\left[{\alpha}_{\alpha}\left (T-{T}^{\gamma}\right )+\Delta {\varepsilon}_{\alpha\gamma }^{\gamma}\right]
$$

The problem then lies in the determination of $Z$ or, more precisely and within the framework
of simple materials with internal variables, in the determination of the evolution function
$f$ such that: $\dot{Z}=f\left (T,Z,\cdots\right )$.

In order to account for an effect of the cooling rate on the evolution of the structural
transformations, we propose, within the framework of simple materials with internal variables,
a modelling of the metallurgical behaviour of steels on cooling which includes, a priori,
$\dot{T}$ among its state variables.

### 2.2 Assumptions

1. Assumption H1: A steel liable to undergo structural transformations is a simple material
with internal variables among which one can choose the quadruplet $Z$ characterising the
metallurgical structure at a point and at a given time instant.
One therefore models the structural transformations at a scale where the material point can be
multiphase. This modelling scale, which may appear metallurgically coarse, is
consistent with the notion of material point used in continuum mechanics and of which
the dilatometry specimen, assumed homogeneous, is representative.
2. Assumption H2: The CCT diagrams completed by the Koistinen-Marburger martensitic-transformation
kinetics completely characterise the metallurgical behaviour
of an austenitised steel during continuous cooling.
This assumption results directly from metallurgical practice and specifies the first of the
objectives to be set for the model: to be compatible with all the experimental data relating
to the metallurgical behaviour which accompanies the cooling of austenitised steels.
Furthermore, this assumption also generates a “natural” choice and restrictions as regards the
variables to be introduced into the model.
3. Assumption H3: The ferritic, pearlitic and (above all) bainitic transformations are impossible
below the temperature of the start of the martensitic transformation ${M}_{s}$.
This assumption, consistent with the representation of the CCT diagrams, makes it possible to decouple the
diffusion transformations from the martensitic transformation.

### 2.3 Choice of the state variables

**Driving state variables**

In the thermo-mechanics of continuous media, the driving state variables are generally the
temperature and the stress or strain state. However, because of assumption H2,
the temperature is the only driving variable retained.
Indeed, the influence of the stress state on the structural transformations does not appear
in the CCT diagrams. Furthermore, there does not exist (apart from an effect of Le Chatelier type) a
theoretical model even if experimental data relating to this influence under isothermal conditions
have been obtained for certain steels.

**Internal state variables**

The first internal variable to be introduced is the quadruplet $Z$ characterising the
metallurgical structure and whose knowledge is sufficient, a priori, to describe from a
mechanical point of view a dilatometric test.

Besides the temperature $T$, its derivative $\dot{T}$ and the stress state $\sigma$,
the austenitic grain size $d$ and the carbon content $C$ of the transforming austenite
also influence the metallurgical behaviour of steels on cooling.
However, still because of assumption H2, one chooses not to introduce $C$ as an
internal variable. Indeed, the diffusion of carbon does not appear explicitly on the
CCT diagrams, although it is implicitly taken into account, at least partially,
in the very notion of metallurgical constituent.

Furthermore, Giusti has shown that if taking $C$ into account was
theoretically possible, it led to evolution equations coupled between $C$ and
$Z$ whose experimental identification “seems very difficult, not to say impossible”.

Nevertheless, an effect of the carbon content on the decomposition of the austenite on cooling
appears indirectly on the CCT diagrams. It is the phenomenon of stabilisation of the austenite
which is expressed by a decrease of the martensitic-transformation temperature
${M}_{s}$ [:ref:r4.04.01-ExempleTRC`].

Unlike the carbon content, the austenitic grain size $d$ appears on the
CCT diagrams which relate to austenitisation conditions to which there corresponds a
value of $d$. We therefore choose to introduce $d$ as an internal variable.
However, the austenitic grain size, which results from the thermal history undergone on heating,
no longer evolves on cooling and $d$ intervenes only as a parameter in the
constitutive model on cooling.

Furthermore, the martensitic-transformation temperature ${M}_{s}$, which depends on
the thermo-metallurgical history undergone, intervenes in the Koistinen-Marburger law adopted in
assumption H2 in order to describe the martensitic transformation.
One therefore chooses to introduce ${M}_{s}$ as an internal variable.

The memory character of the internal variables introduced here in addition to $Z$ appears
clearly: $d$ characterises the thermal history undergone during the passage into the austenitic phase
and ${M}_{s}$ links the decomposition of the austenite to the conditions of its transformation into martensite.

The relation ACIER of the operator CALC_META comprises nine internal variables:

| Variable | Quantity | Description |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\mathrm{V1}$ | ${Z_1}$ | proportion of the ferrite |
| $\mathrm{V2}$ | ${Z_2}$ | proportion of the pearlite |
| $\mathrm{V3}$ | ${Z_3}$ | proportion of the bainite |
| $\mathrm{V4}$ | ${Z_4}$ | proportion of the martensite |
| $\mathrm{V5}$ | ${Z_\gamma}$ | proportion of the austenite (hot phase) |
| $\mathrm{V6}$ | ${Z_c}$ | proportion of the cold phases |
| $\mathrm{V7}$ | $d$ | austenitic grain size |
| $\mathrm{V8}$ | $T_g$ | temperature at the Gauss points |
| $\mathrm{V9}$ | ${M}_{s}$ | martensitic-transformation temperature |

*Example of a CCT diagram*

It is also necessary, in order to model all the phenomena involved during a
welding operation, to introduce other internal variables such as the tensors of
anelastic strains which may correspond to the plastic strains, of transformation
plasticity or of viscosity. But, in accordance with assumption H2, one considers that these
variables do not intervene in the evolution functions of $Z$ and of ${M}_{s}$.

Finally, the following assumptions make it possible to simplify and to specify further the general form of the model:

1. Assumption H4: The spatial gradient of the temperature $\nabla T$ intervenes only in the constitutive
relation expressing the heat-flux vector $q$; its first time derivative
$\dot{\nabla T}$ is not a state variable and the constitutive relation
expressing the heat-flux vector is Fourier's law
$q={-}\lambda \left (T,Z,d\right )\nabla T$.

1. Assumption H5: A CCT diagram makes it possible to identify an empirical relation between ${M}_{s}$, $d$ and
$\sum_{i=1}^{i=3}{Z}_{i}$

$$
{M}_{s}\left ({Z}_{1},{Z}_{2},{Z}_{3};d\right )={{M}_{s}}_{0}\left (d\right )+A\left (d\right ){\left[\sum_{i=1}^{i=3}{Z}_{i}-{Z}^{s}_{\gamma}\left (d\right )\right]}^{+}
$$

Assumption H5 means that the temperature of the start of the martensitic transformation is
constant (for a given grain size) and equal to ${{M}_{s}}_{0}$ as long as the proportion
of transformed austenite is less than a threshold ${Z}^{s}_{\gamma}$ and that its variation is a
linear function of the quantity of transformed austenite (with the linearity coefficient $A\left (d\right )$).
This assumption seems relatively well verified experimentally. It makes it possible to exclude
${M}_{s}$ from all the constitutive relations other than that expressing $z$ and
${Z}_{4}$.

With $z=\left\lbrace {Z}_{1}, {Z}_{2}, {Z}_{3} \right\rbrace$ which one will distinguish clearly
from $Z=\left\lbrace {Z}_{i}; \; i=1, p\right\rbrace$ defined previously.

Finally, and taking into account assumptions H2 and H3, the relations defining the model are therefore written
for the cold phases (excluding martensite)

$$
\dot{z}\left (t \right )=f\left (T,\dot{T},z,{M}_{s};d\right )=f\left (T,\dot{T},z;d\right ){\frac{\left[T-{M}_{s}\right]}{T-{M}_{s}}}^{+}
$$

with $z=\left\lbrace {Z}_{1},{Z}_{2},{Z}_{3}\right\rbrace$

For the martensite, the phase is written (Koistinen-Marburger equation, see )

$$
{Z}_{4}\left (T,z,{M}_{s};d\right )=\left[1-\sum_{i=1}^{i=3}{Z}_{i}\right]\left\lbrace 1-\exp \left (\beta \left (d\right ) \left \lbrace \left[{M}_{s}-T\right] \right \rbrace ^{+}\right )\right\rbrace
$$

and the martensitic-transformation temperature

$$
{M}_{s}\left (t\right )={{M}_{s}}_{0}\left (d\right )+{A}\left (d\right ){\left[\sum_{i=1}^{i=3}{Z}_{i}-{Z}^{s}_{\gamma}\left (d\right )\right]}^{+}
$$

where: $\beta$ is a characteristic of the material (of dimension $°{C}^{-1}$),
possibly a function of $d$, and ${[X]}^{+}$ denotes the positive part of
$X$.

Finally, as it seems difficult to propose a simple form of dependence of the model with respect to
these variables, one has chosen not to impose any particular form on the evolution functions
${f}_{i}$. The approach for computing the evolution rates of the metallurgical
variables then uses interpolation techniques and rests on the fact that any
experimentally known thermo-metallurgical history (a dilatometric test for example) is a
particular solution of the differential evolution equation.

## 3 Identification and implementation of the model on cooling

### 3.1 Principle

The identification of the model and the use of the experimental data constituted by the
CCT diagrams in order to determine the value taken by the function $f$ at a given
thermo-metallurgical state $(T,\dot{T},z;d)$ are based on the observation and
the following assumptions:

- the thermo-metallurgical histories appearing on a CCT diagram are all particular
solutions of the differential equation.
They therefore make it possible to compute, at each thermodynamic state encountered experimentally and
present in a CCT diagram, the value taken by the function $f$.
- the function $f$ is regular; that is to say that if two points ${E}_{k}$ and ${E}_{j}$
are neighbouring with ${E}_{k}=\left\lbrace T({t}_{K}),\dot{T}({t}_{K}),z({t}_{K});d({t}_{K})\right\rbrace$,
their evolution rates in $z$ are likewise neighbouring, that is:

$$
{E}_{k} {\approx} {E}_{j} {\Rightarrow } f({E}_{k}){\frac{\left[T {-}{M}_{s}\right]}{T{-}{M}_{s}}}^{{+}}=\dot{z}({E}_{k}){\approx}f({E}_{j}){\frac{\left[T{-}{M}_{s}\right]}{T{-}{M}_{s}}}^{+}=\dot{z}({E}_{j})
$$

One then determines the rates of the structural transformations of an arbitrary state by
interpolation among all the “pairs” $({E}_{k},f({E}_{k}))$ defined by the CCT diagrams.

### 3.2 Integration of the experimental data into the model

#### 3.2.1 Principle

In general, a CCT diagram defines in a frame $\left[\ln(t)-T\right]$ the
structural transformations associated with a series of thermal histories plotted on this
diagram [r4.04.01-ExempleTRC].
The integration of the experimental data then consists in recording for each history of these
diagrams the successive values of $\left ( T,\dot{T},z \right )$ so that for
any temperature $T$ the model knows the values taken by the function
$f$ at $(T,\dot{T}(T),z(T))$.

In order to be able, from a reduced number of numerical data, to reconstruct in a continuous way
the thermo-metallurgical evolutions, one formulates a few assumptions on the thermal evolutions
and on the metallurgical behaviour of the steels.

#### 3.2.2 Rules for the interpretation of the CCT diagrams

**Thermal evolutions**

In order to define the thermo-metallurgical histories present in a CCT diagram one must
characterise their thermal evolutions. One can note that, in a frame
$\left[\ln(t)-T\right]$ and for temperatures lower than
$820°C$, the thermal histories of the CCT diagrams can, with a fairly good
approximation, be deduced from one another by a horizontal translation [:ref:r4.04.01-ExempleTRC`].

It is therefore possible to define a thermal history ${T}^{i}(t)$ from the data
of a pilot curve ${T}_{p}(t)$ and of the time instant (in seconds) at which this history
crosses the isotherm 820°C by

$$
{t}^{i}(T)=\exp\left\lbrace \ln\left[{t}_{p}(T)\right]+\ln\left[{t}^{i}(820)\right]-\ln\left[{t}_{p}(820)\right]\right\rbrace
$$

where ${t}^{i}(T)$ and ${t}_{p}(t)$ denote the reciprocal functions
of ${T}^{i}(t)$ and ${T}_{p}(t)$.

In fact, one has more readily information relating to the cooling rates of the
thermo-metallurgical histories of the CCT diagrams than to instants of crossing of
the isotherm $820°C$.
This is in particular the case of the welding steels, whose CCT diagrams are plotted in a
frame “cooling rate at $700°C$–temperature”.
Taking  into account, one can then express the instant of crossing of
the isotherm $820°C$ as a function of ${T}_{p}(t)$ and of
${\dot{T}}^{i}(700)$ and one obtains as a characterisation of ${T}^{i}(t)$

$$
{t}^{i}(T)=\exp\left \lbrace  F(T)-F(700)-\ln({\dot{T}}^{i}(700){F}^{'}(700))\right \rbrace
$$

with $F(T)=\ln\left \lbrace  {t}_{p}(T)\right \rbrace$ and, in particular ${\dot{T}}^{i}(t(T))=\frac{1}{{F}^{'}(T){t}^{i}(T)}$

Concretely, one interpolates the function $F(T)$ by a polynomial of degree 5.

An experimental thermal evolution is therefore completely defined by the data of the coefficients
of the polynomial characterising its pilot curve and by its cooling rate at $700°C$.
The validation of this method of parameterisation of the thermal histories “read” on the
CCT diagrams is presented in .

Overall, and taking into account the relative inaccuracies of the plotting of the CCT diagrams, of the
reading of ${T}^{i}(t)$ and of the determination of ${\dot{T}}^{i}(700)$,
the agreement between the thermal histories read and recomputed seems quite sufficient.

If one has the recordings of the thermal evolutions of the CCT diagrams, one can define
each experimental thermal evolution by considering that it is its own pilot curve.
Furthermore, in the case where the dilatometric tests defining the CCT diagram used for
the identification of the model are carried out with constant cooling rates, one
characterises these cooling kinetics solely by their cooling rates
at $700°C$ and a function $F$ identically zero.

**Temperatures of the start and of the end of transformation**

A CCT diagram supplies, for a series of known thermal histories, the proportions of the
different metallurgical constituents which have formed during cooling as well as the
temperatures for which one observes on a dilatometric curve a significant variation of the
global expansion coefficient of the specimen [r4.04.01-Dilato].
These temperatures are then considered as the temperatures of the start and of the end of the
transformations. More precisely:

- the temperatures of the start of transformation indicated on the CCT diagrams correspond to 1%
of constituent already formed;
- the temperatures of the end of transformation correspond to the final proportion of the constituent
being formed minus 1%.

**Kinetics of the ferritic, pearlitic and bainitic transformations**

The observation of a dilatometric curve shows that, except in the neighbourhood of the temperatures of the start
and of the end of transformation, the evolution of the strain as a function of the temperature is
almost linear.

Taking equation  into account, the evolution of the quantity of transformed phase
as a function of the temperature is then not very far from an affine function and one therefore assumes
that:

- for the ferritic, pearlitic and bainitic transformations, the transformation rate
is, between the experimental temperatures of the start and of the end of transformation, a
linear function of the temperature;
- the rates of these transformations are **twice as slow** at the start (from 0 to 1% of transformed
constituent) and at the end of transformation (from ${Z}^{f}-1 \%$ to
${Z}^{f}$) than between the experimental temperatures of the start and
of the end of transformation.

**Martensitic transformations**

One assumes that the martensitic transformations are described by the Koistinen-Marburger law
and the phenomenological equation expressing
${M}_{s}$. One then uses each CCT diagram in order to determine the coefficients
$\beta$, $A$ and ${Z}^{s}_{\gamma}$ as well as the temperature ${M}_{s,0}$.
Finally, in order to avoid the model systematically transforming into martensite the remaining austenite
when one reaches the temperature ${M}_{s}$, one introduces an additional parameter,
called TPLM, characterising (by its cooling rate at $700°C$) the slowest
of the cooling kinetics which generates a martensitic transformation.
More precisely [Martensitic transformation]:

- ${M}_{s,0}$ is considered as the temperature of the start of the
martensitic transformation when the latter is total;
- $\beta$ is assumed constant and computed so as to satisfy, in the case of a
total martensitic transformation, ${Z}_{4}(M^f_s)=0,99$ where
$M^f_s$ is the experimental temperature of the end of transformation;
- $A$ and ${Z}^{s}_{\gamma}$ are determined by linear regression from the
experimental thermo-metallurgical histories leading to a partial
martensitic transformation.

#### 3.2.3 Effect of the austenitic grain size on the kinetics of the transformations on cooling.

The phase transformations proceed by nucleation and growth. The nucleation stage takes
place essentially on the grain boundaries. The grain size of the austenite therefore plays an
important role in the transformations on cooling. For this reason the CCT diagrams are
established for given austenitisation conditions and should strictly speaking be
used only for similar austenitisation conditions.
The experimental results tend to show that the austenitic grain size modifies the
transformation kinetics more than the temperatures of the start and of the end of the transformations, which is
expressed relatively well by a translation of the CCT diagram along the time axis.
To each point $M$ of a CCT diagram there corresponds the n-tuple $(T,\dot{T},z)$. Translating
the CCT along the time axis amounts to multiplying $\dot{T}$ by a coefficient different from
unity (the time axis is given on a logarithmic scale).
One therefore defines an “effective” cooling rate ${\dot{T}}_{\text{eff}}$:

$$
{\dot{T}}^{\text{eff}}=\dot{T}\cdot \exp(a(d-{d}_{\text{ref}}))
$$

with ${d}_{\text{ref}}$ the reference austenitic grain size of the CCT diagram
(homogeneous to a length) and $a$ a material coefficient (homogeneous to the inverse of a length).

The evolution law adopted is therefore written:

$$
\dot{z}(t)=f(T,{\dot{T}}_{\text{eff}},z,{M}_{s})=f(T,{\dot{T}}_{\text{eff}},z){\frac{\left[T-{M}_{s}\right]}{T-{M}_{s}}}^{+}\text{avec}z=\left\lbrace {Z}_{1},{Z}_{2},{Z}_{3}\right\rbrace  .
$$

This expression has the advantage of limiting the interpolation to a single reference CCT diagram.

**Note**

One makes the assumption that the nucleation and the growth of the martensite are considered as
instantaneous and that the density of the nucleation sites has little influence on this transformation.
The effect of the grain size therefore does not concern the evolution of the martensitic phase and bears
here only on the evolution of the ferritic, pearlitic and bainitic phases.

#### 3.2.4 Input of the CCT diagrams

Taking into account the previous assumptions, the input of the experimental data contained in the
CCT diagrams therefore comprises:

- For the CCT diagram:

  - the value of the austenitic grain size ${d}_{\text{ref}}$ of the diagram, which will be
the reference grain size
  - the translation coefficient $a$ for taking into account the effect of the austenitic grain
size
  - the temperature of the start of the total martensitic transformation ${M}_{s,0}$
  - the value of the coefficient $\beta$ of the Koistinen-Marburger law
  - the value of the coefficients $A$ and ${Z}^{s}_{\gamma}$ intervening in the equation
  - the values of the six coefficients of the polynomial of degree five interpolating the function
$\ln\left[{t}_{p}(T)\right]$ (if one knows explicitly the thermal histories,
each of them is considered as being its own pilot curve and the definition of its six
coefficients is to be renewed for each history)
- For each thermal history of a CCT diagram:

  - the cooling rate at $700°C$
  - the final proportions of ferrite, pearlite and bainite $Z^{f}_{1}$, $Z^{f}_{2}$ and $Z^{f}_{3}$
  - the temperatures of the start of each transformation $T^{d}_{1}$, $T^{d}_{2}$ and $T^{d}_{3}$
  - the temperatures of the end of each transformation $T^{f}_{1}$, $T^{f}_{2}$ and $T^{f}_{3}$

The CCT diagrams are introduced by the command [DEFI_TRC](doc:u4.43.04).

The complete definition of the metallurgical constitutive models (values of the parameters
${Ar}_{3}$, “quasi-static” temperature of the ferritic transformation, of ${M}_{s,0}$,
and the complete definition of the model on heating and of austenitic-grain growth) is
carried out within the command [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01) under the factor
keyword META_ACIER.

### 3.3 Evaluation of the evolution function from the experimental data

#### 3.3.1 Evaluation of the evolution function for the experimental histories

Taking into account the assumptions concerning the evolution of the structural transformations associated with the
thermo-metallurgical histories ${H}_{i}$ of a CCT diagram, one therefore has a set
of particular solutions parameterised by ${d}_{\text{ref}}$ of the differential equation
(for $T \ge {M}_{s}$):

$$
\dot{z}(t)=f(T,\dot{T},z;{d}_{\text{ref}})
$$

which make it possible, for any thermo-metallurgical state
${E}_{k}=\left\lbrace T,\dot{T},z;{d}_{\text{ref}}\right\rbrace$ of an experimental history
${H}_{i}$, to compute:

$$
\dot{z}({E}_{k}(t))=f({E}_{k})
$$

Indeed:

$$
\dot{z}({E}_{k}(t))=\frac{{dz}}{{dT}}({E}_{k})\dot{T}({E}_{k})
$$

now, taking into account the linearity assumptions on the evolutions of ${Z}_{i}(t)$ between two
consecutive states ${E}_{k}^{i}$ and ${E}_{k}^{i+1}$ of a same discretised history:

$$
\frac{{dz}}{{dT}}({E}_{k})=\frac{z({E}_{{k}^{i}})-z({E}_{{k}^{i+1}})}{T({E}_{{k}^{i}})-T({E}_{{k}^{i+1}})}
$$

where $\dot{T}({E}_{k})$ can be estimated by derivation of the analytical expression retained
in order to represent ${T}_{i}(t)$.

Thus, one can, for any temperature $T$, know the values taken by the function
$f$ at the thermo-metallurgical states
${E}_{i}=\left\lbrace T,{\dot{T}}_{i}(T),{Z}_{i}(T);{d}_{\text{ref}}\right\rbrace$ where the index
$i$ refers to the histories known experimentally.

#### 3.3.2 Computation of the progress of the transformations for an arbitrary state

It is a matter, knowing $T,\dot{T},z,{M}_{s}$ and $d$ at a given time instant $t$, of
determining the values of the metallurgical variables at the following time instant $(t+\Delta t)$.
More precisely:

- If $T(t) \ge {Ar}_{3}$ or if $\dot{T}>0$, then the metallurgical-transformation model
on cooling is inactive
- If ${Ar}_{3}>T(t)\ge {M}_{s}(t)$

  - then $\dot{z}(t)=f(T,\dot{T},z;d)=f(T,{\dot{T}}_{\text{eff}},z;{d}_{\text{ref}})$ and
$z(t+\Delta t)=z(t)+\dot{z}(t)\Delta t$
  - then one updates the martensitic-transformation temperature:

$$
{M}_{s}(t+\Delta t)={{M}_{s}}_{0}+A{\left[\sum_{i=1}^{i=3}{Z}_{i}(t+\Delta t)-{Z}^{s}\right]}^{+}
$$

  - If $T(t+\Delta t)\ge {M}_{s}(t+\Delta t)$ then ${Z}_{4}(t+\Delta t)={Z}_{4}(t)$
  - Otherwise

$$
{Z}_{4}(t+\Delta t)=\left[1-\sum_{i=1}^{i=3}{Z}_{i}(t+\Delta t)\right]\left\lbrace 1-\exp(\beta {\left[{M}_{s}(t+\Delta t)-T(t+\Delta t)\right]}^{+})\right\rbrace
$$

- If $T(t)<{M}_{s}(t)$, then

  - then $z(t+\Delta t)=z(t)$ and ${M}_{s}(t+\Delta t)={M}_{s}(t)$
  - and

$$
{Z}_{4}(t+\Delta t)=\left[1-\sum_{i=1}^{i=3}{Z}_{i}(t+\Delta t)\right]\left\lbrace 1-\exp(\beta ({d}){\left[{M}_{s}(t+\Delta t)-T(t+\Delta t)\right]}^{+})\right\rbrace
$$

In the case where ${Ar}_{3}>T(t) \ge {M}_{s}(t)$, one determines (thanks to the regularity assumption
on $f$) the value taken by $f$ at $(T,\dot{T},z;d)$ from the knowledge,
for any temperature $T$, of the values taken by $f$ at the
thermo-metallurgical states ${E}_{i}\left\lbrace T,{\dot{T}}_{i}(T),{Z}_{i}(T);{d}_{\text{ref}}(T)\right\rbrace$ of the
histories known experimentally, where ${{\dot{T}}_{i}}(T)$ is the cooling rate
for the history ${H}_{i}$ at the temperature $T$ (obtained by interpolation).

More precisely, one will determine a linear approximation of $f$ in the neighbourhood of
$(T,\dot{T},z;d)$. $f$ is a function from ${\mathbb{R}}^{5}$ (because the dependence with
respect to the parameter $d$ is included in the possible modification of the current
cooling rate) into $\mathbb{R}$; determining a linear approximation of
$f$ in the neighbourhood of $(T,\dot{T},z;d)$ amounts to determining the equation of a hyperplane
in ${\mathbb{R}}^{6}$ and therefore to having the value taken by $f$ at six points
$\left\lbrace {E}_{i}{f}({E}_{i})\right\rbrace$ “close” to $(T,\dot{T},z;d)$.

Concretely, the stages of this interpolation of the values of $f$ at $(T,\dot{T},z;d)$ are the following:

- one computes an “effective” temperature ${\dot{T}}_{\text{eff}}$ making it possible to take
into account the effect of the austenitic grain size if it is different from that of the
diagram, and one then seeks the value taken by $f$ at
$(T,{\dot{T}}_{\text{eff}},z;{d}_{\text{ref}})$
- one computes for all the known experimental histories ${H}_{i}$
the values taken by the function $f$ at the following thermo-metallurgical states
(in order to know a set of values of $f$ in a neighbourhood of
$(T,{\dot{T}}_{\text{eff}},z;{d}_{\text{ref}})$ sufficiently dense in temperature):

$$
{E}_{i}^{1}(t)=\left\lbrace T,{\dot{T}}_{i}(T),{Z}_{i}(T);{d}_{\text{ref}}(T)\right\rbrace
$$

$$
{E}_{i}^{2}(t)=\left\lbrace T+5°C,{\dot{T}}_{i}(T+5°C),{Z}_{i}(T+5°C);{d}_{\text{ref}}(T+5°C)\right\rbrace
$$

$$
{E}_{i}^{3}(t)=\left\lbrace T-5°C,{\dot{T}}_{i}(T-5°C),{Z}_{i}(T-5°C);{d}_{\text{ref}}(T-5°C)\right\rbrace
$$

- one determines the six nearest neighbours of
$E(t)=\left\lbrace T(t),{\dot{T}}_{\text{eff}}(t),z(t);{d}_{\text{ref}}(t)\right\rbrace$ among all
the ${E}_{i}^{j}(t)(j=1,3)$ defining the metallurgical behaviour of the
material in the neighbourhood of the temperature $T(t)$ by minimising the distance from $E(t)$
to each of the ${E}_{i}^{j}(t)$
- one computes the barycentric coordinates of $E(t)$ with respect to its nearest
neighbours ${E}^{v}(t)(v=1,6)$. For this, one solves the linear system associated with
this computation in the least-squares sense and by choosing the solution of minimum norm in the
case where its determinant is zero (this is the case when the nearest neighbours belong to
an affine variety of dimension less than six; in order to solve such a system, see
[Solution of non-regular systems by a singular-value decomposition method](doc:r6.03.01))
- one retains only the neighbours ${E}^{w}(t)(w \le 6)$ such that all the barycentric
coordinates ${\lambda}_{w}$ of $E(t)$ are positive (so that
$E(t)$ is situated inside the convex polyhedron resting on these points);
- one then computes:

$$
\dot{z}({E}_{k}(t))=f({E}_{k})= \frac{{\lambda}_{w}  f\left[{E}^{w}(t)\right]}{\sum_{w}{\lambda}_{w}}
$$

- finally, one computes $z$ at the following time step $z(t+\Delta t)$ according to the following explicit scheme

$$
z(t+\Delta t)=z(t)+\dot{z}(t)\Delta t
$$

**Note**

The definition of a distance used in the proximity criterion is not obvious, taking
into account the non-dimensionless character of the space of the $\left\lbrace T,\dot{T},z,d\right\rbrace$.
At present, the search for the nearest neighbours is carried out by simply non-dimensionalising
each of the variables, but one could envisage introducing weighting coefficients
in each “direction” ($T$, $\dot{T}$ or $z$) in order to account for a
predominant role played by one variable or another.

## 4 Metallurgical constitutive model on heating

### 4.1 Assumptions

On heating, the only transformation liable to occur is the transformation into
austenite, whose rate one assumes to be independent of the heating rate.
Furthermore, one also assumes that the whole of the ferritic, pearlitic, bainitic and
martensitic phases transform in an identical manner into austenite. These assumptions are generally
common to the whole of the austenitisation models ,  and .
Consequently the model adopted is of the form:

$$
{\dot{z}}_{\gamma}=f(T,{Z}_{\gamma})
$$

One recalls that the metallurgical-transformation model proposed by Leblond and Devaux and
implemented in the Sysweld code  is of the form (for the transformations on heating and on
cooling):

$$
\dot{Z}(T,Z)=\lambda \frac{{Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)-Z}{\tau (T)}
$$

where, for the austenitic transformation, the parameter $\lambda$ is taken constant.

The elements of comparison with experiment presented in ,  and  show
that, provided the functions ${Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)$ and $\tau (T)$ are identified
from tests at different heating rates, this model allows a quite
satisfactory description of the austenitic transformation of steels. Nevertheless, it seems that
the identification of the function $\tau (T)$ remains difficult .

The austenitic-transformation model is of the form:

$$
{\dot{Z}}_{\gamma}(T,{Z}_{\gamma})=\frac{{Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)-{Z}_{\gamma}}{\tau (T)}
$$

but with a simple form for the function $\tau (T)$, in order to keep a set of
metallurgical models of easy and rapid identification.

### 4.2 Form of the model adopted

In its continuous form, the model adopted is such that:

$$
{\dot{z}}_{\gamma}(T,{Z}_{\gamma})=\frac{{Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)-{Z}_{\gamma}}{\tau (T)}
$$

where ${Z}_{\gamma}$ denotes the proportion of austenite. The function ${Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)$ is such that

$$
{Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)=\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{cl}0& \text{si }T\le {Ac}_{1}\\ \frac{T-{Ac}_{1}}{{Ac}_{3}-{Ac}_{1}}& \text{si }{Ac}_{1}\le T\le {Ac}_{3}\\ 1& \text{si }T\ge {Ac}_{3}\end{array} \right .
$$

with ${Ac}_{1}$ and ${Ac}_{3}$ positive constants.
The function $\tau (T)$ is such that

$$
\tau(T)=\left \lbrace  \begin{array}{cl}{\tau}_{1}& \text{si }T\le {Ac}_{1}\\ {\tau}_{1}+\frac{T-{Ac}_{1}}{{Ac}_{3}-{Ac}_{1}}({t}_{3}-{t}_{1})& \text{si }{Ac}_{1}\le T\le {Ac}_{3}\\ {\tau}_{3}& \text{si }T\ge {Ac}_{3}\end{array}  \right .
$$

with ${\tau}_{1}$ and ${\tau}_{3}$ positive constants.

The definition of the function ${Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)$ is identical to that given by
Leblond and Devaux in  and . It corresponds to the evolution of the proportion of austenite
transformed for very low heating rates.
Indeed, at a *fixed* temperature $T$, ${Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)$ is the
asymptotic solution towards which the solution of the differential equation 
tends, with the time constant $\tau (T)$.
For low heating rates, the asymptotic solution may be considered as
reached at each time instant and ${Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)$ therefore corresponds to the evolution of the proportion
of austenite transformed during “quasi-static” evolutions. The function
${Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)$ is therefore entirely defined by the data of ${Ac}_{1}$ and
${Ac}_{3}$, which is done under the simple keywords AC1 and AC3 under the factor keyword
META_ACIER of the command [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01).

In the model proposed by Leblond and Devaux, the form of the function $\tau (T)$ is
not specified and this function is identified so as to obtain a satisfactory agreement between
the experimental and computed temperatures of the start and of the end of transformation.
In order to obtain a model of simple and rapid identification one has chosen a simple form
for the function $\tau (T)$. More precisely, in order to be able to integrate the
evolution equation , one first considered the case where the function
$\tau (T)$ is constant. In this case, one can then propose two possibilities
of simple identification of this constant function $\tau$.
The first possibility consists in identifying a value ${\tau}_{1}$ of $\tau$
making it possible to describe correctly the start of the transformations, whereas the second consists in
identifying a value ${\tau}_{3}$ of $\tau$ making it possible to describe correctly the
end of the transformations. One then tested the model obtained with an affine function $\tau (T)$
defined from the values ${\tau}_{1}$ and ${\tau}_{3}$ defined
above. The results obtained being quite satisfactory and comparable to those obtained
with the model available in Sysweld, one has chosen to introduce a model in which the function
$\tau (T)$ is affine and is defined by ${\tau}_{1}$ and ${\tau}_{3}$, which
are entered with AC1 and AC3.

### 4.3 Integration of the evolution equation

One has chosen to integrate the evolution equation  exactly in
${Z}_{\gamma}$ and explicitly in $\dot{T}$ and $\tau$ over each time step
(i.e. by considering $\dot{T}$ and $\tau$ constant over the step and equal to their
values at the start of the time step). One then obtains:

$$
{Z}_{\gamma}(t+\Delta t)=\frac{-\tau (t)\dot{T}(t)}{{Ac}_{3}-{Ac}_{1}}+{Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(t+\Delta t)-\left\lbrace -\frac{\tau (t)\dot{T}(t)}{{Ac}_{3}-{Ac}_{1}}+{Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(t)-{Z}_{\gamma}(t)\right\rbrace \exp\left[-\frac{\Delta t}{\tau (t)}\right]
$$

The consequent evolution of the proportions of all the other metallurgical constituents is then defined by:

$$
{Z}_{i}(t+\Delta t)={Z}_{i}(t) \left ( 1-\frac{{Z}_{\gamma}(t+\Delta t)-{Z}_{\gamma}(t)}{1-{Z}_{\gamma}(t)} \right )
$$

In other words, each of the phases present is transformed into austenite to the extent of its
proportion at the start of the time step.

### 4.4 Evolution of the austenitic grain size on heating

Once austenitised, the steel sees its grain size increase more or less rapidly as a function
of the temperature, but this growth always takes place since the austenite appears with a
zero grain size . Austenitic grain growth is a thermally activated process.
The growth model chosen is that of Grey and Higgins,
adapted in order to treat the material undergoing transformation

**Growth model:**

$$
\frac{d}{{dt}}(d)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left (\frac{1}{d}-\frac{1}{{d}_{\lim}} \right)
$$

**Growth during transformation, the austenite appearing with a zero grain size:**

$$
\frac{d}{{dt}}(d)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\left(\frac{1}{d}-\frac{1}{{d}_{\lim}}\right )-\frac{{dz}/{dt}}{z}d
$$

where one has $\lambda ={\lambda}_{0}\cdot \exp \left(\frac{{Q}_{\text{app}}}{R T} \right )$ and
${d}_{\lim}={d}_{10}\exp \left (-\frac{{W}_{\text{app}}}{R T} \right )$ .
With the following parameters:

- $z$ is the proportion of the austenitic phase
- $d$ is the austenitic grain diameter (of the dimension of a length)
- ${d}_{\lim}$ is the limiting grain size, depending on ${d}_{10}$,
a material parameter of the dimension of a length
- ${Q}_{\text{app}}$ and ${W}_{\text{app}}$ are material parameters of the dimension of
activation energies (${{J.mol}}^{-1}$ )
- $R$ is the perfect-gas constant ($\text{8,314 } {{J.K}}^{-1}.{{mol}}^{-1}$ )
- ${d}_{10}$ is a material parameter of the dimension of seconds per unit length

The material parameters are to be entered under the keyword META_ACIER of [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01).

#### 4.4.1 Numerical treatment

The computation of the grain size is carried out after the computation of the phase proportion and
the integration of the evolution equation is done according to a scheme implicit in $d$

$$
d={d}^{-}+\Delta d
$$

with the increment $\Delta d$

$$
\Delta d=\frac{1}{\lambda} \left ( \frac{1}{d}-\frac{1}{{d}_{\lim}} \right )\cdot \Delta t-\frac{\Delta z}{{z}^{+}}\cdot d
$$

One solves a quadratic equation in $d$.

### 4.5 Direction of the metallurgical evolution

In a structural analysis, certain zones may undergo a heating while others
cool. Moreover, under certain conditions, an austenitic transformation initiated during
heating may continue at the start of cooling. There does not therefore exist, strictly
speaking, an austenitic-transformation model and a transformation model on cooling
but a single model of metallurgical transformations which, according to the temperature considered and the
sign of the rate of thermal evolution, is described either by the model of decomposition of
the austenite, or by the model of formation of the austenite.

The direction of the metallurgical evolution (that is to say formation or decomposition of the austenite) is
determined as follows:

- If the temperature is such that $T(t+\Delta t) <{Ac}_{1}$, there are two cases:

  - If the rate is (strictly) positive $\dot{T}(t) > 0$, one uses the model of
formation of the austenite
  - If the rate is negative or zero $\dot{T}(t) \le 0$, one uses the model of
decomposition of the austenite
- If the temperature is such that $T(t+\Delta t) \in \left[  {Ac}_{1}, {Ar}_{3} \right ]$, one has four cases:

  - If the rate is positive $\dot{T}(t) > 0$, one uses the model of decomposition of the austenite
  - If the rate is zero $\dot{T}(t) > 0$ and if $z_{\gamma} \ge {Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}$, one
uses the model of decomposition of the austenite
  - If the rate is zero $\dot{T}(t) > 0$ and if $z_{\gamma} < {Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}$, one
uses the model of formation of the austenite
  - If the rate is negative $\dot{T}(t) < 0$, one uses the model of formation of the austenite
- If the temperature is such that $T(t+\Delta t) > {Ar}_{3}$, one uses the model of
formation of the austenite

**Note**

${Ar}_{3}$ is also a characteristic of the metallurgical behaviour on
cooling already defined by the transformation model on cooling.

## 5 Identification of the model on heating

### 5.1 Determination of the function ${Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)$

The function ${Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)$ may be considered as the quasi-static solution of
the differential equation  and one chooses to define it (as in )
by the relation .

In this expression, the temperatures ${Ac}_{1}$ and ${Ac}_{3}$ are the
“theoretical” quasi-static temperatures of the start and of the end of the austenitic transformation which correspond to
a proportion of austenite formed still equal to zero or already equal to one.

In fact, these temperatures are difficult to determine experimentally and one generally
considers that the quasi-static temperatures of the start and of the end of the austenitic transformation
determined experimentally correspond, respectively, to 5 and 95% of austenite formed.

In other words, if one denotes by $\widehat{Ac}_{1}$ and $\widehat{Ac}_{3}$ these temperatures,
they satisfy

$$
{Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(\widehat{Ac}_{1})=0,05 \text{ et } {Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(\widehat{Ac}_{3})=0,95
$$

In order to determine $\widehat{Ac}_{1}$ and $\widehat{Ac}_{3}$, one may use
dilatometry tests at a low heating rate or apply formulae from the literature relating
the quasi-static temperatures of the start and of the end of the austenitic transformation to the composition
of the steels.
In general these temperatures are also indicated on the CCT diagrams used for
the identification of the transformation model on cooling or may be estimated with the aid
of formulae knowing the composition of the steel .

Finally, knowing $\widehat{Ac}_{1}$ and $\widehat{Ac}_{3}$, one may then determine
the temperatures ${Ac}_{1}$ and ${Ac}_{3}$ defining the function
${Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)$ from the two equations  above.

A complete example of identification of the austenitic-transformation model is presented in .

### 5.2 Determination of the function $\tau(T)$

In general, it is not easy to bring out a means of simple and rapid identification of the
function $\tau (T)$. This is the reason why one proposes to adopt for this function
the simplified form below.

If ${Ac}_{1}\le T \le {Ac}_{3}$ :

$$
\tau (T)={\tau}_{1}+\frac{T-{Ac}_{1}}{{Ac}_{3}-{Ac}_{1}}({\tau}_{3}-{\tau}_{1})
$$

where ${\tau}_{1}$ and ${\tau}_{3}$ are positive constants.

For the identification phase, one considers in the first place the particular case where $\tau$ is
constant between ${Ac}_{1}$ and ${Ac}_{3}$ . One then proposes two types of identification
making it possible to determine either a value ${\tau}_{1}$ of $\tau$ consistent with the
experimental temperatures of the start of transformation, or a value ${\tau}_{3}$ of
$\tau$ consistent with the experimental temperatures of the end of transformation.

One presents in  the results obtained by these two identifications and one shows
(without any other form of theoretical justification) that the affine function $\tau (T)$ defined with
the values ${\tau}_{1}$ and ${\tau}_{3}$ previously determined makes it possible to obtain
an agreement with experiment quite comparable to that obtained with the Leblond model.

#### 5.2.1 Identification of ${\tau}_{3}$ from $\widehat{Ac}_{3}$

For $\dot{T}$ and $\tau$ constant and the initial condition
${Z}_{\gamma}({Ac}_{1})=0$, the solution of the evolution equation 
is (so long as ${Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{'}(T)$ is constant, that is to say so long as $T\le {Ac}_{3}$ ) :

$$
{Z}_{\gamma}(T)={Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(T)-\tau {Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{'}(T) \dot{T} \left ( 1-\exp\left[\frac{{Ac}_{1}-T}{\tau \dot{T}}\right] \right )
$$

In particular, one therefore has, for $T=\widehat{Ac}_{3}$:

$$
0,95={Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(`\widehat{Ac}_{3}`)-\tau{Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{'}({Ac}_{3}) \dot{T} \left ( 1-\exp\left[\frac{{Ac}_{1}-\widehat{Ac}_{3}}{\tau \dot{T}}\right] \right )
$$

A dilatometry test at a constant (and not very low) heating rate then makes it possible to
determine the value ${\tau}_{3}$ of $\tau$ making it possible to achieve the agreement between the
experimental and computed values of $\widehat{Ac}_{3}$. One presents in  some
comparisons between experiment and computation obtained by thus identifying the function
$\tau$ considered as constant.

#### 5.2.2 Identification of ${\tau}_{1}$ from $\widehat{Ac}_{1}$

In the same way as previously, one may also write, for $T=\widehat{Ac}_{1}$ :

$$
0,05={Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}(\widehat{Ac}_{1})-\tau  {Z}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{'}(T)\dot{T} \left (1-\exp\left[\frac{{Ac}_{1}-\widehat{Ac}_{1}}{\tau \dot{T}}\right] \right)
$$

Here again, having available a test at a constant heating rate, the previous equation makes it possible to
determine a value ${\tau}_{1}$ of $\tau$ making it possible to obtain a good agreement on
the computed and experimental temperatures $\widehat{Ac}_{1}$.

## 6 Hardness-computation model

The metallurgical analyses may be completed by a “quench” hardness computation associated with the
metallurgical structure.

The model chosen uses the assumption according to which the hardness of a multiphase material point is
well represented by a linear mixture law of the microhardnesses of the constituents (here the phases
austenite, ferrite, pearlite, bainite and martensite). The microhardnesses are taken as being
constants of the material and of the phase considered.

The model is written $H_{V}=\sum_{k}{Z}_{k}{H_{V}}_{k}$ with $H_{V}$ the hardness (here
Vickers for example) of the multiphase point, ${Z}_{k}$ the proportion of the phase $k$ and
${H_{V}}_{k}$ the hardness of the phase $k$ .

Although fairly simple, this model gives very correct results, see .

The hardness computation is carried out by means of the post-processing operator
[CALC_META](doc:u4.85.01) with the option DURT_ELGA for the hardness computations at the Gauss points
and DURT_ELNO for the computations at the nodes by element.

The hardnesses of the different metallurgical phases are material data supplied by
the user under the factor keyword DURT_META of the operator [DEFI_MATERIAU](doc:u4.43.01).

## 7 Tempering model

### 7.1 Introduction

A so-called “tempering” heat treatment is a heat treatment carried out at temperatures
lower than the allotropic transformation temperatures of the metals. In the case of steels, it
is the austenitisation temperature.

In the context of welding, it is an operation carried out deliberately in order to relieve
the stresses at the end of the process. From a microstructural point of view, the cooling of the steel and
its passage from a $\gamma$ phase (austenite) to $\alpha$ phases (ferrite,
bainite, pearlite, martensite) is metastable.
One then passes from an “as-quenched” phase to a so-called “tempered” phase. In practice, only
bainite and martensite are concerned by these metallurgical changes and the
mechanical properties of these two phases are different (tempered properties close to those of ferrite).

### 7.2 Principles of the model

The model proposed is based on the work of Y. Vincent  .
One considers the following temperature kinetics, representative of a welding thermal kinetics
observed at a point, on the figure
[Tempering thermal kinetics]

![Figure](/figures/r4.04.01/revenuCinetiqueThermique.png)

*Tempering thermal kinetics*

The tempering phase is a heating phase which starts at a temperature $T^r$ (
$T^r \approx 550°C$), which reaches a temperature of about 700°C (lower
than the austenitisation temperature $Ac_3$) and which decreases again down to $T^r$.

The mathematical model used to model this pseudo-transformation is identical to that
used for the kinetics of the diffusional transformations developed by Leblond .

One denotes:

- ${Z}^{r}(t)$ the proportion of tempered phase at each time instant of the previous thermal cycle
- ${Z}^{b}(t)$ the proportion of as-quenched phase at each time instant of the previous thermal cycle
- ${\tilde{Z}}^{r} = \frac{{Z}^{r}}{{Z}^{b}}$ is the proportion of tempered phase relative
to the as-quenched phase

One considers a law of Johnson-Mehl-Avrami type in order to describe the evolution of the percentage
of tempered phase ${\tilde{Z}}^{r}$ as a function of the holding time $t^{*}$ at a
temperature $T^{m}$

$$
{\tilde{Z}}^{r}(t^{*})=\left ( 1 - \exp\left\lbrace   -b  ({t^{*}})^n  \right\rbrace  \right )
$$

with $b=b(T^{m})$ and $n(T^{m})$ characteristics of the material to be identified which
depend on the holding temperature $T^{m}$. One assumes that the temperature is constant
between two time instants.

Let the list of time instants $t_i$ with $i \in [0, N]$ allowing a
discretised description of a temperature kinetics $T$. One denotes $\Delta t = t_{i+1} - t_{i}$
Under the assumption $T_{i+1} = T(t_{i+1})$ between $t_i$ and $t_{i+1}$, the value of the
percentage of tempered phase at the time instant $t_{i+1}$ is given by the following expression

$$
{\tilde{Z}}^{r}(t_{i+1})=\left ( 1 - \exp\left\lbrace  -b (T_{i+1}) ({ \tau_{i+1}} + \Delta t )^{n(T_{i+1})}  \right\rbrace  \right )
$$

with

$$
\tau_{i+1} = \left (  \frac{- \log \left( 1 -  {\tilde{Z}}^{r}(t_{i})\right )}{b(T_{i+1})} \right )^{\frac{1}{n(T_{i+1})}}
$$

### 7.3 Identification of the parameters of the model

One assumes known a limited number $N_c$ of pairs of values
$\left( {\tilde{Z}}^{r}_k,  t^{*}_k \right )_{k=1,N_c}$ which associate a percentage of
tempered phase with a thermal holding time applied at one and the same given temperature $T_d$.

The application of  makes it possible to have an estimate of the percentage of tempered phase at
the time instant $t=t_1$:

$$
{\tilde{Z}}^{r}(t=t_1)= {\tilde{Z}}^{r}(t^{*} = \Delta t) = \left ( 1 - \exp\left\lbrace   -b  ({t^{*}})^n  \right\rbrace  \right )
$$

One therefore finds a relation between ${\tilde{Z}}^{r}$ and $t^{*}$

$$
\log\left( t^{*} \right ) = \frac{1}{n} \log\left( -\log\left( 1- {\tilde{Z}}^{r} \right) \right ) - \frac{1}{n} \log \left( b \right )
$$

One makes a change of variable by denoting $x = \log\left( -\log\left( 1- {\tilde{Z}}^{r} \right) \right )$
and $y=\log\left( t^{*} \right )$; one may determine by linear regression the straight line
$y=\frac{1}{n} x + \frac{1}{n} \log (b)$.

The data available come from the thesis of Yannick Vincent . These data
characterise the evolution of the hardness of bainite and that of martensite during an
isothermal hold of a duration of one hour, for different holding temperatures lying between 546°C
and 700°C.

For bainite, one has

| --- | --- | --- |

| 550°C | 1h | $268$ |
| 600°C | 1h | $243$ |
| 625°C | 1h | $232$ |
| 650°C | 1h | $221$ |

In order to find the proportion of tempered phase ${\tilde{Z}}^{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}$, one applies the
following formula from the hardness $H_{V,{\alpha_{\alpha}}}$ (with
${{\alpha_{\alpha}}}=b \text{ ou } {{\alpha_{\alpha}}}=m$ for bainite and for martensite) for a

$$
{\tilde{Z}}^{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(T^m, t^{*}) = \frac{H_{V,{\alpha_{\alpha}}}^{max}-H_{V,{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(T^m, t^{*})}{H_{V,{\alpha_{\alpha}}}^{max}-H_{V}^{min,{\alpha_{\alpha}}}}
$$

But these data are insufficient to determine the parameters of the tempering model given
that, for one and the same holding temperature, one associates few holding durations or even a single duration.
These data are then completed by considering the following relation known as
time–temperature equivalence.

$$
P_{a}(T, t^{*}) = \left (  \frac{1}{T} - \frac{n R}{\Delta H} \log\left( \frac{t^{*}}{t_{0}} \right) \right)^{-1}
$$

with $t_0$ the unit of time (in order to non-dimensionalise the ratio $\frac{t^{*}}{t_{0}}$ ),
$n=\log(10)\approx 2,3$ the natural logarithm of 2, $R$ the perfect-gas constant
and $\Delta H$ the activation heat of the phenomenon studied. One will denote $c_0 = \frac{n R}{\Delta H}$
the (dimensionless) activation coefficient.

This equation makes it possible to express the holding time $t^{*}_{i}$ at a temperature $T_i$
in order to observe the same phenomenon as those obtained (experimentally for example) by a
holding time $t^{*}_{j}$ at a temperature $T_j$ .

$$
t^{*}_i = \exp{ \left ( \frac{1}{c_0}  \left( \frac{1}{T_i} - \frac{1}{P_{a}(T_j, t^{*}_j)}  \right) \right )}
$$

By fixing a holding temperature different (for example 610°C) from that of the previous tests,
one has enough points to determine $b_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(610)$ and $n_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(610)$.

### 7.4 Solution algorithm

Tempering therefore introduces two additional phases: tempered bainite and tempered martensite.
By convention the “as-quenched” bainite (or martensite) will be designated by bainite (or
martensite) of the model without tempering.

The algorithm of this post-processing rests on the following elements
which describe the conditions of activation of the tempering phenomenon:

- The temperature is assumed constant over a time increment $\Delta t = t_{i+1} - t_{i}$
and it is equal to $T = T(t_{i+1})$
- The temperature domain concerned is $T \in \left[T^r, {Ac}_3 \right]$
- Tempering is activated only following a complete welding thermal cycle which would have
generated a martensitic or bainitic phase known as as-quenched. For this one introduces an
indicator, denoted ${I}^{th}$, which will take the following values:

  - The cycle takes the value ${I}^{th} = 2$ if one has seen a complete thermal cycle, *i.e.* $T^{max} > {Ac}_3$ and
the proportion of as-quenched phase (bainite or martensite) is strictly greater than
zero $Z^{b}_{\alpha_{\alpha}} > 0$
  - The cycle takes the value ${I}^{th} = 1$ if the first thermal cycle is in progress and the as-quenched phase
is different from 0 $Z^{b}_{\alpha_{\alpha}} > 0$
  - The cycle takes the value ${I}^{th} = 0$ for the other cases.

The value of this indicator is estimated at each node and for each time instant. It is an internal variable
of the model.

The algorithm is the following, for a bainitic or martensitic phase denoted in a general manner
by ${\alpha_{\alpha}}$. One places oneself at the time instant $t_{i+1}$ and at the temperature $T_{i+1}$ (on
each node), with $\Delta t$ the time increment and $\Delta T = T_{i+1}-T_{i}$ the temperature
increment.
One denotes by $T^r$ the tempering temperature and by $T^m$ the holding temperature (different
from that of the experimental data). A first phase has computed the as-quenched phases (by applying
the Waeckel model) ${Z}^{b}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(t+1)$

- If $T_{i+1} > Ac_3$, then one is in the austenitic phase: ${I}_{i+1}^{th} = 0$ and
$\tilde{Z}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}} = 0$
- If $T_{i+1} > T^r$, then one is in the tempering phase

  - If ${I}^{th}_{i} = 2$, one has seen a complete thermal cycle. One computes $\tau_{i+1}$
by , then the time/temperature equivalence parameter $P_{a}$ of

${\tilde{Z}}^{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}$
  - Otherwise, tempering is not activated and ${\tilde{Z}}^{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}} = 0$
- Otherwise, one is not in a tempering phase, one merely maintains the phases and one
detects the cycles

  - If ${I}^{th}_{i} = 2$, then ${I}^{th}_{i+1} = 2$,
${\tilde{Z}}^{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}} = \frac{{{Z}}^{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(t)}{{{Z}}^{b}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(t)}$
  - If ${I}^{th}_{i} = 1$, then ${\tilde{Z}}^{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}} = 0$. Moreover,
if $\Delta T > 0$, this means that one is heating up again and one has ${I}^{th}_{i+1} = 2$
  - If ${I}^{th}_{i} = 0$, then ${\tilde{Z}}^{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}} = 0$. Moreover,
if $\Delta T > 0$ and ${Z}^:{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}} = 0$, then ${I}^{th}_{i+1} = 1$

One may compute the tempered phases:

$$
{Z}^{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(t+1) = {Z}^{b}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(t) {\tilde{Z}}^{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}
$$

and the as-quenched phases

$$
{Z}^{b}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(t+1) = {Z}^{b}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(t) - {Z}^{r}_{{\alpha_{\alpha}}}(t+1)
$$
