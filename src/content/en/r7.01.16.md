# r7.01.16 Integration of the associated (DRUCK_PRAGER) and non-associated (DRUCK_PRAG_N_A) elastoplastic Drucker-Prager mechanical constitutive behaviours, and post-processing

**Summary:**

This document describes the principles of several developments concerning the elastoplastic Drucker-Prager constitutive law in the associated version (DRUCK_PRAGER) and the non-associated version (DRUCK_PRAG_N_A).

One is interested first in the integration of the law itself then, this law being a softening law, in a Rice localisation indicator and finally in the sensitivity computation by direct differentiation for this law.

For the integration of the law, an implicit scheme is used.

**Contents**

## 1 Introduction

The Drucker-Prager law makes it possible to model, in an elementary way, the elastoplastic constitutive behaviour of concrete or of certain soils. Compared with Von-Mises plasticity with isotropic hardening, the difference lies in the presence of a term in $\mathrm{Tr}(\sigma )$ in the formulation of the threshold and of a non-zero spherical component of the plastic strain tensor.

In Code_Aster, the law exists in the associated version (DRUCK_PRAGER) and the non-associated version (DRUCK_PRAG_N_A), which is better suited to certain soils because it makes it possible to take dilatancy into account more accurately.

This note gathers the theoretical aspects of several developments carried out in the code around this law: its integration according to a time-implicit scheme, a Rice localisation indicator and the sensitivity computation by direct differentiation. The material is assumed to be isotropic. The Rice indicator and the sensitivity computation do not work under the plane-stress assumption.

The theory and the developments have been carried out for three types of hardening function: linear, parabolic and exponential (DRUCK_PRAG_N_A only). In the linear and parabolic cases, this function is in every case constant beyond an “ultimate” cumulative plastic strain.

### 2.2 Formulation in the associated version

#### 2.2.1 Expression of the constitutive behaviour

$\sigma$ is the stress tensor, which depends only on $\varepsilon$ and its history. The Drücker-Prager type criterion is considered:

$$
F(\sigma ,p)={\sigma}_{\text{eq}}+{\text{AI}}_{1}-R(p)\le 0
$$

where $A$ is a given coefficient and $R$ is a function of the cumulative plastic strain $p$ (hardening function), of linear or parabolic type:

- linear hardening

$$
R(p) = \sigma_Y + h \cdot p, \quad \text{si } p \in [0, p_{\mathit{ultm}}] \\
    R(p) = \sigma_Y + h \cdot p_{\text{ultm}}, \quad \text{si } p > p_{\mathit{ultm}}
$$

The coefficients $h$ , ${p}_{\mathit{ultm}}$ and ${\sigma}_{Y}$ are given.

- parabolic hardening

| $R(p)={\sigma}_{Y}(1-(1-\sqrt{\frac{{\sigma}_{\text{Yultm}}}{{\sigma}_{Y}}})\frac{p}{{p}_{\text{ultm}}}{)}^{2}$ | if $p\in [0,{p}_{\mathit{ultm}}]$ |
| --- | --- |
| $R(p)={\sigma}_{{Y}_{\text{ultm}}}$ | if $p>{p}_{\mathit{ultm}}$ |

The coefficients ${\sigma}_{\mathit{Yultm}}$ , ${p}_{\mathit{ultm}}$ and ${\sigma}_{Y}$ are given.

**Note 1:**

*One may prescribe, instead of* $A$ *and* ${\sigma}_{Y}$ *, the cohesion coefficient* $c$ *and the friction angle* $\varphi$ *:*

$A=\frac{2\sin\varphi }{3-\sin\varphi }$

${\sigma}_{Y}=\frac{\mathrm{6c}\cos\varphi }{3-\sin\varphi }$

**Note 2:**

*In this document one has chosen to favour the variable* $p$ *. The cumulative plastic shear strain* ${\gamma}^{p}=p\sqrt{3/2}$ *is also very widely used in soil mechanics.*

Considering an associated version, one assumes that the dissipation potential follows the same expression as that of the yield surface $F$ . The plastic flow then reduces to:

$$
d{\varepsilon}^{p}=d\lambda \frac{\partial F(\sigma ,p)}{\partial \sigma }
$$

with:

$$
d\lambda \ge 0\begin{array}{cc}& \end{array};\begin{array}{cc}& \end{array}F\cdot d\lambda =0\begin{array}{cc}& \end{array};\begin{array}{cc}& \end{array}F\le 0
$$

The normality rule with respect to the generalised force $R$ gives the equality between the increment of cumulative plastic strain and the increment of the multiplier $\lambda$ :

$$
\mathit{dp}=-d\lambda \frac{\partial F\left(\sigma ,p\right)}{\partial R}=d\lambda
$$

#### 2.2.2 Analytical solution of the mechanical formulation

In this chapter one places oneself in the framework of a finite increment. The integration of the law follows a purely implicit scheme, and the solution is analytical. The finite strain increment $\Delta \epsilon$ is known and supplied by the global Newton iteration. By convention the following notation is used: a subscript – to denote a component at the beginning of the loading step, no subscript for a component at the end of the loading step, and the operator $\Delta$ to denote the increment of a component. The equations expressing the elastic constitutive behaviour are then written:

$$
s={s}^{-}+2\mu (\Delta e-\Delta {e}^{p})={s}^{e}-2\mu \Delta {e}^{p}
s={s}^{-}+2\mu (\Delta e-\Delta {e}^{p})={s}^{e}-2\mu \Delta {e}^{p}

$$
{I}_{1}={I}_{1}^{-}+3K(\Delta {\epsilon}_{v}-\Delta {\epsilon}_{v}^{p})={I}_{1}^{e}-3K\Delta {\epsilon}_{v}^{p}

Substituting the equation on ${I}_{1}$ and the relation ${\sigma}_{\text{eq}}={\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{e}-3\mu \cdot \Delta p$ back into the formulation of the threshold, one obtains the scalar equation in $\Delta p$ :

One assumes that: $F({\sigma}^{e},{p}^{-})>0$ .

To continue the solution, several cases must now be distinguished:

1. **Case where** ${p}^{-}>{p}_{\mathit{ultm}}$

One has: $R({p}^{-}+\Delta p)=R({p}^{-})$

The scalar equation therefore becomes: $F({\sigma}^{e},{p}^{-})-\Delta p(\mathrm{3\mu }+9{\text{KA}}^{2})=0$

One finds:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{F({\sigma}^{e},{p}^{-})}{3\mu +9{\text{KA}}^{2}}
$$

1. **Case where** ${p}^{-}\le {p}_{\mathrm{ultm}}$

2a) Linear hardening

One has: $R({p}^{-}+\Delta p)=R({p}^{-})+h\Delta p$

The scalar equation therefore becomes: $F\left({\sigma}^{e},{p}^{-}\right)-\Delta p\left(3\mu +9{\text{KA}}^{2}+h\right)=0$

One finds:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{F({\sigma}^{e},{p}^{-})}{3\mu +9{\text{KA}}^{2}+h}
$$

2b) Parabolic hardening

Expressing in the same way $R({p}^{-}+\Delta p)$ as a function of $R({p}^{-})$ and of $\Delta p$ , one finds that the scalar equation is written:

$F\left({\sigma}^{e},{p}^{-}\right)+B\Delta p+G\Delta {p}^{2}=0$

with:

$\left\{ \begin{array}{}G=-\frac{{\sigma}_{Y}}{{p}_{\text{ultm}}^{2}}{(1-\sqrt{\frac{{\sigma}_{\text{Yultm}}}{{\sigma}_{Y}}})}^{2}\\ B=-3\mu -9{\text{KA}}^{2}+\frac{2{\sigma}_{Y}}{{p}_{\text{ultm}}}(1-\sqrt{\frac{{\sigma}_{\text{Yultm}}}{{\sigma}_{Y}}})(1-(1-\sqrt{\frac{{\sigma}_{\text{Yultm}}}{{\sigma}_{Y}}})\frac{{p}^{-}}{{p}_{\text{ultm}}})\end{array}  \right.$

The only positive root of the polynomial is:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{-B-\sqrt{{B}^{2}-4G\cdot F({\sigma}^{e},{p}^{-})}}{\mathrm{2G}}
$$

2c) Final check: Case where $({p}^{-}+\Delta p)>{p}_{\mathrm{ultm}}$

In the two previous cases, once $\Delta p$ has been computed, one must check that ${p}^{-}+\Delta p\le {p}_{\mathit{ultm}}$ . If this inequality is not satisfied, one then has:

$R({p}^{-}+\Delta p)=R({p}_{\mathrm{ultm}})$

The scalar equation therefore becomes:

$F({\sigma}^{e},{p}_{\text{ultm}})-\Delta p(3\mu +9{\text{KA}}^{2})=0$

$$
\Delta p=\frac{F({\sigma}^{e},{p}_{\text{ultm}})}{3\mu +9{\text{KA}}^{2}}

##### 2.2.3.2 Initial computation of the tangent operator

One seeks to express $\frac{\partial {\sigma}^{-}}{\partial {\varepsilon}^{-}}$ . For this purpose one will seek to compute the tangent operator by a rate computation: $\frac{\partial \dot{\sigma}}{\partial \dot{\varepsilon}}$ .

Starting from the expression: $\stackrel{}{F}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial \stackrel{}{\sigma}}+\frac{\partial F}{\partial p}\stackrel{}{p}=0$ one shows that:

$\dot{p}=\frac{\mathrm{3\mu }}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}D}s.\dot{\epsilon}+\frac{3\text{AK}}{D}\dot{{\epsilon}_{v}}$ with $D=\mathrm{3\mu }+9{\text{KA}}^{2}+\frac{\partial R}{\partial p}$

From the expressions: $\dot{\sigma}=\mathrm{H}\left(\dot{\epsilon}-{\dot{\epsilon}}^{p}\right)$ and ${\stackrel{}{\epsilon}}^{p}=\stackrel{}{p}\frac{\partial F}{\partial \sigma }$ one then shows that:

$\frac{\partial \dot{\sigma}}{\partial \dot{\varepsilon}}=H-(\frac{3\mu }{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{eq}}}s+3\mathrm{AK}I)\frac{\partial \Delta p}{\partial \epsilon }$

which is none other than the expression of the consistent matrix of the global system of the previous paragraph where $\mathrm{\Delta p}=0$ .

### 2.3 Formulation in the non-associated version

The non-associated version of the Drucker-Prager law does not claim to model a realistic physical behaviour in detail. The aim is to represent, as simply as possible, a roughly realistic physics, in particular in the case of soil mechanics, for which the dilatancy angle varies with the plastic strain.

In this formulation, the criterion is written in the same way as in the associated version

$F(\sigma ,p)={\sigma}_{\text{eq}}+{\text{AI}}_{1}-R(p)\le 0$

The function $R(p)$ may take several forms, depending on the type of hardening (linear, parabolic, exponential):

- linear hardening (identical to the associated version of the constitutive law)

| $R(p)={\sigma}_{Y}+h.p$ | if $p\in [0,{p}_{\mathit{ultm}}]$ |
| --- | --- |
| $R(p)={\sigma}_{Y}+h.{p}_{\text{ultm}}$ | if $p>{p}_{\mathit{ultm}}$ |

The coefficients $h$ , ${p}_{\mathit{ultm}}$ and ${\sigma}_{Y}$ are given.

- parabolic hardening (different from that of the associated law)

| $R(p)=({\sigma}_{Y}-{\sigma}_{\mathit{ult}}){(\frac{p}{{p}_{\mathit{ult}}})}^{2}+2({\sigma}_{Y}-{\sigma}_{\mathit{ult}})$ | if $p\in [0,{p}_{\mathit{ultm}}]$ |
| --- | --- |
| $R(p)={\sigma}_{\mathit{ult}}$ | if $p>{p}_{\mathit{ultm}}$ |

The coefficients ${\sigma}_{\mathit{ult}}$ , ${p}_{\mathit{ultm}}$ and ${\sigma}_{Y}$ are given.

- exponential hardening (specific to the non-associated law)

| $R(p)=({\sigma}_{Y}-{\sigma}_{\mathit{ult}}){(\frac{p}{{p}_{\mathit{ult}}})}^{2}+2({\sigma}_{Y}-{\sigma}_{\mathit{ult}})$ | if $p\in [0,{p}_{\mathit{ultm}}]$ |
| --- | --- |
| $R(p)={\sigma}_{\mathit{ult}}$ | if $p>{p}_{\mathit{ultm}}$ |

The coefficients ${\sigma}_{\mathit{ult}}$ , ${p}_{\mathit{ultm}}$ and ${\sigma}_{Y}$ are given.

The plastic potential is therefore distinct from the yield surface in this new formulation. The plastic potential is the following: $G(\sigma ,p)={\sigma}_{\text{eq}}+\beta (p){I}_{1}$

where $\beta \left(p\right)$ is a function which decreases with the evolution of the plastic strain. In the case of linear or parabolic hardening, this function $\beta \left(p\right)$ is linear. It is written:

$\beta (p)=\left\{  \begin{array}{cc}\text{}\beta ({\psi}^{0})(1-\frac{p}{{p}_{\text{ult}}})& \text{si}p\in \text{}\left[0,{p}_{\text{ult}}\right]\\ \begin{array}{}\\ 0\end{array}& \begin{array}{}\\ \text{si}p>{p}_{\text{ult}}\end{array}\end{array} \right.$

where ${\psi}^{0}$ denotes the initial dilatancy angle and $\beta ({\psi}^{0})=\frac{2\sin({\psi}^{0})}{3-\sin({\psi}^{0})}$ .

In the case of exponential hardening, this function $\beta (p)$ is written:

$\beta (p)=({\beta}_{0}-{\beta}_{\mathit{ult}})\exp(\frac{-p}{{p}_{c}})+{\beta}_{\mathit{ult}}$

#### 2.3.1 Analytical solution

The method of integration of the constitutive law in its non-associated version differs from that used for the associated version.

##### 2.3.1.1 Interpretation of the flow rule

The flow rule is written:

$\dot{\epsilon}=\lambda M(\sigma )\mathit{avec}\lambda \ge 0,F\ge 0\mathit{et}\lambda F=0$

$\lambda$ désigne ici le multiplicateur plastique, et :math:`M(\sigma )$ the flow direction. This flow direction belongs to the subdifferential of $\sigma$ , appelé :math:`N(\sigma )$ . This subdifferential is defined in the following way:

$N(\sigma )=\lbrace M;\forall \sigma '\mathit{tel}G(\sigma ')\le G(\sigma ),(\sigma '-\sigma ):M\le 0\rbrace$

In the case where the flow potential is differentiable at $\sigma$ , c’est à dire pour :math:`{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}\ne 0$ , the flow direction is written:

$M=\nabla G=\frac{3}{2}\frac{{\sigma}^{D}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}+\beta (p)I$

In this case, one can also show that $\lambda =\dot{p}$ :

$\dot{{\epsilon}_{p}}=\lambda \left(\frac{3}{2}\frac{{\sigma}^{D}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}+\beta (p)I\right)$

$\dot{{\epsilon}_{p}^{D}}=\lambda \frac{3}{2}\frac{{\sigma}^{D}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}$

$\dot{{\epsilon}_{p}^{D}}:\dot{{\epsilon}_{p}^{D}}=\frac{3}{2}{\lambda}^{2}$

$\lambda =\dot{p}$

In the case where the flow potential is not differentiable at $\sigma$ , c’est à dire pour :math:`{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}=0$ ,

:math:`` if and only if:

$max(\sigma '-\sigma ):M\le 0$

From this, using the fact that $G(\sigma ')\le G(\sigma )$ , one arrives at:

$2\beta (p){M}_{\mathit{eq}}-\mathit{tr}(M)\le 0$

This inequality is a first characterisation of the flow direction $M$ in the singular case.

##### 2.3.1.2 Implicit integration of the constitutive behaviour

One defines the elastic predictor at increment n+1:

${\sigma}^{e}=E:(\epsilon -{\epsilon}_{p}^{n})$

with $E$ the Hooke matrix.

**Elastic case:**

If $F({\sigma}^{e},p)\le 0$ , then $\lambda =0$ is a solution.

One has $\Delta {\epsilon}_{p}={\epsilon}_{p}^{n+1}-{\epsilon}_{p}^{n}=0$ and $\Delta p={p}^{n+1}-{p}^{n}=0$

**Singular plastic case:**

One uses ${\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}=0$ :

${\sigma}^{D}=0\Rightarrow 2\mu ({\epsilon}^{D}-{\epsilon}_{p}^{D})=0$

One therefore has ${\epsilon}^{D}={\epsilon}_{p}^{D}$ . Hence: $\Delta {\epsilon}_{p}=\frac{1}{2\mu }{\sigma}_{D}^{e}$ . One finally has:

$\Delta p=\sqrt{\frac{1}{3\mu }{\sigma}_{D}^{e}:{\sigma}_{D}^{e}}$

Now, one necessarily has $F(\sigma )=0$ . One therefore has:

$\mathit{tr}(\sigma )=\frac{R(p)}{\alpha}$

One also has $\mathit{tr}(\sigma )=\mathit{tr}({\sigma}^{e})-3K\Delta \mathit{tr}({\epsilon}_{p})$ . Hence:

$\Delta \mathit{tr}({\epsilon}_{p})=\frac{1}{3K}(\mathit{tr}({\sigma}_{e})-\frac{R(p)}{\alpha})$

The increments of volumetric and deviatoric plastic strains are therefore known. In view of the condition obtained previously on the flow direction in the singular case, this solution is valid if:

$2\beta (p)\Delta {\epsilon}_{\mathit{eq}}-\Delta \mathit{tr}({\epsilon}_{p})\le 0$

$\frac{\beta (p){\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}}{3\mu }\le \frac{1}{9K}(\mathit{tr}({\sigma}^{e})-\frac{R(p)}{\alpha})$

**Plastic case** regular **:**

In the case where ${\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}\ne 0$ , one has:

$\sigma ={\sigma}^{e}-K\Delta \mathit{tr}({\epsilon}_{p})I-2\mu \Delta {\epsilon}_{p}^{D}$

Using the expression of the flow direction in the regular case, one obtains:

$\Delta \mathit{tr}({\epsilon}_{p})=3\lambda \beta (p)$ and $\Delta {\epsilon}_{p}^{D}=\frac{3}{2}\lambda \frac{{\sigma}^{D}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}$

One can therefore express the spherical and deviatoric components of the tensor $\sigma$ as a function of the elastic predictor and of the plastic multiplier:

$\mathit{tr}(\sigma )=\mathit{tr}({\sigma}^{e})-3Kb(p)\lambda$

${\sigma}^{D}={\sigma}_{D}^{e}-3\mu \frac{{\sigma}^{D}}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}}\lambda$

In particular, this last equation makes it possible to express ${\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}$ :

${\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}-3\mu \lambda$

Now one knows that $\Delta p=\lambda ` . En insérant ces expressions dans l'équation :math:`F=0$ , one obtains:

${\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}-3\mu \lambda +3\alpha (\mathit{tr}({\sigma}^{e})-9K\lambda b({p}^{n}+\lambda ))-R({p}^{n}+\lambda )=0$

The solution of this last equation makes it possible to determine the unknown $\lambda$ , and therefore to determine all the unknowns of the incremental problem.

Furthermore one can show that the incremental problem admits a unique solution if the function defined by $f(\lambda )={\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}-3\mu \lambda +3\alpha (\mathit{tr}({\sigma}^{e})-9K\lambda b({p}^{n}+\lambda ))-R({p}^{n}+\lambda )$ is decreasing. In the numerical integration of the constitutive law, one makes sure that this condition is satisfied at each increment.

#### 2.3.2 Computation of the tangent operator

The equations are very similar to those of the associated law; they are simply written here in a more general framework.

One seeks to compute the consistent matrix: $\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \varepsilon }=\frac{\partial \mathrm{s}}{\partial \varepsilon }+\frac{1}{3}\mathrm{I}\otimes \frac{\partial {I}_{1}}{\partial \varepsilon }$

One then obtains:

$\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \epsilon }=\frac{1}{3}\mathrm{I}\otimes \frac{\partial {I}_{1}}{\partial \epsilon }+(1-q)\frac{\partial {s}^{e}}{\partial \epsilon }-{s}^{e}\otimes \frac{\partial q}{\partial \epsilon }$

with $q=3\mu \frac{\lambda}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{eq}}^{e}}$

## 3 Rice localisation indicator for the Drucker-Prager law

One defines the localisation indicator of the Rice criterion within the framework of the Drucker-Prager constitutive law. But the definition of a localisation indicator may be used, more generally, in studies in fracture mechanics, damage mechanics, bifurcation theory, soil mechanics and rock mechanics (and more globally within the framework of materials with a softening constitutive law).

This indicator defines a state from which the evolution of the mechanical system under study (equations, equilibrium, constitutive law) may lose its uniqueness. This theory makes it possible, in other words:

1. to compute the possible initiation state of localisation, which is perceived as the limit of validity of classical finite-element analyses;
2. to determine “qualitatively” the orientation angles of the localisation zones.

The localisation criterion constitutes a reliability limit of “classical” finite-element analyses.

### 3.1 The different ways of studying localisation

Within the framework of studies carried out in soil mechanics, a strong dependence of the numerical solution on the finite-element discretisation has been observed. A concentration of high values of the cumulative plastic strains appears at the level of the finite elements, and one notes that this “localisation zone” changes abruptly with mesh refinement. This localisation phenomenon is a source of numerical problems and gives rise to convergence problems in the finite-element sense.

Localisation may be interpreted as an unstable phenomenon, a precursor of a failure mechanism, characterising certain types of materials loaded in the inelastic domain. To study the instabilities linked to localisation one distinguishes, on the one hand, the classes of materials with time-dependent constitutive behaviour and, on the other hand, those that do not depend on time. For materials with time-independent constitutive behaviour, the approach commonly used is the so-called bifurcation method (it is this method that is of interest in this note). It consists in analysing the losses of uniqueness of the rate problem. For materials with time-dependent constitutive behaviour, uniqueness of the rate problem is often guaranteed, and this does not prevent the observation of instabilities during their deformation. For these materials, one must then resort to other approaches. The most commonly used is the perturbation approach. This approach will not be treated in this note, but for further information consult the notes , .

Rudnicki and Rice  have shown that the study of strain localisation in rock mechanics falls within the framework of bifurcation theory. The latter is based on the notion of unstable equilibrium. Rice  considers that the bifurcation point marks the end of the stable regime. The onset of localisation is associated with a rheological instability of the system, and this instability corresponds locally to the loss of ellipticity of the equations which govern the continuous incremental equilibrium in rates. Rice thus proposes a criterion known as “localisation bifurcation”, which makes it possible to detect the state from which the solution of the mathematical equations which govern the boundary-value problem under consideration and the evolution of the mechanical system under study (equations, equilibrium, constitutive law) lose their uniqueness. This theory makes it possible to compute the initiation state of localisation, which is perceived as the limit of validity of classical finite-element analyses.

### 3.2 Theoretical approach

#### 3.2.1 Statement of the rate problem

One considers a structure occupying, at a time instant $t$ , the open set $\Omega$ of ${\mathrm{\Re }}^{3}$ . The rate problem consists in finding the displacement-rate field $v$ when the structure is subjected to the rates of body forces ${\dot{f}}_{d}$ , to the rates of prescribed displacements ${v}_{d}$ on a part ${\partial}_{1}\Omega$ of the boundary and to the rates of surface forces ${\dot{F}}_{d}$ on the complementary part ${\partial}_{2}\Omega$ .

In the local statement of the problem, the displacement-rate field $v$ must thus satisfy the problem:

1. $v$ sufficiently regular and $v={v}_{d}$ on ${\partial}_{1}\Omega$
2. The equilibrium equations:

$div[\mathrm{L}:\epsilon (v)]+{\dot{\mathrm{f}}}_{d}=0$ on $\Omega$

$\mathrm{L}:\varepsilon (v).\mathrm{n}={\dot{\mathrm{F}}}_{d}$ on ${\partial}_{2}\Omega$

$\mathrm{n}$ being the outward unit normal to ${\partial}_{2}\Omega$ .

- The compatibility conditions (one restricts oneself here to small perturbations):

$\epsilon (v)=\frac{1}{2}\left[\nabla v+(\nabla v{)}^{T}\right]$

where the operator $L$ is defined in a general way, for constitutive laws written in incremental form, by the relation:

$\dot{\sigma}=L(\epsilon ,V):\dot{\epsilon}$

with:

$L=\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}E& & \text{si }F<0\text{ ou }F=0\text{ et }\frac{b:E:\dot{\epsilon}}{h}\le 0\\ H=& E-\frac{(E:a)\otimes (b:E)}{h}& \text{si }F=0\text{ et }\frac{b:E:\dot{\epsilon}}{h}>0\end{array} \right.$

where $\sigma$ is the stress, $\epsilon$ the total strain, $\mathrm{V}$ a set of internal variables and $F$ the plasticity threshold surface. The expressions of $a,b,E$ and h depend on the formulation of the constitutive law.

#### 3.2.2 Existence and uniqueness results, loss of ellipticity

In this chapter a few results are given without proofs. The reference for these proofs is nevertheless specified.

A sufficient condition for existence and uniqueness of the previous problem is: $\dot{\sigma}:\dot{\epsilon}>0$ . This inequality may be interpreted as a definition, in the three-dimensional case, of non-softening. The proof is given by Hill  for standard materials and by Benallal  for non-standard materials.

The loss of ellipticity corresponds to the time instant at which the operator $N.H.N$ becomes singular for a direction $N$ at a point of the structure. This condition is equivalent to the condition: $\det\left(\mathrm{N}.\mathrm{H}.\mathrm{N}\right)=0$ . This is the condition of “continuous bifurcation” 1

in the sense of Rice, also called the acoustic tensor. Rice and Rudnicki  show that this condition of loss of ellipticity of the local rate problem is a necessary condition for “continuous or discontinuous” bifurcation 2

for the solid. The boundary conditions play no role; only the constitutive law defines the localisation conditions (localisation threshold and orientation of the localisation surface.

Continuous bifurcations thus provide the lower bound of the range of strain for which discontinuous bifurcations may occur.

To solve a polynomial of degree n (such as the one defined above, where n=4) it is proposed to use the so-called “Companion Matrix Polynomial” method. In a continuous bifurcation, a plastic strain occurs inside and outside the localisation zone, and one has the same constitutive law inside and outside the band.

In a discontinuous bifurcation, one has, on either side of the band, continuity of the displacement, but one does not have the same constitutive behaviour. An elastic unloading occurs outside the localisation zone, whereas loading and a continuous elastoplastic strain occur inside.

## 4 Sensitivity analyses

The sensitivity analysis concerns only the associated version of the formulation described in chapter  r7.01.16-formulation-version-associee .

### 4.1 Sensitivity to the material data

#### 4.1.1 The direct problem

In this part one places oneself within the framework of the solution of non-linear analyses.

The non-linear static analysis is solved incrementally. It therefore requires, at each load step $i\in \lbrace 1,I\rbrace$ , the solution of the non-linear system of equations:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{c}R({u}_{i},{t}_{i})+{B}^{t}{\lambda}_{i}={L}_{i}\\ {\text{Bu}}_{i}={u}_{i}^{d}\end{array}\right.
$$

with

$$
(\mathrm{R}({\mathrm{u}}_{i},{t}_{i}){)}_{k}={\int}_{\Omega}\sigma ({\mathrm{u}}_{i}):\varepsilon ({\mathrm{w}}_{k})d\Omega
$$

- ${\mathrm{w}}_{k}$ is the shape function of the $k$ -th degree of freedom of the modelled structure,
- $(\mathrm{R}({\mathrm{u}}_{i},{t}_{i}))$ is the vector of nodal forces.

The solution of this system is carried out by the Newton-Raphson method:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}{K}_{i}^{n}{\mathrm{\delta u}}_{i}^{n+1}+{B}^{t}{\mathrm{\delta \lambda }}_{i}^{n+1}& & {L}_{i}-R({u}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})+{B}^{t}{\lambda}_{i}^{n}\\ {\mathrm{B\delta u}}_{i}^{n+1}& & -{\mathrm{Bu}}_{i-1}^{n}\end{array}\right.
$$

where ${K}_{i}^{n}=\frac{\partial R}{\partial u}{\mid}_{({u}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}$ is the tangent matrix at load step $i$ and at Newton iteration $n$ .

The solution is therefore given by:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}{u}_{i}& & {u}_{i-1}+\sum_{n=0}^{N}{\mathrm{\delta u}}_{i}^{n}\\ {\lambda}_{i}& & {\lambda}_{i-1}+\sum_{n=0}^{N}{\mathrm{\delta \lambda }}_{i}^{n}\end{array}\right.
$$

with $N$ , the number of Newton iterations that was necessary to reach convergence.

### 4.2 Sensitivity to the loading

The approach here is quite close to that of the previous paragraph. It is nevertheless developed in full for the sake of clarity, so that this paragraph can be read independently.

#### 4.2.1 The direct problem: expression of the loading

Up to now the direct problem has been expressed in the form:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}R({u}_{i},{t}_{i})+{B}^{t}{\lambda}_{i}& & {L}_{i}\\ {\text{Bu}}_{i}& & {u}_{i}^{d}\end{array}\right.
$$

The loadings are gathered on the right-hand side and comprise the prescribed forces ${L}_{i}$ and the prescribed displacements ${u}_{i}^{d}$ .

Suppose that the prescribed-force loading ${L}_{i}$ depends on a scalar parameter $\alpha$ in the following way:

$$
{L}_{i}(\alpha )={L}_{i}^{1}+{L}_{i}^{2}(\alpha )
$$

Where

- ${L}_{i}^{1}$ is a vector independent of $\alpha$ ,
- ${L}_{i}^{2}$ depends linearly on $\alpha$ .

One wishes to compute the sensitivity of the results of the direct computation to a variation of the parameter $\alpha$ .

#### 4.2.2 The derived problem

##### 4.2.2.1 Differentiation of the equilibrium

As in the previous chapter, taking into account the dependences between the different fields, one differentiates the equilibrium  with respect to $\alpha$ :

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}\frac{\partial R}{\partial \alpha }+\frac{\partial R}{\partial \mathrm{\Delta u}}\cdot \mathrm{\Delta u}{,}_{\alpha}+\frac{\partial R}{\partial {\sigma}_{i-1}}\cdot {\sigma}_{i-1}{,}_{\alpha}+\frac{\partial R}{\partial {p}_{i-1}}\cdot {p}_{i-1}{,}_{\alpha}+{B}^{t}{\lambda}_{i}{,}_{\alpha}& & {L}_{i}^{2}(1)\\ \mathrm{B\Delta u}{,}_{\alpha}& & -{\text{Bu}}_{\iota -1,\alpha }\end{array} \right.
$$

One has used the fact that ${L}_{i}^{2}$ depends linearly on $\alpha$ .

That is:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}{K}_{i}^{N}\mathrm{\Delta u}{,}_{\alpha}+{B}^{t}{\lambda}_{i}{,}_{\alpha}& & {L}_{i}^{2}(1)-R{,}_{\alpha}{\mid}_{\mathrm{\Delta u}\ne \mathrm{\Delta u}(\alpha )}\\ \mathrm{B\Delta u}{,}_{\alpha}& & -{\text{Bu}}_{\iota -1,\alpha }\end{array} \right.
$$

Where

- ${K}_{i}^{N}$ is the last tangent matrix used to reach convergence in the Newton iterations,
- $R{,}_{\alpha}{\mid}_{\Delta u\ne \Delta u(\alpha )}$ is the total variation of $R$ , without taking into account the dependence of $\Delta u$ on $\alpha$ .

The problem lies, as previously, in the computation of $R{,}_{\alpha}{\mid}_{\Delta u\ne \Delta u(\alpha )}$ .

##### 4.2.2.2 Computation of the derivative of the constitutive law

From , one may rewrite $R{,}_{\alpha}{\mid}_{\Delta u\ne \Delta u(\alpha )}$ in the form:

$$
R{,}_{\alpha}{\mid}_{\Delta u\ne \Delta u(\alpha )}={\int}_{\Omega}^{}(\sigma {,}_{\alpha}+\Delta \sigma {,}_{\alpha}{\mid}_{\Delta u\ne \Delta u(\alpha )}):\epsilon ({w}_{k})d\Omega
$$

To do this, the expressions which appear in the numerical integration of the constitutive law will be used in order to compute $\Delta \sigma {,}_{\alpha}{\mid}_{\Delta u\ne \Delta u(\alpha )}$ .

##### 4.2.2.3 Case of linear elasticity

Within the framework of linear elasticity, the constitutive law is expressed by:

$$
\Delta \sigma =\mathrm{2\mu }.\tilde{\epsilon}(\Delta u)+K.\text{Tr}(\epsilon (\Delta u)).\text{Id}
$$

where $\text{Id}$ is the second-order identity tensor.

Then, by computing the total variation of  with respect to $\alpha$ one obtains:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\mathit{\Delta \sigma }{,}_{\alpha}=\mathrm{2\mu }{,}_{\alpha}.\tilde{\epsilon}(\mathit{\Delta u})+K{,}_{\alpha}.\text{Tr}(\epsilon (\mathit{\Delta u})).\text{Id}+\mathrm{2\mu }.\tilde{\epsilon}(\mathit{\Delta u}{,}_{\alpha})+K.\text{Tr}(\epsilon (\mathit{\Delta u}{,}_{\alpha})).\text{Id}\\ =0.+0.+\mathrm{2\mu }.\tilde{\epsilon}(\mathit{\Delta u}{,}_{\alpha})+K.\text{Tr}(\epsilon (\mathit{\Delta u}{,}_{\alpha})).\text{Id}\end{array}
$$

That is:

$\Delta \sigma {,}_{\alpha}{\mid}_{\Delta u\ne \Delta u(\alpha )}=0.$

##### 4.2.2.4 Case of elastoplasticity of Drucker-Prager type

As previously, two cases will be distinguished.

**1st case**: $\mathrm{\Delta p}=0$

Which amounts to saying that, during the present load step, the Gauss point under consideration has seen no increase in its plastification. One is then back in the case of linear elasticity:

$\Delta \sigma {,}_{\alpha}{\mid}_{\Delta u\ne \Delta u(\alpha )}=0.$

**2nd case**: $\mathrm{\Delta p}>0$

Taking into account the dependences between the variables, one may write:

$\left\{  \begin{array}{ccc}\mathit{\Delta \sigma }{,}_{\alpha}& =& \frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta \sigma }}{\partial \alpha }+\frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta \sigma }}{\partial \sigma }\cdot \sigma {,}_{\alpha}+\frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta \sigma }}{\partial p}\cdot p{,}_{\alpha}+\frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta \sigma }}{\partial \epsilon (\mathit{\Delta u})}\cdot \epsilon (\mathit{\Delta u}{,}_{\alpha})\\ \mathit{\Delta p}{,}_{\alpha}& =& \frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta p}}{\partial \alpha }+\frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta p}}{\partial \sigma }\cdot \sigma {,}_{\alpha}+\frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta p}}{\partial p}\cdot p{,}_{\alpha}+\frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta p}}{\partial \epsilon (\mathit{\Delta u})}\cdot \epsilon (\mathit{\Delta u}){,}_{\alpha}\end{array}\right.$

Furthermore, in accordance with the algorithmic integration of the law, the deviatoric and hydrostatic parts will be separated.

$\left\{  \begin{array}{ccc}\mathit{\Delta \sigma }{,}_{\alpha}{\mid}_{\mathit{\Delta u}\mathrm{\ne }\mathit{\Delta u}(\alpha )}& =& \frac{\partial \Delta \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \alpha }+\frac{1}{3}\cdot \frac{\partial \text{Tr}(\mathit{\Delta \sigma })}{\partial \alpha }\cdot \text{Id}\\ & +& \frac{\partial \Delta \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \sigma }\cdot \sigma {,}_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{3}\cdot \frac{\partial \text{Tr}(\mathit{\Delta \sigma })}{\partial \sigma }\cdot \text{Id}\cdot \sigma {,}_{\alpha}\\ & +& \frac{\partial \Delta \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial p}\cdot p{,}_{\alpha}+\frac{1}{3}\cdot \frac{\partial \text{Tr}(\mathit{\Delta \sigma })}{\partial p}\cdot \text{Id}\cdot p{,}_{\alpha}\\ \mathit{\Delta p}{,}_{\alpha}{\mid}_{\mathit{\Delta u}\mathrm{\ne }\mathit{\Delta u}(\alpha )}& =& \frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta p}}{\partial \alpha }+\frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta p}}{\partial \sigma }\cdot \sigma {,}_{\alpha}+\frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta p}}{\partial p}\cdot p{,}_{\alpha}\end{array}\right.$

And so, one computes:

$\frac{\partial \Delta \sigma }{\partial \alpha }$

In so far as there is no explicit dependence of $\Delta \sigma ` par rapport à $\alpha$ , one obtains:

$\frac{\partial \Delta \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \alpha }=0.$

$\frac{\partial \text{Tr}(\Delta \sigma )}{\partial \alpha }=0.$

$\frac{\partial \Delta \sigma }{\partial \sigma }$

$\frac{\partial \Delta \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial \sigma }=-\mathrm{3\mu }\cdot \frac{\frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta p}}{\partial \sigma }}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{e}}\otimes {\tilde{\sigma}}^{e}+\mathrm{3\mu }\cdot \frac{\mathit{\Delta p}}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{{e}^{2}}}\cdot \frac{\partial {\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{e}}{\partial \sigma }\otimes {\tilde{\sigma}}^{e}-\mathrm{3\mu }\cdot \frac{\mathit{\Delta p}}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{e}}\cdot J$

where $\mathrm{J}$ is the deviatoric operator.

$\frac{\partial \Delta \sigma }{\partial p}$

$\frac{\partial \Delta \tilde{\sigma}}{\partial p}=-\frac{\mathrm{3\mu }}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{e}}\cdot \frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta p}}{\partial p}\cdot {\tilde{\sigma}}^{e}$

$\frac{\partial \text{Tr}(\mathit{\Delta \sigma })}{\partial p}=-9\cdot K\cdot {\rm A}\cdot \frac{\partial \mathit{\Delta p}}{\partial p}$

$\mathit{\Delta p}{,}_{\alpha}$

### 7.3 Case of the projection onto the apex of the cone

The principle of the analytical solution consists in determining the effective stresses as the projection of the elastic stresses onto the criterion.

There may be no solution.

If the condition $\mathrm{\Delta p}\le \frac{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{e}}{\mathrm{3\mu }}$ is not satisfied, the effective stresses must be found by projection onto the apex of the cone $\mathrm{\Delta p}=\frac{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{e}}{\mathrm{3\mu }}$ .

In this case, one obtains:

$\frac{\partial \mathrm{\Delta p}}{\partial \Phi }=\frac{1}{\mathrm{3\mu }}\cdot (\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{e}}{\partial \Phi }-\mathrm{\Delta p}\cdot \frac{\partial \mathrm{3\mu }}{\partial \Phi })$

$\frac{\partial \mathrm{\Delta p}}{\partial \sigma }=\frac{1}{\mathrm{3\mu }}\cdot \frac{\partial {\sigma}_{\text{eq}}^{e}}{\partial \sigma }$

$\frac{\partial \mathrm{\Delta p}}{\partial p}=0$
