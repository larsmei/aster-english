# r7.01.09 Constitutive law ENDO_ORTH_BETON

**Summary:**

This documentation presents the theoretical writing and the numerical integration of the constitutive law ENDO_ORTH_BETON developed by [bib1], which describes the anisotropy induced by damage in concrete, as well as the unilateral effects (different behaviour in tension and in compression). The validation of the model against experimental results is also proposed in this document.

> **Contents**

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Characteristics of the damage of concrete

Concrete is a complex material, formed of aggregates and of a paste ensuring the cohesion between these aggregates, and within which microcracks pre-exist, formed during the various stages of manufacture. Concrete is generally considered as an initially isotropic material, the microcracks having no preferred orientation. This isotropy is preserved if the applied loading remains in the elastic domain. From a certain level of loading, the microcracks will develop in particular directions, which induces the appearance of anisotropy in the non-linear phase. The cracks develop preferentially orthogonally to the directions of greatest tension or of smallest compression. The damage process is therefore expressed by a loss of stiffness generated by the decohesion of the material. The stiffness can be recovered when the cracks are closed (unilateral effect). To this is added another strong dissymmetry of the behaviour between tension and compression: the stresses sustained in compression are 10 times (or even more) greater than the stresses sustained in tension. Finally, let us point out other phenomena such as the formation of irreversible strains (caused for example by the locking of the crack lips by friction or the presence of degraded material between these lips) or phenomena of energy dissipation by friction of the crack lips.

### 1.2 Objectives of the law ENDO_ORTH_BETON

Despite the existence of various anisotropic damage models for concrete, isotropic models are still used exclusively in design offices in order to account for the behaviour of concrete structures. This is due, for the anisotropic models, to the complexity of their numerical implementation, to the difficulty of identifying their sometimes numerous parameters, to the mismatch between the objectives of the model and those of the industrial study, to the difficulty of coupling the model with other physical phenomena (creep, plasticity), and in most cases to the long computation times required by the anisotropic models. The use of anisotropic models is moreover not necessary in the cases where the isotropic models describe the same behaviour of the structure. There are nevertheless cases where the anisotropic models can prove interesting, as soon as they predict a behaviour different from that of the isotropic models.

The objective is to have available a simple anisotropic model (small number of parameters), numerically robust, and respecting the rules of thermodynamics (positive dissipation). It is obvious that a certain number of phenomena observed experimentally will not be able to be taken into account. A specification was therefore defined prior to the development of the model in order to define its framework.

Two categories of requirements can be distinguished, the objective being to obtain a reasonable result whatever the loading. One concerns the physical consistency of the prediction of the model (1) to (4), and the other concerns the numerical robustness (5) and (6). The framework of our model is composed of the following points:

1. Account taken of the anisotropy thanks to the introduction of a symmetric tensor of order 2 representing the effects of the damage. It is therefore more accurate to speak of an orthotropic model, inasmuch as the use of such a tensor makes it possible to define only three principal directions of the damage. A tensor of higher order (4 or even 8) is necessary in order to account for the complete anisotropy.
2. Vanishing of the stress at failure. This leads us to define a free energy, a function of the strains, rather than a free enthalpy, a function of the stresses, because it seems easier to obtain a zero stress from finite strains than the converse.
3. Increasing and bounded eigenvalues of the damage. This point accounts for the irreversible character of the damage process (growth of the eigenvalues), of which failure constitutes the limit (bounded eigenvalues). It moreover makes it possible to reach failure in several directions.
4. Account taken of the unilateral behaviour of concrete: closure of the cracks in compression, dissymmetry of the damage thresholds between tension and compression.
5. Continuity of the stress/strain response, particularly at the open–closed transition of the cracks. Besides the fact that a discontinuity would be physically doubtful, it would lead to convergence problems of the numerical algorithm.
6. Respect of the framework of generalised standard materials. This makes it possible to ensure the thermodynamic consistency of the model (positive dissipation) and it provides pleasant mathematical properties for the numerical solution (existence and uniqueness of the solution of the problem of computing the stresses and the final damage at a fixed strain increment, called the “local projection problem” by analogy with plasticity).

**Note:**

| The framework of generalised standard materials (CSMG) as it is understood here is not strictly that defined by Halphen and Nguyen [bib11]. Indeed, the strict CSMG ensures the existence and the uniqueness of the solution of the global problem if the energy is convex with respect to all the variables simultaneously. This cannot be verified in the case of softening constitutive laws. The “degraded” CSMG that we define ensures only the existence and the uniqueness of the solution of the local projection problem (computation of the evolution of the damage at fixed strain). In order to respect the CSMG, one must verify the convexity of the free energy, on the one hand with respect to the strain, and on the other hand with respect to the internal variables: * *The convexity with respect to the strain is necessary in order to ensure the stability of the elastic problem.* * **The convexity with respect to all the internal variables simultaneously** *is necessary in order to have the right mathematical properties for the local projection problem. In the case where several internal variables are used, the separate convexities with respect to each of these variables are not sufficient.* * *The global convexity with respect to the strain and to the internal variables simultaneously is not required, since the strain increment is fixed for the local projection which makes it possible to compute the evolution of the internal variables. It moreover seems impossible to obtain this global convexity in the case of softening constitutive laws.* *The framework that has been defined omits a certain number of physical phenomena associated with the damage of concrete:* * *appearance of irreversible strains* * *volumetric dilatancy of the material in compression* * *hysteretic behaviour for loading–unloading cycles, generated by the friction between the crack lips.* |

## 2 Expression of the free energy

### 2.1 Account taken of crack closure

If one seeks to take the closure effect into account, great attention must be paid to the continuity of the stresses as a function of the strains (which is an indispensable condition for a constitutive law in a finite-element analysis software), cf. [bib2]. Indeed, if one models this effect in too simplistic a manner, the constitutive law has a good chance of presenting a discontinuous response. One solution is to describe finely what one calls tension and compression, knowing that in tension (resp. compression) the crack will be considered “open” (resp. “closed”). A natural solution is to place oneself in a principal frame of strain. In such a frame, the elastic free energy is written ($\lambda$  and $m$ denoting the Lamé coefficients):

$\varphi (\varepsilon )=\frac{\lambda}{2}{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}^{2}+\mu \sum_{i}{\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}$ **eq 2.1-1**

One can then define:

- a volumetric tension or compression, according to the sign of $\text{tr}\varepsilon$ ,
- a tension or compression in each principal direction, according to the sign of ${\varepsilon}_{i}$ .

The elastic free energy can then be written:

$\varphi (\varepsilon )=\frac{\lambda}{2}\left[{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{+}^{2}+{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}^{2}\right]+\mu \left[\text{tr}({\varepsilon}_{+}^{2})+\text{tr}({\varepsilon}_{-}^{2})\right]$ **eq 2.1-2**

with the following definitions for the positive and negative parts:

${(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{+}=H(\text{tr}\varepsilon )\text{tr}\varepsilon ` ; :math:`{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}=H(-\text{tr}\varepsilon )\text{tr}\varepsilon$ ; $\text{tr}({\varepsilon}_{+}^{2})=\sum_{i}H({\varepsilon}_{i}){\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}$ ; $\text{tr}({\varepsilon}_{-}^{2})=\sum_{i}H(-{\varepsilon}_{i}){\varepsilon}_{i}^{2}$

where $H$ is the Heaviside function.

**Note:**

*A more detailed study of the properties of the positive and negative parts of a tensor is made in appendix 1.*

### 2.2 Introduction of the damage variables

Given the complexity of the damage mechanisms, and after having observed that it was difficult to describe the behaviour of concrete by using only a single damage variable, we have chosen to introduce two damage variables:

- A tensor $D$ of order 2 relating to the damage created in tension
- A scalar $d$ relating to the damage created in compression

**Note:**

*The choice of a tensor of order 2 in order to model the tensile damage is relatively classical and intuitive. It makes it possible to describe the preferred orientation of the cracks orthogonally to the direction of greatest tension. The question of the damage created in compression (orthotropic or isotropic?) is much less clear.*

*Suppose for simplicity that the cracks can be generated only in the planes orthogonal to* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{1}$ *,* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{2}$ *or* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{3}$ *. In the case of a simple tension in the direction* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{1}$ *, the cracks are created in the plane orthogonal to* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{1}$ *, which leads to a loss of stiffness in the direction* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{1}$ *. If one then applies a simple tension in the direction* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{2}$ *, one does not “see” the crack because the loading is parallel to the plane of the crack and the stiffness is not affected. The damage is therefore clearly anisotropic in tension. In the case of a simple compression in the direction* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{1}$ *, one creates cracks in the planes orthogonal to* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{2}$ *and* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{3}$ *. If one then loads in compression in the direction* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{2}$ *, one “sees” the cracks orthogonal to* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{3}$ *. The stiffness in the direction* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{2}$ *is therefore weaker than the undegraded normal stiffness, but it is stronger than the stiffness in the direction* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{1}$ *because the compression in the direction* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{1}$ *is sensitive to all the cracks, that is those orthogonal to* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{2}$ *and to* ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{3}$ *. Consequently, the damage in compression seems less anisotropic than in tension, without however being completely isotropic. In the absence of a clear physical argument on the isotropic or anisotropic character of the damage in compression, we have chosen to take it as isotropic for reasons of*  simplicity.

One sets $B=I-D$ representing the integrity of the material in tension. One introduces the tensile damage into the “positive” terms of the free energy [eq 2.1-2] and the compressive damage into the negative terms. The free energy is now defined as follows:

$\varphi (\varepsilon ,\mathrm{\Beta },d)=\frac{\lambda}{2}\left[{(\text{tr}\text{B \varepsilon })}_{+}^{2}+{(1-d)}^{2}{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}^{2}\right]+\mu \left[\frac{1}{4}\text{tr}({(\mathrm{\Beta \; \varepsilon }+\mathrm{\varepsilon \; B})}_{+}^{2})+{(1-d)}^{2}\text{tr}({\varepsilon}_{-}^{2})\right]$ **eq 2.2-1**

**Note:**

The convexity of the free energy with respect, on the one hand, to the strain, and on the other hand to the damage variables simultaneously, is indeed respected. One will refer to [bib2] and [bib1] for a proof.

### 2.3 Bounds on the damage

Failure, or the creation of a crack completely crossing the material element under consideration, imposes an upper bound on the damage. This bound is imposed on each eigenvalue of the tensile damage (${D}_{i}\in \left[0,1\right]$  where the ${D}_{i}$ denote the eigenvalues of $D$ ), which makes it possible to reach failure in 3 orthogonal directions. A convex indicator function with respect to the damage, ${I}_{]-\infty ,1]}$ for *D*  or ${I}_{[0,\infty [:ref:$}` for $B$ , is therefore used to control each of the eigenvalues of the damage (cf. [bib2 <}` for $B$ , is therefore used to control each of the eigenvalues of the damage (cf. [bib2>`]). Likewise, one uses an indicator on the value of the scalar compressive damage. One obtains the following expression for the free energy:

$\begin{array}{cc}\varphi (\varepsilon ,B,d)& =\frac{\lambda}{2}\left[{(\text{tr}B\varepsilon )}_{+}^{2}+{(1-d)}^{2}{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}^{2}\right]+\mu \left[\frac{1}{4}\text{tr}({(B\varepsilon +\varepsilon B)}_{+}^{2})+{(1-d)}^{2}\text{tr}({\varepsilon}_{-}^{2})\right]\\ & +{I}_{[0,\infty [}\left[\min({B}_{i})\right]+{I}_{]-\infty ,1]}\left[d\right]\end{array}$ **eq 2.3-1**

**Note:**

*It follows from this expression that once the damage is equal to 1 in a principal direction, this principal direction is henceforth locked and the damage can no longer evolve* *except in the plane perpendicular to this direction. The proof of this point is found in appendix 2.*

### 2.4 Introduction of a blocked energy

We propose to introduce a blocked energy in order to control better the evolution of the damage as a function of the loading, in the form used by [bib4]. The idea consists in introducing an additional term into the energy depending only on the damage, and not on the strain state. There then results an additional term in the derivation of the thermodynamic forces which control the evolution of the damage (cf. section [§ 3 ]). This additional term implies, on the other hand, no modification of the expression for the stress.

The energy is written in the following manner:

$\begin{array}{cc}\varphi (\varepsilon ,B,d)& =\frac{\lambda}{2}\left[{(\text{tr}B\varepsilon )}_{+}^{2}+{(1-d)}^{2}{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}^{2}\right]+\mu \left[\frac{1}{4}\text{tr}({(B\varepsilon +\varepsilon B)}_{+}^{2})+{(1-d)}^{2}\text{tr}({\varepsilon}_{-}^{2})\right]\\ & +{I}_{[0,\infty [}\left[\min({B}_{i})\right]+{I}_{]-\infty ,1]}\left[d\right]+{\psi}^{\text{bloquée}}(B,d)\end{array}$ **eq 2.4-1**

where ${\psi}^{\text{bloquée}}(B,d)$ is a convex function of the damage. One chooses to take this additional energy as zero when the material is sound. It must moreover be expressed by means of invariants of the damage tensor. One finally wishes the additional term in the expression of the thermodynamic forces to depend on the damage, which makes it possible to eliminate the choice of a term linear in the damage for the energy ${\psi}^{\text{bloquée}}(B,d)$ .

We have opted for the following expression:

$\begin{array}{cc}{\psi}^{\text{bloquée}}(B,d)& =\frac{{\gamma}_{B}}{2}\text{tr}({(\text{I - B})}^{2})+{\gamma}_{d}{d}^{2}\\ & =\frac{{\gamma}_{B}}{2}\text{tr}({D}^{2})+{\gamma}_{d}{d}^{2}\end{array}$ **eq 2.4-2**

where ${\gamma}_{B}$ and ${\gamma}_{d}$ are parameters of the model.

**Note:**

| *One may ask oneself the question of the physical meaning of this additional energy. The evolution of the damage does indeed give rise, in the energy, to the appearance of an energy “blocked” by the system, and this energy is not zero when the material has failed. Consequently, if one draws up the energy balance of the creation of a crack, only part of the energy consumed corresponds to the dissipation of energy in the form of heat, whereas another part remains blocked.* *One can find a physical origin of this blocked energy in the case of compression. Indeed, if one releases the macroscopic stress, the cracks opened by the compression do not close completely, as proved by the existence of a residual* macroscopic strain. There are therefore probably local residual strains and stresses which store energy (cf. [Figure *2.4-a]* *), by analogy with plasticity. (One recalls that the law* *ENDO_ORTH_BETON* *does not describe the irreversible strains.).*![Figure](images/1000000000000173000000B997410A41784F8D04.png)Figure 2.4-a *Apart from the physical considerations, the interest of this blocked energy, equivalent to a hardening energy, is that the model remains well within the framework of generalised standard materials. The advantage, compared with a hardening energy, is that this energy will have a different effect on the two damage variables (we shall explain this point in the following section).* |

### 2.5 Final expression of the free energy, of the stresses and of the thermodynamic forces associated with the damage variables

The final expression of the free energy is written:

$\begin{array}{cc}\varphi (\varepsilon ,B,d)& =\frac{\lambda}{2}\left[{(\text{tr}B\varepsilon )}_{+}^{2}+{(1-d)}^{2}{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}^{2}\right]+\mu \left[\frac{1}{4}\text{tr}({(B\varepsilon +\varepsilon B)}_{+}^{2})+{(1-d)}^{2}\text{tr}({\varepsilon}_{-}^{2})\right]\\ & +{I}_{[0,\infty [}\left[\min({B}_{i})\right]+{I}_{]-\infty ,1]}\left[d\right]+{\psi}^{\text{bloquée}}(B,d)\end{array}$ **eq 2.5-1**

The expression of the stresses is deduced from the energy by differentiation with respect to the strains:

$\begin{array}{}\sigma (\varepsilon ,B,d)=\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial \varepsilon }=\lambda \left[{(\text{tr}B\varepsilon )}_{+}B+{(1-d)}^{2}{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}I\right]\\ +\mu \left[\frac{1}{2}({(B\varepsilon +\varepsilon B)}_{+}B+B{(B\varepsilon +\varepsilon B)}_{+})+2{(1-d)}^{2}{\varepsilon}_{-}\right]\end{array}$ **eq 2.5-2**

The continuity of the stress results from the continuity of the eigenvalues of a matrix with respect to this matrix (cf. Ostrowski's theorem in [bib4]).

**Note:**

A defect nevertheless appears at the “open–closed” transition. The domains of activation and of deactivation of the crack-opening and crack-closure terms do not coincide. This is due to the fact that opening and closure are not associated with the separation into positive and negative parts of the same quantities (opening: damage combined with the strain, closure: strain alone). This does not however affect the continuity property. It moreover appears that this defect is limited to a zone close to the origin of the strains, where it does not generate any physical aberration, and that it has no incidence outside this interval (cf. [bib2]). The formalism proposed in [bib3], making it possible to ensure the continuity of the stress while taking into account the crack-closure effect, presents the same defect.

One furthermore deduces from the free energy the expression of the thermodynamic forces associated with the damage variables:

${F}^{B}(\varepsilon ,B)=-\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial B}=-\lambda {(\text{tr}B\varepsilon )}_{+}\varepsilon -\frac{\mu}{2}\left[{(B\varepsilon +\varepsilon B)}_{+}\varepsilon +\varepsilon {(B\varepsilon +\varepsilon B)}_{+}\right]+{\gamma}_{B}(\text{I - B})$ **eq 2.5-3**

${F}^{d}(\varepsilon ,d)=-\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial d}=\lambda (1-d){(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}^{2}+2\mu (1-d)\text{tr}({\varepsilon}_{-}^{2})-2{\gamma}_{d}d$ **eq 2.5-4**

Each thermodynamic force is constituted of two parts:

- A part depending on the strain and on the damage, which is derived from the elastic part of the energy.
- A part depending only on the damage, which is derived from the blocked energy. This term will play the role of a hardening, and makes it possible to control the stress–strain response. One sees that the terms deriving from the blocked energy in each of the thermodynamic forces are independent of one another, which makes it possible to control more easily the evolution of each of the damage variables.

## 3 Evolution law of the damage variables

### 3.1 Evolution law

In order for the model ENDO_ORTH_BETON to enter the framework of generalised standard materials, one must equip oneself with a dissipation potential. For reasons of simplicity, one rather defines the reversibility domain, as for the law ENDO_ISOT_BETON (cf. doc. [R7.01.04](doc:r7.01.04)).

**Note:**

*From a formal point of view, generalised standard materials are characterised by a dissipation potential which is a positively homogeneous function of degree 1, the Legendre–Fenchel transform of the indicator function of the reversibility domain. One can therefore choose to define either a dissipation potential, or a reversibility domain.*

The first idea is to define two evolution criteria corresponding to each of the internal variables. This solution is entirely conceivable inasmuch as the thermodynamic forces are dissociated from one another. It turns out however that this solution has two drawbacks:

- The first is of a “physical” order. Consider a specimen subjected to a uniaxial compression. One can imagine that the compression criterion alone is reached and that the tension criterion is not activated. Only the compressive damage then evolves. If one subjects this specimen, after unloading, to a tension in a direction orthogonal to the previous compression axis, the material behaves according to the model as a sound material, despite the creation, in reality, of microcracks parallel to the compression axis, hence perpendicular to the tension axis.
- The second drawback is of a practical order. It is indeed easier to treat numerically a single criterion, involving a single Lagrange multiplier (cf. [Figure 3.1-a]), rather than two separate criteria, involving two Lagrange multipliers and able to create zones where the flow direction is not defined *a priori*  (cf. [Figure 3.1-b]).

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.09/1000000000000127000000DD4D23D3D57F547861.png)

**Figure 3.1-a**

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.09/1000000000000127000000DD0DE16E90AD3857D5.png)

**Figure 3.1-b**

One therefore decides to introduce a single criterion coupling the evolution of the two damage variables:

$g({F}^{B},{F}^{d})=\sqrt{\alpha {\parallel {F}_{-}^{B}\parallel }^{2}+(1-\alpha ){({F}_{+}^{d})}^{2}}-K(\varepsilon )\le 0$ **eq 3.1-1**

where $K(\varepsilon )$ is a threshold depending on the strain state (this point will be commented on in section [§ 3.2 ]).

The evolution of the damage variables is then determined by the Kuhn–Tucker conditions:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\begin{array}{}\dot{d}=0\\ \dot{B}=0\iff \dot{D}=0\end{array}& \text{pour}g<0\\ & \\ \begin{array}{}\dot{d}\ge 0\\ {\dot{B}}_{i}\le 0\iff {\dot{D}}_{i}\ge 0\end{array}& \text{pour}g=0\end{array}$ **eq 3.1-2**

**Note:**

*The criterion involves only the positive part* ${F}_{+}^{d}$ *of* ${F}^{d}$ *and the negative part* ${F}_{-}^{B}$ *of* ${F}^{B}$ *in order to impose the growth of the damage. This condition is ensured in the dissipation potential by the introduction of the indicator functions* ${I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}(\dot{d})$ *and* ${I}_{{ℝ}^{-}}({\dot{B}}_{i})$ *(equivalent to* ${I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}({\dot{D}}_{i})$ *).*

**Note:**

*The elliptic criterion is convex in the space of the thermodynamic forces, which ensures the convexity of the dissipation potential.*

Within the framework of generalised standard materials, the evolution of the internal variables follows the flow law associated with the criterion via the normality principle:

$\dot{B}=\dot{\eta}\frac{\partial g}{\partial {F}^{B}}=\dot{\eta}\frac{\alpha {F}_{-}^{B}}{\sqrt{\alpha {\parallel {F}_{-}^{B}\parallel }^{2}+(1-\alpha ){({F}_{+}^{d})}^{2}}}$ **eq 3.1-3**

$\dot{d}=\dot{\eta}\frac{\partial g}{\partial {F}^{d}}=\dot{\eta}\frac{(1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}}{\sqrt{\alpha {\parallel {F}_{-}^{B}\parallel }^{2}+(1-\alpha ){({F}_{+}^{d})}^{2}}}$ **eq 3.1-4**

One can integrate the common denominator into the Lagrange multiplier in order to have a simpler relation:

$\dot{B}=\dot{\eta}\frac{\partial g}{\partial {F}^{B}}=\dot{\gamma}\alpha {F}_{-}^{B}$ **eq 3.1-5**

$\dot{d}=\dot{\eta}\frac{\partial g}{\partial {F}^{d}}=\dot{\gamma}(1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}$ **eq 3.1-6**

This system involves a single plastic multiplier $\dot{\gamma}$ .

The evolution equations ensure the positivity of the dissipation potential:

${F}^{B}:\dot{B}+{F}^{d}\dot{d}=\dot{\gamma}\left[\alpha {\parallel {F}_{-}^{B}\parallel }^{2}+(1-\alpha ){({F}_{+}^{d})}^{2}\right]\ge 0$ **eq 3.1-7**

**Note:**

*From a theoretical point of view, the compressive damage and the eigenvalues of the tensile damage tensor can reach a value of 1. This corresponds to a completely damaged element whose stiffness is zero, as is the stress. From a numerical point of view, this situation leads to instabilities because the stiffness matrix becomes singular. A programming choice is made in order to avoid this problem: bounding the damage at a value* $1–\mathrm{tol}$ *where* $\mathrm{tol}$ *is fixed at 0.01. The necessity of thus bounding the damage has as a consequence the appearance of a residual stiffness. In other words, even if the material is completely damaged in one direction, it continues to resist, admittedly weakly, in that direction.*

### 3.2 Threshold function depending on the strain

In order to control better the dissymmetry of the behaviour between tension and compression (ratio 10 of the failure limits), we have introduced a threshold function depending on the strain state into the criterion [eq 3.1-1]. The role of this threshold function is to push back the elasticity limit in compression. One moreover wishes that the failure stress in simple tension cannot be exceeded during a biaxial test (cf. [bib1] for a detailed study of the threshold function).

The function that we propose is the following:

$K(\varepsilon )={k}_{0}-{k}_{1}{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}\arctan(-\frac{{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}}{{k}_{2}})$ **eq 3.2-1**

This threshold function introduces 3 parameters for the model.

**Note:**

*This function has not been optimised for the case of triaxial compression loadings. One recalls in this respect that the law* *ENDO_ORTH_BETON was designed to describe in a finer manner the tensile damage, the description of the compressive damage remaining isotropic. Another constitutive law must therefore be used for applications involving loadings of strong triaxial compression.*

When the trace of the strains is positive, the threshold remains constant: $K(\varepsilon )={k}_{0}$ . The threshold increases when one passes into compression, which makes it possible to push back the elasticity limit, and consequently the failure limit. One notes that the function “*arctan*” has been introduced in order to represent better the failure envelope in the case of biaxial tests (a detailed study is found in [bib1]).

[Figure 3.2-a] shows us the comparison between the elasticity limit supplied by a constant threshold and that obtained with a threshold depending on the trace of the strains in the case of biaxial tests in plane stress.

Let us now study in a little more detail the influence of the various parameters on the response of the model.

### 4.1 Influence of the parameter $\alpha$

The role of the parameter $\alpha$ is to control the ratio of influence of the two associated thermodynamic forces in the evolution criterion. A parameter $\alpha$ close to $1$ favours the evolution of the tensile damage and a parameter $\alpha$ close to $0$ favours the evolution of the compressive damage. We have decided to take a constant parameter for reasons of simplicity.

If one damages in simple compression in direction 1, one creates cracks in the planes orthogonal to ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{2}$ and ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{3}$ . If one then applies a tension in direction 2 or 3, one “sees” these cracks. In order to obtain this effect in the model, a compression must generate not only a compressive damage, but also a tensile damage. If on the other hand one begins with a tension in direction 1, the cracks in the plane perpendicular to ${\overrightarrow{e}}_{1}$ will be little opened (small strain at failure), so one may think that one will not “see” them if one then applies a compression in direction 2 or 3. One concludes from this that one must take $\alpha \ne 0$ and $\alpha \ne 1$ in order to have a coupling, and close to 1 in order to favour the evolution of the tensile damage during compressions. There will also be a small evolution of the compressive damage during tensions, devoid of physical meaning, but this will not be troublesome if this damage remains small.

#### 4.1.2 Warning

In a simple compression test, cracks are created orthogonally to the directions of positive strain, and influence the subsequent behaviour in tension. We have introduced the coupling in order to represent this phenomenon. One sees however in the simple compression test that the lateral tensile damage does not reach failure (${D}_{yy}^{\lim}<1$  ) when the compressive damage $d$ tends towards $1$ . This represents a limitation of the model.

It is however possible to reach failure for a value of $\alpha$ closer to $1$ . The tensile damage will then evolve faster than the compressive damage (cf. [Figure 4.1.2-a]). Unfortunately the stress–strain response then exhibits a snap-back (cf. [Figure 4.1.2-b]) devoid of physical meaning, so that these values of $\alpha$ must be excluded.

#### 4.1.3 Identification of the parameter $\alpha$

There exists a critical value of the parameter $\alpha$ beyond which one falls into the drawbacks stated in section [§ 4.1.2 ]. This critical value depends on the other parameters but we do not have an empirical formula making it possible to find it. One will therefore prefer to use a value of $\alpha$ around $0,9$ which makes the compressive damage evolve more rapidly than the tensile damage in the simple compression test, so that one cannot then observe failure in tension in the directions perpendicular to that of the compression.

### 4.2 Influence of the parameters ${\gamma}_{B}`et :math:`{\gamma}_{d}$

The introduction of a blocked energy depending on the damage variables makes it possible to control the rate of evolution of the damage, and thereby makes it possible to control the shape of the stress–strain curve.

#### 4.2.1 Simple tension test

In the simple tension test, only the part of the blocked energy relating to the tensile damage has a real influence. The stress–strain curve is represented in [Figure4.2.1-a] for various values of ${\gamma}_{B}$ . One sees that the larger ${\gamma}_{B}$ is, the larger the failure stress, and the wider the peak. This is due to the fact that the blocked energy slows down the evolution of the damage, as [Figure4.2.1-b] shows.

#### 4.2.2 Simple compression test

In the simple compression test, only the part of the blocked energy relating to the compressive damage has a real influence. The parameter ${\gamma}_{d}$ is however less easy to calibrate because the threshold of the criterion depends on the strain state. We shall immediately explain this point in the following warning.

**Note:**

*Theoretically, the warning that we are going to set out is also valid in tension. In practice, we are not confronted with it because one always favours the tensile damage by taking a coupling parameter* $\alpha$ *close to 1, and one does not take a value of* ${\gamma}_{d}$ *that is too large (the behaviour of concrete in tension is quasi-brittle).*

##### 4.2.2.1 Warning

The introduction of the blocked energy must make it possible to slow down the evolution of the compressive damage, and thereby must make it possible to round the shape of the peak in a simple compression test. A warning must however be formulated concerning the use of this blocked energy. The thermodynamic force which drives the evolution of the compressive damage is the sum of two terms: one corresponding to the derivative of the elastic energy, depending on the strain and on the damage, positive, and the other corresponding to the derivative of the blocked energy, depending only on the damage, negative. Now, when the damage increases, it may be that the term corresponding to the blocked energy is too large in absolute value with respect to that corresponding to the elastic energy, which can prevent the evolution of the damage.

$\begin{array}{ccc}{F}^{d}={F}_{\mathrm{élastique}}^{d}(\varepsilon ,d)+{F}_{\mathrm{bloquée}}^{d}(d)& \text{avec}& \lbrace \begin{array}{}{F}_{\mathrm{élastique}}^{d}(\varepsilon ,d)\ge 0\\ {F}_{\mathrm{bloquée}}^{d}(d)\le 0\end{array}\\ {F}_{\mathrm{élastique}}^{d}(\varepsilon ,d)+{F}_{\mathrm{bloquée}}^{d}(d)\le 0& \Rightarrow & d\text{n'évolue plus}\end{array}$

This problem appears when one considers a constant threshold in the criterion. [Figure 4.2.2.1-a] shows us in this case the influence of the introduction of the blocked energy on the stress–strain curve in a simple compression test. One observes that the introduction of this energy, by slowing down the evolution of the compressive damage, does indeed make it possible at first to increase the height of the stress peak as well as the width of the peak. One notes however that a snap-back appears on the two curves where a blocked energy has been added. This is due to the fact that one slows down the evolution of the compressive damage but that one does not act on the tensile damage. This is illustrated in [Figure 4.2.2.1-b], which shows a stabilisation of the compressive damage combined with a rapid increase of the tensile damage.

**Figure 4.2.2.1-a: Influence of the blocked energy in a simple compression test**

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.09/10000000000003170000026397D3D8DB77EA7A36.jpg)

When one uses a threshold depending on the strain state, the force associated with the elastic energy remains more important than that associated with the blocked energy because, for the same damage state, the criterion is reached for higher strain levels. One sees in this case the influence of the introduction of a blocked energy on the response of the model in compression. This does indeed make it possible to control the shape of the peak (cf. One sees moreover in [Figure 4.2.2.2-b] that the larger the parameter associated with the blocked energy, the more the lateral tensile damage evolves with respect to the compressive damage. It is however obvious that if one takes a parameter ${\gamma}_{d}$ that is too large, one finds again the problems stated previously.

#### 4.2.3 Identification of the parameters

The parameter ${\gamma}_{B}$ is identified on the simple tension test. It makes it possible to adjust the height and the width of the peak for this test. It must be adjusted at the same time as the parameter ${k}_{0}$ of the threshold.

The parameter ${\gamma}_{d}$ is identified on the simple compression test. It makes it possible to adjust the height and the width of the peak for this test. It must be adjusted at the same time as the parameters ${k}_{1}$ and ${k}_{2}$ of the threshold.

### 4.3 Influence of the parameters of the threshold function

The threshold function that one uses involves three parameters:

$K(\varepsilon )={k}_{0}-{k}_{1}{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}\arctan(-\frac{{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}}{{k}_{2}})$ **eq 4.3-1**

In the case of a simple tension, only the parameter ${k}_{0}$ is involved. As soon as the trace of the strains becomes negative, as is the case in simple compression, the three parameters are involved. The parameter ${k}_{0}$ must therefore be calibrated beforehand on a simple tension test. The parameters ${k}_{1}$ and ${k}_{2}$ will then be calibrated on a simple compression test and a biaxial test, with ${k}_{0}$ fixed.

#### 4.3.1 Simple tension: influence of the parameter ${k}_{0}$

If one takes a blocked energy not depending on the tensile damage (${\gamma}_{B}=0$ ), the value of the failure stress is completely determined by the value of ${k}_{0}$ , $\alpha$ , and the elastic parameters:

${\sigma}_{\mathrm{rupture}}^{2}=\frac{{k}_{0}E}{\sqrt{\alpha +2(1-\alpha )\frac{{\nu  }^{4}}{{1+\nu  }^{2}}}}$ **eq 4.3.1-1**

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.09/100000000000031800000264CE3508AFFA169B63.jpg)

**Figure** **4.3.1-a: Dependence of the failure stress in tension** **with respect to the parameter** ${k}_{0}$ **(** $E=32000\mathrm{Mpa}$ **,** $\nu =0.2$ **,** $\alpha =0.87$ **)**

Obviously, the larger ${k}_{0}$ is, the larger the failure stress in tension, as [Figure 4.3.1-a] shows.

There unfortunately exists no analytical expression for the failure stress when ${\gamma}_{B}\ne 0$ . The dependence of the response with respect to the parameter ${\gamma}_{B}$ is studied in paragraph [§ 4.2.1 ]. The parameters ${k}_{0}$ and ${\gamma}_{B}$ must be calibrated simultaneously.

#### 4.3.2 Compression: influence of the parameters ${k}_{1}`et :math:`{k}_{2}$

The role of the threshold function, which depends on the negative trace of the strain, is to increase the failure limit in compression, in order to control better the dissymmetry of the behaviour of concrete between tension and compression.

Moreover, we have chosen to take a threshold function making it possible to describe the failure envelope of concrete in the case of biaxial loadings. This choice was made for two reasons:

We have available for these tests experimental results (cf. [bib5]). These tests certainly concern only the concretes used by [bib5], but they present common particularities which one can, it seems, generalise.

This makes it possible to widen the range of use of the model. The uniaxial tests are indeed insufficient to ensure the relevance of the model in the case of 3D analyses.

**Note:**

*The model must not be used in the case of strong triaxial compressions, the free energy and the threshold function not having been established to treat this case. One recalls that the principal objective of the model is to describe the tensile damage in concrete.*

##### 4.3.2.1 Role of the parameter ${k}_{1}$

The parameter ${k}_{1}$ is the parameter which makes it possible to increase the failure limit in compression. [Figure 4.3.2.1-a].

##### 4.3.2.2 Role of the parameter ${k}_{2}$

In order to understand the role of the parameter ${k}_{2}$ , let us return to the path which led us to choose the threshold function [eq 3.2-1].

The first threshold function to have been tested is the linear function:

$K(\varepsilon )={k}_{0}-{k}_{1}{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}$ **eq 4.3.2.2-1**

This function does indeed make it possible to increase the failure stress in simple compression, but it poses a problem when one is interested in biaxial tests. [Figure 4.3.2.2-a] shows us the envelope of the elastic domain for biaxial tests.

**Note:**

*The envelope of the elasticity domain is different from the failure envelope (for which we have experimental results available). We study the envelope of the elasticity domain because it can be computed analytically, unlike the failure envelope, and because it is from it that we chose our threshold function. The user will however have to calibrate the parameters properly on the failure envelope.*

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.09/100000000000031700000263923C9CFA76561F58.jpg)

The linear variation of the threshold with the strain does not seem suitable, since one sees a swelling appear in the zone $({\sigma}_{1}>0,{\sigma}_{2}<0)$ which gives limit stresses in tension that are too large.

We have therefore modified the threshold function:

$K(\varepsilon )={k}_{0}-{k}_{1}{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}\arctan(-\frac{{(\text{tr}\varepsilon )}_{-}}{{k}_{2}})$ **eq 4.3.2.2-2**

The fact that the arctan function presents a plateau makes it possible to recover a linear threshold when the trace of the strains increases in absolute value. The fact that it is zero at the origin makes it possible to slow down the increase of the threshold near this origin.

One can see in [Figure 4.3.2.2-b] the effect of the introduction of the new threshold function with respect to the linear function on the elasticity domain for the same value of the parameter ${k}_{1}$ . This new threshold function makes it possible to avoid the swelling phenomenon observed with the linear function when one increases the value of the parameter ${k}_{2}$ (the case ${k}_{2}=0$ corresponds to the linear function). The other effect of the parameter ${k}_{2}$ is to decrease the stress of initiation of the damage, which implies a decrease of the failure stress (effect opposite to that of ${k}_{1}$ ) as [Figure 4.3.2.2-c] shows.

#### 4.3.3 Identification of the parameters

The parameters ${\gamma}_{d}$ , ${k}_{1}$ and ${k}_{2}$ are identified simultaneously. The parameters ${k}_{1}$ and ${\gamma}_{d}$ make it possible to adjust the failure stress in simple compression and the parameter makes it possible to avoid the “swelling” of the envelope of the elastic domain (and therefore of the failure envelope) for the biaxial tests. The larger one takes ${k}_{1}$ , the larger one must take ${k}_{2}$ .

### 4.4 Summary of the study of the parameters

Despite the relative simplicity of the model, and the small number of parameters to be identified (6), the number of experimental data available to the engineer is often, or even always, smaller than the number of parameters to be identified. This implies the arbitrary character of the choice of certain parameters. Here, in summary, is the order in which the parameters must be chosen:

- For the parameter $\alpha$ , a value between $0,85$ and $0,9$ is recommended.
- The parameters ${k}_{0}$ and ${\gamma}_{B}$ must be identified simultaneously on a simple tension test (no analytical formula in the case ${\gamma}_{B}\ne 0$ ).
- The parameters ${\gamma}_{d}$ , ${k}_{1}$ and ${k}_{2}$ must be identified simultaneously. The simplest is to fix the parameter ${k}_{2}$ at ${6.10}^{-4}$ (a value for which it is probable that one does not observe any swelling of the failure envelope (cf. [§ 4.3.2.2 ]) for biaxial tests), and to calibrate ${\gamma}_{d}$ and ${k}_{1}$ on a simple compression test. One then checks whether the failure envelope is correct, one modifies ${k}_{2}$ if necessary and one starts again for ${\gamma}_{d}$ and ${k}_{1}$ .

Examples of sets of parameters are found in section 6 (validation on experimental tests). One will moreover find in the document [V6.04.176](doc:v6.04.176) the test cases making it possible to identify the parameters.

## 5 Numerical implementation

For the integration of the constitutive law in *Code_Aster* , we have placed ourselves within the framework of the implicit integration of constitutive laws.

### 5.1 Evaluation of the damage

One denotes by ${\varepsilon}^{-}$ the strain and by ${B}^{-}$ and ${d}^{-}$ the damage variables at the end of the previous time step (after convergence). One wishes to determine the evolution of the damage when one applies a strain increment $\Delta \varepsilon ` . La déformation finale :math:$varepsilon ={varepsilon}^{-}+Delta varepsilon` is therefore fixed.

One evaluates the damage criterion:

$f(\varepsilon ,{B}^{-},{d}^{-})=\sqrt{\alpha {\parallel {F}_{-}^{B}\parallel }^{2}+(1-\alpha ){({F}_{+}^{d})}^{2}}-K(\varepsilon )$ **eq 5.1-1**

If $f\le 0$ , the damage variables do not evolve and one can pass to the next iteration for the mechanical equilibrium.

If $f>0$ , the damage variables evolve, respecting both the criterion and the normal flow. One therefore seeks $(\Delta B,\mathrm{\Delta \; d},\mathrm{\Delta \; \gamma })$ solution of the system:

$R(\Delta B,\Delta d,\Delta \gamma )=(\begin{array}{c}-\Delta B+\Delta \gamma \alpha {F}_{-}^{B}({B}^{-}+\Delta B)\\ -\Delta d+\Delta \gamma (1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}({d}^{-}+\Delta d)\\ f(\varepsilon ,{B}^{-}+\Delta B,{d}^{-}+\Delta d)\end{array})=(\begin{array}{c}0\\ 0\\ 0\end{array})$ **eq 5.1-2**

This system is non-linear and requires the use of an iterative method. We have chosen to use a Newton–Raphson method. The index $n$ represents the Newton iterations. Let ${R}^{n}(\Delta {B}^{n},{\mathrm{\Delta \; d}}^{n},{\mathrm{\Delta \; \gamma }}^{n})$ be the residual of the system at iteration $n$ ; the linearisation of the system is written:

${R}^{n+1}(\Delta {B}^{n+1},\Delta {d}^{n+1},\Delta {\gamma}^{n+1})={R}^{n}(\Delta {B}^{n},\Delta {d}^{n},\Delta {\gamma}^{n})+{({R}^{n}(\Delta {B}^{n},\Delta {d}^{n},\Delta {\gamma}^{n}))}^{\prime }:(\begin{array}{c}\Delta {B}^{n+1}-\Delta {B}^{n}\\ \Delta {d}^{n+1}-\Delta {d}^{n}\\ \Delta {\gamma}^{n+1}-\Delta {\gamma}^{n}\end{array})$ **eq 5.1-3**

where    ${R}^{n}=(\begin{array}{c}-\Delta {B}^{n}+{\mathrm{\Delta \; \gamma }}^{n}\alpha {F}_{-}^{Bn}\\ -{\mathrm{\Delta \; d}}^{n}+{\mathrm{\Delta \; \gamma }}^{n}(1-\alpha ){F}^{dn}\\ f(\varepsilon ,{B}^{-}+\Delta {B}^{n},{d}^{-}+{\mathrm{\Delta \; d}}^{n})\end{array})$

and ${R}^{{n}^{\prime }}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}-I+\alpha \Delta {\gamma}^{n}\frac{\partial {F}_{-}^{B}}{\partial {F}^{B}}:\frac{\partial {F}^{B}}{\partial B}& 0& \alpha {F}_{-}^{B}\\ 0& -1+(1-\alpha )\Delta {\gamma}^{n}\frac{\partial {F}_{+}^{d}}{\partial {F}^{d}}\frac{\partial {F}^{d}}{\partial d}& (1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}\\ \frac{\alpha {F}_{-}^{B}}{\sqrt{\alpha {\parallel {F}_{-}^{B}\parallel }^{2}+(1-\alpha ){\mid {F}_{+}^{d}\mid }^{2}}}:\frac{\partial {F}^{B}}{\partial B}& \frac{(1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}}{\sqrt{\alpha {\parallel {F}_{-}^{B}\parallel }^{2}+(1-\alpha ){\mid {F}_{+}^{d}\mid }^{2}}}\frac{\partial {F}^{d}}{\partial d}& 0\end{array}\right]$ .

One then solves the linearised system ${R}^{n+1}(\Delta {B}^{n+1},\Delta {d}^{n+1},\Delta {\gamma}^{n+1})=0$ . One obtains $(\Delta {B}^{n+1},\Delta {d}^{n+1},\Delta {\gamma}^{n+1})$ . This procedure is repeated until the residual is smaller than a convergence parameter.

### 5.2 Computation of the tangent matrix

The tangent matrix is the tensor $M$ of order 4 defined by:

$\delta {\sigma}_{ij}={M}_{ijkl}\delta {\varepsilon}_{kl}\iff {M}_{ijkl}=\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial \Delta {\varepsilon}_{kl}}$ **eq 5.2-1**

This matrix is not computed on the continuous problem but on the incremental problem. One therefore seeks the effects of a variation of the strain increment between two successive time steps on the variation of the final stress, taking into account the fact that the internal variables can also evolve. One has, to first order:

> ${\text{\delta  \sigma }}_{ij}(\varepsilon ,B,d)=\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; \varepsilon }}_{kl}}{\mid}_{B,d}{\mathrm{\delta \; \Delta \; \varepsilon }}_{kl}+\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; B}}_{kl}}{\mid}_{\varepsilon ,d}{\mathrm{\delta \; \Delta \; B}}_{kl}+\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial \mathrm{\Delta \; d}}{\mid}_{\varepsilon ,B}\mathrm{\delta \; \Delta \; d}$ **eq 5.2-2**

that is:

${\text{\delta  \sigma }}_{ij}(\varepsilon ,B,d)=\left[\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; \varepsilon }}_{kl}}{\mid}_{B,d}+\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; B}}_{\text{mn}}}{\mid}_{\varepsilon ,d}\frac{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; B}}_{\text{mn}}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; \varepsilon }}_{kl}}+\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial \mathrm{\Delta \; d}}{\mid}_{\varepsilon ,B}\frac{\partial \mathrm{\Delta \; d}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; \varepsilon }}_{kl}}\right]{\mathrm{\delta \; \Delta \; \varepsilon }}_{kl}$ **eq 5.2-3**

The notation $\frac{\partial A}{\partial B}{\mid}_{C,D}$ used here means that one differentiates $A$ with respect to $B$ for $C$ and $D$ constant.

One thus decomposes the tangent matrix into two parts, one at constant damage, and the other expressing the evolution of the internal variables:

$M={M}^{\text{cst}}+{M}^{\text{evol}}$ **eq 5.2-4**

with ${M}_{ijkl}^{\text{cst}}=\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; \varepsilon }}_{kl}}{\mid}_{B,d}$ and ${M}_{ijkl}^{\text{evol}}=\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; B}}_{\text{mn}}}{\mid}_{\varepsilon ,d}\frac{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; B}}_{\text{mn}}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; \varepsilon }}_{kl}}+\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial \mathrm{\Delta \; d}}{\mid}_{\varepsilon ,B}\frac{\partial \mathrm{\Delta \; d}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; \varepsilon }}_{kl}}$

#### 5.2.1 Term at constant damage

The matrix ${M}^{\text{cst}}$ is the derivative of the stress with respect to the strain at constant damage:

$\begin{array}{}{M}_{ijkl}^{\text{cst}}=\mathrm{\lambda \; H}(\text{tr}\text{B \varepsilon }){B}_{ij}{B}_{kl}+\mathrm{\lambda \; f}(d)H(-\text{tr}\varepsilon ){\delta}_{ij}{\delta}_{kl}\\ +\frac{\mu}{4}\frac{\partial {A}_{+\text{ip}}}{\partial {A}_{\text{mn}}}({B}_{\text{mk}}{\delta}_{\text{nl}}+{\delta}_{\text{mk}}{B}_{\text{nl}}+{B}_{\text{ml}}{\delta}_{\text{nk}}+{\delta}_{\text{ml}}{B}_{\text{nk}}){B}_{\text{pj}}\\ +\frac{\mu}{4}{B}_{\text{ip}}\frac{\partial {A}_{+\text{pj}}}{\partial {A}_{\text{mn}}}({B}_{\text{mk}}{\delta}_{\text{nl}}+{\delta}_{\text{mk}}{B}_{\text{nl}}+{B}_{\text{ml}}{\delta}_{\text{nk}}+{\delta}_{\text{ml}}{B}_{\text{nk}})\\ +\mathrm{2\; \mu \; f}(d)\frac{\partial {\varepsilon}_{-ij}}{\partial {\varepsilon}_{kl}}\end{array}$ **eq 5.2.1-1**

where $A=\mathrm{B\; \varepsilon }+\mathrm{\varepsilon \; B}$ , $H$ is the Heaviside function and the derivatives $\frac{\partial {\varepsilon}_{-}}{\partial \varepsilon }$ and $\frac{\partial {A}_{+}}{\partial A}$ are defined in appendix 1.

#### 5.2.2 Term linked to the evolution of the damage

The matrix ${M}^{\text{evol}}$ is computed by differentiating the criterion and the normality law. This differentiation is carried out on the incremental problem and not on the continuous problem.

One first differentiates the criterion:

$f({F}^{B},{F}^{d})=\sqrt{\alpha {\parallel {F}_{-}^{B}\parallel }^{2}+(1-\alpha ){({F}_{+}^{d})}^{2}}-K(\varepsilon )\le 0\underset{\text{diff}}{\Rightarrow }\frac{\alpha {F}_{-}^{B}:\delta {F}^{B}+(1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}{\mathrm{\delta \; F}}^{d}}{\sqrt{\alpha {\parallel {F}_{-}^{B}\parallel }^{2}+(1-\alpha ){({F}_{+}^{d})}^{2}}}-\mathrm{\delta \; K}=0$     eq 5.2.2-1

One then differentiates the flow law of the incremental problem, discretised implicitly:

$\begin{array}{}\begin{array}{}\Delta B=\Delta \gamma \alpha {F}_{-}^{B}\\ \Delta d=\Delta \gamma (1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}\end{array}\rbrace \Rightarrow (1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}\Delta B=\alpha {F}_{-}^{B}\Delta d\\ \\ \underset{\text{diff}}{\Rightarrow }(1-\alpha )\delta {F}_{+}^{d}\Delta B+(1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}\delta \Delta B=\alpha \delta {F}_{-}^{B}\Delta d+\alpha {F}_{-}^{B}\delta \Delta d\end{array}$ **eq 5.2.2-2**

One seeks the relation between $\delta \Deltab$ *,* $\delta \Deltad$ and $\delta \Delta \varepsilon$ . One can express the variations of the thermodynamic forces as a function of the variations of the strains and of the damage variables:

$\begin{array}{}\delta {F}_{-}^{B}=\frac{\partial {F}_{-}^{B}}{\partial {F}^{B}}:\left[\frac{\partial {F}^{B}}{\partial \Delta \varepsilon }:\delta \Delta \varepsilon +\frac{\partial {F}^{B}}{\partial \Delta B}:\delta \Delta B\right]\\ \delta {F}^{d}=\frac{\partial {F}_{+}^{d}}{\partial {F}^{d}}\left[\frac{\partial {F}^{d}}{\partial \Delta \varepsilon }:\delta \Delta \varepsilon +\frac{\partial {F}^{d}}{\partial \Delta d}\delta \Delta d\right]\end{array}$ **eq 5.2.2-3**

The system of equations defined by [eq 5.2.2-1], [eq 5.2.2-2] and [eq 5.2.2-3] leads to the following expressions:

$\begin{array}{ccc}\delta \Delta \text{B}& =& {\chi }^{-1}:\xi :\delta \Delta \varepsilon \\ \delta \Delta d& =& -\left[\frac{\tau}{\rho}+\frac{\theta}{\rho}:{\chi }^{-1}:\xi \right]:\delta \Delta \varepsilon \end{array}$ **eq 5.2.2-4**

with

${\mathrm{\khi }}_{ijkl}={\left[\frac{\partial {F}_{+}^{d}}{\partial {F}^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{\Delta \; B}}{{F}_{+}^{d}}-\frac{{\mathrm{\alpha \; F}}_{-}^{B}}{(1-\alpha ){F}^{d}\frac{\partial {F}^{d}}{\partial d}}\right]}_{ij}{\left[{\mathrm{\alpha \; F}}_{-}^{B}:\frac{\partial {F}_{-}^{B}}{\partial {F}^{B}}:\frac{\partial {F}^{B}}{\partial B}\right]}_{kl}+\mathrm{\alpha \; \Delta \; d}{\left[\frac{\partial {F}_{-}^{B}}{\partial {F}^{B}}:\frac{\partial {F}^{B}}{\partial B}\right]}_{ijkl}-\frac{1}{2}(1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}({\delta}_{\text{ik}}{\delta}_{\text{jl}}+{\delta}_{\text{il}}{\delta}_{\text{jk}})$

$\begin{array}{}{\xi}_{ijkl}={\left[\frac{{\mathrm{\alpha \; F}}_{-}^{B}}{(1-\alpha ){F}^{d}\frac{\partial {F}^{d}}{\partial d}}-\frac{\partial {F}_{+}^{d}}{\partial {F}^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{\Delta \; B}}{{F}_{+}^{d}}\right]}_{ij}{\left[{\mathrm{\alpha \; F}}_{-}^{B}:\frac{\partial {F}_{-}^{B}}{\partial {F}^{B}}:\frac{\partial {F}^{B}}{\partial \varepsilon }+(1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}\frac{\partial {F}^{d}}{\partial \varepsilon }-\sqrt{\alpha {\parallel {F}_{-}^{B}\parallel }^{2}+(1-\alpha ){({F}_{+}^{d})}^{2}}\frac{\partial K}{\partial \varepsilon }\right]}_{kl}\\ +(1-\alpha )\frac{\partial {F}_{+}^{d}}{\partial {F}^{d}}{\mathrm{\Delta \; B}}_{ij}\frac{\partial {F}^{d}}{\partial {\varepsilon}_{kl}}-\mathrm{\alpha \; \Delta \; d}{\left[\frac{\partial {F}_{-}^{B}}{\partial {F}^{B}}:\frac{\partial {F}^{B}}{\partial \varepsilon }\right]}_{ijkl}\end{array}$

${\tau}_{ij}={\left[{\mathrm{\alpha \; F}}_{-}^{B}:\frac{\partial {F}_{-}^{B}}{\partial {F}^{B}}:\frac{\partial {F}^{B}}{\partial \varepsilon }+(1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}\frac{\partial {F}^{d}}{\partial \varepsilon }-\sqrt{\alpha {\parallel {F}_{-}^{B}\parallel }^{2}+(1-\alpha ){({F}_{+}^{d})}^{2}}\frac{\partial K}{\partial \varepsilon }\right]}_{ij}$

${\theta}_{ij}={\left[{\mathrm{\alpha \; F}}_{-}^{B}:\frac{\partial {F}_{-}^{B}}{\partial {F}^{B}}:\frac{\partial {F}^{B}}{\partial B}\right]}_{ij}$

$\rho =(1-\alpha ){F}_{+}^{d}\frac{\partial {F}^{d}}{\partial d}$

${\chi }^{-1}$ such that ${\chi }_{ijkl}^{-1}{\chi }_{\text{klmn}}=\frac{1}{2}({\delta}_{\text{im}}{\delta}_{\text{jn}}+{\delta}_{in}{\delta}_{\text{jm}})$

One therefore obtains the expressions of $\frac{\partial \Delta B}{\partial \Delta \varepsilon }$ and of $\frac{\partial \Delta d}{\partial \Delta \varepsilon }$ .

Moreover, according to the definition of the stress and of the thermodynamic forces, which derive from the same energy, one has:

$\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \mathrm{\Delta \; B}}{\mid}_{\varepsilon ,d}=\frac{\partial {F}^{B}}{\partial \varepsilon }{\mid}_{B}$ **and**     $\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \mathrm{\Delta \; d}}{\mid}_{\varepsilon ,B}=\frac{\partial {F}^{d}}{\partial \varepsilon }{\mid}_{d}$ **eq 5.2.2-5**

which allows us to compute the part of the tangent matrix relating to the evolution of the damage:

${M}_{ijkl}^{\text{evol}}=\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; B}}_{\text{mn}}}{\mid}_{\varepsilon ,d}\frac{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; B}}_{\text{mn}}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; \varepsilon }}_{kl}}+\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{ij}}{\partial \mathrm{\Delta \; d}}{\mid}_{\varepsilon ,B}\frac{\partial \mathrm{\Delta \; d}}{\partial {\mathrm{\Delta \; \varepsilon }}_{kl}}$ **eq 5.2.2-6**

**Note:**

*It is to be noted that the tangent matrix is not symmetric. This is due to the fact that the damage threshold depends on the strain. In the case of a constant threshold, the matrix is indeed symmetric, since the model is then indeed generalised standard and one uses an implicit integration scheme.*

## 6 Validation

The first step of the validation of the model is the comparison of the prediction with various experimental results for simple tests. Given the simplifying assumptions which have been made for our modelling, we shall of course not be able to reproduce all the experiments, in particular the triaxial compression experiments. One concentrates here on the simple tension tests, the simple compression tests, as well as on biaxial tests.

### 6.1 Identification of the parameters

The identification of the parameters must be carried out in three successive phases:

- One must choose a value for the coupling constant $\alpha$ . It must be taken so that the scalar compressive damage remains negligible in a tension test. One decides to take it equal to $0,87$ for all the tests of the present section in order to avoid the snap-back phenomenon in compression (cf. [§ 4.1.2 ]).
- The second phase is the identification of the parameters ${k}_{0}$ and ${\gamma}_{B}$ . These parameters can be identified directly on the simple tension test because the other parameters of the model do not intervene in this test.
- The third phase is the identification of the parameters ${\gamma}_{d}$ , ${k}_{1}$ and ${k}_{2}$ on the simple compression tests and the biaxial tests.

Strictly speaking, the identification of the parameters of our model for a material requires having available the experimental curves in simple tension, in simple compression and under biaxial loading. Unfortunately, all these results are generally not available simultaneously for a material. We shall therefore have to make a certain number of assumptions in order to calibrate our parameters. For example, we shall choose the parameter ${k}_{2}$ for all the tests in such a way that the tensile stresses in the biaxial tests do not exceed the failure stress in tension. Moreover, we do not have available, for the compression tests, the failure stress in tension for the materials studied. We shall therefore take in an arbitrary manner parameters ${k}_{0}$ and ${\gamma}_{B}$ equal to those that we computed for the simple tension test.

### 6.2 Simple tension

The experiments aiming to observe the behaviour of concrete under tensile loading are extremely difficult to carry out, which explains the relatively small number of results of simple tension tests. The difficulty lies in the fact that the damage concentrates in localisation bands corresponding to cracks, which has the effect of making the specimen under study inhomogeneous. As soon as strong heterogeneities appear, it becomes impossible to deduce a stress–strain curve from the force–displacement curve, and therefore to establish a constitutive law for the material. The PIED measuring apparatus ([bib6], [bib7]) makes it possible to measure a relatively homogeneous stress and strain field by limiting the localisation. This is why we use the results obtained by [bib6] in order to test our model.

In the case of simple tension, only 3 of the 6 parameters of the model will play a role:

- The coupling parameter $\alpha$ .
- The threshold constant ${k}_{0}$ .
- The blocked-energy constant associated with the tensile damage ${\gamma}_{B}$ .
- The parameters ${k}_{1}$ and ${k}_{2}$ have no influence on this test and the influence of the parameter ${\gamma}_{d}$  is negligible.

The parameter ${k}_{0}$ governs the height of the stress peak and the parameter ${\gamma}_{B}$ controls its width as well as the post-peak slope. The larger ${\gamma}_{B}$  is, the less rapidly the damage evolves, which makes the non-linearity appear before the peak and increases the strain at the peak and its width. [Figure 6.2-a] shows the response of the model, compared with the experimental data of [bib6]. The computation is carried out on a single element so as not to encounter a localisation phenomenon. The parameters are the following:

**A1.1** **Definition of the spectral decomposition and of the positive and negative parts of a tensor**

Let $X$ be a symmetric tensor of order 2. In order not to burden the notation, one will also denote by $X$ , abusively, the matrix of this tensor in the fixed basis of the observer. Let moreover $\tilde{X}$ be the (diagonal) matrix of this tensor in its principal basis:

> $\tilde{X}=(\begin{array}{ccc}{\tilde{X}}_{1}& 0& 0\\ 0& {\tilde{X}}_{2}& 0\\ 0& 0& {\tilde{X}}_{3}\end{array})$ **eq A1.1-1**

Denoting by ${U}_{i}$ the eigenvector associated with the i-th eigenvalue, and by $Q=({U}_{1},{U}_{2},{U}_{3})$ the change-of-basis matrix between the fixed basis and the principal basis of $X$ , one has the relation:

$X=Q.\tilde{X}.{Q}^{T}$ **eq A1.1-2**

The positive and negative parts of the tensor $X$ are defined by:

${X}_{+}={P}_{+}:X=Q.{\tilde{X}}_{+}.{Q}^{T}$ with ${\tilde{X}}_{+}=(\begin{array}{ccc}H({\tilde{X}}_{1}){\tilde{X}}_{1}& 0& 0\\ 0& H({\tilde{X}}_{2}){\tilde{X}}_{2}& 0\\ 0& 0& H({\tilde{X}}_{2}){\tilde{X}}_{3}\end{array})$ **eq A1.1-3**

${X}_{-}={P}_{-}:X=Q.{\tilde{X}}_{-}.{Q}^{T}$ with ${\tilde{X}}_{-}=(\begin{array}{ccc}H(-{\tilde{X}}_{1}){\tilde{X}}_{1}& 0& 0\\ 0& H(-{\tilde{X}}_{2}){\tilde{X}}_{2}& 0\\ 0& 0& H(-{\tilde{X}}_{2}){\tilde{X}}_{3}\end{array})$ **eq A1.1-4**

where $H$ is the Heaviside function.

**A1.2** **Computation of the derivatives**

For the computation of the tangent matrix, as well as for the computation of the evolution of the damage, we need to evaluate the derivative of the positive and negative parts of a tensor with respect to the latter. It is sufficient for this to imagine that the tensor $X$ depends on time and to compute the tensors ${M}_{+}$ and ${M}_{-}$ defined by:

${\dot{X}}_{+}={M}_{+}:\dot{X}$ **and** ${\dot{X}}_{-}={M}_{-}:\dot{X}$ **eq A1.2-1**

The differentiation of equation [eq A1.1-2] gives us:

${\dot{X}}_{+}=Q.{\tilde{\dot{X}}}_{+}.{Q}^{T}+\dot{Q}.{\tilde{X}}_{+}.{Q}^{T}+Q.{\tilde{X}}_{+}.{\dot{Q}}^{T}$ **eq A1.2-2**

**Assumption:**

The expression of $\dot{Q}$ must be established. The proof which follows is valid only if the eigenvalues of $X$ are distinct. Inasmuch as the computation of ${M}_{+}$ and ${M}_{-}$ will be used only in numerical solution algorithms, we shall allow ourselves to perturb numerically any identical eigenvalues in order to make them distinct and to be able to use the results below.

In order to compute $\dot{Q}$ , one needs to express the derivative of the eigenvectors ${\dot{U}}_{i}$ . For this, following the approach of [bib11], one differentiates the expression of $X$ :

$X=\sum_{i}{\tilde{X}}_{i}{U}_{i}\otimes {U}_{i}\Rightarrow \dot{X}=\sum_{i}{\tilde{\dot{X}}}_{i}{U}_{i}\otimes {U}_{i}+{\tilde{X}}_{i}{\dot{U}}_{i}\otimes {U}_{i}+{\tilde{X}}_{i}{U}_{i}\otimes {\dot{U}}_{i}$ **eq A1.2-3**

Moreover, one has the following relations between the eigenvectors and their derivatives:

${U}_{i}.{U}_{j}={\delta}_{ij}$ **,**     ${\dot{U}}_{i}.{U}_{j}+{U}_{i}.{\dot{U}}_{j}=0$ **eq A1.2-4**

By contracting equation [eq A1.2-3] on the left and on the right by the eigenvectors, and by using the relations [eq A1.2-4], one obtains the following relations:

${U}_{i}.\dot{X}.{U}_{i}\Rightarrow {(\dot{\tilde{X}})}_{ii}={\tilde{\dot{X}}}_{i}$ **eq A1.2-5**

> ${U}_{j}.\dot{X}.{U}_{k}\Rightarrow {(\dot{\tilde{X}})}_{\text{jk}}={\tilde{X}}_{k}{U}_{j}.{\dot{U}}_{k}+{\tilde{X}}_{j}{\dot{U}}_{j}.{U}_{k}=({\tilde{X}}_{j}-{\tilde{X}}_{k}){\dot{U}}_{j}.{U}_{k}\text{pour}j\ne k$         eq A1.2-6

In these expressions, there is no summation on the indices, the ${(\dot{\tilde{X}})}_{\text{jk}}$ denote the components of $\dot{X}$ in the **fixed basis**  coinciding with the principal basis of $X$ at the instant under consideration $({(\dot{\tilde{X}})}_{\text{jk}}={U}_{j}.\dot{X}.{U}_{k})$ , and the ${\tilde{\dot{X}}}_{i}$ denote the derivatives of the eigenvalues of $X$ (not the eigenvalues of the derivative $\dot{X}$ .

One deduces from the relation [eq A1.2-6] the expression of ${\dot{U}}_{j}$ :

${\dot{U}}_{j}=\sum_{k\ne j}({\dot{U}}_{j}.{U}_{k}).{U}_{k}=\sum_{k\ne j}\frac{{(\dot{\tilde{X}})}_{\text{jk}}}{({\tilde{X}}_{j}-{\tilde{X}}_{k})}{U}_{k}$ **eq A1.2-7**

This allows us to express $\dot{Q}$ :

$\dot{Q}=({\dot{U}}_{1},{\dot{U}}_{2},{\dot{U}}_{3})\Rightarrow {\dot{Q}}_{ij}=\sum_{k\ne j}\frac{{(\dot{\tilde{X}})}_{\text{jk}}}{({\tilde{X}}_{j}-{\tilde{X}}_{k})}{Q}_{\text{ik}}$ **eq A1.2-8**

The ${(\dot{\tilde{X}})}_{\text{jk}}$ , components of $\dot{X}$ in the **fixed basis**  coinciding with the principal basis of $X$ at the instant under consideration, can be expressed as a function of the components of $\dot{X}$ in the fixed basis. Thus, $\dot{\tilde{X}}$ denoting the matrix of the ${(\dot{\tilde{X}})}_{\text{jk}}$ , one has:

$\dot{\tilde{X}}={Q}^{T}.\dot{X}.Q$ **eq A1.2-9**

One deduces from the expression [eq A1.2-8] the expression of $\dot{Q}$ :

${\dot{Q}}_{ij}=\sum_{k\ne j}\sum_{m,n}\frac{{Q}_{\text{mj}}{Q}_{\text{nk}}{\dot{X}}_{\text{mn}}}{({\tilde{X}}_{j}-{\tilde{X}}_{k})}{Q}_{\text{ik}}$ **eq A1.2-10**

One finally has the obvious relations between the eigenvalues of ${X}_{+}$ and $X$ :

${\tilde{X}}_{+{}_{i}}=H({\tilde{X}}_{i}){\tilde{X}}_{i}\Rightarrow {\tilde{\dot{X}}}_{+{}_{i}}=H({\tilde{X}}_{i}){\tilde{\dot{X}}}_{i}$ **eq A1.2-11**

One deduces from the relations [eq A1.2-5], [eq A1.2-9] and [eq A1.2-11] the following relation:

${\tilde{\dot{X}}}_{+{}_{i}}=H({\tilde{X}}_{i}){\tilde{\dot{X}}}_{i}=H({\tilde{X}}_{i}){(\dot{\tilde{X}})}_{ii}=H({\tilde{X}}_{i}){Q}_{ji}{Q}_{\text{ki}}{\dot{X}}_{\text{jk}}$ **eq A1.2-12**

The relations [eq A1.2-10], [eq A1.2-11] and [eq A1.2-12] allow us to express the relation [eq A1.2-2]:

${\dot{X}}_{+{}_{ij}}=\sum_{k,l}\left[\sum_{m}{Q}_{\text{im}}{Q}_{\text{jm}}{Q}_{\text{km}}{Q}_{\text{lm}}H({\tilde{X}}_{m})\right]{\dot{X}}_{kl}+\sum_{k,l}\left[\sum_{\begin{array}{}m\ne n\\ m,n\end{array}}\frac{{Q}_{\text{km}}{Q}_{\ln}}{\tilde{{X}_{m}}-\tilde{{X}_{n}}}H(\tilde{{X}_{m}})\tilde{{X}_{m}}({Q}_{in}{Q}_{\text{jm}}+{Q}_{\text{im}}{Q}_{\text{jn}})\right]{\dot{X}}_{kl}$

> **eq A1.2-13**

Since $X$ is a symmetric tensor, one has:

$X=\frac{1}{2}(X+{X}^{T})\Rightarrow \dot{X}=\frac{1}{2}(\dot{X}+{\dot{X}}^{T})$ **eq A1.2-14**

This allows us to rewrite equation [eq A1.2-13]:

$\begin{array}{}{\dot{X}}_{+{}_{ij}}=\sum_{k,l}\left[\sum_{m}{Q}_{\text{im}}{Q}_{\text{jm}}{Q}_{\text{km}}{Q}_{\text{lm}}H({\tilde{X}}_{m})\right]{\dot{X}}_{kl}\\ +\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k,l}\left[\sum_{\begin{array}{}m\ne n\\ m,n\end{array}}({Q}_{\text{km}}{Q}_{\ln}+{Q}_{\text{kn}}{Q}_{\text{lm}})\frac{H(\tilde{{X}_{m}})\tilde{{X}_{m}}}{\tilde{{X}_{m}}-\tilde{{X}_{n}}}({Q}_{in}{Q}_{\text{jm}}+{Q}_{\text{im}}{Q}_{\text{jn}})\right]{\dot{X}}_{kl}\end{array}$ **eq A1.2-15**

One deduces from it the expression of the components of ${M}_{+}$ :

${M}_{\text{+ijkl}}=\sum_{m}{Q}_{\text{im}}{Q}_{\text{jm}}{Q}_{\text{km}}{Q}_{\text{lm}}H({\tilde{X}}_{m})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{array}{}m\ne n\\ m,n\end{array}}({Q}_{\text{km}}{Q}_{\ln}+{Q}_{\text{kn}}{Q}_{\text{lm}})\frac{H(\tilde{{X}_{m}})\tilde{{X}_{m}}}{\tilde{{X}_{m}}-\tilde{{X}_{n}}}({Q}_{in}{Q}_{\text{jm}}+{Q}_{\text{im}}{Q}_{\text{jn}})$ **eq A1.2-16**

By analogy, one easily deduces from it the expression of the components of ${M}_{-}$ :

${M}_{\text{-ijkl}}=\sum_{m}{Q}_{\text{im}}{Q}_{\text{jm}}{Q}_{\text{km}}{Q}_{\text{lm}}H({\tilde{-X}}_{m})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{array}{}m\ne n\\ m,n\end{array}}({Q}_{\text{km}}{Q}_{\ln}+{Q}_{\text{kn}}{Q}_{\text{lm}})\frac{H(\tilde{-{X}_{m}})\tilde{{X}_{m}}}{\tilde{{X}_{m}}-\tilde{{X}_{n}}}({Q}_{in}{Q}_{\text{jm}}+{Q}_{\text{im}}{Q}_{\text{jn}})$ **eq A1.2-17**

One wishes to show that when an eigenvalue of the damage reaches 1 in one direction, then this direction is locked and the damage can no longer evolve except in the plane orthogonal to this direction.

One places oneself in the principal basis of the damage:

$D=\sum_{i}{\tilde{D}}_{i}{e}_{i}\otimes {e}_{i}$ **eq A2-1**

where ${e}_{i}$ denotes the eigenvector associated with the eigenvalue ${\tilde{D}}_{i}$ .

The energy adopted is the sum of an elastic energy and of an indicator function of the eigenvalues of the damage:

$\varphi (\varepsilon ,D)={\varphi}^{\text{el}}(\varepsilon ,D)+{I}_{]-\infty ,1]}\left[\max({\tilde{D}}_{i})\right]$ **eq A2-2**

The evolution criterion of the damage is written:

$f({F}^{D})=\sqrt{\text{tr}({({F}^{D})}_{+}^{2})}-k\le 0$ **eq A2-3**

The evolution of the internal variable follows the following flow law obeying the normality principle:

$\dot{D}=\dot{\eta}\frac{\partial f}{\partial {F}^{D}}=\dot{\eta}\frac{{F}_{+}^{D}}{\sqrt{{F}_{+}^{D}:{F}_{+}^{D}}},\eta \ge 0.$ **eq A2-4**

The derivative $\dot{D}$ is thus collinear with ${F}_{+}^{D}$ .

The thermodynamic force derives from the free energy [eq A2-2]:

${F}^{D}=-\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial D}=-\frac{\partial {\varphi}^{\text{el}}}{\partial D}-\frac{\partial {I}_{]-\infty 1]}(\max({\tilde{D}}_{i}))}{\partial D}$ **eq A2-5**

**Assumption:**

One supposes that the damage is equal to 1 in direction 1: ${\tilde{D}}_{1}=1$ and that it is different from 1 in the other directions. The proof would be similar in the case where the damage is equal to 1 in two orthogonal directions.

The derivative of the indicator function with respect to the damage is written:

$\frac{\partial I(\max({\tilde{D}}_{i}))}{\partial D}=\frac{\partial I({\tilde{D}}_{1})}{\partial D}=\frac{\partial I({\tilde{D}}_{1})}{\partial {\tilde{D}}_{1}}\frac{\partial {\tilde{D}}_{1}}{\partial {D}_{11}}\frac{\partial {D}_{11}}{\partial D}$         eq A2-6

Now one has: $\frac{\partial I({\tilde{D}}_{1})}{\partial {\tilde{D}}_{1}}=+\infty$ because ${\tilde{D}}_{1}=1$ ; ${(\frac{\partial {D}_{11}}{\partial D})}_{ij}={\delta}_{\mathrm{1i}}{\delta}_{\mathrm{1j}}$ ; $\frac{\partial {\tilde{D}}_{1}}{\partial {D}_{11}}=1$ because ${(\dot{D})}_{11}={\tilde{\dot{D}}}_{1}$ (see Appendix 1).

This allows us to write the derivative of the indicator with respect to the damage:

$-\frac{\partial I({\tilde{D}}_{i})}{\partial D}=(\begin{array}{ccc}T& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array})$ with $T=-\infty$ **eq A2-7**

The derivative of the elastic part of the energy has finite values. One writes it in the form of a symmetric 3x3 matrix:

$-\frac{\partial {\varphi}^{\text{el}}}{\partial D}=(\begin{array}{ccc}{a}_{11}& {a}_{12}& {a}_{13}\\ {a}_{12}& {a}_{22}& {a}_{23}\\ {a}_{13}& {a}_{23}& {a}_{33}\end{array})$ **eq A2-8**

This allows us to write the expression of the thermodynamic force:

> ${F}^{D}=(\begin{array}{ccc}T+{a}_{11}& {a}_{12}& {a}_{13}\\ {a}_{12}& {a}_{22}& {a}_{23}\\ {a}_{13}& {a}_{23}& {a}_{33}\end{array})$ **eq A2-9**

One now seeks to compute the positive part of the thermodynamic force. For this, one must compute the eigenvalues of ${F}^{D}$ and the associated eigenvectors. In order to carry out this computation conveniently, we consider the term $T$ as very large and negative (tending towards $-\infty$ ) but not strictly infinite.

One then has $\mid T\mid \text{>>}\mid {a}_{ij}\mid$ for all the indices $i,j$ . One can therefore write the matrix ${F}^{D}$ in the form:

${F}^{D}\approx T(\begin{array}{ccc}1& O(1/T)& O(1/T)\\ O(1/T)& O(1/T)& O(1/T)\\ O(1/T)& O(1/T)& O(1/T)\end{array})$ **eq A2-10**

Let $\lambda ` une valeur propre de :math:`{F}^{D}$ and $U$ the associated eigenvector; then the components of $U$ are solutions of the following system:

${F}^{D}.U=\lambdau \Rightarrow \lbrace \begin{array}{cc}{U}_{1}+O(1/T){U}_{2}+O(1/T){U}_{3}=\frac{\lambda}{T}{U}_{1}& (i)\\ O(1/T){U}_{1}+O(1/T){U}_{2}+O(1/T){U}_{3}=\frac{\lambda}{T}{U}_{2}& (ii)\\ O(1/T){U}_{1}+O(1/T){U}_{2}+O(1/T){U}_{3}=\frac{\lambda}{T}{U}_{3}& (\mathrm{iii})\end{array}$ **eq A2-11**

Among the eigenvectors, there exists at least one for which the component ${U}_{1}$ is non-zero, because the eigenvectors form a basis of ${R}^{3}$ . Let us consider this eigenvector. Then the equations of the system [eqA2-11] impose:

$\begin{array}{ccc}(i)& \Rightarrow & \lambda =T\\ (i)\text{et}(ii)& \Rightarrow & {U}_{2}=0\\ (i)\text{et}(\text{iii})& & {U}_{3}=0\end{array}$ **eq A2-12**

One deduces from this that $T$ is an eigenvalue of ${F}^{D}$ and that the vector **e1**  is the associated eigenvector.

Moreover, the principal basis of a symmetric matrix being orthogonal, the two other eigenvectors of ${F}^{D}$ are in the plane defined by ${e}_{2}\mathrm{et}{e}_{3}$ .

Thus the component ${U}_{1}$ of these eigenvectors is zero. Under these conditions, equation (i) supplies, in the limit $T\to -\infty$ , only the identity $0=0$ , and equations (ii) reduce, after multiplication by $T$ , to:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{a}_{22}{U}_{2}+{a}_{23}{U}_{3}={\mathrm{\lambda \; U}}_{2}\\ {a}_{23}{U}_{2}+{a}_{33}{U}_{3}={\mathrm{\lambda \; U}}_{3}\end{array}$ **eq A2-13**

Therefore the eigenvectors of ${F}^{D}$ distinct from ${e}_{1}$ and the associated eigenvalues are the eigenvectors and the eigenvalues of the projection ${a}_{\mathrm{2D}}=(\begin{array}{cc}{a}_{22}& {a}_{23}\\ {a}_{23}& {a}_{33}\end{array})$ of the matrix $a=(\begin{array}{ccc}{a}_{11}& {a}_{12}& {a}_{13}\\ {a}_{12}& {a}_{22}& {a}_{23}\\ {a}_{13}& {a}_{23}& {a}_{33}\end{array})$ in the plane $({e}_{2,}{e}_{3})$ .

It follows from what precedes that the positive part of ${F}^{D}$ is equal to:

${F}_{+}^{D}=(\begin{array}{ccc}0& 0& 0\\ 0& & \\ 0& & {({a}_{\mathrm{2D}})}_{+}\end{array})$ **eq A2-14**

Since $\dot{D}$ is collinear with ${\mathrm{F}}_{+}^{\mathrm{D}}$ , this implies that only the components ${D}_{22},{D}_{23},{D}_{33}$ can still evolve, the components ${D}_{11},{D}_{12},{D}_{13}$ being henceforth fixed.
