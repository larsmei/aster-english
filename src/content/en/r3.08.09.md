# r3.08.09 Multi-fibre beams in large displacements

**Summary:**

This document presents the treatment of the large displacements of the multi-fibre beam element POU_D_TGM activated by the keyword DEFORMATION='GROT_GDEP'.

It makes it possible to combine both material non-linearity (description of the cross-section in fibres) and geometric non-linearity. The description of the large displacements remains approximate and assumes small time steps.

This element is suited to the characterisation of the collapse of lattice structures.

Note 1: the treatment of large displacements for the other beam elements is described in [R3.08.01](doc:r3.08.01).

Note 2: The keyword RIGI_GEOM='OUI' makes it possible to deactivate the particular treatment of large displacements and is intended for writing the equilibrium of the beam on the deformed configuration.

## 1 Statement of the problem

In the field of the modelling of beam elements there exists a large number of theories to describe the kinematics (Euler-Bernoulli, Timoshenko, Vlassov, …) and *Code_Aster* naturally contains a counterpart of these theories in as many finite-element models (POU_D_E, POU_D_T, POU_D_TG).

Besides the assumptions on which they are based, these models can be enriched with a non-linear behaviour, whether of material origin (plasticity criterion for the beams, 1D behaviour based on a description by fibre) or geometric (large displacements and large three-dimensional rotations).

This document describes the element POU_D_TGM, which is based on a Timoshenko model with Vlassov restrained torsion. This element associates a description by fibre of the cross-section, in order to benefit from a non-linear 1D behaviour in tension-compression/bending, and a second-order kinematics in order to allow a description in large displacements and large rotations.

Attention will be concentrated on the geometric aspect, the multi-fibre behaviour being dealt with in detail in .

## 3 Presentation of the approach

### 3.1 Introduction

Taking large displacements into account, whether they result from a rigid-body motion or from an arbitrary transformation of the structure studied, introduces an additional non-linearity (in addition to that introduced by the constitutive non-linearity, for example). This non-linearity is reflected by the fact that the initial configuration of the structure and the final (or “deformed”) configuration can no longer be confused, as is usually the case for the treatment of problems in small perturbations.

For structural finite elements (*i.e.* beams, plates, shells), an additional problem arises: the rotations no longer have a vector character, and one can therefore no longer transform them as vectors when passing from the local frame to the global frame.

Indeed, when displacement vectors are expressed in a local frame of a finite element (the frame attached to the SEG2 of the beam, in which all the computations are carried out in order to simplify the writing of the various terms), it is entirely physical to transform them into a global frame attached to the structure. When one is interested in structural elements, these also carry rotational degrees of freedom; when these rotations are “small”, one can show that they are identified with vectors and therefore transform them as displacements.

This is no longer the case when the rotations are “large”, and this is reflected by the non-commutativity of the rotations. In the absence of a description taking this particularity into account, the continuity of the degrees of freedom at the nodes of the elements is then no longer ensured.

### 3.2 The geometrically exact element POU_D_T_GD

The beam finite element POU_D_T_GD, already integrated into the code for many years, models a Timoshenko beam in large displacements in the sense that the displacement field used in the formulation (when passing from 3D to beams) is written exactly. It takes account in particular of the exact rotation operator between two configurations of the element (that is to say that one makes no simplifying assumption on the displacements).

**The behaviour, which is elastic only, is always written in small strains; this element had in fact been introduced in order to treat “almost rigid” large displacements of a structure in dynamics (motion of the spacers between the conductors 1**

of an overhead line).

This element is formulated with a relatively complex Total Lagrangian approach, the exact treatment of large rotations requiring the use of the theory of quaternions in order to update the displacements correctly. The behaviour is formulated directly on the generalised forces (without passing through the stresses) and does not really lend itself to a multi-fibre extension which by nature works on the stresses 2
.

### 3.3 The multi-fibre element POU_D_TGM

It is a Timoshenko beam element with warping of the transverse cross-section and a multi-fibre approach in order to account for the progress of plasticity in the cross-section. This element already had a generic option 'PETIT_REAC' (now removed) capable of updating the geometry at each iteration of the step-by-step solution algorithm. This made it possible to treat problems in large displacements under the assumption of small rotations. However, the absence of geometric stiffness in the formulation makes convergence very difficult in the case of instability, even for plane problems. In addition, one does not exploit all the possibilities of the updating of the geometry (cf. 4.4.1).

### 3.4 A short history

It is from the 1970s that the geometrically non-linear analysis of structures formed of beams developed with the use of the finite-element method. The analysis was first directed at plane structures, then at three-dimensional structures with the founding work of Bathe in 1979. It is indeed at that time that a so-called “*Updated Lagrangian*” (UL) formulation appears for the first time, that is to say a formulation which updates the geometry of the structure at each iteration of the solution algorithm, making it possible to obtain a simplified formulation while remaining robust.

Subsequently many studies were inspired by those of Bathe and enriched them, in particular with the treatment of the thorny problem of large rotations highlighted by Argyris. One may also cite the work of Yang and McGuire as well as that of Conci.

Another important point in the non-linear analysis of structures is the need to estimate the residual well in order to obtain correct results. Now, in the case of beam elements, the geometric interpolation functions are of first order (the beam elements are not isoparametric). This leads to errors in the computation of the residual if the structure deforms a great deal and if one does not use enough elements.

One solution obviously consists in increasing the number of finite elements, especially for curved structures. However, this can become costly, and authors have therefore proposed so-called “*force recovery* 3
*”* techniques in order to circumvent this problem and make it possible to use only a single finite element per beam. Some, such as Conci, separated for this purpose the rigid-body motion and the motion producing a non-zero strain work. This approach gave rise to a new formulation which is also described as a “co-rotational” formulation.

Others undertook to correct the value of the residual by adding an additional term in the formulation.

### 3.5 Approach

The approach described here is based on an exhaustive bibliographical study and more particularly on the work of two theses on the non-linear analysis of structures formed of beams. The element retained is based on an Updated Lagrangian Formulation at each Iteration (FLAI) 4
and the rotations are assumed “moderate” per iteration, so that one can simply reduce to the case where the rotations are commutative up to second order (whence the term “moderate”, as opposed to “small”, *i.e.* of first order).

The approach presented in detail in the following section can be decomposed as follows:

1. As for all structural elements, assumptions are formulated on the kinematics making it possible to determine a three-dimensional displacement field at every point of the cross-section from the degrees of freedom considered.
2. The principle of virtual work is written within the framework of an Updated Lagrangian Formulation at each Iteration. One is in small strains.
3. The displacement field and the interpolation functions (which interpolate the generalised displacements as a function of the unknowns at the nodes) are introduced in order to obtain the expressions of the tangent matrix, which will be decomposed into several terms (each referring to the different phenomena that one seeks to take into account).
4. Taking large rotations of the structure into account rests on a modification of the initial displacement field, assuming the rotations moderate between two iterations.

The spacers are used to guarantee a minimum spacing between the conductors of a bundle

There exist other approaches for introducing plasticity, but with the power of present-day computers the multi-fibre approach seems the most suitable

literally to recover the force, that is to say to estimate the internal work of the structure

## 4 Theoretical description of the element

### 4.1 Displacement field and associated strain field

#### 4.1.1 Assumptions on the kinematics

The beam kinematics adopted is the following:

- The beam element is assumed straight, the cross-section constant.
- The transverse cross-section is undeformable in its plane but may warp axially
- The transverse shear strains are not neglected and produce a rotation of the cross-section about its axes
- The strains are small, but the displacements and the rotations may be large

One places oneself at the level of a finite element, that is to say of a segment with 2 nodes; a local frame of the element is defined by the axis of the segment and the principal axes of geometric inertia. The centre $O$ of the frame coincides with node no. 1 (which is the centre of gravity of the cross-section); one also defines the torsion (or shear) centre $C$ of the cross-section, where the application of a shear force produces no work in torsion and vice versa.

It is easy to show that if the axial displacements are expressed from the displacements of the point $O$ and the displacements in the plane of the cross-section from those of the point $C$, then all the forces are decoupled, that is to say that the behaviour matrix (for the beam elements, this is the matrix obtained after expressing Hooke’s law and integrating over the cross-section) is diagonal. The return to the global frame is then done in two stages: passage of all the displacements into the local frame of centre $O$, then passage from the local frame to the global frame.

#### 4.1.2 Expression of the displacement field

For such a cross-section, the displacement of a point $P$ of abscissa $x$, of coordinates $(y,z)$ in the frame of centre $O$, is expressed as the sum of a translation term and of a rotation term (assumption of undeformable plane sections).

In order to take account of the warping of the sections, a non-uniform axial displacement term is added. The displacement field $\overrightarrow{\xi}$ is then written:

The passage from the displacements at $C$ to those at $O$ is carried out by a simple change of frame. The function $\omega$, commonly called the warping function, and which depends only on the shape of the cross-section, makes it possible to describe the non-uniform axial displacement. The degrees of freedom of the element are carried at the nodes and are interpolated along the length of the element. There are 14 degrees of freedom, that is 7 at each node, which are: $(u,v,w,{\theta}_{x},{\theta}_{y},{\theta}_{z},{\theta}_{x,x})$ . One naturally finds 3 translational degrees of freedom and 3 rotational degrees of freedom to describe the kinematics; in addition the rate of twist ${\theta}_{x,x}$ is also taken as a measure of the warping.

#### 4.1.3 Associated strain field

Let us also compute the Green-Lagrange strains of the displacement field (); we already use the small-strain assumption. Indeed, consider the complete expression of the Green-Lagrange strains (the sections being assumed undeformable in their plane, half of the terms are already zero):

$$

$$

$$
E=\frac{1}{2}({}^{t}\mathrm{FF}-I)=\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{E}_{xx}& =& {u}_{,x}+\frac{1}{2}({{u}_{,x}}^{2}+{{v}_{,x}}^{2}+{{w}_{,x}}^{2})\\ 2{E}_{xy}& =& ({u}_{,y}+{v}_{,x})+({u}_{,x}{u}_{,y}+{w}_{,x}{w}_{,y})\\ 2{E}_{xz}& =& ({u}_{,z}+{w}_{,x})+({u}_{,x}{u}_{,z}+{v}_{,x}{v}_{,z})\end{array}

One can therefore simplify the complete expression of $E$ by neglecting the quadratic terms in $∣{u}_{,i}∣$ where $i=(x,y,z)$ . In these terms are not neglected; they then lead to a more complex expression of the tangent matrix and to an improved convergence. On the other hand they do not modify the accuracy of the results.

### 4.2 Principle of virtual work

#### 4.2.1 The assumptions used

In order to write the principle of virtual work (PVW), the assumption of an Updated Lagrangian Formulation at each Iteration (FLAI) will be made, that is to say that the geometry of the structure studied will be constantly updated. Locally this is reflected by the position of the nodes of the element, which changes at each iteration of the Newton algorithm, making it possible to compute a new transformation matrix from the local frame to the global frame.

**Let an element be in the initial configuration ${C}_{0}$ . One can describe the strains of the element from 3 configurations, the initial configuration**

therefore, as well as 2 other configurations: ${C}_{1}$, which denotes a known deformed configuration but not necessarily in equilibrium, and ${C}_{2}$, which denotes an unknown deformed configuration close to ${C}_{1}$ . The last known configuration is taken as the reference; the PVW will be written in this configuration.

In order to situate oneself in the solution algorithm, one can for example imagine that ${C}_{0}$ denotes the configuration at the beginning of the time steps $(t=0)$ . In addition a first iteration has already been carried out, so one has determined ${C}_{1}$, which is not in equilibrium. A new iteration will therefore be carried out, choosing ${C}_{1}$ as the reference and writing the PVW in an unknown configuration, and so on... until a configuration in equilibrium is found and one then passes to the following time step.

Illustration 4.1: The different reference configurations.

A first remark can be made: the supports of the beam elements in *Code_Aster* are segments with two nodes (SEG2); consequently their geometric interpolation functions are linear. One will therefore never be able, with these elements, to reproduce exactly the “last known configuration”; this represents an approximation and one must therefore take care to have a sufficiently fine mesh for the problems where the curvature of the elements becomes non-negligible. The solution which consists in correcting the residual or in using a co-rotational formulation has not been implemented, because it would have required profound modifications of the code
.

The FLAI presents many advantages; for example one will be able to identify the Cauchy stress tensor (expressed on the final configuration at the previous iteration) and the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (expressed on the initial configuration at the current iteration), because between the two iterations the geometry will have been updated:

$$
S=\det(F){F}^{-1}\sigma {{}^{t}F}^{-1}=\sigma
$$

This means that in the reference configuration (that which in particular makes it possible to express the virtual strains) the initial displacements already acquired by the element will always be zero, because the last computed configuration will be taken as the reference. Only the initial stress state in a given configuration will in general be non-zero.

As regards the behaviour, it is written incrementally and it is expressed in Lagrangian quantities thanks to the Piola-Kirchhoff tensor $\mathrm{II}S$ and to the Green-Lagrange strains $E$ . The small strains make it possible to linearise the constitutive law and consequently:

$$
\Delta S=C:\Delta E\approx C:\Delta \epsilon
$$

For beam elements, the assumption of plane stresses in the cross-section is made. This amounts to supposing that the beam is constituted of longitudinal fibres working in tension-compression.

This assumption (${\sigma}_{yy}={\sigma}_{zz}={\sigma}_{yz}=0$ ) makes it possible to express the constitutive law in the case of beams in the form:

$$
C:\Delta \epsilon =\lbrace \begin{array}{c}E\delta {\epsilon}_{xx}\\ \mathrm{2G}\delta {\epsilon}_{xy}\\ \mathrm{2G}\delta {\epsilon}_{xz}\end{array}
$$

Finally, the assumption of a displacement field with moderate rotations will be legitimate in order to obtain large rotations between steps and for the total loading. Indeed, the FLAI means that, from the beginning of a step and at the end of each iteration, the computed increments of displacements and of stresses will be summed. The total increment of displacement and of rotation over a step may then be large.

#### 4.2.2 Writing of the PVW and differentiation

The principle of virtual work, which is the weak formulation of the laws of continuum mechanics, is written:

$$
{\int}_{{C}_{2}}\sigma :{\epsilon}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{dV}={\int}_{{C}_{2}}\overrightarrow{f}.\overrightarrow{{\xi}^{\text{*}}}\mathrm{dV}+{\int}_{\partial {C}_{2}}\overrightarrow{t}.\overrightarrow{{\xi}^{\text{*}}}\mathrm{dS}
$$

where all the quantities are expressed on the unknown deformed configuration; $\sigma$ is the Cauchy stress tensor. In addition $\overrightarrow{{\xi}^{\text{*}}}$ denotes a virtual displacement field that is kinematically admissible in the sense of the boundary conditions.

In order to solve such a problem, one must first change configuration by means of a change of variable. The principle of virtual work written in Lagrangian quantities (in the reference configuration, therefore, and not the deformed configuration) is expressed by means of the virtual Green-Lagrange strains ${E}^{\text{*}}$ and of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor (PKII) $S$ **.**

$$
{W}_{int}={\int}_{{C}_{1}}S:{E}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{dV}={\int}_{{C}_{1}}\overrightarrow{f}.\overrightarrow{{\xi}^{\text{*}}}\mathrm{dV}+{\int}_{\partial {C}_{1}}\overrightarrow{t}.\overrightarrow{{\xi}^{\text{*}}}\mathrm{dS}
$$

where $\overrightarrow{f}$ represents the body-force density, $\overrightarrow{t}$ the surface-force density. In what follows, ${W}_{\mathrm{ext}}$ will denote the right-hand side, and it will be assumed that the loading is conservative and not a follower, so that the work of the external forces is constant over a time step.

The PKII stress tensor expresses the same quantity as the Cauchy stress tensor but taking the initial configuration as the reference. The virtual Green-Lagrange strain tensor is written:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cccc}& {E}^{\text{*}}& =& \frac{1}{2}({}^{t}{F}^{\text{*}}F+{}^{t}\mathrm{FF}^{\text{*}})\\ \text{avec}& F& =& I+\frac{\partial \overrightarrow{{\xi}^{\text{*}}}}{\partial \overrightarrow{x}}\\ \text{et}& {F}^{\text{*}}& =& \frac{\partial \overrightarrow{{\xi}^{\text{*}}}}{\partial \overrightarrow{x}}\end{array}
$$

In order to solve the equilibrium one must check  for every virtual displacement kinematically admissible to zero $\overrightarrow{{\xi}^{\text{*}}}$ . The Newton method is applied to the functional ${W}_{int}-{W}_{\mathrm{ext}}$ and, for this, the functional is differentiated with respect to the unknown $\overrightarrow{\xi}$ ; this makes it possible to transform the solution of a non-linear problem (${W}_{int}$ does not depend linearly on $\overrightarrow{\xi}$ ) into a sequence of solutions of linear systems.

One therefore solves:

$$
{W}_{\mathrm{ext}}={W}_{int}^{1}+{\int}_{{C}_{1}}(\Delta S:{E}^{\text{*}}+S:\Delta {E}^{\text{*}})\mathrm{dV}
$$

where ${W}_{int}^{1}$ represents the work already equilibrated in configuration 1. The principle is to equilibrate this work with that of the internal forces ${W}_{\mathrm{ext}}$ by means of successive iterations.

The first term under the integral models the material stiffness. Indeed, since the constitutive law is linearised and since the reference configuration is the last known configuration (hence $\overrightarrow{\xi}=\overrightarrow{0}$
), one can write, using  and :

$$
{\int}_{{C}_{1}}\Delta S:{E}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{dV}={\int}_{{C}_{1}}\Delta \epsilon :C:{\epsilon}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{dV}
$$

The FLAI in fact makes it possible here to dispense with an additional term which couples non-linear virtual strains and linear strains and which is called the initial-displacement term (these displacements are the displacements already undergone by our structure with respect to its reference configuration, and they are always zero here). In fact, thanks to the FLAI, the change of geometry and the non-linearity which follows from it are transferred into the local-global change of frame specific to the finite-element method.

The second term under the integral models the geometric stiffness of the structure. It is thanks to this term that one will be able to represent the second-order effects and to converge rapidly towards the solution. This term indeed involves the non-linear strains directly, since according to , $\Delta {E}^{\text{*}}=\Delta {\eta}^{\text{*}}$ . If the displacement field moreover contains non-linear terms for taking moderate rotations into account, then of course the non-linear strains also contain an additional term.

It remains to remark that the Cauchy and PKII stress tensors are identified, because the initial and final configurations are coincident following the updating.

#### 4.2.3 Summary

One therefore has:

$$
\Delta W={\int}_{{C}_{1}}(\Delta \epsilon :C:{\epsilon}^{\text{*}}+\sigma :\Delta {\eta}^{\text{*}})\mathrm{dV}={W}_{\mathrm{ext}}-{W}_{int}^{1}
$$

Physically, everything happens as if the residual ${W}_{\mathrm{ext}}-{W}_{int}^{1}$ is dissipated iteration after iteration by a work $\Delta W$ due in part to the deformation of the material (material stiffness) and in part to the large displacements of the structure (geometric stiffness). The geometric stiffness has a meaning as soon as the structure undergoes large displacements.

- The material-stiffness term is a classical term which is already present in the current formulation of the POU_D_TGM elements. It can possibly represent, through the multi-fibre approach, the material non-linearity (plasticity, for example).
- The geometric-stiffness term which one usually finds in large displacements is new (in the updated reference configuration, there exists in general a non-zero stress state). It therefore represents the geometric non-linearity, and it is thanks, among other things, to this term, omitted in the PETIT_REAC formulation, that convergence will be faster or even at last possible in the case of instabilities.
- The second term on the right-hand side is the work of the internal forces at the previous iteration; it is its exact computation which guarantees convergence towards a correct result, which is why an improvement in its computation will be proposed.

It must be carefully noted that in the geometric-stiffness term the unknown appears through the virtual strains; indeed, the latter depend linearly on the unknowns.

### 4.3 Determination of the tangent matrix

#### 4.3.1 Reminder on the discretisation

It is now a matter of determining the tangent matrix, which is the combination of the various terms cited above. For this, the finite-element discretisation must be introduced, in particular the interpolation functions, and then the volume integrals which appear in  must be made explicit.

For structural elements such as beams, this discretisation is carried out in two stages: on the one hand the integration over the cross-sections located at the Gauss points, and on the other hand the integration along the length. Since a multi-fibre behaviour has been chosen for the treatment of plasticity, the integration in the cross-section is done thanks to the fibres for the axial behaviour (normal force, bending moments) and by means of constitutive laws in generalised forces for the rest of the forces.

Integrals over the cross-section are computed at each Gauss point (of the beam element), because it is the quantities at these points which will then make it possible to integrate numerically along the length (by means of Gauss quadrature formulae). The generalised strains are then expressed by means of a matrix of interpolation functions. The numerical integration then makes it possible to determine a tangent matrix: one will therefore seek here only to express the matrix after integration over the cross-section, because it is its expression which will be necessary for the implementation in the code, the numerical integration being transparent.

#### 4.3.2 Expression of the material stiffness

We can make the material-stiffness term explicit, by first making the constitutive law  explicit, then by replacing the linearised strains $\epsilon$ by their expression  and finally by integrating over the cross-section, thus keeping only a length integral.

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}{\int}_{{C}_{1}}\Delta \epsilon :C:{\epsilon}^{\text{*}}\mathrm{dV}& =& {\int}_{{C}_{1}}(E\delta {\epsilon}_{xx}{\epsilon}_{xx}^{\text{*}}+\mathrm{2G}\delta {\epsilon}_{xy}{\epsilon}_{xy}^{\text{*}}+\mathrm{2G}\delta {\epsilon}_{xz}{\epsilon}_{xz}^{\text{*}})\mathrm{dV}\\ & =& {\int}_{{L}_{1}}({}^{t}(\Delta {D}_{s}){K}_{s}{D}_{s}^{\text{*}})\mathrm{dl}\end{array}
$$

where one has: ${D}_{s}=BU$ , $B$ being the interpolation matrix of the generalised strains $({u}_{O,x},({v}_{C,x}-{\theta}_{z}),({w}_{C,x}+{\theta}_{y}),{\theta}_{x,x},{\theta}_{y,x},{\theta}_{z,x},{\theta}_{x,xx})$ and $U$ the vector of the nodal unknowns.

${K}_{s}$ is a behavioural matrix which represents both the characteristics of the material and the geometric characteristics of the cross-section (whence the appearance of the constants ${S}_{y}^{'},{S}_{z}^{'},J$ and ${I}_{\omega}$ referring respectively to the reduced cross-sections, to the torsion constant and to the warping constant):

$$
{K}_{s}=(\begin{array}{ccccccc}{\int}_{S}E\mathrm{ds}& 0& 0& 0& {\int}_{S}\mathrm{Ez}\mathrm{ds}& -{\int}_{S}\mathrm{Ey}\mathrm{ds}& 0\\ & {\mathrm{GS}}_{y}^{'}& 0& 0& 0& 0& 0\\ & & {\mathrm{GS}}_{z}^{'}& 0& 0& 0& 0\\ & & & \mathrm{GJ}& 0& 0& 0\\ & & & & {\int}_{S}{\mathrm{Ez}}^{2}\mathrm{ds}& -{\int}_{S}\mathrm{Eyz}\mathrm{ds}& 0\\ & \mathrm{sym}& & & & {\int}_{S}{\mathrm{Ey}}^{2}\mathrm{ds}& 0\\ & & & & & & {\mathrm{EI}}_{\omega}\end{array})
$$

$$
B=(\begin{array}{cccccccccccccc}-\frac{1}{L}& 0& 0& 0& 0& 0& 0& \frac{1}{L}& 0& 0& 0& 0& 0& 0\\ 0& {\phi }_{1}& 0& 0& 0& {\phi }_{2}& 0& 0& {\phi }_{3}& 0& 0& 0& {\phi }_{4}& 0\\ 0& 0& {\psi}_{1}& 0& {\psi}_{2}& 0& 0& 0& 0& {\psi}_{3}& 0& {\psi}_{4}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0& {N}_{1,x}& 0& 0& {N}_{2,x}& 0& 0& 0& {N}_{3,x}& 0& 0& {N}_{4,x}\\ 0& 0& {\xi}_{5,x}& 0& {\xi}_{6,x}& 0& 0& 0& 0& {\xi}_{7,x}& 0& {\xi}_{8,x}& 0& 0\\ 0& -{\xi}_{5,x}& 0& 0& 0& {\xi}_{6,x}& 0& 0& -{\xi}_{7,x}& 0& 0& 0& {\xi}_{8,x}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0& {N}_{1,xx}& 0& 0& {N}_{2,xx}& 0& 0& 0& {N}_{3,xx}& 0& 0& {N}_{4,xx}\end{array})
$$

$$

The surface integrals which still appear in the expression of ${K}_{s}$ refer to the terms computed thanks to the mesh of the transverse cross-section in fibres. Except in the elastic case, the off-diagonal terms are in general not zero, because Young’s modulus $E$ is then no longer homogeneous in the cross-section.

The expressions of the interpolation functions ${\xi}_{i=(1,\dots ,8)},{N}_{j=(1,\dots ,4)}$ are given in .

One may remark that the approach chosen in order to determine the material-stiffness matrix is not that adopted in *Code_Aster* for the Euler beam elements POU_D_E and the Timoshenko elements POU_D_T. Indeed, in our work, the approach is derived from the laws of 3D continuum mechanics and one gives oneself for this a 3D displacement field, which allows us to introduce the large displacements by writing the PVW in Lagrangian quantities.

In order to formulate the Euler and Timoshenko beam elements under the assumption of elastic small perturbations, the approach used in *Code_Aster* consists in writing directly the fundamental principle of dynamics on elementary beam segments, then in writing a weak formulation of it by means of test functions, and finally in introducing constitutive laws in generalised forces (of the type $N=\mathrm{ES}\epsilon$ ). One then observes that, by choosing the test functions well, it is possible to express the work developed in the test field solely as a function of the unknowns at the nodes, this without at any moment having discretised the unknowns.

Thus, when one works in elastic small perturbations, it may be more advantageous to use these exact elements rather than the element presented in this work, which integrates the stiffness matrix numerically by Gauss quadrature.

#### 4.3.3 Expression of the geometric stiffness

In the same way, one can make the geometric stiffness explicit. For this, each component $\Delta {\eta}_{xi}^{\text{*}}$ of $\Delta {\eta}^{\text{*}}$ can be put in the form of a pseudo-quadratic form, *i.e.* ${}^{t}C{L}_{xi}{C}^{\text{*}}$ where ${L}_{xi}$ is a matrix which does not depend on the displacements and $C$ a vector depending on the generalised unknowns and on their derivatives: ${}^{t}C=({\theta}_{x},{v}_{C,x},{w}_{C,x},{\theta}_{x,x})$ . With obvious notation, one deduces:

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}{\int}_{{C}_{1}}\sigma :\Delta {\eta}^{\text{*}}\mathit{dV}& =& {\int}_{{L}_{1}}\left({}^{t}\Delta C\underset{{R}_{s}}{\underset{⏟}{\left({\int}_{{S}_{1}}\left({L}_{xx}{\sigma}_{xx}+2{L}_{xy}{\sigma}_{xy}+2{L}_{xz}{\sigma}_{xz}\right)\mathit{dS}\right)}}{C}^{\text{*}}\right)\mathit{dl}\\ & =& {\int}_{{L}_{1}}\left({}^{t}(\Delta C){R}_{s}{C}^{\text{*}}\right)\mathit{dl}\end{array}
$$

The integration along the length is also carried out by interpolating the generalised unknowns, the unknowns becoming the degrees of freedom at the nodes. Thus one replaces $C$ by ${B}_{\sigma}U$ . Below, the expression of the interpolation matrix ${B}_{\sigma}$ and of the matrix ${R}_{s}$ is given:

$$
{R}_{s}=(\begin{array}{cccc}0& -{T}_{z}& {T}_{y}& \frac{1}{2}{K}_{,x}\\ & {N}_{x}& 0& -{M}_{y}+{z}_{C}{N}_{x}\\ & & {N}_{x}& -{M}_{z}-{y}_{C}{N}_{x}\\ \mathrm{sym}& & & K\end{array})
$$

$$
{B}_{\sigma}=(\begin{array}{cccccccccccccc}0& 0& 0& {N}_{1}& 0& 0& {N}_{2}& 0& 0& 0& {N}_{3}& 0& 0& {N}_{4}\\ 0& {\xi}_{1,x}& 0& 0& 0& -{\xi}_{2,x}& 0& 0& {\xi}_{3,x}& 0& 0& 0& -{\xi}_{4,x}& 0\\ 0& 0& {\xi}_{1,x}& 0& {\xi}_{2,x}& 0& 0& 0& 0& {\xi}_{3,x}& 0& {\xi}_{4,x}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0& {N}_{1,x}& 0& 0& {N}_{2,x}& 0& 0& 0& {N}_{3,x}& 0& 0& {N}_{4,x}\end{array})
$$

In the geometric-stiffness matrix there appear the generalised forces (normal force $N$ *,* shear forces ${T}_{y}$ and ${T}_{z}$ , bending moments ${M}_{y}$ and ${M}_{z}$ ) as well as geometric characteristics of the cross-section. The coefficient $K$ is called the Wagner coefficient and is written:

$$
K={N}_{x}\left(\frac{{I}_{y}+{I}_{z}}{S}+{y}_{C}^{2}+{z}_{C}^{2}\right)+{M}_{y}\left(\frac{{I}_{\mathit{zr2}}}{{I}_{y}}-2{z}_{C}\right)-{M}_{z}\left(\frac{{I}_{\mathit{yr2}}}{{I}_{z}}-2{y}_{C}\right)+{M}_{\omega}\frac{{I}_{\omega \mathit{r2}}}{{I}_{\omega}}
$$

The non-trivial section characteristics are:

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}{I}_{\mathrm{yr2}}& =& {\int}_{S}y({y}^{2}+{z}^{2})\mathrm{dS}\\ {I}_{\mathrm{zr2}}& =& {\int}_{S}z({y}^{2}+{z}^{2})\mathrm{dS}\\ {I}_{\omega \mathrm{r2}}& =& {\int}_{S}\omega (y,z)({y}^{2}+{z}^{2})\mathrm{dS}\end{array}
$$

These characteristics are in general non-zero for sections which do not present a double symmetry; this is the case, for example, of the angles equipping lattice supports.

The last term in the Wagner coefficient is not taken into account in the code, because one does not in general have the expression of the warping function $\omega$ in order to compute ${I}_{\omega \mathrm{r2}}$ .

#### 4.3.4 Expression of the correction matrix for taking large rotations of the structure into account

In order to take large rotations of the structure into account, one must express the correction matrix brought to the geometric stiffness by the addition of the non-linear strain field $\Delta {\eta}_{\mathrm{gr}}$ . The detailed approach of the derivation is given in or in . The computations are not presented here, because they are long and tedious. Other authors have obtained the same correction matrix from considerations on the nature of the bending moments and of the torsional moment.

**It is interesting to remark that the only modification brought by taking large rotations into account concerns an addition to the tangent matrix. The residual is unchanged, that is to say that without taking the large rotations into account, one does not risk obtaining wrong results**

but rather of encountering convergence difficulties when one treats certain problems.

Indeed, the problem of large rotations in the sense of size is not strictly speaking an obstacle: for plane problems, it is absolutely not necessary to use the correction matrix (more exactly, its contribution cancels itself). The real problem lies in the transformation of the rotations during a change of frame. Now, when the rotations are infinitesimal, one can replace them by their expansion to first order and then they are identified with vectors, which makes it possible to transform them as displacements when passing from the local frame to the global frame. On the other hand, when they are large, this is no longer possible, but one risks encountering convergence problems only for precise cases where the continuity of the rotations at the nodes is lost.

Thus the problem arises for structures presenting a bending-torsion coupling and non-collinear finite elements.

The tangent stiffness matrix is therefore enriched with a correction term ${K}_{c}$ following the taking into account of moderate rotations in the expression of the displacements:

$$
{K}_{c}=(\begin{array}{ccccc}{\left[0\right]}_{3\times 3}& & & & \\ & -{\left[{k}_{c}^{1}\right]}_{3\times 3}& & (0)& \\ & & {\left[0\right]}_{4\times 4}& & \\ & (0)& & {\left[{k}_{c}^{2}\right]}_{3\times 3}& \\ & & & & 0\end{array})
$$

$$
\textrm{avec}
{\left[{k}_{c}^{i}\right]}_{3\times 3}=\frac{1}{2}(\begin{array}{ccc}0& -{M}_{z}^{i}& {M}_{y}^{i}\\ -{M}_{z}^{i}& 0& 0\\ {M}_{y}^{i}& 0& 0\end{array})
$$

### 4.4 Computation of the residual

In order to complete the model, two improvements suggested by the thesis work and intended to improve the accuracy of the results are presented in what follows.

The computation of the residual, and more particularly of the work of the internal forces ${W}_{int}^{1}$ , is carried out by numerical integration after having integrated the constitutive law:

$$
{W}_{int}^{1}={\int}_{{C}_{1}}\sigma :\epsilon \mathrm{dV}=({\int}_{{L}_{1}}{}^{t}QBU\mathrm{dl})
$$

$Q$ is a vector containing the 7 generalised forces and $B$ the interpolation matrix of the generalised strains already given in , and which makes it possible to express the strain increments associated with the nodal displacements $U$ *.*

The improvements proposed concern respectively the expression of the constitutive law in torsion, hence $Q$ , and the computation of the linearised strains $\epsilon$ . They must allow a better accuracy but also an acceleration of the convergence.

#### 4.4.1 Computation of the strain increment

On the one hand, it seems interesting to take account of the updating of the geometry at each iteration; indeed, one can then compute more cleverly the strain increment since the previous step. Instead of computing the strain increment from the displacement increment of the step, one can compute the strain due to the displacement of the last Newton iteration and accumulate it at each iteration. Since the displacement between two iterations tends towards zero, one is more accurate.

Indeed, if $\delta {u}_{i}$ denotes the displacement increment consecutive to iteration $i$ of the step-by-step solution algorithm and if $\epsilon (u)$ denotes the generalised-strain increment associated with a displacement increment $u$, then at iteration $n$ *,* one proceeds as follows:

$$
\Delta U=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\delta {u}_{i}
$$

$$
\textrm{au lieu d'écrire:}
\Delta \epsilon =\epsilon (\Delta U)
$$

$$
\textrm{on écrira plutôt:}
\Delta \epsilon =\sum_{i=0}^{n}\epsilon (\delta {u}_{i})
$$

This requires a little more memory, because one must be able to store the vector of the strain increments $\epsilon (\delta {u}_{i})$ ; however, this computation technique is more relevant when the geometry is updated at each iteration.

One therefore adds to the torsional moment the so-called “Wagner moment”. Its computation involves the Wagner coefficient $K$ already cited in .

## 5 Implementation in Code_Aster

### 5.1 Use

One accesses the treatment of large displacements for the multi-fibre beams POU_D_TGM by selecting the strain 'GROT_GDEP' under COMPORTEMENT.

All the non-linear 1D behaviours are available.

### 5.2 Development

#### 5.2.1 Correction matrix

The expression of the correction matrix due to the taking into account of moderate rotations is given in . It appears at the end of the computation of the tangent stiffness, since it presents itself in the form of a fully integrated term. The only obstacle concerns the bending moments which constitute this fully integrated term; indeed, these are the bending moments at the nodes of the element, whereas one knows them only at the Gauss points (following the integration of the constitutive law).

It is therefore either possible to use the computation of ${W}_{int}^{1}$ in order to extract the moments from it, or else to interpolate the values at the nodes by means of a polynomial of order 2. It is this latter solution which has been chosen, because at the beginning of each time step it is not possible to extract these values from the work, because the latter is not computed (at the beginning of each time step, only a tangent stiffness matrix in rate form is assembled for the prediction phase).

Once the values have been interpolated, one merely corrects the geometric-stiffness matrix. This is done just after the end of the loop over the Gauss points.

#### 5.2.2 Improved computation of the strains

As has been pointed out, this computation is relevant only when one updates the geometry of the structure at each iteration.

In order to be able to carry out this new computation, one must be able to store the strain increment at each Gauss point and after each iteration (it is a vector of dimension 7). This information is stored in the field specific to the structural elements STRX_ELGA.

This field is also used to store the generalised forces, because the data of the stress field by fibres alone cannot suffice to recover the shear forces, the torsional moment and the bimoment.

## 6 Validation

The validation of the new element is carried out in 3 stages. First one compares the results obtained on test cases having analytical solutions (essentially plane), then on academic test cases (3D) and finally on experimental results.

### 6.1 Analytical test cases

#### 6.1.1 Beam in buckling (SSNL502)

This test represents a stability analysis of a cantilever beam subjected to a compressive force at one end. It makes it possible to validate the modelling in the non-linear quasi-static domain in large displacements and in the presence of instability (Euler buckling).

#### 6.1.2 Cantilever plate subjected to a moment (SSNV138)

This test constitutes the quasi-static analysis of an elastic plate clamped on one side and subjected to a bending moment on the other side, leading to large displacements in the plane.

#### 6.1.3 Equilibrium in the deformed configuration of a multi-fibre beam (SSLL109)

This test represents an equilibrium analysis in the deformed configuration of a multi-fibre beam. This test validates the option RIGI_GEOM=’OUI’.

### 6.2 Academic test cases

#### 6.2.1 Large displacements of the arc with an opening angle of 45° (SSNL136)

In this test, one studies a plane arc of 45° opening, clamped at one end and subjected to a bending force perpendicular to its plane at the other end. This very severe test makes it possible to validate the multi-fibre beam element in the geometrically non-linear domain of large displacements and large rotations.

#### 6.2.2 Lateral-torsional buckling of an angle strip (SSNL133)

An L-shaped structure composed of two slender beams of thin rectangular cross-section is subjected to a force at one end and is clamped at the other end. The domain of the test is that of non-linear elastic mechanics in large displacements and large rotations, with an instability of the beam lateral-torsional buckling type.

#### 6.2.3 Elastoplastic collapse of the Lee frame (SSNL134)

The purpose of this test is to validate simultaneously the material and geometric non-linear possibilities of the multi-fibre beam element. The element is applied on a test case commonly treated in the literature as regards the elastic behaviour, because it presents a complex response with *snap-back* and *snap-through:* it is the Lee frame. An elastoplastic behaviour of the frame is assumed here, which makes it possible to test the correct integration of the constitutive law of the multi-fibre elements but also the correct treatment of the large displacements.

### 6.3 Experimental validation

#### 6.3.1 The MEKELEC cantilever (SSNL135)

MEKELEC denotes a structure built in 1991 from a cantilever of a P4T pylon. The dimensions and the profiles used were adapted to the carrying out of tests. The wish at the time was to establish a base of precise experimental results with a view to validating the results of the simulations.

The validation consists in comparing the experimental and numerical collapse loads of 3 loading cases.
