# r5.05.03 Harmonic response

**Summary:**

This document presents the theoretical foundations of the computation of the steady-state regime of the response of a complex mechanical system, with linear behaviour, subjected to a harmonic dynamic excitation. The computation is carried out equally well directly on the system modelled by finite elements, or on the system represented by a modal basis; in the latter case, if the modal basis is the product of the substructuring technique, one will refer to the document [R4.06.03](doc:r4.06.03).

> **Contents**

## 1 Introduction

In harmonic problems, the system studied is subjected to an excitation varying as the product of an arbitrary function of space by a sinusoidal function of time.

Seeking the response consists in computing the field of the quantities represented by the degrees of freedom of the finite-element modelling of the system. When the system has a linear behaviour, the response of the field of the observed quantities tends rapidly (because of the dying-out of its transient component by internal dissipation) towards a steady-state regime: the resulting field finally varies harmonically like the excitation. It is this steady-state regime of the response which one proposes to compute.

## 2 Harmonic equation

We establish the dynamic equation in the case of a harmonic excitation for three kinds of mechanical systems:

- pure structures (without fluid),
- pure fluids (without structure) with a linear acoustic-type behaviour,
- mixed structure and fluid systems in fluid–structure interaction.

### 2.1 Harmonic equation of structures

The vibratory behaviour of a pure structure results from the external forces which are applied to it. The quantity to be computed is the displacement at every point $P$ of the model.

#### 2.1.1 Direct computation of elastic structures

In the case of the direct computation on the finite-element model we may write:

$$
\mathrm{M}\ddot{\mathrm{u}}+\mathrm{C}\dot{\mathrm{u}}+\mathrm{K}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}\mathrm{u}={\mathrm{f}}_{\text{ext}}(P,t)
$$

where $\mathrm{M}$ is the (real) mass matrix, $C$ is the (real) damping matrix, $K$ is the (real) stiffness matrix, ${\mathrm{f}}_{\text{ext}}(P,t)$ is the (complex) vector of the field of external forces applied to $S$ . Finally $\mathrm{u}$ , $\dot{\mathrm{u}}$ and $\ddot{\mathrm{u}}$ are the (complex) displacement, velocity and acceleration vectors, functions of $P$ and $t$ .

In a harmonic problem, one imposes a dynamic loading, arbitrary in space, but sinusoidal in time. One is then interested in the stabilised response of the system, without taking the transient part into account. The field of external forces is written:

$$
{\mathrm{f}}_{\text{ext}}(P,t)=\lbrace {\mathrm{f}}_{\text{ext}}(P)\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}
$$

The displacement field is written:

$$
\mathrm{u}(P,t)=\lbrace \mathrm{u}(P)\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}
$$

The velocity and acceleration fields are written by time differentiation of the displacements:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\dot{\mathrm{u}}(P,t)=j\omega \lbrace \mathrm{u}(P)\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}\\ \ddot{\mathrm{u}}(P,t)=-{\omega}^{2}\lbrace \mathrm{u}(P)\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}\end{array}
$$

Finally the structure $S$ satisfies the following equation:

$$
(\mathrm{K}+j\omega \mathrm{C}-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{M})\lbrace u\rbrace =\lbrace {\mathrm{f}}_{\text{ext}}(P)\rbrace
$$

#### 2.1.2 Computation on an elastic-structure modal basis

The computation of the harmonic response by the modal-synthesis method consists in seeking the unknown displacement field, coming from the finite-element modelling, on an appropriate space of reduced dimension (Ritz transformation). One will refer to the documents [R4.06.02](doc:r4.06.02) and [R4.06.03](doc:r4.06.03).

If one uses this method instead, equation () is projected onto the modal basis of $S$ and one arrives at the following harmonic equation:

$$
(\overline{\mathrm{K}}+j\omega \overline{C}-{\omega}^{2}\overline{\mathrm{M}})\lbrace \eta \rbrace =\lbrace {\overline{\mathrm{f}}}_{\text{ext}}\rbrace
$$

where $\overline{\mathrm{M}}={\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{M}\Phi ` is the (real) generalised mass matrix, :math:$overline{mathrm{C}}={Phi}^{mathrm{T}}mathrm{C}Phi ` is the (real) generalised damping matrix, $\overline{\mathrm{K}}={\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathrm{K}\Phi ` is the (real) generalised stiffness matrix and :math:$lbrace {overline{mathrm{f}}}_{text{ext}}rbrace ={Phi}^{mathrm{T}}lbrace {mathrm{f}}_{text{ext}}rbrace` is the (complex) vector of the applied generalised harmonic external forces. One has denoted by $\Phi ` the (real) matrix of the modal vectors of the Ritz basis, :math:$lbrace eta (P)rbrace` is the (complex) vector of the generalised harmonic displacements.

Once $\lbrace \eta (P)\rbrace$ has been determined by () one carries out a restitution on the physical basis (cf. [R4.06.02](doc:r4.06.02)).

#### 2.1.3 Direct computation of viscoelastic structures

The constitutive law of a linear viscoelastic material is of the type $L\sigma =Mϵ$ where $L\mathit{et}M$ are linear differential operators in the time variable (see [R5.05.04](doc:r5.05.04)). In the harmonic domain, this constitutive law becomes of the type $\sigma ={H}_{c}(\omega )ϵ$ where ${H}_{c}(\omega )$ is a complex tensor depending on the angular frequency. The discretised equation of the structure is then written, in the harmonic regime:

$$
({\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\omega )+j{\mathrm{K}}_{i}(\omega )-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{M})\lbrace u\rbrace =\lbrace {\mathrm{f}}_{\text{ext}}(P)\rbrace
$$

One denotes by ${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}}={\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\omega )+j{\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{r}}(\omega )$ the complex stiffness matrix depending on the frequency.

**Particular case**: if the damping is of **hysteretic** “global” type, the constitutive law is written $\sigma =(1+j\eta )Hϵ$ where $H$ is the real Hooke tensor and $\eta$ is a global loss coefficient (cf. [R5.05.04](doc:r5.05.04)). Equation () is then written:

$$
((1+j\eta )\mathrm{K}-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{M})\lbrace u\rbrace =\lbrace {\mathrm{f}}_{\text{ext}}(P)\rbrace
$$

One notes that ${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{c}}=\mathrm{K}+j\eta \mathrm{K}$ is a *complex* stiffness matrix, which no longer depends on the frequency.

### 2.2 Harmonic equation of acoustic fluids

The document [R4.02.01](doc:r4.02.01) describes the finite-element modelling of a fluid system (without transport) having a linear acoustic behaviour.

The fluid system $F$ undergoes a harmonic excitation of acoustic velocity on part of its boundary. The harmonic response is described by the following equation, in which the quantity to be computed is the acoustic pressure at every point $P$ of the model.

$$
(\mathrm{K}+j\omega \mathrm{C}-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{M})\lbrace \mathrm{p}(P)\rbrace =-j\omega \lbrace {\mathrm{v}}_{\mathrm{n}}(P)\rbrace
$$

where $\mathrm{M}$ is the (complex) acoustic “mass” matrix, $\mathrm{C}$ is the (complex) acoustic “damping” matrix (and in this instance that of the boundary ${\partial}_{z}F$ where one applies an acoustic impedance) and $\mathrm{K}$ is the (complex) acoustic “stiffness” matrix.

One denotes by ${\mathrm{v}}_{\mathrm{n}}(P,t)=\lbrace {\mathrm{v}}_{\mathrm{n}}(P)\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}$ the velocity, with $\lbrace {V}_{n}(P)\rbrace$ the (complex) vector of the field of normal acoustic velocities applied to the boundary ${\partial}_{v}F$ of $F$ where one applies acoustic velocities, and by $\mathrm{p}(P,t)=\lbrace \mathrm{p}(P)\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}$ the pressure, with $\lbrace p(P)\rbrace$ the (complex) vector of the acoustic pressures.

### 2.3 Harmonic equation of fluid–structure systems

The document [R4.02.02](doc:r4.02.02) describes the finite-element modelling of a system $F+S$ made up of a fluid part (without transport) $F$ in interaction with a structure part $S$ (interaction on $F\cup S$ ). Fluid and structure have a linear behaviour.

The fluid system $F$ undergoes a harmonic excitation of normal acoustic velocity on part of its boundary. The harmonic response is described by the following equation, in which the quantities to be computed are:

- the acoustic pressure at every point $P$ of the fluid $F$ ,
- the displacement at every point $P$ of the structure $S$ ,
- in addition, the displacement potential $\varphi$ at every point $P$ of the fluid $F$ or other auxiliary variables described in [R4.02.02](doc:r4.02.02).

$$
(K-{\omega}^{2}M-j{\omega}^{3}I)\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}u(P)\\ p(P)\\ \varphi (P)\end{array}\right\rbrace \text{=+}j\omega \lbrace {v}_{n}(P)\rbrace
$$

where $\mathrm{M}$ is the (real) fluid–structure “mass” matrix arising from the domains $F$ and $S$ , $I$ is the (real) fluid “impedance” matrix arising from the boundary ${\partial}_{z}F$ of the domain $F$ where one applies an impedance, and $K$ is the (real) fluid–structure “stiffness” matrix arising from the domains $F$ and $S$ .

The solution fields are of harmonic form. ${v}_{n}(P,t)=\lbrace {v}_{n}(P)\rbrace {e}^{j\omegat }$ is the (real) vector of the field of normal acoustic velocities applied to the boundary ${\partial}_{v}F$ of $F$ , $u(P,t)=\lbrace u(P)\rbrace {e}^{j\omegat }$ is the (complex) vector of the displacement field in the structure $S$ , $p(P,t)=\lbrace p(P)\rbrace {e}^{j\omegat }$ is the (complex) vector of the acoustic-pressure field in the fluid $F$ and $\varphi (P,t)=\lbrace \varphi (P)\rbrace {e}^{j\omegat }$ is the (complex) vector of the displacement-potential field in the fluid $F$ .

### 2.4 General harmonic equation

In order to take into account all the cases of harmonic equations, the operator DYNA_VIBRA solves the following general harmonic equation:

$$
(-j{\omega}^{3}I-{\omega}^{2}M+j\omegac +K)\lbrace q\rbrace =\left\lbrace \sum_{i=1}^{k}{h}_{i}(f)\cdot {\omega}^{{n}_{i}}\cdot {e}^{j\pi \frac{{\varphi}_{i}}{180}}\cdot {g}_{i}(P)\right\rbrace
$$

where $\mathrm{I}$ is the possible fluid “impedance” matrix, $M$ is the “mass” matrix, $C$ the “damping” matrix and $K$ the “stiffness” matrix.

The solution is $\left\lbrace q(P)\right\rbrace$ . The right-hand side contains $\left\lbrace {g}_{i}(P)\right\rbrace$ , which is a field vector at the nodes corresponding to one or more loads of force or acoustic velocity or potential or imposed motion, ${h}_{i}(f)$ a real or complex function of the frequency $f$ , $\omega =2\pi f$ is the angular frequency, ${n}_{i}$ is the power of the angular frequency when the loading is a function of the angular frequency, and ${\varphi}_{i}$ is the phase in degrees of each component of the excitation with respect to a phase reference.

By way of example, if one takes the case of a fluid system modelled in acoustics, without imposed degrees of freedom, simply excited on part of its boundary by a normal-velocity field ${\mathrm{v}}_{\mathrm{n}}(P,t)=\lbrace {\mathrm{v}}_{\mathrm{n}}(P)\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}$ , the terms of equation () become:

$$
(-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{M}+j\omega \mathrm{C}+\mathrm{K})\lbrace \mathrm{p}\rbrace =-{\omega}^{}\left\lbrace {\mathrm{v}}_{\mathrm{n}}(P)\right\rbrace
$$

- no impedance component $\mathrm{I}$ ,
- acoustic mass matrix $\mathrm{M}$ ,
- *Where applicable*, a damping matrix $\mathrm{C}$ arising from the acoustic finite-element modelling if there is an impedance on the boundary,
- acoustic stiffness matrix $\mathrm{K}$ ,
- the vector of the unknowns $\left\lbrace \mathrm{q}(P)\right\rbrace$ is reduced to $\left\lbrace \mathrm{p}(P)\right\rbrace$ , the vector of the pressures at the nodes
- the loading $\left\lbrace {\mathrm{g}}_{i}(P)\right\rbrace$ is $\left\lbrace {\mathrm{v}}_{\mathrm{n}}(P)\right\rbrace$ , the normal-velocity field vector on the faces
- ${h}_{i}(f)$ is constant and equal to $-1.$
- the power ${n}_{i}$ is equal to $1$ and the phase ${\varphi}_{i}$ is zero
