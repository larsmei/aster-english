# u2.05.00 Methodological guide on the approaches in fracture mechanics

**Summary:**

This note constitutes a methodological guide for the use of fracture mechanics with *Code_Aster*. It
provides an overall view of the approaches in fracture mechanics existing or planned in *Code_Aster* and
constitutes a signpost towards the dedicated notes and documentations.

## 1 Glossary

*Acronyms*

- **SENT** : Single Edge Notched Tension: tensile specimen with a lateral notch
- **SENB** : Single Edge Notched Bending : three-point bending specimen with a lateral notch
- **CT**  : Compact Tension specimen for the measurement of the toughness and of the resistance to ductile tearing
- **AE** : axisymmetric notched specimen
- **LCF:**  cycle of a warm preloading of Loading Cooling Fracture type
- **LUCF :**  cycle of a warm preloading of Loading Unloading Cooling Fracture type
- **DSR** : defect under cladding
- **DDR** : defect in the cladding

*Definitions*

- Initiation/Propagation of a crack:

According to the reference or the discipline, there may be a dispute over these definitions. For example, in fatigue, the word
initiation is more commonly employed to designate the appearance of the crack in a sound material. Whereas in
fracture mechanics, one evaluates the initiation of an existing crack. In fatigue, one implies the propagation under
cyclic loading. Whereas in fracture mechanics, one employs the word propagation to designate a tear under
monotonic loading. In order to remove any ambiguity, one employs in this document the word **initiation** to designate
the stability aspect of an existing crack; will it propagate or not? One also employs the word **propagation**
to designate the evolution of the crack front under monotonic loading:

- if rapid evolution - unstable propagation: cleavage
- if progressive evolution - stable propagation: ductile tearing
- ${K}_{I}$ **,** ${K}_{\mathit{II}}$ **and** ${K}_{\mathit{III}}$ **, the stress intensity factors** of modes $I$ , $\mathit{II}$ and $\mathit{III}$ , respectively, valid in linear thermo-elastic mechanics, supplying the characteristics of the stress fields in the vicinity of the crack tip
- $G$ **, energy release rate** , due to Griffith, valid in linear or non-linear elasticity. A link (Irwin formulae) exists between the factors ${K}_{I}$ **,** ${K}_{\mathit{II}}$ **,** ${K}_{\mathit{III}}$ and $G$ in linear elasticity. For example, in plane strain: $G=\frac{1-{\nu }^{2}}{E}({K}_{I}^{2}+{K}_{\text{II}}^{2})+\frac{1+\nu }{E}{K}_{\text{III}}^{2}$
- ${G}_{\mathit{ENL}}$ , energy release rate in the post-processing of a non-linear thermoelastic analysis
- $J$ **, contour integral** , due to Rice, equivalent to $G$ in plane linear elasticity, characterises the energy release rate of a structure containing a crack during the advance of the latter. This integral is independent of the contour when the material is elastic
- ${J}_{\mathit{MOD}}$ **,** **contour integral** $J$ **modified** to take account of the initial stresses, valid under a non-proportional loading.
- The **toughness** of a material is its ability to resist the propagation of a crack; it is defined by the stress intensity factor ${K}_{\mathit{IC}}$ or, when the plasticity is confined at the crack tip, by the Rice–Cherepanov integral (${J}_{\mathit{IC}}$ ). ${G}_{\mathit{IC}}$ is the critical energy release rate. ${K}_{\mathit{IC}}$ and ${G}_{\mathit{IC}}$ characterise the criteria of resistance of the material to the propagation of cracks
- In plane elasticity, there exists an equivalence between the propagation criteria:
- $G<{G}_{\mathit{IC}};K<{K}_{\mathit{IC}};J<{J}_{\mathit{IC}}$ **The crack is stable**
- $G\mathrm{\ge }{G}_{};K\mathrm{\ge }{K}_{\text{IC}};J\mathrm{\ge }{J}_{\text{IC}}$ **The crack is unstable**
- ${K}_{\mathit{IC}}$ or ${J}_{\mathit{IC}}$ are obtained on standardised specimens.
- **Brittle fracture** : ${J}_{\mathit{IC}}$ is the toughness at initiation
- **Ductile tearing** : The energy of initiation and of propagation of a fatigue crack under quasi-static conditions is necessary in order to know the resistance to tearing of the steels.
- ${J}_{0,2}$ : initiation energy for a value of $\mathit{\Delta a}=0,2\text{mm}$
- $\frac{\text{dJ}}{\text{da}}:$ resistance to propagation
- **Resilience** corresponds to fracture conditions under dynamic conditions (impact bending tests). There exist few data on the resistance to tearing of the materials in a power plant, whereas there exists a very large database on the resilience values. For certain materials, correlations have been established between the resilience and the resistance to tearing
- **Charpy test** : test measuring the resilience, the energy necessary for the fracture by impact of a standardised notched specimen (KCV or KCU according to the shape of the notch)
- **Master Curve** : Master curve of the toughness (proposed by Wallin), which describes both the evolution of the toughness as a function of the temperature and the scatter of the toughness at a given temperature. This scatter is characterised by a Weibull distribution law.
- **Margin factor** : ratio between the toughness of the material ${K}_{\mathit{IC}}$ and the plastically corrected stress intensity factor ${K}_{\mathit{CP}}$ at a given point of the crack front. $\mathit{FM}=\frac{{K}_{\mathit{IC}}}{{K}_{\mathit{CP}}}$

- $\mathit{FM}\ge 1$ : the mechanical integrity of the vessel is justified
- $\mathit{FM}<1$ : the mechanical integrity of the vessel is not justified by this method

- **Plastically corrected stress intensity factor** : depends on the elastic stress intensity factors ${K}_{\mathit{elas}}(t)$ and plastic ${K}_{\mathit{plas}}(t)$ evolving in time $t$ . One has:

- ${\text{K}}_{\text{CP}}\left(\text{t}\right)={\text{K}}_{\text{elas}}\left(\text{t}\right)+\chi {\text{∆K}}_{max}\left(\text{t}\right)$
- where $∆{K}_{max}(t)=max\left\{0,max\left[\begin{array}{c}0<u\le t,u\in \mathit{phases}\mathit{croissantes}\\ i.e.u\mathit{tel}\mathit{que}\frac{{\mathit{dK}}_{\mathit{elas}}}{\mathit{ds}}(u)>0\\ \chi ({K}_{\mathit{plas}}(u)-{K}_{\mathit{elas}}(u))\end{array}\right]\right\}$
- and $\chi =\left\{\begin{array}{ccc}1& \mathit{si}max({K}_{\mathit{plas}})\ge max({K}_{\mathit{elas}})& :\mathit{cas}\mathit{DSR}\\ -1& \mathit{sinon}& :\mathit{cas}\mathit{DDR}\end{array}\right\}$

- **Regulatory margin factor** : it is defined as:

- ${\mathit{FM}}_{\mathit{ASN}}=\left\{\begin{array}{ccc}\mathit{FM}& \mathit{si}max({K}_{\mathit{plas}})\ge max({K}_{\mathit{elas}})& :\mathit{cas}\mathit{DSR}\\ \frac{{K}_{\mathit{IC}}}{{K}_{\mathit{elas}}}& \mathit{sinon}& :\mathit{cas}\mathit{DDR}\end{array}\right\}$

## 2 Introduction

This methodological guide constitutes an aid to facilitate the understanding of a study of the harmfulness of generic
or exceptional defects revealed on metallic structures. The main aim of this document is to inform
the fracture-mechanics engineer about the approaches available in *Code_Aster* for solving a given problem.
This document refers to the documents of type U2 in the documentation base of *Code_Aster*; it does not recall the
formulations detailed in the reference documents R, nor the means of using these functionalities, which are sufficiently
explained in the user's documents U4 or U2, but it delimits the field of application of each approach.

Thus, one proposes to recall the various approaches available and to define briefly their field
of application. The field of validity of these approaches may be linked either to the definition of the model (brittle fracture,
ductile fracture, …), or to the degree of maturity of the approach as regards its industrial implementation (heaviness of the analyses,
developments still necessary, material parameters not available…).

However, we cannot describe all the types of study possible or carried out in fracture mechanics.
The user will choose the approach according to the requirements and conditions of his or her study. References to
a few study notes whose aim was to validate these approaches or to apply them to industrial cases are
also given.

The approaches on initiation and stability are dominant in this document, to the detriment of those which treat
tearing. The methods of propagation under monotonic loading for simulating ductile tearing remain to be
developed in Code_Aster.

## 3 Brief overview of fracture mechanics

Any metallic component subjected to mechanical, thermal, hydraulic or chemical loadings is exposed to
cracking. The defect or the crack comes from manufacture (welding) or from a process of damage of the
material in the course of operation (fatigue, creep, stress-corrosion, ...). As soon as the cracks become
of a size large enough for the volume which surrounds them to satisfy the continuity assumptions of continuum
mechanics, the mechanical study must be carried out in the field of fracture mechanics. The studies
must then estimate the degree of harmfulness of these cracks: are they harmless, and will their presence therefore be tolerated
for a more or less long time without risks, or on the contrary are they harmful, which makes it necessary to repair or
replace the component?

### 3.1 Initiation / Propagation

On certain types of component, degradation mechanisms such as creep, stress-corrosion or
fatigue are at the origin of the creation of cracks which may then give rise to sudden fracture.

Fracture mechanics makes it possible to predict the harmfulness **of an existing crack**: the kinetics of propagation of
defects and their critical sizes. In the case where the propagation phase would be very rapid, the final fracture is
said to be sudden or unstable; in the contrary case, the fracture is stable, which characterises for example ductile
tearing. Propagation may lead to a total fracture or to an arrest of the crack.

### 3.2 Brittle fracture (cleavage) / Ductile fracture (tearing)

The temperature and the strain rate of a material are two parameters which characterise its state: brittle or
ductile. Certain metallic materials are brittle below a temperature called the brittle/ductile transition
temperature (cf. schematic figure Figure 3.2-a ), and in particular the ferritic steels
(low-alloy steels and carbon-manganese steels).

Structures are generally designed in such a way that the nominal stress undergone does not exceed the
yield stress of the material, which protects them from irreversible strains. A fracture of brittle type may then
occur on a pre-existing crack or on a crack created by fatigue, at low temperature and/or at high strain rates.

**Ductile fracture (tearing)**

A material is ductile when it deforms plastically before fracturing; the plasticity is generalised. This
plastic deformation may lead to the appearance of a crack which becomes critical when it propagates. After
initiation, the growth of the crack is in general slow and stable (according to the characteristics of the applied
loading). Ductile fracture concerns materials such as copper, lead, mild steels at ambient temperature,
and austenitic stainless steels. The fracture mechanism comprises three phases: the generation of microscopic
voids around preferential sites, the growth and the coalescence of the cavities leading to fracture. The
fracture surface is in this case of a dull and rough appearance.

**Brittle fracture (cleavage)**

Brittle fracture occurs without notable local plastification. After initiation, the propagation of the crack is
rapid (the tests reveal speeds of a few hundred $m\mathrm{/}s$ ) and most often
unstable (with arrest or sudden fracture of the component according to the case). It characterises brittle materials such as
glass, ceramics and irradiated steels or steels at low temperature. Resilience characterises the capacity of the material
to store energy when it deforms in an elastic manner. The brittle material fractures under the effect of a
low energy. The greater the energy which must be supplied in order to deform it, the tougher the material is. The
resilience tests are carried out on specimens of Charpy type and the toughness tests are carried out most often on
specimens of type $\mathit{CT}$ . In the case of a brittle material — unlike the ductile material — the
plasticity remains confined at the crack tip; this type of phenomenon is known under the term “cleavage”: the propagation
of the crack takes place by separation of atomic planes and the fracture surface is made up of planes of a bright appearance.
To a brittle material there corresponds a low toughness. The toughness at initiation ${K}_{\mathrm{1c}}$ is not an
intrinsic datum of the material; it depends on the geometry and on the level of triaxiality.

## 5 Panorama of the approaches in fracture mechanics

The global approach has been known for a long time, but it has a limited domain of validity. The local approach has a
more extended validity, but it is within the reach only of experienced mechanicians. The local approaches make it possible
to apprehend and to understand phenomena which are all the more complex in that they can reduce the degree of conservatism
of the codified approaches based on the global approaches.

The theory of brittle fracture rests on fracture criteria of “global approach” established in a macroscopic manner
and it does not address the problems of constitutive behaviour, because the material is often considered elastoplastic with
confined plasticity. The purely macroscopic approach to brittle fracture is no longer valid when the material is
strongly plastified, because it is no longer possible to represent the crack as a surface of discontinuity of the
displacements and of the stresses. The dissipation is concentrated at the crack tip and it is difficult to separate
the plastic dissipation from the fracture dissipation. In ductile fracture, a “local approach” based on a mechanical
procedure is often necessary.

When an approach becomes generic and applicable over a fairly wide range of situations, it also becomes
complex.

### 5.1 Classical approach

Linear elastic fracture mechanics rests on the stress intensity factor, which characterises the
stress and strain fields in the neighbourhood of a crack. In plasticity, classical fracture mechanics possesses
rigorous bases only by analogy with non-linear elastic constitutive behaviours (in the case of
non-proportional loadings and isotropy), for which the contour integral $J$ or the energy release
rate is the fundamental parameter.

In linear or non-linear elasticity (cf. §  7.1 and §  7.2 ), three
parameters can be computed:

- $J$ , the Rice integral: contour integral – the experimental computation of $J$ is obtained by the area under the force–opening curve of the specimens ($\mathit{CT}$  or $\mathit{SENB}$ in general). This parameter is available in *Code_Aster* (cf. [65])
- $G$ , the energy release rate: domain integral suited to computation by finite elements (cf. [57], [58]),
- $K$ , the stress intensity coefficients corresponding to the 3 modes, associated with the singular fields at the crack tip. The latter have a meaning only in linear elasticity (cf. [60], [62]).

In elastoplasticity, this parameter is strictly valid only when elastoplasticity can be assimilated to
non-linear elasticity, that is to say **for a radial monotonic loading**. However, a modified form of
the contour integral, ${J}_{\mathit{MOD}}$ , has been proposed, which is valid for non-proportional stress
conditions (cf. [18], [47], [65]). The expression of the modified integral (${J}_{\mathit{MOD}}$ ) reduces to the classical integral, $J$ .

The classical approach presents several limitations when it is applied outside its domain of validity
(“small-defect effect”, “triaxiality effect”, “warm-preloading effect”, …) or in the case of non-proportional loading, which has led to the developments of new global or energy approaches or of
local approaches. It turns out that in certain particular cases ${J}_{\mathrm{1C}}$ or ${K}_{\mathrm{1C}}$ may
depend on the geometry and are not intrinsic to the material.

Small-defect effect:

It turns out that the toughness, which is supposed to be intrinsic to the material, seems to depend on the size of the defect, or more
exactly on the ratio of the size of the defect to the size of the ligament (in fact, it is the level of triaxiality affecting
the neighbourhood of the defect which is responsible for this effect). The toughness in the case of a small defect is greater than
that of a large defect.

Geometry effect:

The geometry of the medium ‘specimen or structure’ influences the nature of the fracture, in particular in the
transition zone. At a given temperature, for the same material and at equivalent loading, a
$\mathit{CT}$ specimen fractures in a brittle manner whereas a cracked pipe fractures in a ductile manner. For homothetic notched
$\mathit{AE}$ specimens, the scatter and the mean value of the diametral strain at fracture
decrease when the diameter of the specimen increases. The smaller $a\mathrm{/}w$ is, the higher the apparent value
of ${J}_{\mathrm{1C}}$ is.

“Mismatch” effect:

In the presence of two materials, the classical approach is valid when the plasticity remains confined in a
homogeneous zone. When the plastification domain covers two materials, the classical approach is no longer valid.

Triaxiality effect “constraint effect”:

In order to be able to use the classical approach in elastoplasticity, one must assume that the loading is proportional, which
is often far from being the case for real structures. The loading and the geometry influence the type of
stress field at the crack tip in a structure. The stress field at the crack tip may become
complex compared with the usual conditions of proportional loading adopted on a specimen.

Warm-preloading effect:

Warm-preloading effect or “WPS: Warm Pre-Stressing”: following a rise in temperature, the material passes from the
brittle domain to the ductile domain, and a mechanical loading is then applied (warm preloading). After
cooling of the specimen and a toughness-measurement test, one finds a value of the toughness greater than that
of the material tested without the preloading. The warm preloading has a beneficial effect induced by the blunting of the
crack and the appearance of residual stresses.

Non-proportional loading effect:

Several situations induce a non-proportional loading at the crack point, which makes the classical approach
invalid:

- unloading problem following a thermal shock, although in certain cases the Beta correction can be applied in order to assume that the constitutive behaviour after unloading is elastic (cf. [75]),
- problem of non-monotonic or non-proportional loading, following a propagation, a thermal transient, the presence of residual stresses or a mixed mode.

If the loading is arbitrary, one may use a local approach (Beremin (cf. [59]) or Bordet
(cf. [61]) or an energy approach (cf. [64]) in the case of cleavage, or Rousselier and GTN
for tearing (cf. [67], [68] and [87) which do not need
the assumption of proportional loading.

### 5.2 Two-parameter approach

This approach is developed in order to respond to the dependence of the toughness on the triaxiality. It rests on
the extension of the expression of the fields at the crack tip with higher-order terms. More complex than
the classical approach, it enriches it by the introduction of a second parameter which takes account of the triaxiality, of the
stability and of the plasticity zone. It loses its validity when the loading is no longer proportional: loading followed
by an unloading, propagation of cracks, residual stresses, mixed mode ... or when the material is viscous or
subjected to a thermal transient. This approach requires the setting up of a very large number of tests in order to
construct the locus of fracture (cf. §  7.13 ). The global two-parameter approach
is not developed in *Code_Aster* (cf. [50]).

### 5.3 Energy approach

In an elastic or non-linear elastic framework, the energy approach leads to the computation of the parameter $G$ ,
energy release rate. In the case of a confined plasticity, it has given rise to the development of the
$\mathit{Gp}$ approach (cf . [64], [73]), which is an energy approach avoiding the
Rice paradox by the use of a notch (cf. § 7.4 ). It is possible to estimate the
energy release rate, thanks to the use of contour integral methods (cf. [18],
[47], [65]).

In the case of a ductile material or of extended plasticity, it is declined as the computation of the parameter $\mathit{Gtp}$
(cf. [37]), which is now withdrawn from Code_Aster.

This approach has been applied within the framework of the European project VOCALIST on the small-defect-effect case and within the framework
of the European project SMILE on the warm-preloading WPS case.

It is possible to employ this approach in the cases of non-proportional loadings.

### 5.4 Local approach to fracture

The description of fracture passes through a local modelling of the damage. Fracture is characterised by an
intrinsic variable of the material. It allows the definition of a local fracture criterion based on
material and geometric considerations at the level of the crack tip.

In brittle fracture:

In this case, it is sufficient to analyse the harmfulness of the defect at initiation. The models are in general criteria in
the post-processing of an analysis.

For brittle fracture or cleavage, the model most used is the Beremin model (cf. [8], ,
[59], [69]), based on the weakest-link theory, with a Weibull stress
and a statistical law of distribution of micro-defects (cf. §  7.5 ).
This model also makes it possible to account for the scale effect, that is to say the variation of the risk of fracture with
respect to the size of the specimen. The users of the Beremin approach recommend the use of a crack or,
more recently, of a notch, with a mesh-cell size of $50\mu m$ as has long been accepted.
Indeed, the notch has a morphology better suited than the crack for capturing the fields in elastoplasticity.
The advantage of the Beremin approach is that it is widely tested. The account taken of the triaxiality is intrinsic
to the method. The local approach of Bordet (cf. §  7.6 ), introduced recently into *Code_Aster*
(cf. [61], [70], [85]) is an extension of Beremin. It furthermore allows an
improved treatment in the case of warm preloading thanks to the account taken of the active plasticity. Whereas
the active plasticity is taken into account in modified Beremin, the Bordet model furthermore makes it possible to take into account
the effect of the plasticity on the micro-defects at the origin of cleavage.

*In ductile fracture:*

The models for ductile tearing raise several difficulties. The determination of the material parameters is
not standardised and is carried out case by case. The approaches proposed in the literature are classified into 3 families:

- **The uncoupled models** : the damage of the material does not affect its global constitutive behaviour. The fracture of the structure occurs when the damage reaches a critical value which is supposed to be intrinsic to the material. These models are used to analyse the initiation of the crack.
- **The coupled models** : based on damageable elastoplastic potentials or on micro-mechanical approaches. The constitutive behaviour of the material and the damage are linked. The fracture of the structure is described implicitly by the softening of the global response of the structure.
- **The semi-coupled models** : models intermediate between the two preceding families. The global constitutive behaviour is not affected by the damage but there is a local coupling of the damage with the constitutive behaviour. The fracture of the structure takes place when the local softening is reached.

The uncoupled model of Rice & Tracey (cf. [61] and §  7.12 ), or the coupled model of Gurson
[87] and of Rousselier (cf. [67] and §  7.10 ) are intended for
ductile fracture. The limitations relating to these approaches come from the difficulty of identification of the parameters and of the
transferability from specimen to structure.

### 5.5 Alternative approach - CZM

This is an approach widely developed in the literature which is global as regards the minimisation of the energy and local
through the fine description of the residual forces at the crack tip. It makes it possible to predict in a robust manner the
kinetics of propagation (cf. [81], [51]). The ambition of these cracking models is to represent the evolution of the
damage and/or fracture surfaces of a solid in 2D or in 3D, sound or partially cracked, subjected to a wide
range of loadings (mixed mode, cyclic of any amplitude, thermal, ...). The characterisation of the
material parameters, in particular the critical stress, is a primordial step for the proper use of these elements. It is
an approach which makes it possible to predict initiation and propagation along a path known a priori
(cf.  7.9 ). However, the size of the crack at initiation is not precise because it is
dependent on the size of the cohesive element in the mesh. It is less influential on the expected result in
the analysis of the propagation.

### 6.1 Mesh

#### 6.1.1 Crack or notch

The mesh constitutes an important part of the study and especially in fracture mechanics when one must mesh very
precisely the defect. In every case, a precise computation of the mechanical fields at the tip of the crack/notch is
primordial. To do this, it is widely recommended to mesh finely at the crack tip or to use
quadratic elements with Barsoum elements at the crack tip if the analysis is elastic, and to employ
under-integrated elements or incompressible elements if the analysis is elastoplastic. It is known that a radiating mesh at
the crack tip leads to a quality of the solution in terms of energy release rate superior to that
obtained with other types of mesh and at a lower cost in terms of performance in computation time.

Crack meshes are widely used in the business tools when one must use the
classical approaches of the computations of $G$ , energy release rate. Cracks are distinctly easier to
model than notches on 3D meshes.

When one is interested in a global result obtained on a $\mathit{CT}$ or $\mathit{AE}$ specimen, the
defect may be represented by a crack or a notch. On the other hand, in order to know precisely the
mechanical fields around the tip of the defect, it is recommended to mesh a notch. Indeed, the notch makes it possible to
smooth the singular mechanical fields at the crack tip and to reduce the zone in which the stresses are
disturbed.

The meshing of a notch in 3D is tedious. As the analyses with $\mathit{Gp}$ require meshes
of notches with slices, on which this method rests, Gibi scripts have been developed for this purpose. The
definition of the cracked block defined with Gibi, some twenty years ago, is inherent in the presence of a torus around
the crack tip with a radiating mesh.

The specification of the meshes is complicated for the contour integrals, ${J}_{\mathit{MOD}}$  , because they
must satisfy specific mathematical criteria [1]. Moreover, the elements which define a
contour must be extracted. Inevitably, this still impacts the time of creation of a mesh and the
post-processing time.

Under development:

- A procedure for insertion of a crack with a radiating mesh into a sound structure with Salomé.
- A procedure for insertion of a crack with a free mesh with the tool Zcracks.
- A library of meshes of cracked specimens in 3D is planned in Salomé-Meca. The parametric mesh
of a $\mathit{CT}$ specimen in 3D is already available (as an overload) in the form of a plug-in.

#### 6.1.2 XFEM

This method makes it possible to avoid meshing the crack. It is based on the definition and the enrichment of the model by
level-sets (cf. [82]). The implementation of an analysis with XFEM is recalled in the document
[80]. The criteria and the refinement method for the analyses as well as a synthesis of the developments and
applications are the subject of the references [55] and [56]. This method is operational at
the present time on elastic structures. The extension of the validation of its domain to plasticity is in the course
of development.

### 6.2 Evaluating the risk of initiation: Will the crack propagate?

#### 6.2.1 In thermoelasticity

*In* *linear thermoelasticity*, the classical global approaches are applicable:

- Computation of the energy release rate $G$ (cf. [71], [57]),
- Estimation of the energy release rate ${J}_{\mathit{MOD}}$ (cf. [18],
[47], [65]),
- Computation of the stress intensity factors $K$ (cf. [60], [72]),
- Comparison of the stress intensity factor with the toughness or of the energy release rate with the
critical energy,

*In* *non-linear thermoelasticity*, the classical global approaches are applicable only in the case of a
radial and proportional loading. They are based on the computation of the energy release rate
${G}_{\text{ENL}}$ in the post-processing of a non-linear thermo-elastic analysis (cf. [71],
[58]). A method for the case of non-proportional loading has been proposed, which is
${J}_{\mathit{MOD}}$ (cf. [18], [47], [65]).

In **brittle fracture**, computation of ${G}_{\text{ENL}}$ in comparison with ${G}_{\text{c}}$ or computation of
${K}_{G}$ from ${G}_{\text{ENL}}$ and comparison with the toughness of the material ${K}_{\mathrm{1C}}$.
Estimation of the probability of fracture via the “Master Curve” (cf.[[25]]),

In **ductile fracture**, ${G}_{\text{ENL}}$ is compared with ${J}_{0,2}$ in order to study the risk of initiation of
tearing,

**These approaches are applicable in a well-defined domain of validity (non-linear elasticity, hence
proportional loading, and when the crack is sufficiently large), outside which we observe effects**:

- non-proportional loading following:
- an unloading,
- a propagation,
- a thermal transient,
- a presence of residual stresses,
- a “constraint effect” or triaxiality effect
- Mismatch effect (2 materials)
- “Small defect” effect
- Warm-preloading effect or “WPS”

#### 6.2.2 In elastoplasticity - brittle fracture

For a medium in which the plasticity remains confined, the medium remaining elastic in its entirety, the global and
local approaches of fracture mechanics or energy approaches make it possible to apply the criteria of fracture mechanics.
However, according to the material parameters which one has available, the time which one can devote to the carrying out of the study,
the availability of an adequate mesh or the capacity to produce it are arguments which argue for one approach or
for another (cf. Table 6.2.2-1 ):

- Energy approach $\mathit{Gp}$ (cf. [73]): The advantage of the $\mathit{Gp}$ approach is
that it is deterministic and comes close to the classical engineer's approach $J$ . A relation between the
two parameters $\mathit{Gpc}$ , the critical value of $\mathit{Gp}$ and the radius of the notch has been able to be
established, which facilitates the data setup of the parameters. The account taken of the triaxiality is direct with
this approach. Let us note that there remain points to be improved in the $\mathit{Gp}$ approach in order to make it still
more accessible to the engineer:
- Although the energy approach $\mathit{Gp}$ comes close to the global approach by virtue of its foundation on
energy principles, it depends on the radius of the notch in the case of non-proportional/non-
monotonic loadings and therefore on the thickness of the sliced zone.
- The meshing of the slices is not easily achievable, and is obligatory in 3D. One must in addition master
mesh-refinement techniques in order to avoid the stretching of the mesh cells at the notch tip causing
stress concentrations.
- One assumes that the $\mathit{Gp}$ approach is valid for all non-proportional loadings. The analyses
carried out under these conditions cannot be validated without accompanying them with large test campaigns.
- Local approach of Beremin: This approach has the advantage of being widely used. However, it is a
probabilistic approach, so the results are difficult to interpret and it is not recommended in the upper part of
the transition zone. The material parameters must be finely identified in dependence on the temperature.
- Local approach of Bordet: This approach requires a large number of parameters which are not obvious to identify. The
macro POST_BORDET is developed in Code_Aster.
- Approach based on the critical stress (Hajjaj [9], Ritchie, Knott, Rice [15], Dahl
[12]): Approach developed for the treatment of crack arrest in viscoplasticity. The
fracture criterion is a comparison between the maximum principal stress and the critical stress. This approach
makes it possible to simulate the advance of the crack by cancelling the stiffness of the elements at the crack tip, when the
criterion is reached.
- Approach with cohesive elements (cf. [63], [72]). This approach has been compared with
the classical approach in the note cited in [21]; it is not often used in brittle fracture for
lack of material parameters. However, the identification of these parameters (only 2) can be done by
comparison with other models or with the experimental results. It is an approach which makes it possible to predict cleavage
on a known path.
- Two-parameter approach $K-T$ in brittle fracture and $J-Q$ in ductile fracture, which makes it possible to take account of the
triaxiality effect in a qualitative manner (cf. [50], [29]). These approaches are not
yet developed in *Code_Aster*.
- Damage laws in quasi-static conditions may be used in brittle fracture (cf. [84]).
However, they are rather used for the simulation of the cracking of geomaterials, which does not prevent
their use for the simulation of the damage of metallic materials with the appropriate material parameters and
regularisation.

#### 6.2.3 In elastoplasticity - ductile fracture

The models for ductile tearing raise several difficulties. The determination of the material parameters is
not standardised and is carried out case by case. Same remark as for brittle fracture: according to the material parameters
which one has available, the time which one can devote to the carrying out of the study, the availability of an adequate mesh or
the capacity to produce it are arguments which argue for one approach or for another
(cf. Table 6.2.3-1 ):

- Energy approach $\mathit{Gtp}$ : This approach is based on the computation of the total energy in the post-
processing of an elastoplastic analysis with the operator CALC_G (cf. [71]). This approach was
used some ten years ago and showed an advantage with respect to the classical approach $J-\Delta a$ ,
by virtue of its applicability in the case of complex loadings. Its weak point lies in the definition of the
criterion, which encompasses fracture and plasticity, which may overestimate the energy linked to fracture. It has been
compared recently with the $\mathit{CZM}$ on a study of crack initiation in an elastoplastic medium
(cf. [21]). This approach is now withdrawn from Code_Aster.
- Uncoupled local approach – Rice and Tracey: This is a simple ductile-initiation criterion because it rests on an
analytical formula as a function of the stresses and strain rates at each computation instant. The advantage of this
approach is that it is simple and widely used. Its use must remain limited to preliminary analyses
(cf. [61])
- Coupled local approach of Rousselier: ROUSS_PR, ROUSS_VISC (cf. [68]) and ROUSSELIER model in
large strains (cf.[67]) and of GTN [87]. These models present several
difficulties of numerical implementation: identification of the parameters, mesh adaptation around the crack,
adaptation of the boundary conditions, non-convergence of the constitutive law. Recommendations which help to
overcome these difficulties (incompressible elements – large strains – mesh adaptation in order to take
account of the unloading) must facilitate convergence.
- Two-parameter approach (cf. [50]): The $J-Q$ approach is promising because it will allow an
application in the three domains, brittle, ductile and transition, and it makes it possible to represent the branching of
cracks. It nevertheless remains applicable only within a framework of proportional loading. This approach is
not available in *Code_Aster*.
- The cohesive models (cf. [81], [63]). The account taken of the triaxiality has
proved necessary for their good representativeness in 3D.

#### 6.2.4 In elastoplasticity - brittle/ductile transition zone

Certain types of thermomechanical loading place the potential fracture in the
brittle/ductile transition zone. One gives a summary of the approaches tested or in the course of being tested in this zone
(cf. Table 6.2.4-1 ):

- The Rousselier/Beremin chaining is implemented in the study on the passage from resilience to toughness
(cf. [31]). In this case, the ductile fracture is described by the coupled damage model of
Rousselier and the model of post-processing of brittle fracture by cleavage of Beremin. No coupling between the
two phenomena of ductile and brittle fracture.
- The chaining Rousselier/Rice and Tracey and Rousselier/Beremin has been implemented in a study of fracture of
welded piping lines of ASG N4 type (cf. [34]).
- The coupling of Le Corre/ $\mathit{Gp}$ : $\mathit{Gp}$ for the prediction of the moment of initiation, Le
Corre for the prediction of cleavage. This coupling is carried out with a representation of the defect by a notch. The
simulation of the ductile propagation remains to be determined, the $\mathit{Gtp}$ approach seeming legitimate since
it is based on a notch. The coupling capability must still be verified.
- The chaining $J-\mathit{Gfr}$ /Le Corre: $J-\mathit{Gfr}$ for the ductile propagation and Le-Corre for the
prediction of the nature of the initiation. The Le-Corre model proposed at the CEA (cf.  7.7 ) uses
the notion of threshold stress, coupled with a threshold-volume value. This model is based on the computation of $J$ ,
and therefore can predict initiation only in the case of monotonic/proportional loadings (cf. [48],
[85]). This model is not available in *Code_Aster*.

### 6.3 Evaluating the size of the crack: If the crack propagates, by how much? in which direction? Will it arrest?

#### 6.3.1 Cleavage

In brittle fracture, among the approaches cited in §  6.2.2 :

- The CZM approach can evaluate the advance of the crack, along a path known a priori. It has also been used
within the framework of the Vessel project to simulate the crack arrest of a defect under cladding (cf. [55]).
- Approach based on the critical stress (Hajjaj [9], Ritchie, Knott, Rice [15], Dahl
[12]): Approach developed for the treatment of crack arrest in viscoplasticity. The
fracture criterion is a comparison between the maximum principal stress and the critical stress. This approach
makes it possible to simulate the advance of the crack by cancelling the stiffness of the elements at the crack tip, when the
criterion is reached.

The thesis work of S. Cuvilliez (cf. [54]) aims to achieve a transition between a
damage model and the cohesive elements in concrete. It is the only reference for a model of prediction of the
cracking path by cleavage.

#### 6.3.2 Tearing

In the case of extended plasticity in ductile fracture, the evaluation of the risk of propagation does not come within the framework of the
Griffith theory; other approaches exist, based in most cases on notions of critical stresses and
energies. Likewise, we do not have tools allowing the simulation of ductile propagation in a
transition zone: ductile propagation followed by cleavage. Several avenues are conceivable but remain to be
consolidated:

- The $\mathit{Gtp}$ approach for the simulation of the propagation along a known path by excavation of
slices. A single application exists at present (cf.[37]).
- The $J-\mathit{Gfr}$ approach of the CEA, along a known path.
- The $\mathit{CZM}$ approach, along a known path (cf. [32]).
- The Gurson or Rousselier model can predict the cracking path. This model poses a problem of dependence on the
mesh or on the regularisation parameters (cf.[30]).

## 8 Conclusions

After a reminder of the basic notions and an overview of the various approaches which exist in fracture mechanics,
a procedure for carrying out a study in fracture mechanics is proposed, making it possible to direct the user at
each stage towards the choice which is the most adequate for the case treated, with reference to the documents of *Code_Aster*.
As one cannot be exhaustive with respect to all the particularities and difficulties encountered during a study
in fracture mechanics, one proposes a signpost towards the dedicated notes and documentations in order to go more deeply into each
subject. In each of the cases, one tries to specify the limitations of the approaches or of the modellings. For a
rapid and occasional reading, one adds tables which make it possible to target certain key information.

The approaches on stability and initiation are dominant in this document, to the detriment of those which treat
tearing. The methods of propagation under monotonic loading for simulating ductile tearing will be developed
subsequently.
