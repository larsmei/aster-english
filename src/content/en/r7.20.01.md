# r7.20.01 Projection of a field onto a mesh

**Summary:**

The command PROJ_CHAMP makes it possible to "project" fields (known on a mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ ) onto another mesh $\mathit{ma2}$ .

This document describes the various projection methods accessible in this command. Paragraph [§ 6 ] gives a few elements of validation of these methods.

## 1 General points on the command PROJ_CHAMP

The command PROJ_CHAMP makes it possible to project the fields at the nodes or the fields by element onto another mesh.

For the fields at the nodes, 4 methods are available:

METHODE: 'COLLOCATION'

METHODE: 'NUAGE_DEG_0'

METHODE: 'NUAGE_DEG_1'

METHODE: 'COUPLAGE'

The method 'COLLOCATION' uses the shape functions of the elements of the mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ . It is detailed in paragraph [§ 2 ].

The 2 other methods use a smoothing of the values of the field in the neighbourhood of the point where one wants to project the field. These 2 methods are detailed in paragraph [§ 3 ].

Finally, the method 'COUPLAGE' is a variant of the method 'COLLOCATION'. It is developed specially for the fluid-structure coupling with *Code_Saturne* and must therefore normally not be used outside this framework.

For the fields by element 'ELNO', one can use the method 'COLLOCATION'. The problem is the same as for a field at the nodes but the projected field is always continuous whereas the initial field is discontinuous. Moreover, if a node of the mesh $\mathit{ma2}$ lies on the boundary of 2 elements of the mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ , the projected value will be random: it will come from one or the other side of the boundary according to the numbering of the elements. This is why it is not advised to project the 'ELNO' fields.

For the fields by element 'ELEM', one can still use the method 'COLLOCATION'. The value of the field (constant per element) is duplicated onto the nodes of the element and one is brought back to the case of the 'ELNO' fields.

For the fields by element 'ELGA', a dedicated method ('ECLA_PG') is described in paragraph [§ 4 ]

Whatever the method, the user has the possibility of projecting only a "piece" of field onto a "piece" of the mesh $\mathit{ma2}$ . This possibility is offered by the factor keyword VIS_A_VIS. A piece of field is the restriction of the field to a set of nodes (or of mesh cells) of the mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ . A piece of the mesh $\mathit{ma2}$ is a subset of the nodes of $\mathit{ma2}$ .

In the remainder of the document, one will no longer speak of a subset of a mesh; one will proceed as if one were projecting the whole mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ onto the whole mesh $\mathit{ma2}$ .

## 2 Method 'COLLOCATION'

**Note on the vocabulary:**

*The word "project" is sometimes ambiguous in this document.*

*When one says "project" the field from* $\mathit{ma1}$ *towards* $\mathit{ma2}$ *, one seeks the field on* $\mathit{ma2}$ *knowing that on* $\mathit{ma1}$ *: the projection goes from 1 towards 2.*

*For the method* *'COLLOCATION', one must find for each node* $\mathit{n2}$ *of* $\mathit{ma2}$ *which is the point of* $\mathit{ma1}$ *that occupies the same position as* $\mathit{n2}$ *; for this one projects the node* $\mathit{n2}$ *onto the mesh* $\mathit{ma1}$ *: the projection goes from 2 towards 1.*

### 2.1 Algorithm implemented

One loops over all the nodes of the mesh $\mathit{ma2}$ :

for each node ($\mathit{n2}$ ), one proceeds in 4 stages:

1. One seeks which is the mesh cell $\mathit{m1}$ of $\mathit{ma1}$ which geometrically "contains" the node n2,
2. One determines an approximate position of $\mathit{n2}$ in $\mathit{m1}$ (i.e. its coordinates in the reference element associated with the mesh cell $\mathit{m1}$ ),
3. One "refines" the position computed previously by solving a non-linear problem in order to take into account the fact that the mesh cells are not linear (the Jacobian of the geometric transformation varies over the mesh cell).
4. One uses the shape functions of the mesh cell $\mathit{m1}$ in order to determine the value of the field on $\mathit{n2}$ knowing the value of the field on the nodes of $\mathit{m1}$ .

#### 2.1.1 Note

The third stage shows that this method assumes that all the nodes of the mesh cell m1 know the field to be projected. For example, one would not know how to project a field which would carry different degrees of freedom on its "vertex" nodes and on its "edge-midpoint" nodes. The projection of such a field is on the other hand possible with the 2 other projection methods [§ 3 ].

### 2.2 The difficulties encountered and their treatment

#### 2.2.1 Stage 1

- One does not treat the possible curvature of the edges of the elements. For example, in the figure below (plane problem), the node $\mathit{n2}$ will be declared to belong to the mesh cell $\mathit{m1a}$ whereas it belongs to the mesh cell $\mathit{m1b}$ . In reality, this problem is rare because, most often, the "curved" edges exist only on the boundary of the meshed domains (the internal facets are plane).

![Figure](/figures/r7.20.01/1000077A0000569C00003FAA35C8219B0D2A06CD.svg)

- If the node $\mathit{n2}$ is in reality "outside" the mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ , one will assign to it the mesh cell $\mathit{m1}$ closest to it. This behaviour makes it possible to project, without stopping in fatal error, a field onto a mesh whose boundary differs slightly from that of the initial mesh (which is always the case in practice).

#### 2.2.2 Stages 2 and 3

In order to find the point of the reference element which gives, by the geometric transformation, the node $\mathit{n2}$ , one must in general solve a non-linear problem, because it is only for the triangle with 3 nodes (in 2D) and for the tetrahedron with 4 nodes (in 3D) that the geometric transformation is linear.

One assumes at first that the mesh cell is linear by forgetting its mid-side nodes and by cutting it arbitrarily into triangles or tetrahedra. In this "linearised" mesh cell one determines an approximate position of the point facing the node $\mathit{n2}$ .

Then one solves by a Newton algorithm the non-linear problem of the precise search for the point, starting from the approximation computed previously.

Note: the non-linear problem is solved in two cases, 2D (projection of a 2D mesh onto another 2D mesh) or 3D (projection of a 3D mesh onto another 3D mesh), but not for the 2.5D cases (shells immersed in 3D space) or 1.5D (wire mesh in 2D),

#### 2.2.3 Stage 4

One does not always use the true shape functions of the elements of the initial mesh. Indeed, in *Code_Aster*, it is the finite elements which choose their shape functions: a thermal triangle is not obliged to choose the same shape functions as a mechanical triangle. An element may also not need shape functions, or it may choose different functions according to the variables to be interpolated. The formulation of the element does not always bring out a reference element and an associated geometric transformation either.

For all these reasons and so that the programming of PROJ_CHAMP is independent of the finite elements present in the model, one assigns to all the mesh cells of $\mathit{ma1}$ the shape functions of the isoparametric elements 1D, 2D or 3D [R3.01.01](doc:r3.01.01).

### 2.3 Details concerning stages 1 and 2

In terms of the software, stages 1 and 2 are carried out simultaneously. We shall discuss in succession the way of treating the following 3 problems:

- treatment of a node $\mathit{n2}$ found inside the boundary of the mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ (the most frequent case),
- treatment of a node $\mathit{n2}$ outside the boundary of $\mathit{ma1}$ ,
- treatment of the case of meshes of "shell" type (surfaces immersed in ${\mathrm{ℝ}}^{3}$ ).

#### 2.3.1 Node n2 "interior"

In order to understand the treatment of an interior node, let us take the case of a 2D mesh cell QUAD8 ($\mathit{abcd}$ ). One begins by forgetting its mid-side nodes (and therefore their possible curvature) then one cuts it into 2 triangles ($\mathit{abc}$  and $\mathit{acd}$ ). This cutting is arbitrary (it depends on the local numbering of the nodes of the QUAD8). Note that the other possible cutting (the other diagonal) would in general give another point in the reference element.

![Figure](/figures/r7.20.01/10001462000069D500002A4500CD708D9C95528E.svg)

$\mathit{n2}$ belongs to the triangle $\mathit{abc}$ . One seeks its barycentric coordinates in this triangle. These are the 3 numbers $\mathit{xa},\mathit{xb},\mathit{xc}$ such that one can write: $\mathit{n2}=\mathit{xa}\times a+\mathit{xb}\times b+\mathit{xc}\times c$

The point N2 of the reference element retained by the algorithm will be:

$\mathit{N2}=\mathit{xa}\times A+\mathit{xb}\times B+\mathit{xc}\times C$

For the volume mesh cells (hexahedra, pentahedra, pyramids and tetrahedra), one proceeds in the same way: one forgets the mid-side nodes and one cuts the non-tetrahedral mesh cells into tetrahedra.

#### 2.3.2 Node n2 "exterior"

A node $\mathit{n2}$ is declared exterior to the mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ if one has found no mesh cell for which it is interior. When this is observed, one searches for the mesh cell $\mathit{m1}$ closest to $\mathit{n2}$ . The distance computed is that which separates the node $\mathit{n2}$ and the boundary of the mesh cell $\mathit{m1}$ . Let us call $\mathit{p2}$ the point of $\mathit{m1}$ closest to $\mathit{n2}$ . This point may be on a face of a volume element or on an edge or on a vertex.

Example (in 2D):

![Figure](/figures/r7.20.01/10000E20000069BB00004F7AE5395A679ADB99A0.svg)

With the node $B$ , one associates the point $\mathit{pB}$ obtained here by orthogonal projection of $B$ onto an edge of $\mathit{T1}$ .

With the node $A$ , one associates the point $\mathit{pA}$ of the triangle $\mathit{T1}$ . One could just as well have associated with it the point pA of the triangle $\mathit{T2}$ , but this would have changed nothing since the field to be projected is known at the nodes of the mesh. It is therefore continuous between the adjacent elements.

Once one has found the point $\mathit{p2}$ of the mesh cell $\mathit{m1}$ which realises the minimum of the distance to $\mathit{n2}$ , one is brought back to the previous problem: the point $\mathit{N2}$ of the reference element which will be associated with $\mathit{n2}$ will be the correspondent of $\mathit{p2}$ by the procedure of paragraph [§ 2.3.1 ].

**Notes:**

*For a given triangle (or tetrahedron)* $T$ *, there is only one point* $\mathit{p2}$ *realising the minimum distance between* $\mathit{n2}$ *and* $T$ *because* $T$ *is convex. This property would disappear if one took into account the curvature of the edges of the mesh cells. One sees there that the two simplifications of the implementation (forgetting the mid-side nodes and cutting the mesh cells into linear triangles) are linked to each other.*

*An exterior point will always have a value interpolated between the values of the nodes of the mesh* $\mathit{ma1}$ *and never extrapolated; which is not the case of the 2 methods* *NUAGE_DEG_0/1.*

#### 2.3.3 Case of the "shell" meshes

When one seeks to project the nodes of a surface mesh onto another surface mesh, one falls in general systematically on the case of the "exterior" points above. Indeed, the inaccuracy on the coordinates of the nodes means that a node $\mathit{n2}$ is never rigorously in the plane of the triangles of the mesh cells of $\mathit{ma1}$ .

It is therefore the procedure of [§ 2.3.2 ] which applies:

For each node $\mathit{n2}$ :

- search for the triangle (or the tetrahedron) which realises the minimum distance to $\mathit{n2}$ . Identification of the point $\mathit{p2}$ which realises this distance,
- computation of the point $\mathit{n2}$ of the reference element which corresponds to $\mathit{p2}$ by the procedure of [§3.3.1].

## 3 Method 'NUAGE_DEG_0' or 'NUAGE_DEG_1'

### 3.1 Principle of the method

These 2 methods are based on the same principle: one chooses a priori basis functions $\mathit{Fi}(x,y,z)$ (here polynomials of degree 0 or 1). One seeks, in the vector space generated by these basis functions, the function $F=\sum({\alpha}_{i}{F}_{i})$ which realises the minimum distance (in the least-squares sense) to the "cloud" of the known points. Once this function has been found, one evaluates it at the point sought.

In order to lighten the notations, one places oneself in 2D (but the analyses can be done in the same way in dimension 3 or more ...). The field to be projected is a set of pairs $(\mathit{Xj},\mathit{Vj})$ where $\mathit{Xj}=(\mathit{xj},\mathit{yj})$ is a node of the mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ and $\mathit{Vj}$ is a real (value of the field on this node). This field constitutes the cloud of the known points.

Let a node $\mathit{n2}$ $(x,y)$ of the mesh $\mathit{ma2}$ for which one wants to compute the value of the field ($V$ ).

Choice of the basis functions:

| NUAGE_DEG_0: | a single function: $\mathit{F1}=1$ |
| --- | --- |
| NUAGE_DEG_1: | 3 functions: $\mathit{F1}=1$ ; $\mathit{F2}=x$ ; $\mathit{F3}=y$ |

In the remainder of this paragraph, one will choose NUAGE_DEG_1 so that the formulae do not degenerate too much.

The function sought is $F=\alpha 1\mathrm{F1}+\alpha 2\mathrm{F2}+\alpha 3\mathrm{F3}$ . One defines a sort of "distance" between $F$ and the pairs $(\mathit{Xj},\mathit{Vj})$ :

$D=\sum(\mathit{wj}{(F({X}_{j})-{V}_{j})}^{2})$

where the $\mathit{wj}$ are the weights assigned to each pair $(\mathit{Xj},\mathit{Vj})$ .

One wants to minimise $D$ with respect to the 3 variables $\alpha 1,\alpha 2,\alpha 3$ . $D$ is a quadratic function of $\alpha 1,\alpha 2,\alpha 3$ . Minimising $D$ amounts to cancelling its derivatives and therefore to solving a linear system with 3 unknowns: $\alpha 1,\alpha 2,\alpha 3$ .

When this problem is solved, one computes:

$V=\alpha 1+\alpha 2x+\alpha 3y$

### 3.2 Choice of the weighting of the points of the cloud

The whole trick of these methods lies in the (difficult) choice of the weights $\mathit{wj}$ assigned to the points of the cloud.

- one decides a priori that the weight of a point ($\mathit{Xj}$ ) depends only on the distance ($d$ ) separating this point from the node $\mathit{n2}$ (isotropic weighting),
- if $\mathit{wj}$ is a constant, the problem no longer depends on the unknown node $\mathit{n2}$ . The function $F$ is unique (for all the nodes of $\mathit{ma2}$ ): it is "the linear regression line" of the cloud,
- if $\mathit{wj}(d)$ is a function that decreases too little, the function $F$ is too "smoothed": the local "accidents" are "smoothed out" by the large number of distant points taken into account,
- if $\mathit{wj}(d)$ is a function that decreases too much, one takes the risk of catching no point of the cloud. The numerical consequence is that the linear system to be solved becomes singular (and therefore insoluble).

We have chosen to write $w(d)$ as a decreasing exponential parameterised by 2 parameters: $\mathit{dref}$ and $\beta$ :

$w(d)={e}^{-{(d/\mathit{dref})}^{2\beta }}$

$\mathit{dref}$ is a reference distance (depending on the node $\mathit{n2}$ ). We shall see below how it is computed. $\beta$ is a constant chosen in order to cancel more or less rapidly the weight of the points distant from $\mathit{n2}$ . In the code, $\beta$ has been chosen equal to 0.75.

$\mathit{dref}$ is the distance from which one wishes to see the weight of the points decrease rapidly. In the programming, $\mathit{dref}$ is computed as the product of a distance $\mathit{d1}$ by a coefficient $\mathit{C1}$ . Today, we have chosen $\mathit{C1}=0.45$ .

$\mathit{d1}$ is defined as follows:

- in 3D, $\mathit{d1}$ is the radius of the smallest ball with centre $\mathit{n2}$ which contains 4 non-coplanar points of the cloud,
- in 2D, $\mathit{d1}$ is the radius of the smallest ball with centre $\mathit{n2}$ which contains 3 non-aligned points of the cloud.

The problem is said to be 2D if all the nodes of the mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ have the same coordinate $z$ ; it is said to be 3D otherwise.

### 3.3 Current restriction of the programming

The 2 projection methods NUAGE_DEG_0/1 are programmed only for real fields (and not for complex fields)

## 4 Method 'ECLA_PG'

This method is dedicated to the projection of the fields at the Gauss points (ELGA).

Let us illustrate this method on a 2D example.

One assumes that the initial mesh is made up of 3 triangles (green) having 3 Gauss points (see the figure below).

Each triangle is cut into as many sub-elements as there are Gauss points (here 3). The value carried by the Gauss point is assigned to the whole sub-element.

## 5 Method 'COUPLAGE'

This method is dedicated to the fluid-structure coupling with *Code_Saturne* for the fluid domain. It is based on the method 'COLLOCATION' taking into account specific features of the fluid-structure problems envisaged (*cf.* [bib2]).

One therefore seeks to project data from a fluid mesh onto a structure mesh, and conversely.

The structure mesh is based on an FE discretisation, therefore with knowledge of the associated shape functions, whereas the fluid mesh is based on a finite-volume (FV) discretisation, therefore without shape functions *a priori*. The kinematic fields on the FV fluid domain are defined at the vertex nodes whereas their dual fields (forces) are constant per face and defined at the midpoints of the faces.

Moreover, the structure mesh is very generally markedly coarser than the fluid mesh (which must be very refined at the wall in order to represent the boundary layer well).

The first stage of pairing of the nodes of the fluid mesh onto the solid is done by a method of classical 'COLLOCATION' type. It must be pointed out that this method can be used only to generate the mesh-correspondence matrix data structure (CORRESP_2_MAILLA *cf.* documentation U4.72.05). This data structure will then be reused by the operators of exchange at the interfaces for the fluid-structure coupling:

- ENV_CINE_YACS (in order to send the displacement and velocity fields to the fluid code, *cf.* documentation U7.06.11),
- MODI_CHAR_YACS (in order to recover the fluid forces and to add them to the existing structural loading, *cf.* documentation U7.06.22).

Apart from this limitation, the main difference with the method 'COLLOCATION' will be in the construction of the correspondence-matrix data structure (CORRESP_2_MAILLA *cf.* documentation U4.72.05) at the output of PROJ_CHAMP.

Indeed, one will seek to respect as well as possible the conservation of energy at the interface, therefore the conservation of the work of the discretised forces during the passage from fluid to solid (*cf.* [bib2]). Obviously, this result is valid only under particular assumptions:

- fluid force fields constant per element,
- fluid mesh finer than the solid mesh,
- pairing method of 'COLLOCATION' type of *Code_Aster*.

In practice, for any coupled fluid-structure problem, one will have to use the method 'COUPLAGE' twice in order to compute two mesh-correspondence data structures. Indeed, one must manage separately the projection of the kinematic fields, which is based on fluid fields defined at the vertex nodes of the fluid mesh, from that of the forces, which are defined at the midpoints of the fluid faces. In each of these two cases, the corresponding fluid mesh will be either the mesh of the vertex nodes, or that of the face-midpoint nodes. The projection method is then the same and the structure mesh is, for its part, unique. All the fluid fields (displacements, velocities and forces at the interface) will be considered by *Code_Aster* as classical fields at the nodes.

## 6 Elements of validation for the projection of the fields at the nodes

In order to validate the 3 proposed methods, we shall treat the example of the projection of a "one-dimensional" temperature field $T=T(x)$ .

The field to be projected is equal to:

$T(x)=\sin(\mathrm{3x})+\mathrm{Heavyside}(x-1)$

This field is assigned to the nodes of a rather coarse linear mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ (14 mesh cells) of the segment $[0,2]$ . This field has the property of being discontinuous (in theory) at the point $x=1$ . Because of the discretisation on the mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ , the field seems only to vary very abruptly between the 2 points $x=0.99$ and $x=1.01$ .

One projects this field onto a very fine mesh $\mathit{ma2}$ of the same segment (300 elements of length $2/300$ ).

### 6.3 Method: 'NUAGE_DEG_1'

One notes that with this method, the projected field is correct in the 2 regular zones ($x<0.99$  and $x>1.01$ ).

On the other hand the discontinuity of the field between these 2 values is strongly exaggerated. This example illustrates well the ability of this method to extrapolate the values of the initial points (because of the estimation of the gradient of the field).

In the 2 regular parts, the projected field is fairly close to that obtained with the method 'COLLOCATION'. One simply notes that the method 'NUAGE_DEG_1' rounds the corners a little

### 6.4 Choice of the best projection method for the fields at the nodes

In view of the few preceding curves, it seems clear that the method 'COLLOCATION' is in general preferable to the methods NUAGE_DEG_0/1. This method is "natural" within the framework of the finite elements and it depends on no numerical adjustment coefficient.

The methods NUAGE_DEG_0/1 must be reserved, in our opinion, for somewhat special uses:

- case of a "non-existent" mesh $\mathit{ma1}$ : one has available only the nodes but not the mesh cells (for example, the "nodes" of $\mathit{ma1}$ are in fact measurement sensors),
- case of a field (result of an analysis or obtained by measurements) which one wants to smooth deliberately. But in this case, the 2 numerical parameters ($\mathit{C1}$  and $\beta$ ) would have to be accessible to the user, which is not the case today.
