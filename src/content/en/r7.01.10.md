## 1 Introduction

The THM modules are those which treat the equations of continuum mechanics using the theory of porous media, possibly unsaturated, and considering that the mechanical, thermal and hydraulic phenomena are fully coupled.

We present here the equilibrium equations, or conservation equations solved by these modules. We give a definition of the generalised stresses and generalised strains, making it possible to define in a fairly general way what a THM constitutive law is — at least what the modules considered regard as such — and making it possible to treat the non-linear equations exhibited within the framework of the algorithms of the operator STAT_NON_LINE.

The THM constitutive laws proper are not developed in this document, but in the document [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11).

Chemical phenomena (transformations of the constituents, reactions producing constituents, etc.), as well as radiological phenomena, are not taken into account. The mechanical, hydraulic and thermal phenomena are or are not taken into account according to the constitutive behaviour invoked by the user in the command STAT_NON_LINE, according to the following nomenclature:

| **Modelling** | **Phenomena taken into account** |
| --- | --- |
| KIT_HM | Mechanics, hydraulics with one unknown pressure |
| KIT_HHM | Mechanics, hydraulics with two unknown pressures |
| KIT_THH | Thermal, hydraulics with two unknown pressures |
| KIT_THM | Thermal, mechanics, hydraulics with one unknown pressure |
| KIT_THHM | Thermal, mechanics, hydraulics with two unknown pressures |

Certain modellings (see documents [U3.13.08](doc:u3.13.08) and [U3.14.07](doc:u3.14.07)) of THM are available with regularisation by a second gradient of dilatation. For the aspects relating to the second gradient of these modellings, refer to [R5.04.03](doc:r5.04.03).

## 2 Presentation of the problem: assumptions, notation

In this chapter, the aim is mainly to present the porous medium and its characteristics.

### 2.1 Description of the porous medium

The porous medium considered is a volume made up of a solid matrix that is more or less homogeneous, more or less cohesive (very cohesive in the case of concrete, little so in the case of sand). Between the solid elements, one finds pores. One distinguishes the closed pores, which exchange nothing with their neighbours, and the connected pores, in which the exchanges are numerous. When one speaks of porosity, it is indeed the connected pores that one means. Inside these pores there are at most two constituents present in at most two phases. The system is considered closed.

### 2.2 Notation

The quantities associated with a constituent $c$ present in a phase $p$ are denoted ${X}_{c}^{p}$ . The index of the component $c$ may vary from $1$ to $2$ and that of the phase likewise. These constituents may be (and will be indexed as such when needed):

$w$ for liquid water,

$\text{ad}$ for dissolved air,

$\text{as}$ for dry air,

$\text{vp}$ for water vapour.

The porous medium at the current time instant is denoted $\Omega ` , sa frontière :math:$partial Omega ` , and it is denoted ${\Omega}_{0},\partial {\Omega}_{0}$ at the initial time instant.

$n$ denotes the normal at a point of $\partial \Omega$ , the image of the normal ${n}_{0}$ to $\partial {\Omega}_{0}$ . We shall denote $d(\partial \Omega )$ (respectively $d(\partial {\Omega}_{0})$ ) the surface element of $\partial \Omega$ (respectively $\partial {\Omega}_{0}$ ).

The medium is defined by:

- parameters (position vector $x$ , time $t$ ),
- variables (displacements, pressures, temperature),
- intrinsic quantities (stresses and strains, mass supplies, heat, enthalpies, hydraulic fluxes, thermal...).

For the solid phase, the small-displacement assumption is made.

The various notations are set out below.

#### 2.2.1 Descriptive variables of the medium

These are the variables whose knowledge as a function of time and of place makes it possible to know completely the state of the medium. These variables fall into two categories:

- geometric variables,
- thermodynamic state variables.

##### 2.2.1.1 Geometric variables

In all that follows, a Lagrangian representation with respect to the skeleton is adopted (in the sense of [bib1]) and the coordinates $\mathrm{x}={\mathrm{x}}_{s}(t)$ are those of a material point attached to the skeleton. All the spatial differentiation operators are defined with respect to these coordinates.

The displacements of the skeleton are denoted $u(x,t)=(\begin{array}{c}{u}_{x}\\ {u}_{y}\\ {u}_{z}\end{array})$ .

##### 2.2.1.2 Thermodynamic state variables

The thermodynamic variables are:

- the pressures of the constituents: given that we consider that there are at most two constituents, there will be at most two equations of conservation of mass, and therefore by duality at most two pressure variables,
- the temperature of the medium $T(x,t)$ .

##### 2.2.1.3 Descriptive fields of the medium

The principal unknowns, which are also the nodal unknowns (denoted $U(x,t)$ in this document) are:

- 2 or 3 (according to the space dimension) displacements ${u}_{x}(x,t),{u}_{y}(x,t),{u}_{z}(x,t)$ for the modellings KIT_HM, KIT_HHM, KIT_THM, KIT_THHM,
- the temperature $T(x,t)$ for the modellings KIT_THH, KIT_THM, KIT_THHM,
- two pressures ${p}_{1}(x,t),{p}_{2}(x,t)$ for the modellings KIT_HHM, KIT_THH, KIT_THHM,
- one pressure ${p}_{1}(x,t)$ for the modellings KIT_HM, KIT_THM.

#### 2.2.2 Quantities

The equilibrium equations are:

- the conservation of momentum for the mechanics,
- the conservation of the fluid masses for the hydraulics,
- the conservation of energy for the thermal problem.

The equilibrium equations involve the generalised stresses directly. The generalised stresses are related to the generalised strains by the constitutive laws. The generalised strains are computed directly from the state variables and from their spatial and temporal gradients.

The constitutive laws may use auxiliary quantities, often stored in the internal variables. These quantities are not described in this document, which does not deal with the constitutive laws proper.

##### 2.2.2.1 Characteristic quantities of the heterogeneous medium

- The Eulerian porosity: $\phi$ .

If one denotes by ${\Omega}_{\varphi}$ the part of the volume $\Omega$ occupied by the voids in the current configuration, one has:

$\varphi =\frac{{\Omega}_{\varphi}}{\Omega}$ **eq 2.2.2.1-1**

The definition of the porosity $\varphi$ is therefore that of the Eulerian porosity.

- The saturation of the phase $p$ : ${S}^{p}$ .

If one denotes by ${\Omega}^{p}$ the total volume occupied by the phase $p$ , in the current configuration, one has by definition:

${S}^{p}=\frac{{\Omega}^{p}}{{\Omega}_{j}}$ **eq 2.2.2.1-2**

This saturation is therefore finally a proportion varying between $0$ and $1$ . In the balance equations, it is clear that it is the product of the porosity by the saturation $\phi {S}^{p}$ which will appear. One may therefore legitimately wonder why it is not this quantity which is taken as the unknown. The answer comes from the fact that it is the saturation ${S}^{p}$ which appears more simply in the constitutive laws.

- The Eulerian density of the constituent $c$ in the phase $p$ : ${\rho}_{c}^{p}$ .

If one denotes by ${M}_{c}^{p}$ the mass of the phase $p$ of the constituent $c$ , in the volume $\Omega$ of the skeleton in the current configuration, one has by definition:

${M}_{c}^{p}={\int}_{{\Omega}^{p}}{{\rho}_{c}^{p}d\Omega }^{p}={\int}_{{\Omega}_{\phi }}{\rho}_{c}^{p}{S}^{p}d{\Omega}_{\phi }={\int}_{\Omega}{\rho}_{c}^{p}{S}^{p}\phid \Omega$ **eq 2.2.2.1-3**

The density of the phase $p$ is simply the sum of the densities of its constituents:

${\rho}^{p}=\sum_{c}{\rho}_{c}^{p}$

- The homogenised Lagrangian density: $r$ .

At the current time instant, the mass of the volume $\Omega$ , ${M}_{\Omega}$  is given by:

${M}_{\Omega}={\int}_{{\Omega}_{0}}rd{\Omega}_{0}$ **eq 2.2.2.1-4**

##### 2.2.2.2 Mechanical quantities

- The strain tensor $\varepsilon (u)(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla u{}^{T}+\nabla u)$ ,
- The stress tensor which acts on the porous medium: $\sigma$ .

This tensor is decomposed into an effective-stress tensor plus a pressure-stress tensor $\sigma =\sigma '+{\sigma}_{p}I$ . $\sigma '\text{et}{\sigma}_{p}$ are components of the generalised stresses. This splitting is finally rather arbitrary, but it does correspond to an assumption that is fairly commonly accepted, at least for media saturated with liquid.

##### 2.2.2.3 Hydraulic quantities

- The mass supplies of constituents ${m}_{c}^{p}$ (unit: kilogram per cubic metre). They represent the mass of fluid supplied between the initial and current time instants. They form part of the generalised stresses.

${m}_{c}^{p}=J{\rho}_{c}^{p}\phi {S}^{p}-{\rho}_{c}^{{p}_{0}}{\phi }_{0}{S}_{0}^{p}$ **eq 2.2.2.3-1**

The mass supplies make it possible to define the global density seen with respect to the reference configuration: $r(x,t)={r}_{0}+{m}_{\mathrm{lq}}(x,t)+{m}_{\mathrm{vp}}(x,t)+{m}_{\mathrm{as}}(x,t)$ , where ${r}_{0}$ denotes the homogenised density in the initial state.

- The hydraulic fluxes:

${w}_{c}^{p}$ (unit: kilogram/second/square metre) in Eulerian representation

${M}_{c}^{p}$ (unit: kilogram/second/square metre) in Lagrangian representation

One denotes by ${v}_{c}^{p}$ the velocity of the constituent $c$ in the phase $p$ , $J$ the Jacobian of the material transformation and ${v}_{S}=\frac{du}{\mathrm{dt}}$ the velocity of the skeleton. ${\rho}_{c0}^{p},{\phi }_{0},{S}_{0}^{p}$ denote the densities, the porosity and the saturations at the initial time instant. By definition:

${w}_{c}^{p}={\rho}_{c}^{p}\phi {S}^{p}({v}_{c}^{p}-{v}_{s})$ **eq 2.2.2.3-2**

The Lagrangian form of

denoted

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.10/100001E20000422500003ED747D484C701BC6F62.svg)

is obtained by writing:

${M}_{c}^{p}.{n}_{0}d(\partial {\Omega}_{0})={w}_{c}^{p}.nd(\partial \Omega )$ **eq 2.2.2.3-3**

The variables ${m}_{1},{M}_{1}$ and ${m}_{2},{M}_{2}$ each relate to a constituent **of conservative mass** .

One sets in principle:

$\begin{array}{}{m}_{1}={m}_{1}^{1}+{m}_{1}^{2};{M}_{1}={M}_{1}^{1}+{M}_{1}^{2}\\ {m}_{2}={m}_{2}^{1}+{m}_{2}^{2};{M}_{2}={M}_{2}^{1}+{M}_{2}^{2}\end{array}$

Which we shall write:

$\begin{array}{}{m}_{\mathrm{constituant}}=\sum_{\begin{array}{}\mathrm{nb}\mathrm{phase}\mathrm{du}\\ \mathrm{constituant}\end{array}}{m}_{\mathrm{constituant}}^{\mathrm{phase}}\\ {M}_{\mathrm{constituant}}=\sum_{\begin{array}{}\mathrm{nb}\mathrm{phase}\mathrm{du}\\ \mathrm{constituant}\end{array}}{M}_{\mathrm{constituant}}^{\mathrm{phase}}\end{array}$

In the applications, one could for example have:

- 2 constituents: air and water,
- 2 phases for the water,
- 1 phase for the air.

One would then have: ${m}_{1}^{1}\text{et}{M}_{1}^{1}$ : mass supply and flux of liquid water

${m}_{1}^{2}\text{et}{M}_{1}^{2}$ : mass supply and flux of vapour

${m}_{2}^{1}\text{et}{M}_{2}^{1}$ : mass supply and flux of dry air

${m}_{2}^{2}\text{et}{M}_{2}^{2}$ : non-existent

- The pressures:

Since we consider that there may be two constituents other than the solid, there are two equations of conservation of mass, and therefore two associated multipliers, that is two pressures ${p}_{1}\text{et}{p}_{2}$ . No assumption is made as to what these two pressures ${p}_{1}\text{et}{p}_{2}$ mean. That will depend on the constitutive laws and on the way of writing them. For example one may choose:

$\begin{array}{}{p}_{1}=\text{pression capillaire}(\text{p}(\text{gaz})-\text{p}(\text{liquide}))\\ {p}_{2}=\text{pression de gaz}(\text{vapeur + air})\end{array}$

##### 2.2.2.4 Thermal quantities

- The non-convected heat $Q'$ (see below) (unit: Joule),
- The mass enthalpies of the constituents ${{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}$  (unit: Joule/Kelvin/kilogram),
- The heat flux: $q$ (unit: J/s/square metre).

#### 2.2.3 External data

- The mass force ${\mathrm{F}}^{m}$ (in practice gravity),
- The heat sources $\Theta$ ,
- The boundary conditions bearing either on imposed variables or on imposed fluxes.

### 2.3 Particle derivatives, volumetric and mass densities

The description we make of the medium is Lagrangian with respect to the skeleton. One will find in [bib1] a definition of the notion of skeleton: “the matrix (solid part + occluded porosity) constituting the material part of the skeleton and the connected pore space of the elementary volume in question constitute the material point of the skeleton or particle of the skeleton”.

Let $a$ be any field on $\Omega$ , let ${x}_{s}(t)$ be the coordinate of a point attached to the skeleton which we follow in its motion and let ${x}_{\mathrm{fl}}(t)$ be the coordinate of a point attached to the fluid. One denotes by $\dot{a}=\frac{{d}^{s}a}{\mathrm{dt}}$ the time derivative in the motion of the skeleton:

$\dot{a}=\frac{{d}^{s}a}{\mathrm{dt}}=\underset{\deltat \to 0}{\lim}\frac{a({x}_{s}(t+\deltat ),t+\deltat )-a({x}_{s}(t),t)}{\deltat }$

$\dot{a}$ is called the particle derivative and is often denoted $\frac{\mathrm{da}}{\mathrm{dt}}$ (for example in [bib1]). We prefer to use a notation which recalls that the configuration used to locate a particle is that of the skeleton, with respect to which a fluid particle has a relative velocity. For a fluid particle the location ${x}_{s}(t)$ is arbitrary, that is, the fluid particle which occupies the position ${x}_{s}(t)$ at the time instant $t$ is not the same as the one which occupies the position ${x}_{S}(t')$ at another time instant $t'$ .

Let then $A=\underset{\Omega}{\int}ad\Omega$ be a quantity linked to a *volumetric* density $a$ , which density is itself carried *partly by the solid grains and by the fluids* . Let ${{a}^{m}}_{c}^{p}$ be the *mass* density of $a$ carried by the fluid phase $p$ of the constituent $c$ and let ${a}_{s}$ be the *volumetric* density of $a$ linked to the solid grains. All these definitions finally amount to writing:

$A={\int}_{\Omega}\mathrm{ad}\Omega ={A}_{s}+{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}={\int}_{\Omega}{a}_{s}d\Omega +{\int}_{\Omega}{a}_{\mathrm{fl}}d\Omega ={\int}_{\Omega}({a}_{s}+\sum_{p,c}{\rho}_{c}^{p}{\mathrm{jS}}^{p}{{a}^{m}}_{c}^{p})d\Omega$ **eq 2.3-1**

Following [bib1], we denote by $\frac{{d}^{\mathrm{fl}}{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}}{\mathrm{dt}}$ the derivative of ${A}_{\mathrm{fl}}$ if we follow $\Omega$ in the motion of the fluid and $\frac{{d}^{s}{A}_{s}}{\mathrm{dt}}$ the derivative of ${A}_{s}$ if we follow $\Omega$ in the motion of the skeleton.

We then define:

$\frac{\mathrm{DA}}{\mathrm{Dt}}=\frac{{d}^{s}{A}_{s}}{\mathrm{dt}}+\frac{{d}^{\mathrm{fl}}{A}_{\mathrm{fl}}}{\mathrm{dt}}=\frac{{d}^{s}}{\mathrm{dt}}\int{}_{\Omega}\text{}{a}_{s}d\Omega +\frac{{d}^{\mathrm{fl}}}{\mathrm{dt}}\int{}_{\Omega}\text{}\sum_{p,c}{\rho}_{c}^{p}\phi {S}^{p}{a}^{{m}_{c}^{p}}d\Omega$ **eq 2.3-2**

The density ${{a}^{m}}_{c}^{p}$ is transported with a relative velocity of $({v}_{c}^{p}-{v}_{s})$ with respect to the skeleton. Taking into account the definition of $\dot{a}=\frac{{d}^{s}a}{\mathrm{dt}}$ , and of the definition ${w}_{c}^{p}={\rho}_{c}^{p}\phi {S}^{p}({v}_{c}^{p}-{v}_{s})$ , one easily sees that the total derivative of $A$ with respect to time is finally written:

$\frac{\mathrm{DA}}{\mathrm{Dt}}=\int{}_{\Omega}\text{}(\dot{a}+\sum_{p,c}\text{Div}({{a}^{m}}_{c}^{p}{w}_{c}^{p}))d\Omega$ **eq 2.3-3**

**Note:**

*In so far as we have made the assumption of small displacements of the skeleton,* $\dot{a}=\frac{{d}^{s}a}{\mathrm{dt}}$ *may be identified with the partial derivative with respect to time* $\frac{\partial a}{\partial t}$ *and* ${v}_{s}$ *may be considered as zero. Likewise, in the remainder of the note we shall identify the Lagrangian and Eulerian representations of the fluxes,* ${M}_{c}^{p}$ *and* ${w}_{c}^{p}$ *.*

## 3 Continuous equations

### 3.1 Mechanics: conservation of momentum

We denote by $\sigma$ the Cauchy stress tensor and $s$ the second (symmetric) Piola-Kirchhoff tensor.

We denote by $P$ the gradient of the transformation ${x}_{0}={x}_{S}(0)\to {x}_{S}({x}_{0},t)$ .

$P=\frac{\partial {x}_{S}({x}_{0},t)}{\partial {x}_{0}}$

One has: $s=\detP.{P}^{-1}.\sigma .{P}^{-T}$ .

The mechanical equilibrium equations are written in the configuration ${\Omega}_{0}$ :

${\text{Div}}_{0}(P.s)+r{F}^{m}=0$

We have denoted by ${\text{Div}}_{0}$ the divergence operator with respect to the space variables ${x}_{0}$ of the configuration ${\Omega}_{0}$ .

In so far as we make the assumption of small displacements and small strains of the skeleton, this equation may be approximated by:

$\text{Div}\sigma +r{F}^{m}=0$ **eq 3.1-1**

We shall see below that we always adopt the decomposition $\sigma =\sigma '+{\sigma}_{p}I$ , where $\sigma '$ denotes the effective stress. It is therefore the responsibility of the module for integration of the equilibrium equations to form the sum: $\sigma =\sigma '+{\sigma}_{p}I$ .

### 3.2 Hydraulics: conservation of mass

The Eulerian writing of the conservation of the fluid mass for the constituent $c$ is written:

$\frac{{d}^{\mathrm{fl}}}{\mathrm{dt}}\int{}_{\Omega}\text{}\sum_{p}{\rho}_{c}^{p}\phi {S}^{p}d\Omega =0$

One may then apply [eq 2.3-1] by taking: ${a}_{s}=0$ and ${{a}^{m}}_{c}^{p}=1$ and [eq 2.3-3] will give:

$\sum_{p}\frac{{d}^{s}{\rho}_{c}^{p}\phi {S}^{p}}{\mathrm{dt}}+\sum_{p}\text{Div}({w}_{c}^{p})=0$

Using the definition of the mass supplies [eq 2.2.2.3-3] , the definition of the Lagrangian fluxes [eq 2.2.2.3-2] one finds the Lagrangian form of the conservation of the fluid mass:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}\dot{{m}_{1}}+{\text{Div}}_{0}({M}_{1})=0\\ \dot{{m}_{2}}+{\text{Div}}_{0}({M}_{2})=0\end{array}$ **eq 3.2-1**

### 3.3 Energy equation

For the thermodynamic functions, we systematically adopt a decomposition of the type [eq 2.3-1]. This corresponds to the fact that the various energies all have a part carried by the solid and a part carried by the fluids. The part carried by the solid is characterised by a volumetric density whereas the parts carried by the fluid are characterised by mass densities, as we have shown in paragraph [§2.3].

**Total internal energy:** $E=\int{}_{\Omega}\text{}({e}_{s}+\sum_{p,c}{\rho}_{c}^{p}\phi {S}^{p}{{e}^{m}}_{c}^{p})d\Omega$ **eq 3.3.1**

**Total entropy:** $S=\int{}_{\Omega}\text{}({s}_{s}+\sum_{p,c}{\rho}_{c}^{p}\phi {S}^{p}{{s}^{m}}_{c}^{p})d\Omega$ **eq 3.3.2**

**Total enthalpy:** $H=\int{}_{\Omega}\text{}({h}_{s}+\sum_{p,c}{\rho}_{c}^{p}\phi {S}^{p}{{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p})d\Omega$ **eq 3.3.3**

**Free energy:** $\lbrace \begin{array}{}\Psi =E-TS\\ {\Psi}_{s}={e}_{s}-{\text{Ts}}_{s}\\ {{\Psi}^{m}}_{c}^{p}={{e}^{m}}_{c}^{p}-T{{s}^{m}}_{c}^{p}\end{array}$ **eq 3.3.4**

**Free enthalpy:** $\lbrace \begin{array}{}G=H-TS\\ {g}_{s}={h}_{s}-T{s}_{s}\\ {{g}^{m}}_{c}^{p}={{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}-T{{s}^{m}}_{c}^{p}\end{array}$ **eq 3.3.5**

Finally, denoting by $\dot{Q}(\Omega )$ the rate of heat received by a volume $\Omega$ , one has by definition:

$\dot{Q}(\Omega )=\underset{\partial \Omega }{\int}q.nd\Gamma +\underset{\Omega}{\int}\Thetad \Omega$ **eq 3.3.6**

One finally recalls that the enthalpy of the fluids is computed by the formula:

$h=e+\frac{p}{\rho}$ **eq 3.3.7**

#### 3.3.1 The first principle

With the definitions given above, it is written:

$-\sum_{p,c}\text{Div}\left({{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}{\mathrm{M}}_{c}^{p}\right)+\sigma :\dot{\epsilon}+\sum_{p,c}{\mathrm{M}}_{c}^{p}.{\mathrm{F}}^{m}+\Theta -\text{Div}q=0$ **eq 3.3.1-1**

This writing corresponds to equation (22) of chapter III-2-3 of [bib1], in which we have neglected the inertia terms. For homogeneous media, it corresponds to equation (31) of paragraph IV-3-2 of [bib3].

#### 3.3.2 The second principle

Its fairly well-known form is:

$\dot{s}+\sum_{p,c}\text{Div}({{s}^{m}}_{c}^{p}{M}_{c}^{p})+\text{Div}(\frac{q}{T})-\frac{\Theta}{T}\ge 0$ **eq 3.3.2-1**

Using the classical thermodynamic considerations [bib1] linked to the introduction of the free enthalpy [eq 3.3.5], one shows that one must necessarily have:

$\sigma -\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial \varepsilon }=0$ **eq 3.3.2-2**

${{g}^{m}}_{c}^{p}-\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial {m}_{c}^{p}}=0$ **eq 3.3.2-3**

$s+\frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial T}=0$ **eq 3.3.2-4**

#### 3.3.3 Energy equation

Quite often, one considers that, the transformations being reversible, the second principle finally provides an equality. Moreover, one replaces in [eq 3.3.2-1] the unknown temperature $T$ by a constant value called the reference temperature. This is finally a linearisation of [eq 3.3.2-1] justified if the temperature variations are “small”. Note that the transport term $\sum_{p,c}\text{Div}({{s}^{m}}_{c}^{p}{M}_{c}^{p})$ complicates the treatment of the non-linearity due to the presence of the temperature in the denominator of the other terms of [eq 3.3.2-1].

We work in enthalpy so as to remove this difficulty. One starts from the equation of the first principle [eq 3.3.1-1] into which one injects the equations [eq 3.3.2-2], [eq 3.3.2-3], [eq 3.3.2-4], and the definition of the free enthalpy [eq 3.3-5] and one obtains:

$T\dot{s}+\sum_{p,c}({{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}\dot{{m}_{c}^{p}}-T{{s}^{m}}_{c}^{p}\dot{{m}_{c}^{p}})=-\sum_{p,c}\text{Div}({{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}{M}_{c}^{p})+\sum_{p,c}{M}_{c}^{p}.{F}^{m}+\Theta -\text{Div}q$ **eq 3.3.3-1**

One then sets:

$\deltaq '=T\deltas -T\sum_{p,c}{s}^{{m}_{c}^{p}}\delta {m}_{c}^{p}$ **eq 3.3.3-2**

The quantity $Q'$ has the dimension of an energy per unit volume. It represents the heat received by the system in a transformation for which there is no heat supply by entry of a fluid having an enthalpy. Although $\deltaq '$ is not an exact differential, we take this quantity as a state variable.

Finally, the energy equation retained has the following form:

$\sum_{p,c}{{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}\dot{{m}_{c}^{p}}+\dot{Q'}+\sum_{p,c}\text{Div}({{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}{M}_{c}^{p})+\text{Div}q-\sum_{p,c}{M}_{c}^{p}.{F}^{m}=\Theta$ **eq 3.3.3-3**

## 4 Variational writing of the equilibrium equations

### 4.1 Mechanics

We denote by ${U}_{ad}$ the set of kinematically admissible displacement fields, that is the elements of ${({H}^{1}(\Omega ))}^{3}$ satisfying the boundary conditions in displacement on the part of $\partial \Omega$ supporting such conditions [bib3].

The variational form of [eq 3.1-1] is:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\sigma =\sigma '+{\sigma}_{p}\mathrm{I}\\ \int{}_{\Omega}\text{}\sigma .\epsilon \left(\mathrm{v}\right)=\int{}_{\Omega}\text{}r{\mathrm{F}}^{m}.\mathrm{v}d\Omega +\int{}_{\partial \Omega }\text{}{\mathrm{f}}^{\mathit{ext}}.\mathrm{v}d\Gamma \text{}\forall \mathrm{v}\in {U}_{\mathit{ad}}\end{array}$ **eq 4.1-1**

### 4.2 Hydraulics

We denote by ${P}_{1\mathrm{ad}}$ (resp. ${P}_{2\mathrm{ad}}$ ) the set of admissible pressure fields ${\pi}_{1}$ (resp. ${\pi}_{2}$ ), that is the elements of ${H}^{1}(\Omega )$ satisfying the boundary conditions in pressure ${P}_{1}$ (resp. ${P}_{2}$ ) on the part of $\partial \Omega$ supporting such conditions [bib3]. The variational form of [eq 3.2-1] is:

$\begin{array}{}-{\int}_{\Omega}(\dot{{m}_{1}^{1}}+\dot{{m}_{1}^{2}}).{\pi}_{1}+{\int}_{\Omega}({M}_{1}^{1}+{M}_{1}^{2}).\nabla {\pi}_{1}d\Omega =\text{}\\ {\int}_{\partial \Omega }({M}_{1\mathrm{ext}}^{1}+{M}_{1\mathrm{ext}}^{2}).{\pi}_{1}d\Gamma \forall {\pi}_{1}\in {P}_{1\mathrm{ad}}\\ -{\int}_{\Omega}(\dot{{m}_{2}^{1}}+\dot{{m}_{2}^{2}}).{\pi}_{2}+{\int}_{\Omega}({M}_{2}^{1}+{M}_{2}^{2}).\nabla {\pi}_{2}d\Omega =\text{}\\ {\int}_{\partial \Omega }({M}_{2\mathrm{ext}}^{1}+{M}_{2\mathrm{ext}}^{2}).{\pi}_{2}d\Gamma \forall {\pi}_{2}\in {P}_{2\mathrm{ad}}\end{array}\rbrace$ **eq 4.2-1**

which brings out the scalar hydraulic fluxes ${M}_{i\mathrm{ext}}^{j}$ on the boundaries.

### 4.3 Thermal

We denote by ${T}_{\mathrm{ad}}$ the set of admissible temperature fields $\tau$ , that is the elements of ${H}^{1}(\Omega )$ satisfying the boundary conditions in temperature on the part of $\partial \Omega$ supporting such conditions. [bib3]. The variational form of [eq 3.3.3-3] is:

$\begin{array}{}{\int}_{\Omega}\dot{Q}'.\tau d\Omega +\sum_{p,c}{\int}_{\Omega}{{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}\dot{{m}_{c}^{p}}.\tau d\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}(\sum_{p,c}{{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}{M}_{c}^{p}+q).\nabla \tau d\Omega =\text{}\\ {\int}_{\Omega}(\Theta +\sum_{p,c}{M}_{c}^{p}.{F}^{m}).\tau d\Omega -{\int}_{\partial \Omega }(\sum_{p,c}{{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}{M}_{c\mathrm{ext}}^{p}+{q}_{\mathrm{ext}}).\tau d\Gamma \\ \forall \tau \in {T}_{\mathrm{ad}}\end{array}$ **eq 4.3-1**

Note that, unlike other presentations, and in particular [bib8], we have not injected the equations of conservation of mass, and we have integrated by parts the transport term $\sum_{p,c}\text{Div}({{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}{M}_{c}^{p})$ .

This last point has the advantage of not bringing out derivatives of higher order, and, on the contrary, of bringing out naturally boundary conditions relating to the entry of heat linked to the hydraulic fluxes: $\sum_{p,c}{\int}_{\partial \Omega }{{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}{M}_{c\mathrm{ext}}^{p}.\tau d\Gamma$ .

One may in fact consider that the thermal-flux conditions define directly:

$\stackrel{~}{{q}_{\mathit{ext}}}={{h}^{m}}_{c}^{p}{M}_{c\mathit{ext}}^{p}+{q}_{\mathit{ext}}$

## 5 Discretisation in time

In this chapter, we merely take up the variational formulations again, applying to them a discretisation with respect to time of theta-scheme type for the hydraulics and the thermal problem. This is a general method of integration of differential equations [bib12] and [bib13].

$\theta$ is a numerical parameter lying between $0$ and $1$ . For linear differential equations (which is not our case …) this scheme is unconditionally stable for $\theta \ge 1/2$ , it is of order $1$ for $\theta \ne 1/2$ and of order 2 for $\theta =1/2$ . ## 6 Principle of virtual work, generalised strains and stresses, constitutive laws

### 6.1 Generalised stresses and strains

Referring to the variational formulations [eq 4.1-1], [eq 4.2-1] and [eq 4.3-1], it appears that one may choose:

For the generalised stresses:

$\Sigma =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{}\sigma ',{\sigma}_{p};\\ {m}_{1}^{1},{M}_{1}^{1},{h}_{1}^{m1};{m}_{1}^{2},{M}_{1}^{2},{h}_{1}^{m2};\\ {m}_{2}^{1},{M}_{2}^{1},{h}_{2}^{m1};{m}_{2}^{2},{M}_{2}^{2},{h}_{2}^{m2};\\ Q',q\end{array}\right\rbrace$ **eq 6.1-1**

For the generalised strains:

$\mathrm{\Epsilon }=\left\lbrace u,\varepsilon (u);{p}_{1},\nabla {p}_{1};{p}_{2},\nabla {p}_{2};T,\nabla T\right\rbrace$ **eq 6.1-2**

One notes the fact that the generalised strains contain the displacements. This is due to the term ${\int}_{\Omega}r{F}^{m}.v$ of the variational formulation of the equation of conservation of momentum [eq 4.1-1], which term finally couples the generalised stresses and the displacements because of [eq 6.3.4-1]. The generalised strains contain the pressure and the temperature because the associated equations are parabolic.

### 6.2 Principle of virtual work

The set of non-linear equations to be solved may be put in the form:

$\mathrm{R}\left(\mathrm{U}\right)={\mathrm{L}}^{\mathit{meca}}$ **eq 6.2-1**

where $U$ denotes the generalised displacements, that is: $U=\left\lbrace {u}_{x},{u}_{y},{u}_{z},{p}_{1},{p}_{2},T\right\rbrace$ in the most general case. The internal forces $R$ are expressed from a generalised principle of virtual work. In the case of “classical” continuum mechanics, that is when there is no constituent other than the solid, one is used to defining the internal forces by:

${w}^{T}.R={\int}_{\Omega}\varepsilon (w).\sigma d\Omega$ $\forall w$ , kinematically admissible displacement field.

In this formulation, the strain field $\varepsilon$ depends only on the displacement field and on its spatial derivatives, possibly in a non-linear way if one takes finite strains into account. One writes symbolically:

$R={Q}^{T}\sigma$

The constitutive law relates the stresses $\sigma$ to the strains $\varepsilon$ .

Within the framework of the theory of porous media developed here, we try to stay as close as possible to this formulation by introducing generalised stresses $\Sigma$ and generalised strains $E$ ; The generalised strains depend only on the generalised displacement field $U$ and on its spatial derivatives. The operator $U\to E(U)$ is an operator of differentiation with respect to the coordinate field.

The constitutive law makes it possible to compute $\Sigma$ as a function of $E$ .

On the other hand, we cannot write directly ${W}^{T}.R={\int}_{\Omega}\mathrm{\Epsilon }(W).\Sigmad \Omega$ , for the following reasons:

- the equations we treat are evolutionary equations in time and the derivatives with respect to time of the quantities appear,
- the equations are non-linear because of the transport terms linked to the Eulerian representation of the fluids: these non-linear terms appear only in the thermal equation,
- the choice of the unknowns means that the non-linear transport terms appear in the generalised stresses. Take a transport term in equation [eq 4.3-1], ${\int}_{\Omega}(\sum_{p,c}{h}_{c}^{mp}{M}_{c}^{p}+q).\nabla \taud \Omega$ , given that one has taken the pressures as the principal unknowns for the hydraulics, the quantities ${M}_{c}^{p}$ linked to the velocities of the fluids belong to the generalised stresses, as do the enthalpies ${h}_{c}^{mp}$ , and the transport term given as an example is linear in generalised strain and quadratic in generalised stress. This makes a difference with the formulation of the classical continuum theory where the large-strain terms are quadratic quantities of the strains.

For all these reasons, we introduce a field denoted $\overline{\Sigma}$ such that:

$R={Q}^{T}\overline{\Sigma}$ **eq 6.2-2**

${Q}^{T}$ is defined by:

$\begin{array}{}{W}^{T}.R={\int}_{\Omega}E(W).\overline{\Sigma}d\Omega \\ \forall W\text{champ de déplacement généralisé cinématiquement admissible}\end{array}$ **eq 6.2-3**

One sees easily and very classically that ${Q}^{T}$ is the transpose of the operator $Q$ such that:

$E=\mathrm{QU}$ **eq 6.1-4**

The field $\overline{\Sigma}$ is a linear function of $\dot{\Sigma}$ and a non-linear function of $\Sigma$ :

$\overline{\Sigma}=\overline{\Sigma}(\dot{\Sigma},\Sigma )$ **eq 6.1-5**

After discretisation in time, $\overline{{\Sigma}^{+}}$ will become a non-linear function of ${\Sigma}^{+}$ and ${\Sigma}^{-}$ :

$\overline{{\Sigma}^{+}}=\overline{{\Sigma}^{+}}({\Sigma}^{+},{\Sigma}^{-})$ **eq 6.1-6**

Let us finally note that for algorithmic reasons (among others), one needs to know the derivative of the internal forces with respect to the generalised displacements:

$\frac{\partial R}{\partial U}=\frac{\partial R}{\partial \overline{\Sigma}}\frac{\partial \overline{\Sigma}}{\partial \Sigma }\frac{\partial \Sigma }{\partial E}\frac{\partial E}{\partial U}={Q}^{T}\frac{\partial \overline{\Sigma}}{\partial \Sigma }\frac{\partial \Sigma }{\partial \Sigma }Q$

It is clear that $\frac{\partial \overline{\Sigma}}{\partial \Sigma }$ depends only on the form of the equilibrium equations.

### 6.3 Constitutive laws

A constitutive law will be defined simply as any relation between generalised stresses and generalised strains. The internal variables are defined as fields necessary for the computation of the stresses, whose evolution is given by the constitutive laws, but which do not appear directly in the equilibrium equations.

Moreover, we consider that the constitutive laws are written in incremental form and that they are local. Denoting by :math:`` the internal variables, a constitutive law is therefore a relation:

$\Sigma ,\khi ,\dot{E}\to \dot{\Sigma},\dot{\khi }$

After discretisation in time, the constitutive law becomes a relation:

${\Sigma}^{-},{\khi }^{-},{E}^{-},{E}^{+}\to {\Sigma}^{+},{\khi }^{+}$

The constitutive law must also supply the only term which in the expression $\frac{\partial R}{\partial U}={Q}^{T}\frac{\partial \overline{\Sigma}}{\partial \Sigma }\frac{\partial \Sigma }{\partial E}Q$ depends on it, namely $\frac{\partial \Sigma }{\partial E}$ . Finally a constitutive relation is a relation:

${\Sigma}^{-},{\khi }^{-},{E}^{-},{E}^{+}\to {\Sigma}^{+},{\khi }^{+},\frac{\partial {\Sigma}^{+}}{\partial {E}^{+}}$ **eq 6.3-1**

In the following paragraphs, we specify certain aspects of the constitutive laws by distinguishing the mechanical, hydraulic and thermal contributions.

#### 6.3.1 Mechanical constitutive law

##### 6.3.1.1 General writing

The internal variables are denoted $\khi$ . A mechanical constitutive law, within the THM framework, is written:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}{\sigma}^{+}={\sigma}^{+}({\varepsilon}^{+},{p}_{1}^{+},{p}_{2}^{+},{T}^{+};{\varepsilon}^{-},{p}_{1}^{-},{p}_{2}^{-},{T}^{-},{\sigma}^{-},{\khi }^{-})\\ {\khi }^{+}={\khi }^{+}({\varepsilon}^{+},{p}_{1}^{+},{p}_{2}^{+},{T}^{+};{\varepsilon}^{-},{p}_{1}^{-},{p}_{2}^{-},{T}^{-},{\sigma}^{-},{\khi }^{-})\end{array}$ **eq 6.3.1.1-1**

##### 6.3.1.2 Case of the effective stresses

In the case of the effective-stress assumption, one has the decomposition: $\sigma =\sigma '+{\sigma}_{p}I$  where $\sigma '$ is the effective-stress tensor and ${\sigma}_{p}$ is a scalar.

The internal variables are separated into two parts: the mechanical internal variables ${\khi }_{\sigma}$ and the hydraulic internal variables ${\khi }_{H}$  . The mechanical constitutive law then splits into two laws, **of which the first may be a law already existing within the usual framework of thermomechanics.**

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}\sigma {'}^{+}=\sigma {'}^{+}({\varepsilon}^{+},{T}^{+};{\varepsilon}^{-},{T}^{-},\sigma {'}^{-},\khi {'}_{\sigma}^{-})\\ \khi {'}_{\sigma}^{+}=\sigma {'}_{\sigma}^{+}({\varepsilon}^{+},{T}^{+};{\varepsilon}^{-},{T}^{-},\sigma {'}^{-},\khi {'}_{\sigma}^{-})\end{array}$ **eq 6.3.1.2-1**

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}{\sigma}_{p}^{+}={\sigma}_{p}^{+}({p}_{1}^{+},{p}_{2}^{+};{p}_{1}^{-},{p}_{2}^{-},{\khi }_{H}^{-})\\ {\khi }_{H}^{+}={\khi }_{H}^{+}({p}_{1}^{+},{p}_{2}^{+};{p}_{1}^{-},{p}_{2}^{-},{\khi }_{H}^{-})\end{array}$ **eq 6.3.1.2-2**

The dependences indicated by the equations [eq 6.3.1.2-1] and [eq 6.3.1.2-2] have no a priori theoretical justification. It is simply a matter of showing the most general dependences possible from the point of view of computer programming. One notes in this decomposition that the dependence with respect to the thermal problem has been left in the effective stresses; typically, one thinks that the laws on the effective stresses are written as in classical thermomechanics:

$\sigma {'}^{+}=\sigma {'}^{+}({\varepsilon}^{+}-{\alpha}^{+}{T}^{+}I,{\varepsilon}^{-}-{\alpha}^{-}{T}^{-}I,\sigma {'}^{-},{\khi }_{\sigma}^{-})$

##### 6.3.1.3 Choice of the stresses

Because of the fairly frequent use of the effective-stress assumption, one decides that the stress vector for the mechanical part contains in all cases the effective-stress tensor $\sigma '$ and the scalar ${\sigma}_{p}$ . In the general case where the effective-stress assumption is not retained, one will simply have: ${\sigma}_{p}=0$ . It is therefore the responsibility of the module for integration of the equilibrium equations (and not of the constitutive laws) to form the sum: ${\sigma}^{+}=\sigma {'}^{+}+{\sigma}_{p}^{+}I$ .

#### 6.3.2 Hydraulics

The hydraulic constitutive law supplies the following relations:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}\begin{array}{}{m}_{c}^{p+}={m}_{c}^{p+}({\varepsilon}^{+},{p}_{1}^{+},{p}_{2}^{+},{T}^{+};{\varepsilon}^{-},{p}_{1}^{-},{p}_{2}^{-},{T}^{-},{m}_{c}^{p-},{M}_{c}^{p-},{\chi }_{H}^{-})\\ {M}_{c}^{p+}={M}_{c}^{p+}(\begin{array}{}{\varepsilon}^{+},{p}_{1}^{+},\nabla {p}_{1}^{+},{p}_{2}^{+},\nabla {p}_{2}^{+},{T}^{+},\nabla {T}^{+};\\ {\varepsilon}^{-},{p}_{1}^{-},\nabla {p}_{1}^{-},{p}_{2}^{-},\nabla {p}_{2}^{-};\\ {T}^{-},\nabla {T}^{-},{M}_{c}^{p-},{\chi }_{H}^{-};{F}^{m+}\end{array})\end{array}\rbrace \begin{array}{}\forall c\\ \forall p\end{array}\\ {\chi }_{H}^{+}={\chi }_{H}^{+}({\varepsilon}^{+},{p}_{1}^{+},{p}_{2}^{+},{T}^{+};{\varepsilon}^{-},{p}_{1}^{-},{p}_{2}^{-},{T}^{-},{m}_{c}^{p-},{\chi }_{H}^{-})\end{array}$ **eq 6.3.2-1**

One notes that the gravity field is a datum of the hydraulic constitutive law because the evolution of the flux vector follows relations of the type:

$M={\lambda}_{H}{\rho}^{\mathrm{fl}}\left[-\nabla P+{\rho}^{\mathrm{fl}}{F}^{m}\right]$

#### 6.3.3 Thermal constitutive law

The constitutive laws give:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}Q{'}^{+}=Q{'}^{+}({\varepsilon}^{+},{p}_{1}^{+},{p}_{2}^{+},{T}^{+};{\varepsilon}^{-},{p}_{1}^{-},{p}_{2}^{-},{T}^{-},Q{'}^{-})\\ {h}_{c}^{mp+}={h}_{c}^{mp+}({\varepsilon}^{+},{p}_{1}^{+},{p}_{2}^{+},{T}^{+};{\varepsilon}^{-},{p}_{1}^{-},{p}_{2}^{-},{T}^{-},{h}_{c}^{mp-})\forall c\text{et}\forall p\\ {q}^{+}={q}^{+}({\varepsilon}^{+},{p}_{1}^{+},{p}_{2}^{+},{T}^{+},\nabla {T}^{+};{\varepsilon}^{-},{p}_{1}^{-},{p}_{2}^{-},{T}^{-},\nabla {T}^{-},{q}^{-})\\ {\khi }_{T}^{+}={\khi }_{T}^{+}({\varepsilon}^{+},{p}_{1}^{+},{p}_{2}^{+},{T}^{+},\nabla {T}^{+};{\varepsilon}^{-},{p}_{1}^{-},{p}_{2}^{-},{T}^{-},\nabla {T}^{-},{\khi }_{T}^{-})\end{array}$ **eq 6.3.3-1**

Note that we have introduced possible internal variables linked to the thermal problem.

#### 6.3.4 Homogenised density

By definition, the homogenised density, which appears in the equilibrium equation of the mechanics [eq 3.1-1] is given by:

${r}^{+}={r}_{0}+{m}_{1}^{1+}+{m}_{1}^{2+}+{m}_{2}^{1+}+{m}_{2}^{2+}$ **eq 6.3.4-1**

This equation is not a constitutive law, but it forms part of the conservation equations. It is integrated in the module for the analysis of the equilibrium equations, the modules for the analysis of the constitutive laws not having to treat it.

#### 6.3.5 Mass operator

This operator can be called by the option MASS_MECA for all the elements concerned by the modellings containing hydromechanics in 3D and in D_PLAN, namely: 'THM', 'THHM', 'HHM', 'HH2M', 'THH2M'. It thus makes it possible to treat a dynamic analysis. It comprises only terms associated with the translational degrees of freedom, namely DX, DY in 2D, and DX , DY , DZ in 3D, respectively.

These terms are evaluated in each element before assembly from the shape functions of the isoparametric elements $N$ described in the document [R3.01.00](doc:r3.01.00) according to the expression:

${M}_{ij}^{e}=\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}\rho {N}_{i}.{N}_{j}d\Omega$

The preceding expression contains the initial homogenised density $\rho$ (corresponding to ${r}_{0}$ in paragraph [§6.3.4]) entered under the operand RHO of the keyword THM_DIFFU of the command DEFI_MATERIAU. Indeed, one judges for the usual dynamic applications of short duration (of the seismic-loading type) that the modification of the density is sufficiently small, cf. [bib15]. One thus avoids an update of the mass matrix.

The elementary terms ${M}_{ij}^{e}$ are completed on assembly by zero terms associated with the additional degrees of freedom specific to the coupled modelling. There results, on their expansion in the assembled mass matrix, an offset of degrees of freedom linked to the possible presence of the components 'PRESS1', 'PRESS2', and 'TEMPE' in the modelling.

## 7 Solution algorithm

### 7.1 Non-linear algorithm for the solution of the equilibrium equations

In the general case of the modelling (variable coefficients, desaturation, convection) the variational problem presented above [eq 4.1-1] to [eq 4.3-1] is non-linear with respect to the displacement, pressure and temperature fields. After discretisation by finite elements, one obtains a non-linear matrix system. The matrix of the tangent operator moreover contains a non-symmetric term treated as such. One uses in all cases of modelling the non-linear solver STAT_NON_LINE based on a Newton-Raphson method, described in [bib5]. One introduces the vector functional:

$F(U)=R(U)-{L}^{\mathrm{meca}}$ **eq 7.1-1**

The associated tangent operator is denoted: $DF=\frac{\partial F}{\partial U}$ .

For the THM modules, the subject of the present note, the operator ${L}^{\text{meca}}$ does not depend on the generalised displacements. All the terms depending on the generalised displacements have been introduced into $R$ , and it is precisely for this reason that the displacements are found in the generalised strains. Let us note on this subject the very particular treatment of the term ${\int}_{\Omega}r{F}^{m}.v$ of equation [eq 4.1-1].

According to [eq 6.3.4-1], ${\int}_{\Omega}r{F}^{m}.vd\Omega ={\int}_{\Omega}({r}_{0}+{m}_{1}^{1+}+{m}_{1}^{2+}+{m}_{2}^{1+}+{m}_{2}^{2+}){F}^{m}.vd\Omega$ .

We have chosen to split this term in two:

The term ${\int}_{\Omega}{r}_{0}{F}^{m}.vd\Omega$ is a contribution to ${L}^{\mathrm{meca}}$ if the user has supplied the operand PESANTEUR of the loading used (defined by the command AFFE_CHAR_MECA), whereas the term ${\int}_{\Omega}({m}_{1}^{1+}+{m}_{1}^{2+}+{m}_{2}^{1+}+{m}_{2}^{2+}){F}^{m}.vd\Omega$ , which depends on the generalised stresses, is a contribution to $\mathrm{R}$ .

### 7.2 Passage from the nodal values to the values at the Gauss points

As in all finite-element codes, the terms are computed by a loop over the elements and a loop over the Gauss points. Denoting by ${R}_{g}^{\mathrm{el}}$ and $D{F}_{g}^{\mathrm{el}}$ the values at the Gauss point $g$ of the element $\mathrm{el}$ of the nodal forces and of the tangent operator, and ${w}_{g}^{\mathrm{el}}$ the integration weight linked to this Gauss point, one has:

$R(U)=\sum_{\mathrm{el}}\sum_{g}{w}_{g}^{\mathrm{el}}{R}_{g}^{\mathrm{el}}(U)$ **eq 7.2-1**

$DF(U)=\sum_{\mathrm{el}}\sum_{g}{w}_{g}^{\mathrm{el}}D{F}_{g}^{\mathrm{el}}(U)$ **eq 7.2-2**

Let us then denote by ${U}^{\mathrm{el}}$ the vector of the nodal unknowns on a finite element $\mathrm{el}$ . One may thus have:

$\text{par exemple}{U}^{\mathrm{el}}=\begin{array}{c}\begin{array}{c}u\\ v\\ w\\ {p}_{1}\\ {p}_{2}\\ T\end{array}\rbrace \text{noeud 1}\\ \begin{array}{c}u\\ v\\ w\\ {p}_{1}\\ {p}_{2}\\ T\end{array}\rbrace \text{noeud 2}\\ \begin{array}{c}u\\ v\\ w\\ {p}_{1}\\ {p}_{2}\\ T\end{array}\rbrace \text{noeud 3}\end{array}$ .

$\begin{array}{cc}\left[D\overline{\Sigma}iD\sigma \right]=\left[\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial \sigma '},\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {\sigma}_{p}}\right]& \left[D\overline{\Sigma}i\text{DP}21\right]=\left[\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {m}_{2}^{1}},\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {M}_{2}^{1}},\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {h}^{{m}_{2}^{1}}}\right]\\ \left[D\overline{\Sigma}i\text{DP}11\right]=\left[\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {m}_{1}^{2}},\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {M}_{1}^{1}},\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {h}^{{m}_{1}^{2}}}\right]& \left[D\overline{\Sigma}i\text{DP}22\right]=\left[\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {m}_{2}^{2}},\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {M}_{2}^{2}},\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {h}^{{m}_{2}^{2}}}\right]\\ \left[D\overline{\Sigma}i\text{DP}12\right]=\left[\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {m}_{1}^{2}},\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {M}_{1}^{1}},\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial {h}^{{m}_{1}^{2}}}\right]& \left[D\overline{\Sigma}i\text{DT}\right]=\left[\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial Q'},\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{i}}{\partial q}\right]\end{array}$

It is then clear that:

$D\overline{\Sigma}\mathrm{DE}=D\overline{\Sigma}D\Sigma .D\Sigma \mathrm{DE}$

And the contribution of the Gauss point to the tangent matrix $D{F}_{g}^{\text{el}}$ is obtained by:

$D{F}_{g}^{\text{el}}={Q}_{g}^{{\text{el}}^{T}}.D\overline{\Sigma}\text{DE}.{Q}_{g}^{\text{el}}$

### 7.4 Global algorithm

The algorithm then becomes:

*Initialisations:*

Computation of ${L}^{{\text{meca}}^{+}}$ (option CHAR_MECA)

Computation of $D{F}^{-}$ (option RIGI_MECA_TANG)

Computation of $\Delta {U}_{0}$ by: $D{F}^{-}.\Delta {U}_{0}={L}^{{\text{meca}}^{+}}-{L}^{{\text{meca}}^{-}}$

*Newton equilibrium iterations*

Loop over elements el

> Loop over Gauss points g

> computation ${Q}_{g}^{\text{el}}$
> computation ${E}_{g}^{{\text{el}}^{-}}={Q}_{g}^{\text{el}}.{U}^{{\text{el}}^{-}}$ and ${E}_{g}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}={Q}_{g}^{\text{el}}.{U}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}$
> computation of: ${\Sigma}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}},{\alpha}_{g}^{{\text{el}}^{+}},\frac{\partial {\Sigma}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}}{\partial {E}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}}$ (according to the option) from ${E}_{g}^{{\text{el}}^{-}},{\Sigma}_{g}^{{\text{el}}^{-}},{\alpha}_{g}^{{\text{el}}^{-}},{E}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}$
> computation of ${\overline{\Sigma}}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}$ from ${\Sigma}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}$ ; ${R}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}={Q}_{g}^{{\text{el}}^{T}}.{\overline{\Sigma}}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}$
> computation of $\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}}{\partial {\Sigma}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}}$ from ${\Sigma}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}$ ; $D{F}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}={Q}_{g}^{{\text{el}}^{T}}.\frac{\partial {\overline{\Sigma}}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}}{\partial {\Sigma}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}}.\frac{\partial {\Sigma}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}}{\partial {E}_{gn}^{{\text{el}}^{+}}}.{Q}_{g}^{\text{el}}$ (according to the option)

> *Computation* of $\delta {U}_{n+1}$ by:
> $D{F}_{n}^{+}.\delta {U}_{n+1}=-{R}_{n}^{+}+{L}^{{\text{meca}}^{+}}$
> *Update* :
> $\Delta {U}_{n+1}=\Delta {U}_{n}+\rho \delta {U}_{n+1}$

If convergence test OK

> end of Newton: next time step

Otherwise

> $n=n+1$

## 8 The option FORC_NODA

At the level of the continuous equations, the option FORC_NODA corresponds to the computation of the operator $R={Q}^{T}\overline{\Sigma}$ . At the discrete level, the option FORC_NODA amounts to computing the vector ${R}_{g}^{\text{el}}={Q}_{g}^{{\text{el}}^{T}}.{\overline{\Sigma}}_{g}^{\text{el}}$ .

As we have already noted that $\overline{\Sigma}$ depends not only on $\Sigma$ , but also on $\dot{\Sigma}$ , one should not be surprised to see the time step $\Delta t$ appear and the stresses both at time + and at time 

The Newton-Raphson algorithm of the command STAT_NON_LINE uses the option FORC_NODA for the computation of the prediction at the beginning of each time step. It is therefore not immaterial to compute correctly all the terms for this option, including those which depend on the time step. We illustrate this question by a simple example corresponding to the hydraulics equation alone.

Let a simplified version of the hydraulic equation:

$-{\int}_{\Omega}\frac{\text{dm}}{\text{dt}}.{p}^{\text{*}}d\Omega +{\int}_{\Omega}M.\nabla {p}^{\text{*}}d\Omega ={M}_{\text{ext}}.{p}^{\text{*}}$ **eq 8-1**

After discretisation in time:

$-{\int}_{\Omega}\Delta \text{m}.{p}^{\text{*}}d\Omega +\Delta t{\int}_{\Omega}(\theta {M}^{+}+(1-\theta ){M}^{-}).\nabla {p}^{\text{*}}d\Omega ={M}_{\text{ext}}^{\theta}.{p}^{\text{*}}$

Bringing out $\Delta M={M}^{+}-{M}^{-}$ and $\Delta p={p}^{+}-{p}^{-}$ , and writing a constitutive law: $\Delta m =N\Delta p$ , one finds:

$-{\int}_{\Omega}N\Delta \text{p}.{p}^{\text{*}}d\Omega +\Delta t{\int}_{\Omega}\Delta M.\nabla {p}^{\text{*}}d\Omega ={M}_{\text{ext}}^{\theta}{p}^{\text{*}}-\Delta t{\int}_{\Omega}{M}^{-}.\nabla {p}^{\text{*}}d\Omega$

By definition the prediction phase of STAT_NON_LINE is written:

${K}_{0}\Delta {u}^{0}={F}_{\text{ext}}^{1}-{Q}^{T}{\sigma}_{0}$

It is then clear that one must take $\underset{\Omega}{\int}{Q}^{T}{\sigma}_{0}{p}^{\text{*}}d\Omega =-\Delta t \underset{\Omega}{\int}{M}^{-}\nabla {p}^{\text{*}}d\Omega$

## 9 Spatial discretisation

The THM finite elements are mixed elements, in the sense that they have unknowns of displacements, of pressures and of temperatures at the same time. A choice of discretisation in which the displacements, the pressures and the temperatures are interpolated with the same order of approximation leads to oscillations, especially for choices of time step that are too small with respect to the spatial discretisation. One will consult on this subject for example [bib10]. This problem is also related to the way of computing the so-called mass matrix, and one may consult on this subject [bib14]. We moreover give in the appendix, in order to illustrate our point, the solution for the first time step of a one-dimensional consolidation problem with a $\mathit{P1P1}$ interpolation. One sees that for a small time step, it is very oscillatory.

For this reason, the quadratic THM elements are elements in $\mathit{P2P1}$ , that is the interpolation of the displacements is quadratic and that of the temperatures and pressures is linear. We have nevertheless kept all the unknowns on all the nodes, including the mid-side nodes, but we have imposed in the computation of the stiffness matrices that the pressure of a mid-side node of a segment be equal to the half-sum of the vertex nodes of the segment to which it belongs.

Moreover, in the programming, we have taken into account the following property:

Let $s$ be a vertex node, ${w}_{s}^{1}$ its shape function as belonging to a linear element (for example QUAD4), and ${w}_{s}^{2}$ its shape function as belonging to a quadratic element (for example QUAD8). Let $\mathrm{na}$ be the number of edges having $s$ as an end and ${w}_{\text{ma}}^{2}$ the shape function of the quadratic interpolation attached to a mid-edge node, one then has the relation:

${w}_{s}^{1}={w}_{s}^{2}+\sum_{\text{ma}=1}^{\text{na}}\frac{{w}_{\text{ma}}^{2}}{2}$

That said, and including in $\mathit{P2P1}$ interpolation, non-oscillation conditions exist on the time step. [bib10] gives the relation:

$\Delta t >\frac{\Delta {x}^{2}}{20{C}_{v}}$

where ${C}_{v}$ is the consolidation coefficient: ${C}_{v}=\frac{\text{kE}(1-\nu )}{{\rho}_{\text{lq}}(1+\nu )(1-\mathrm{2\nu })}$ , $k$ being the permeability measured in $m/s$ .

**One-dimensional problem P1P1**

One considers a one-dimensional consolidation problem whose unknowns vary only as a function of the single space variable $x$ .

A rectangular domain of length $L$ is filled with a porous material of Lamé coefficients $\lambda$ and $\mu$ , of Biot coefficient $b$ and of Biot modulus $N=\frac{1}{M}$ and of hydraulic conductivity ${\lambda}_{h}$ . The density of the fluid is denoted $\rho$ . One denotes by $\sigma$ the stress ${\sigma}_{xx}$ , $u$ the displacement in the direction $x$ , $\varepsilon =\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}$ the strain, $p$ the pressure, $m$ the mass supply of fluid, $M$ the fluid flux.

The boundary conditions are:

at $x=0$ : $\sigma =0;p=0$

at $x=L$ : $u=0;p=1$ for $t>0$

The initial conditions at $t=0$ are $\sigma =u=p=0$ .

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.10/10000936000069BB000009EC8757105EFF851568.svg)

The setting into equations gives:

Mechanical equilibrium and linear elasticity: $\sigma =(\lambda +2\mu )\varepsilon -\text{bp}=0$

Conservation of mass: $\frac{\partial m}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial M}{\partial x}=0$

Darcy's law: $M=-{\lambda}_{h}\rho \frac{\partial p}{\partial x}$

Couplings: $\frac{m}{\rho}=\text{Np}+b\varepsilon$

We carry out an implicit discretisation in time **and we are interested in the analysis of the first time step** :

One obtains the system of two equations:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}\sigma =(\lambda +2\mu )\varepsilon (u)-\text{bp}=0\\ \text{Np}+b\varepsilon -{\lambda}_{h}\Delta t \Delta p =0\end{array}$

The mixed variational formulation of this problem is:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}{\int}_{\Omega}\left[(\lambda +2\mu )\varepsilon (u)\varepsilon ({u}^{\text{*}})-\text{bp}\varepsilon ({u}^{\text{*}})\right]=0\forall {u}^{\text{*}}\\ {\int}_{\Omega}\left[{\text{Npp}}^{\text{*}}+b\varepsilon (u){p}^{\text{*}}+{\lambda}_{h}\Delta t \nabla p\nabla {p}^{\text{*}}\right]=0\forall {p}^{\text{*}}\end{array}$ **eq An 1-1**

Concerning the spatial discretisation, we divide the medium into $n$ finite elements. The nodes of element $i$ are $i$ and $i+1$ . One denotes by ${u}_{e}^{1}$ and ${p}_{e}^{1}$ the displacement and the pressure of the first node of the element $e$ , ${u}_{e}^{2}$ and ${p}_{e}^{2}$ the displacement and the pressure of its second node. One assumes that one uses P1P1 finite elements, that is the displacements as well as the pressures are interpolated linearly. The discretisation of the first equation then gives:

$\sum_{e}\frac{{u}_{e}^{\text{*}2}-{u}_{e}^{\text{*}1}}{\Delta x }\left[(\lambda +2\mu )\frac{{u}_{e}^{2}-{u}_{e}^{1}}{\Delta x }-b\frac{{p}_{e}^{1}+{p}_{e}^{2}}{2}\right]=0$

Whence one deduces:

$({u}_{e}^{2}-{u}_{e}^{1})=\frac{b\Delta x }{\lambda +2\mu }\frac{{p}_{e}^{1}+{p}_{e}^{2}}{2}\text{}\forall e$ **eq An 1-2**

The discretisation of the second equation gives:

$\sum_{e}\frac{{p}_{e}^{\text{*}2}+{p}_{e}^{\text{*}1}}{2}\left[b\frac{{u}_{e}^{2}-{u}_{e}^{1}}{\Delta x }+N\frac{{p}_{e}^{1}+{p}_{e}^{2}}{2}\right]+{\lambda}_{h}\Delta t \sum_{e}\frac{{p}_{e}^{\text{*}2}-{p}_{e}^{{\text{*}}^{1}}}{\Delta x }\frac{{p}_{e}^{2}-{p}_{e}^{1}}{\Delta x }=0$

Carrying [eq An 1-2] into it, one finds:

$\sum_{e}\frac{{p}_{e}^{\text{*}2}+{p}_{e}^{\text{*}1}}{2}(N+\frac{{b}^{2}}{\lambda +2\mu })\frac{{p}_{e}^{1}+{p}_{e}^{2}}{2}+{\lambda}_{h}\frac{\Delta t }{\Delta {x}^{2}}\sum_{e}({p}_{e}^{\text{*}2}-{p}_{e}^{\text{*}1})({p}_{e}^{2}-{p}_{e}^{1})=0$ **eq An 1-3**

If now we let the time step tend to zero at constant space step, ${\lambda}_{h}\frac{\Delta t }{\Delta {x}^{2}}\text{<<}(N+\frac{{b}^{2}}{\lambda +2\mu })$ , and [eq An 1-3] reduces to:

$\sum_{e}({p}_{e}^{\text{*}2}+{p}_{e}^{\text{*}1})({p}_{e}^{1}+{p}_{e}^{2})=0$

One introduces a global numbering of the nodes and of the pressure unknowns:

${p}_{j}^{1}={p}_{j};{p}_{j}^{2}={p}_{j+1}$

One can see that this set of relations finally gives:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}{p}_{1}=0\\ {p}_{i}+2{p}_{i+1}+{p}_{i+2}=0\forall i\in \left[1,n-1\right]\\ {p}_{n+1}=1\end{array}$

The solution of this sequence is:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}{p}_{2j-1}=\frac{j-1}{n}\\ {p}_{2j}=-\frac{2j-1}{2n}\end{array}$

Which gives the following pressure distribution:

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.10/10004EA600001E2D000016843C8AA5037CDD0B53.svg)
