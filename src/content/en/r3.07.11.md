# r3.07.10 Mixed hybrid finite elements via FENICSx  – PLAQ_MITC

**Summary:**

With the aim of completing the library of flat-plate finite elements [R3.07.03](doc:r3.07.03) currently available (DKT, DST, Q4G...), one proposes to introduce a mixed hybrid finite element based on the MITC approach (in English, *Mixed Interpolation of Tensorial Component*). This model makes it possible to carry out analyses on thin structures while respecting the Reissner-Mindlin assumptions.

AIP studies and the element PLAQ_MITC is outside

**Contents**

## 1 Introduction

The Reissner-Mindlin model constitutes a generalisation of the classical Kirchhoff-Love theory of thin plates. It was developed in order to represent better the effects of transverse shear in plates of moderate to high thickness. Whereas the Kirchhoff model rests on the assumption that the sections normal to the middle surface remain perpendicular after deformation, this assumption becomes too restrictive as soon as the thickness of the plate becomes significant relative to its in-plane dimensions.

The Reissner-Mindlin theory introduces a field of rotations independent of the normals to the middle surface, which makes it possible to model more faithfully the real strains of thick plates subjected to complex loadings. It retains a relatively simple formulation, offering a good compromise between mechanical realism and numerical cost. The transverse-shear effects are modelled by a strain term defined as the difference between the gradient of the transverse displacement and the rotation field, weighted by a correction factor $\kappa$ making it possible to adjust the shear energy.

However, the direct discretisation of this model by means of standard conforming finite elements leads to numerical difficulties in the case of thin plates, in particular a phenomenon of transverse-shear locking (or *shear locking*, in English) which impairs the accuracy and slows the convergence of the simulations. In order to remedy this limitation, mixed formulations have been developed. These rest on the introduction of auxiliary fields for the shear, interpolated in suitable spaces (in particular of Nédélec type), and on the use of Lagrange multipliers in order to impose the compatibility constraints in a weak manner.

The aim of the present document is to present the development and the integration of the new finite element `PLAQ_MITC`, designed to avoid transverse-shear locking and to improve the numerical accuracy in the case of thick plates. This element rests on a mixed formulation of MITC type (*Mixed Interpolation of Tensorial Components*, in English) and relies on automatic code generation via *FENICSx* in order to simplify the development of the elementary functions, while ensuring their efficient integration.

## 2 Constitutive equations and assumptions

### 2.1 Assumptions of the Reissner--Mindlin model

The Reissner-Mindlin model makes it possible to represent the mechanical constitutive behaviour of thick plates, by taking explicitly into account the effects of transverse shear. Unlike the Kirchhoff-Love model, it does not assume that the sections normal to the middle surface remain perpendicular after deformation.

For what follows:

- the transverse displacement is denoted $w(x, y)$ ,
- the rotations of the fibres normal to the middle surface are described by an independent vector field $\vector{\theta}(x, y) = \left(\theta_x (x,y), \theta_y (x, y)\right)$ .

The thickness $h$ of the plate is assumed small compared with its dimensions in the plane, but not negligible, which authorises the introduction of a transverse-shear strain. The latter is defined by:

$$
\vector{\gamma} = \gradScal{w} - \vector{\theta},
$$

where $\gradScal{w} = \left( \cfrac{\partial w}{\partial x}, \cfrac{\partial w}{\partial y} \right)$ represents the gradient of the transverse displacement.

### 2.2 Elastic constitutive law

The elastic constitutive behaviour of the material is described with the aid of the classical parameters:

- Young's modulus $E$ ,
- Poisson's ratio $\nu$ ,
- a shear correction factor $\kappa$ , generally taken equal to $\cfrac{5}{6}$ for homogeneous plates.

#### 2.2.1 Curvature tensor

The curvature tensor $\tensTwo{\kappa}$ represents the spatial variations of the rotation field $\vector{\theta}$ . It is defined as the symmetric part of the gradient of $\vector{\theta}$ :

$$
\tensTwo{\kappa}
=
\operGradSyme{\vector{\theta}}
=
\frac{1}{2} \left( \gradVector{\vector{\theta}} + \transpose{\gradVector{\vector{\theta}}} \right).
$$

#### 2.2.2 Bending energy

The stresses due to bending $\tensTwo{M}$ are related to the curvature tensor by a linear constitutive law:

$$
\tensTwo{M} = \tensFour{D} : \tensTwo{\kappa},
$$

where $\tensFour{D}$ denotes the bending stiffness tensor, expressed by:

$$
\tensFour{D}
=
D \left[ (1 - \nu)\, \tensFourUnit + \nu\, \tensTwoUnit \otimes \tensTwoUnit \right],
\qquad \text{avec} \qquad D = \frac{E h^3}{12(1 - \nu^2)}.
$$

The notations used are:

- $\tensFourUnit$ : identity tensor of order 4,
- $\tensTwoUnit$ : identity tensor of order 2,
- $\otimes$ : tensor product.

The corresponding bending energy is given by:

$$
\psi_b = \frac{1}{2} \tensTwo{M} : \tensTwo{\kappa}.
$$

#### 2.2.3 Transverse-shear energy

The elastic energy associated with the transverse shear is written:

$$
\psi_s = \frac{1}{2} S \left( \vector{\gamma}_R \cdot \vector{\gamma}_R \right)
$$

where $S = \cfrac{E \kappa h}{2(1 + \nu)}$ and $\vector{\gamma}_R$ denotes the "reduced" shear field, interpolated in a suitable functional space (for example of Nédélec type), in order to prevent the phenomenon of numerical locking during the discretisation by finite elements.

## 3 Variational formulations

### 3.1 Unknown fields and approximation spaces

The modelling adopted rests on the Reissner-Mindlin theory, enriched by a mixed interpolation of MITC type (Mixed Interpolation of Tensorial Components), in the variant proposed by Durán and Liberman  .

This approach makes it possible to correct effectively the numerical locking linked to the transverse shear, thus ensuring a better accuracy in the reproduction of the strains, even for thin plates.

The unknown fields are discretised in the following finite-element spaces:

$$
w^h \in P_1, \quad
\vector{\theta}^h \in [P_2]^2, \quad
\vector{\gamma}_R^h, \, \vector{p}^h \in \mathrm{NED}_1,
$$

where:

- $P_1$ denotes the space of the linear Lagrange elements, that is to say the continuous functions whose restriction to each element of the mesh is a polynomial of degree less than or equal to 1;
- $[P_2]^2$ denotes a space of vector fields with two components, each being a $P_2$ function, that is a polynomial of degree less than or equal to 2, piecewise;
- $\mathrm{NED}_1$ corresponds to the Nédélec space of the first type, suited to the discretisation of tangential vector fields.

On a quadrangular element, this results in the distribution of the degrees of freedom illustrated below:

![Figure](/figures/r3.07.11/function_space_scheme.svg)

*Distribution of the degrees of freedom for the fields $w^h \in P_1$ , $\vector{\theta}^h \in [P_2]^2$ and $\vector{\gamma}_R^h, \vector{p}^h \in \mathrm{NED}_1$ on a quadrangular element.*

This discretisation allows a faithful and stable representation of the various mechanical effects brought into play, while preventing numerical artefacts such as shear locking.

#### 3.1.1 Nédélec functions

These are vector functions principally associated with the edges of the elements. An important property is that they ensure the continuity of the tangential component of a vector field from one element to another. In our case, this makes it possible to guarantee that the projection of the field $\gamma_R$ is consistent at the interface of the elements.

### 3.2 Energy of the system and variational coupling

Let $\Omega \subset \setR^2$ be the plane domain occupied by the plate, discretised by means of a conforming mesh $\mathcal{T}^h$ composed of quadrangular elements. The set of the edges is denoted $\mathcal{E}^h$ and, for every edge $E \in \mathcal{E}^h$ , $\vector{t}$ denotes the unit tangent vector.

The variational formulation adopted is expressed by the minimisation of a functional integrating the whole of the contributions of the system:

- the bending energy, linked to the bending moments and to the curvature of the plate;
- the transverse-shear energy, computed from a conforming field $\vector{\gamma}_R^h$ ;
- a weak coupling term, imposing the consistency between $\vector{\gamma}_R^h$ and the field reconstructed from $w^h$ and $\vector{\theta}^h$ , via a Lagrange multiplier $\vector{p}^h$ .

The functional is written for the discretised quadruplet  $q^h = (w^h, \vector{\theta}^h, \vector{\gamma}_R^h, \vector{p}^h)$ :

$$
\begin{aligned}
    \Pi(q^h) =\ &
    \frac{1}{2} \int_\Omega \tensFour{D} \tensTwo{\kappa} (\vector{\theta}^h) : \tensTwo{\kappa} (\vector{\theta}^h) \measDomain
    +
    \frac{1}{2} \int_\Omega S \vector{\gamma}_R^h \cdot \vector{\gamma}_R^h \measDomain \\
    &
    +
    \sum_{E \in \mathcal{E}^h} \int_E \left( \vector{\gamma}(w^h, \vector{\theta}^h) - \vector{\gamma}_R^h \right)\!\cdot\!\vector{t} \, (\vector{p}^h \cdot \vector{t}) \measBound
    -
    W_{\text{ext}}
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{split}\begin{aligned}
    \Pi(q^h) =\ &
    \frac{1}{2} \int_\Omega \tensFour{D} \tensTwo{\kappa} (\vector{\theta}^h) : \tensTwo{\kappa} (\vector{\theta}^h) \measDomain
    +
    \frac{1}{2} \int_\Omega S \vector{\gamma}_R^h \cdot \vector{\gamma}_R^h \measDomain \\
    &
    +
    \sum_{E \in \mathcal{E}^h} \int_E \left( \vector{\gamma}(w^h, \vector{\theta}^h) - \vector{\gamma}_R^h \right)\!\cdot\!\vector{t} \, (\vector{p}^h \cdot \vector{t}) \measBound
    -
    W_{\text{ext}}
\end{aligned}\end{split}
$$

where:

- $\tensTwo{\kappa}(\vector{\theta}^h) = \operGradSyme{\vector{\theta}^h}$ is the curvature tensor;
- $\vector{\gamma}(w^h, \vector{\theta}^h) = \gradScal{w}^h - \vector{\theta}^h$ is the reconstructed shear field;
- $\vector{\gamma}_R^h$ : conforming field used in the discretisation;
- $\vector{p}^h$ : Lagrange multiplier acting on the edges;
- $W_{\text{ext}}$ : work of the external forces.

The coupling term expresses, in weak form, the equality between the two representations of the transverse shear: the reconstructed field $\vector{\gamma}(w^h, \vector{\theta}^h)$ and the conforming field $\vector{\gamma}_R^h$ . This constraint, imposed on each edge $E \in \mathcal{E}^h$ via $\vector{p}^h$ , is written in the following variational form:

$$
\int_E \left( \vector{\gamma}(w^h, \vector{\theta}^h) - \vector{\gamma}_R^h \right) \cdot \vector{t} \, (\vector{p}^h \cdot \vector{t}) \measBound
$$

This coupling mechanism guarantees the consistency between the shear fields while ensuring the numerical stability of the scheme, and plays a key role in avoiding locking in the thin-plate regime.

### 3.3 Motivation for the use of MITC

#### 3.3.1 Shear-locking problem

In the Reissner–Mindlin model, when the thickness $h$ tends towards zero, the expected constitutive behaviour is that of the Kirchhoff model, for which:

$$
\vector{\gamma} = \gradScal{w} - \vector{\theta} \to \vector{0}
$$

However, with a standard discretisation using $P_1$ elements (continuous finite elements of degree 1) for the displacement $w$ and the rotations $\vector{\theta}$ , and under a clamped condition, one has:

- $\gradScal{w}^h \in \mathrm{P}_0$ : piecewise constant field;
- $\vector{\theta}^h \in [\mathrm{P}_1]^2$ : continuous field, zero on $\partial \Omega$ .

It then becomes impossible, except trivially, to obtain $\gradScal{w}^h = \vector{\theta}^h$ . This approximation defect causes numerical locking: the discrete solution is too stiff and does not correctly reproduce the bending modes, in particular for thin plates.

#### 3.3.2 Solution: the MITC elements

The MITC elements (*Mixed Interpolation of Tensorial Components*) circumvent this locking by modifying the interpolation of the transverse shear.

The principle consists in projecting the reconstructed shear $\gradScal{w} - \vector{\theta}$ onto a suitable subspace (often defined on the edges or inside the elements), thus ensuring a weak compatibility between the fields.

This technique limits the spurious constraints, restores the consistency of the model and allows a robust and uniform convergence, including in the thin-plate regime, without recourse to an excessive refinement of the mesh.

### 3.4 Weak variational formulation of the problem

One seeks the stationary points of the functional $\Pi$ by imposing that its directional derivative vanishes for every admissible variation $\tilde{q} = (\tilde{w}, \tilde{\vector{\theta}}, \tilde{\vector{\gamma}}_R, \tilde{\vector{p}})$ :

$$
F(q^h; \tilde{q}) := D_{\tilde{q}}[\Pi(q^h)] = 0
\quad \forall \tilde{q}.
$$

The residual $F = {F}_{\text{ext}} - {F}_{\mathrm{int}}$ where ${F}_{\mathrm{int}}$ is decomposed into four bilinear contributions:

$$
\begin{aligned}
    \text{Flexion :} \quad
    & a^{\mathrm{b}}(\vector{\theta}^h; \tilde{\vector{\theta}}) =
    \int_\Omega \tensFour{D} \tensTwo{\kappa}(\vector{\theta}^h) : \tensTwo{\kappa}(\tilde{\vector{\theta}}) \, \mathrm{d}x,
    \\
    \text{Cisaillement :} \quad
    & a^{\mathrm{s}}(\vector{\gamma}_R^h; \tilde{\vector{\gamma}}_R) =
    \int_\Omega S \vector{\gamma}_R^h \cdot \tilde{\vector{\gamma}}_R \, \mathrm{d}x,
    \\
    \text{Couplage (primal) :} \quad
    & a^{\mathrm{MITC}}_1(q^h; \tilde{\vector{p}}) =
    \sum_{E \in \mathcal{E}^h} \int_E
    \left( \gradScal{w}^h - \vector{\theta}^h - \vector{\gamma}_R^h \right) \cdot \vector{t} \,
    (\tilde{\vector{p}} \cdot \vector{t}) \, \mathrm{d}s,
    \\
    \text{Couplage (dual) :} \quad
    & a^{\mathrm{MITC}}_2(\vector{p}^h; \tilde{q}) =
    \sum_{E \in \mathcal{E}^h} \int_E
    (\vector{p}^h \cdot \vector{t}) \,
    \left( \nabla \tilde{w} - \tilde{\vector{\theta}} - \tilde{\vector{\gamma}}_R \right) \cdot \vector{t} \, \mathrm{d}s.
\end{aligned}
$$

$$
\begin{split}\begin{aligned}
    \text{Flexion :} \quad
    & a^{\mathrm{b}}(\vector{\theta}^h; \tilde{\vector{\theta}}) =
    \int_\Omega \tensFour{D} \tensTwo{\kappa}(\vector{\theta}^h) : \tensTwo{\kappa}(\tilde{\vector{\theta}}) \, \mathrm{d}x,
    \\
    \text{Cisaillement :} \quad
    & a^{\mathrm{s}}(\vector{\gamma}_R^h; \tilde{\vector{\gamma}}_R) =
    \int_\Omega S \vector{\gamma}_R^h \cdot \tilde{\vector{\gamma}}_R \, \mathrm{d}x,
    \\
    \text{Couplage (primal) :} \quad
    & a^{\mathrm{MITC}}_1(q^h; \tilde{\vector{p}}) =
    \sum_{E \in \mathcal{E}^h} \int_E
    \left( \gradScal{w}^h - \vector{\theta}^h - \vector{\gamma}_R^h \right) \cdot \vector{t} \,
    (\tilde{\vector{p}} \cdot \vector{t}) \, \mathrm{d}s,
    \\
    \text{Couplage (dual) :} \quad
    & a^{\mathrm{MITC}}_2(\vector{p}^h; \tilde{q}) =
    \sum_{E \in \mathcal{E}^h} \int_E
    (\vector{p}^h \cdot \vector{t}) \,
    \left( \nabla \tilde{w} - \tilde{\vector{\theta}} - \tilde{\vector{\gamma}}_R \right) \cdot \vector{t} \, \mathrm{d}s.
\end{aligned}\end{split}
$$

The global weak formulation becomes:

$$
F(q^h; \tilde{q}) =
{F}_{\text{ext}} - \left(
    a^{\mathrm{b}} + a^{\mathrm{s}} + a^{\mathrm{MITC}}_1 + a^{\mathrm{MITC}}_2
\right)
= 0, \quad \forall \tilde{q}.
$$

The linear system coming from the linearisation takes the block form:

$$
\begin{split}\begin{bmatrix}
    A & 0 & C \\
    0 & B & D \\
    C^\top & D & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
    \delta z \\
    \delta \vector{\gamma}_R \\
    \delta \vector{p}
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
    f_z \\
    f_\gamma \\
    f_p
\end{bmatrix},\end{split}
$$

with:

- $\discVect{\delta z} = (\discVect{\delta w}, \discVect{\delta \theta})$ : transverse displacement and rotations;
- $\discVect{\delta \gamma_R}$ : reduced shear, introduced in order to improve the numerical performance;
- $\discVect{\delta p}$ : Lagrange multiplier of the MITC constraint;
- $\discMatr{A}$ , $\discMatr{B}$ , $\discMatr{C}$ and $\discMatr{D}$ : matrices coming from the linearisation of the contributions of the residual;
- $\discVect{f_z}$ , $\discVect{f_\gamma}$ and $\discVect{f_p}$ : vectors which contribute to the global residual $F$ .

The local system serves to assemble the global matrices and vectors. The global system will then be solved in order to obtain the increments of the unknowns.

**Note:**

The blocks $\discMatr{A}$ and $\discMatr{B}$ come from the linearisation of the symmetric bilinear forms $a^{\mathrm{b}}$ and $a^{\mathrm{s}}$ , respectively.
The block $\discMatr{D}$ is a diagonal matrix. The global matrix system is therefore symmetric.

### 3.5 Static condensation

At the elementary level, it is possible to eliminate the variables associated with the Lagrange multipliers. To this end, one computes the reduced system in terms of the primary variables $\discVect{z}$ .

$$
\begin{split}\begin{aligned}
    \discMatr{A_s} \discVect{\delta z} &= \discVect{f_s} \\
    \discMatr{A_s} &= \discMatr{A} + \discMatr{C} \discMatr{D}^{-1} \discMatr{B} \discMatr{D}^{-1} \discMatr{C}^\top \\
    \discVect{f_s} &= \discVect{f_z} - \discMatr{C} \discMatr{D}^{-1} \discVect{f_{\gamma}} + \discMatr{C} \discMatr{D}^{-1}  \discMatr{B} \discMatr{D}^{-1} \discVect{f_p}
\end{aligned}\end{split}
$$

The blocks $\discMatr{A_s}$ and $\discVect{f_s}$ are then assembled at the global level in order to form the final system to be solved. It should be noted that the block $\discMatr{D}$ in the elementary matrix is diagonal by construction, which facilitates its local inversion on each element.
