# r4.05.07 Non-linear soil–structure interaction with the Laplace–Time method

**Summary:**

This document is a theoretical note describing the Laplace–Time method (*cf*. [U2.06.05](doc:u2.06.05)) developed in the operator CALC_MISS (*cf*. [U7.03.12](doc:u7.03.12)) for the result type 'FICHIER_TEMPS'. It belongs to the framework of non-linear soil–structure interaction (SSI) analyses based on the *Code_Aster*–MISS3D coupling.

The principle of the solution of such a non-linear analysis rests on a substructuring technique in which two subdomains are generally considered: on the one hand, a bounded non-linear domain corresponding essentially to the structural part (and possibly the soil surrounding the foundation) and, on the other, an unbounded soil domain with linear elastic behaviour. This domain decomposition makes it possible to apply the finite-element method (*Code_Aster*) for the transient modelling of the non-linear problem and a boundary-element method (MISS3D) for the computation of the impedance matrix representing the dynamic behaviour in time of the semi-infinite soil domain.

In this framework, the Laplace–Time method makes it possible to compute the impedance operator in time from a particular sampling of the complex-frequency space (Laplace domain). In particular, this sampling is based on the characteristic polynomial of a second-order linear multistep method. Likewise, the Laplace–Time method makes possible the evaluation, at each time instant of the seismic analysis, of the convolution integral expressing the soil–structure interaction forces of the coupled system.

## 1 Introduction

Dynamic soil–structure interaction (SSI) problems, or even soil–fluid–structure interaction problems, are often solved using a substructuring technique in which the complete system is decomposed into two or more subdomains. In particular, for the cases of non-linear SSI, it is decomposed into a linear domain (the far-field soil) and a non-linear domain (the building and possibly a part of the surrounding soil).

The advantage of using this decomposition technique rests essentially on the possibility of using the numerical methods best suited to each domain. Thus, the linear far-field soil, which corresponds to an infinite domain, can be modelled with the boundary-element method (BEM, the English acronym) and the non-linear part, which is rather confined to a bounded domain, often of complex geometry for the structural part, with the finite-element method (FEM). This BEM–FEM numerical approach is indeed the one used in the *code_aster*–MISS3D coupling.

In this framework, two particular features deserve to be recalled. First, the fact that any non-linear problem must in general be solved with a transient analysis. And second, the fact that MISS3D, based on a frequency formulation of the boundary-element method, makes it possible to model the unbounded linear soil by means of an impedance matrix (soil stiffness that depends on the frequency) projected onto a basis of modes representative of the kinematics of the interface between the linear and non-linear domains (also known, by misuse of language, as the SSI interface). In view of these two considerations, the coupled problem is formulated entirely in the time domain, but a convolution product coming from the frequency dependence of the impedance appears at the interface.

To evaluate this convolution product, the literature proposes the time–frequency method or the hidden-variable method which, using the impedance function or its inverse, rest on a formulation in the frequency domain. Formulations of the impedance function in the Laplace domain are also found, which can be combined with convolution-quadrature methods. Nevertheless, the method developed in CALC_MISS (the Laplace–Time method), which is the one presented in this document, makes it possible to evaluate the convolution integrals by bringing out the inertia, damping and stiffness terms that characterise dynamic problems.

### 1.1 Problem statement: evaluation of the convolution integrals

As mentioned previously, the Laplace–Time method proceeds by the evaluation of a convolution product. The main difficulty of its computation comes from the singular character of the convolution kernel (the impedance matrix), which indeed makes its evaluation difficult with a simpler and classical approach linked to FFT-type algorithms.

To illustrate quickly the origin of this product, it will be assumed that the system is linear, so as to be able to start from the equations formulated in the Fourier domain:

$$
-{\omega}^{2}M\widehat{u}(\omega )+i\omega C\widehat{u}(\omega )+K\widehat{u}(\omega )+\left[\begin{array}{c}0\\ {\widehat{Z}}_{s}(\omega ){\widehat{u}}_{\Gamma}(\omega )\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}0\\ {\widehat{F}}_{s}(\omega )\end{array}\right]
$$

where $M$ , $C$ and $K$ are the mass, damping and stiffness matrices of the system, ${\widehat{Z}}_{s}(\omega )$ the soil impedance matrix, ${\widehat{F}}_{s}(\omega )$ the equivalent seismic force and ${\widehat{u}}_{\Gamma}(\omega )$ the vector of unknown displacements at the interface. Then, the convolution product appears directly on applying the Fourier transform to the previous equation:

$$
M\ddot{u}(t)+C\dot{u}(t)+Ku(t)+\left[\begin{array}{c}0\\ {R}_{\Gamma}(t)\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}0\\ {F}_{s}(t)\end{array}\right]
$$

with ${R}_{\Gamma}(t)={\int}_{0}^{t}Z(t-\tau ){u}_{\Gamma}(\tau )\text{}d\tau ,\text{}0\le t\le T$ and $Z(t)$ the operator analogous to the soil impedance in time.

If the structural part (plus possibly the near-field soil) were non-linear, the convolution product would also appear in the equations of the unknowns of the SSI interface, because the soil–structure interaction forces depend only on the nature of the unbounded soil domain, which itself is assumed always to be linear.

## 2 The Laplace–Time method

### 2.1 General principle

Consider the particular case of two subdomains separated by an interface $\Gamma$ : l’un noté :math:`{\Omega}_{1}$ , which is unbounded and which presents a linear behaviour, and the other, ${\Omega}_{2}$ , bounded and possibly non-linear. The interaction effects between the two subdomains can be represented on the interface by an impedance that is assumed to be defined in the Fourier or Laplace domain. In this framework, the global problem is solved in time in ${\Omega}_{2}$ , taking into account the effects of the subdomain ${\Omega}_{1}$ by means of an external force applied on the boundary. This interaction force, which brings into play the impedance function in the time domain $Z(t)$ , corresponds to a convolution integral with the unknown field ${u}_{\Gamma}(t)$ , which will be denoted $(Z\ast {u}_{\Gamma})(t)$ .

Thus, in a general manner, the evaluation of the interaction forces between the subdomains proceeds by the computation of a convolution integral between two causal functions, defined as:

$$
(Z\ast {u}_{\Gamma})(t)={\int}_{0}^{t}Z(t-\tau ){u}_{\Gamma}(\tau )\text{}d\tau ,\text{}0\le t\le T
$$

Let $\widehat{Z}(s)$ be the Laplace transform of $Z(t)$ ; it will be assumed that it is analytic in the complex half-plane $\mathit{\Re e}(s)>{\sigma}_{0}$ and of slow growth for $\mid s\text{|}$ large:

| :math: |  | widehat{Z}(s) |  | le C({sigma}_{0}) | s{ | }^{mu}text{avec}C({sigma}_{0})text{et}mu in ℝ` |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

In this framework, let $\widehat{Z}(s)={\widehat{Z}}_{m}(s)\widehat{P}(s)$ with $\widehat{P}(s)$ a polynomial function in $s$ with matrix values 1
of degree $m\ge \mu$ :

$$
\widehat{P}(s)=\sum_{p=0}^{m}{\Lambda}_{p}{s}^{p}
$$

If this polynomial decomposition is considered, the convolution kernel $Z(t)$ , defined in the sense of distributions, can be expressed in the form of a differentiation operator of order $p$ as:

$$
Z(t)=\left({Z}_{m}\ast \sum_{p=0}^{m}{\Lambda}_{p}\frac{{d}^{p}\delta }{d{t}^{p}}\right)(t)
$$

Consequently, the convolution product can finally be written as:

$$
(Z\ast {u}_{\Gamma})(t)=\sum_{p=0}^{m}({\int}_{0}^{t}{Z}_{m}(\tau ){\Lambda}_{p}{u}_{\Gamma}^{(p)}(t-\tau )d\tau )
$$

with ${u}_{\Gamma}(t)$ a causal function that is sufficiently differentiable.

Using Lubich’s quadrature method, the previous equation can be expressed in the form of a discrete convolution. Indeed, if $\Delta t$ denotes the discretisation time step, the latter can be written at the time instants $n\Delta t$ ($0\le n\Delta t\le t$ ) as follows:

$$
(Z\ast {u}_{\Gamma})(n\Delta t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}({\Psi}_{1}^{n-k+1}{u}_{\Gamma ,k}+...+{\Psi}_{p}^{n-k+1}{u}_{\Gamma ,k}^{(p)}+...+{\Psi}_{m}^{n-k+1}{u}_{\Gamma ,k}^{(m)})
$$

where the coefficients ${\Psi}_{k}^{j}$ contain the contribution of the matrices ${\Lambda}_{k}$ .

### 2.2 Application to soil impedance operators

The soil impedance matrix, that is the spatially discretised version of the impedance operator, relates a displacement vector defined on the degrees of freedom of the soil–structure interface (or, in a generalised manner within the framework of the Aster–MISS3D coupling, on the FEM–BEM boundary) to the force vector defined on the same interface, denoted $\Gamma$ in what follows:

$$
\widehat{Z}(s){\widehat{u}}_{\Gamma}(s)={\widehat{F}}_{\Gamma}(s)
$$

In a general manner, the soil impedance matrix, which is assumed analytic in the half-space $\mathit{\Re e}(s)>{\sigma}_{0}$ for reasons of causality, can be expressed in the following form:

$$
\widehat{Z}(s)={\widehat{Z}}_{\mathit{sing}}(s)+{\widehat{Z}}_{\mathit{nsing}}(s)
$$

where ${\widehat{Z}}_{\mathit{nsing}}(s)$ corresponds to the regular part of the impedance, whose inverse Laplace transform ${Z}_{\mathit{nsing}}(t)$ , which exists in the classical sense, is defined as an exponentially bounded function, continuous in time, which vanishes for $t<0$ :

$$
{Z}_{\mathit{nsing}}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}{\int}_{{\sigma}_{0}+iR}{e}^{st}{\widehat{Z}}_{\mathit{nsing}}(s)\mathit{ds}
$$

By contrast, ${\widehat{Z}}_{\mathit{sing}}(s)$ denotes the singular part of the impedance, understood here as a function of slow growth and unbounded at high frequency. In particular, by analogy with an FEM formulation, this singular part of the impedance can be written as follows:

$$
{\widehat{Z}}_{\mathit{sing}}(s)={M}_{\Gamma}{s}^{2}+{C}_{\Gamma}s+{K}_{\Gamma}
$$

where ${M}_{\Gamma}$ , ${C}_{\Gamma}$ and ${K}_{\Gamma}$ model respectively the contributions of the soil in terms of inertia, damping and stiffness at the interface $\Gamma$ . On met en évidence deux points intéressants à mentionner. Le premier vise à souligner que cette formulation permet de montrer facilement que l'impédance vérifie explicitement la condition explicité dans l'équation () pour :math:`m=2$ . The second concerns rather the non-singular character of the inverse Laplace transform, which this time exists only in the sense of distributions and which, consequently, is written by means of the Dirac delta and of its derivatives of first and second order:

$$
{Z}_{\mathit{sing}}(t)={M}_{\Gamma}\ddot{\delta}(t)+{C}_{\Gamma}\dot{\delta}(t)+{K}_{\Gamma}\delta (t)
$$

The numerical handling of ${\widehat{Z}}_{\mathit{sing}}(t)$ is very delicate and at the same time indispensable, because this evolution must be computed in order to obtain the transient soil–structure interaction forces. Consequently, it can be stated that the particular character of the time impedance makes necessary the use of more efficient time-discretisation approaches, and among these one finds the Laplace–Time method, resting on a convolution-quadrature method.

In this framework, the convolution-quadrature method presented by Lubich can be applied for the numerical evaluation of a convolution integral such as that given in equation (), where one considers for $Z(t)$ the inverse Laplace transform of the soil impedance $\widehat{Z}(s)$ and ${u}_{\Gamma}(t)$ the displacement field on the interface. This method makes it possible to evaluate equation () approximately by a discrete convolution (with a time step $\Delta t>0$ ):

$$
(Z\ast {u}_{\Gamma})(n\Delta t)=\sum_{0\le n\Delta t\le t}{\Phi}_{k}{u}_{\Gamma}(t-n\Delta t)
$$

where the coefficients ${\Phi}_{k}$ correspond to the weights of the following power series:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty }{\Phi}_{k}{\zeta}^{k}=\widehat{Z}({s}_{\Delta t})
$$

The sampling points ${s}_{\Delta t}$ of the dynamic soil impedance are given in the following section.

Having arrived at this point, and taking into account that expression () is homogeneous with a force, it seems judicious to express this convolution not only in terms of displacement, but also as a function of the accelerations and of the velocities. The approach proposed belongs to this line of thought and rests on the factorisation of the polynomial part $\widehat{P}(s)$ of the impedance:

$$
\widehat{Z}(s)={\widehat{Z}}_{m}(s)\widehat{P}(s)={\widehat{Z}}_{m}(s)({\tilde{M}}_{\Gamma}{s}^{2}+{\tilde{C}}_{\Gamma}s+{\tilde{K}}_{\Gamma})
$$

Where ${\tilde{K}}_{\Gamma}$ , ${\tilde{C}}_{\Gamma}$ and ${\tilde{M}}_{\Gamma}$ are respectively estimators of the stiffness, damping and inertia matrices of the soil. Thus, the convolution can be written by means of the inverse Laplace transform as follows:

$$
(Z\ast {u}_{\Gamma})(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}{\int}_{{\sigma}_{0}+iR}{\widehat{Z}}_{m}(s)\widehat{P}(s){\widehat{u}}_{\Gamma}(s){e}^{st}\mathit{ds}
$$

Consequently, the polynomial function $\widehat{P}(s)$ being seen as a differentiation operator that acts on the displacement field, equation () becomes:

$$
(Z\ast {u}_{\Gamma})(t)=({Z}_{m}\ast {\tilde{M}}_{\Gamma}{\ddot{u}}_{\Gamma})(t)+({Z}_{m}\ast {\tilde{C}}_{\Gamma}{\dot{u}}_{\Gamma})(t)+({Z}_{m}\ast {\tilde{K}}_{\Gamma}{u}_{\Gamma})(t)
$$

where the interaction forces (denoted in what follows by ${R}_{\Gamma}(t)$ ) are computed by convolutions with the accelerations, velocities and displacements of the interface.

Taking a discretisation time step $\Delta t>0$ , the convolution integral becomes a particular case of equation () for $m=2$ :

$$
{R}_{n}=(Z\ast {u}_{\Gamma})(n\Delta t)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}({\Psi}_{2}^{n-k+1}{\ddot{u}}_{\Gamma ,k}+{\Psi}_{1}^{n-k+1}{\dot{u}}_{\Gamma ,k}+{\Psi}_{0}^{n-k+1}{u}_{\Gamma ,k})
$$

where the matrices multiplying the displacement, velocity and acceleration vectors are given by:

$$
{\Psi}_{0}^{k}={Z}_{m}^{k}{\tilde{K}}_{\Gamma}
{\Psi}_{1}^{k}={Z}_{m}^{k}{\tilde{C}}_{\Gamma}
$$

Indeed, in a manner analogous to the reasoning followed for equations () and (), the coefficients of the matrices ${\Psi}_{0}^{k}$ , ${\Psi}_{1}^{k}$ and ${\Psi}_{2}^{k}$ correspond to the weights of the following power series:

$$
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty }{\Psi}_{j}^{k}{z}^{k}={\widehat{Z}}_{m}({s}_{\Delta t}){\Lambda}_{j}
$$

With ${\Lambda}_{0}={\tilde{K}}_{\Gamma}$ , ${\Lambda}_{1}={\tilde{C}}_{\Gamma}$ and ${\Lambda}_{2}={\tilde{M}}_{\Gamma}$ .

To simplify the theoretical development, all the quantities already discretised in space (for example, with a finite-element method) are considered in what follows.

## 3 Computation of the impedance in time

As mentioned previously, the Laplace–Time method is a numerical approach making it possible to discretise a convolution integral in time.

### 3.1 Implementation in code_aster

Equation () shows that the numerical evaluation of the weights ${\Psi}_{k}^{j}$ first proceeds by that of ${\widehat{Z}}_{m}^{k}$ . In turn, the latter can be obtained from a Cauchy integral applied on a contour $|z|=\rho$ :

| :math:{Z}_{m}(kDelta t)=frac{1}{2pi i}{int}_{ | z | =rho }{widehat{Z}}_{m}left(frac{delta (z)}{Delta t}right){z}^{-k-1}dz` |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

If this integral is expressed in polar coordinates $z=\rho {e}^{i\theta }$ , then the trapezoidal rule is applied to the phase in order to discretise it in $L$ identical steps of value $\Delta \theta =\frac{2\pi }{L}$ , one obtains:

$$
{Z}_{m}(k∆t)\approx {Z}_{m}^{k}=\frac{{\rho}^{-k}}{L}\sum_{l=0}^{L-1}{\widehat{Z}}_{m}({s}_{l}){e}^{-i\frac{2\pi l}{L}k}
$$

where the operator defined in the Laplace domain is sampled on ${s}_{l}=\frac{\delta (\rho {e}^{2\pi il∕L})}{\Delta t}$ with $\delta (z)=\frac{3}{2}-2z+\frac{1}{2}{z}^{2}$ the characteristic polynomial of a second-order linear multistep method. Note that $N$ and $L$ are not necessarily equal, $N$ being the number of time steps of the time window of interest (for example, as a function of the duration of the seismic signal) and $L$ being the number of time steps of the computation of the impedance in time.

If it is considered that ${\widehat{Z}}_{m}({s}_{l})$ is evaluated with a variability 2
${ϵ}_{\mathit{CQM}}$ , the coefficients ${\widehat{Z}}_{m}^{k}$ will be computed with an accuracy 3
$O(\sqrt{{ϵ}_{\mathit{CQM}}})$ if $L=N$ and ${\rho}^{N}=\sqrt{{ϵ}_{\mathit{CQM}}}$ . With the values of $L$ and $\rho$ fixed, the weights of the series can be obtained from a classical FFT, which brings the complexity of the computation algorithm down to $O(L\logL)$ instead of $O({L}^{2})$ . Nevertheless, the value of ${ϵ}_{\mathit{CQM}}$ is not obvious to calibrate and depends in part on the nature of the problem to be solved. Indeed, values of ${ϵ}_{\mathit{CQM}}$ that are too small, such as ${10}^{-20}$ or ${10}^{-30}$ , can lead to integration radii $\mid z\mid =\rho$ that are too small. Since the Cauchy integrand contains a singularity at zero, values of the radius that are too small can result in a poor evaluation of the integral. In this respect, parametric studies have been carried out in in order to conclude that a good compromise is reached when ${ϵ}_{\mathit{CQM}}={10}^{-10}$ 4
.

Likewise, it will be noted that the dependence on ${\rho}^{-k}$ leads, for large values of $k$ , to inaccuracies on the coefficients ${\widehat{Z}}_{m}^{k}$ computed. Knowing that the use of more accurate integration methods, of Simpson type for example, does not correct this problem, the idea is to widen the time range (i.e. $L=\mathit{mN},m\in \mathrm{ℝ}$ ) over which the impedance is computed, in order to reduce the perturbations on the solution of interest. In this respect, it has been observed that, in general, the inaccuracies of ${\widehat{Z}}_{m}^{k}$ propagate onto the response only beyond $t\approx 0,7{T}_{\mathit{FIN}}$ , where ${T}_{\mathit{FIN}}$ is the final time instant of the impedance computation 5
.

### 3.2 Main numerical considerations

It has been seen previously that, in general, the singular part of the soil impedance can be approximated by a polynomial of order two. If the coefficients of this polynomial are real, it is easy to show that the soil impedance satisfies $\widehat{Z}(\sigma -i\omega )=\mathit{conj}[\widehat{Z}(\sigma +i\omega )]$ in the analytic plane of the Laplace domain 6
. It will be noted that when a hysteretic damping is used in the soil (the case of MISS3D), the imaginary part of its static stiffness is non-zero and the assumption of real coefficients is no longer satisfied.

A second interesting property, coming directly from the definition of the one-sided Laplace transform, is the following:

$$
\widehat{Z}(s=0)={\int}_{0}^{ꝏ}Z(t)\mathit{dt}\approx \sum_{k}^{N}Z(k\Delta t)
$$

for $N$ chosen sufficiently large (~ a hundred time steps). Note that the soil impedance evaluated at $s=0$ corresponds to the static stiffness of the soil (real part), which is very different from the instantaneous stiffness of the soil. This must be well taken into account when a dynamic analysis ($Z(t=0)]$ is assembled on the left-hand side) is carried out following a static analysis ($\mathrm{\Re }[\widehat{Z}(s=0)]$ is assembled on the left-hand side)
.

## 4 Seismic analysis

The seismic analysis of non-linear soil–structure interaction must be solved in the time domain. A few elements on the computation of the seismic force and the time discretisation of the equations of the coupled system are supplied below.

### 4.1 Computation of the seismic force in time

The Laplace–Time method could also be applied for the computation of the seismic force; nevertheless the nature of the incident field developed in MISS3D invalidates the assumptions on the solution fields sought in the Laplace domain. In this framework, it cannot be guaranteed that the computation of the seismic force at complex frequency with MISS3D is correct for every type of foundation (surface and embedded) and stratigraphy. The approach recommended in the studies thus proceeds by the inverse Fourier transform of the seismic force evaluated by MISS3D in the frequency domain.

### 4.2 Time-integration scheme

This part seeks to illustrate the numerical treatment of the convolution integral within the framework of a solution with a time-integration scheme of the Newmark family. Nevertheless, the reasoning also applies to the $\alpha$ -HHT time-integration scheme, also available in code_aster.

One will thus seek to solve in time the following linear dynamic problem, assumed discretised in space with the classical finite-element method:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc}{L}_{bb}(\cdot )& {L}_{b\Gamma }(\cdot )\\ (sym.)& {L}_{\Gamma \Gamma }(\cdot )\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}{u}_{b}(t)\\ {u}_{\Gamma}(t)\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}{c}0\\ {R}_{\Gamma}(t)\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}{F}_{b}(t)\\ {F}_{\Gamma}(t)\end{array}\right]
$$

where a time differentiation operator ${L}_{\mathit{\alpha \beta }}(\cdot )$ is introduced for $\alpha ,\beta \in \lbrace b,\Gamma \rbrace$ , $\Gamma$ for the degrees of freedom of the interface as opposed to the rest of the degrees of freedom of the system, denoted $b$ , and where the vector of soil–structure interaction forces, denoted ${R}_{\Gamma}(t)$ , corresponds to the convolution integral that will be discretised with the Laplace–Time method.

The solution of the coupled soil–structure system proceeds by the discretisation of all the quantities in time and by isolating the unknowns at the time instant $t=n\Delta t$ , where the time step is denoted $\Delta t$ . In particular, for the soil–structure interaction forces, it is interesting to start from equation () and to group the non-unknown terms corresponding to the time instants preceding $t=n\Delta t$ into a single term, in order to separate them from the unknowns to be solved at time step $n$ . The corresponding vector ${R}_{n}\approx {R}_{\Gamma}(n\Delta t)$ is thus written as follows:

$$
{R}_{n}={\Psi}_{2}^{1}{\ddot{u}}_{\Gamma ,n}+{\Psi}_{1}^{1}{\dot{u}}_{\Gamma ,n}+{\Psi}_{0}^{1}{u}_{\Gamma ,n}+{R}_{\Sigma (n-1)}
$$

With ${\Psi}_{0}^{1}$ , ${\Psi}_{1}^{1}$ and ${\Psi}_{2}^{1}$ referring respectively to the instantaneous stiffness, damping and inertia
and ${R}_{\Sigma (n-1)}$ depending only on the quantities computed at the previous time instants
:

$$
{R}_{\Sigma (n-1)}=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}({\Psi}_{2}^{n-k+1}{\ddot{u}}_{\Gamma ,k}+{\Psi}_{1}^{n-k+1}{\dot{u}}_{\Gamma ,k}+{\Psi}_{0}^{n-k+1}{u}_{\Gamma ,k})
$$

This grouping of terms makes it possible to introduce on the right-hand side the part of the convolution coming from the previous time instants, and on the left-hand side the terms bearing on unknowns. Indeed, this can easily be brought out by starting from a displacement formulation of an unconditionally stable Newmark integration scheme ($\beta =0.25$ , $\gamma =0.5$ ) applied to equation ():

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\tilde{K}}_{11}& {\tilde{K}}_{12}\\ {\tilde{K}}_{21}& {\tilde{K}}_{22}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}{u}_{b,n}\\ {u}_{\Gamma ,n}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}{F}_{b,n}\\ {F}_{\Gamma ,n}\end{array}\right]-\left[\begin{array}{c}0\\ {R}_{\Sigma (n-1)}\end{array}\right]
$$

where the matrix $\tilde{K}$ corresponds to the Newmark operator that will have to be inverted at each time step, and the right-hand side to the sum of the equivalent Newmark vector and the known part of the discrete convolution product. In particular, the term $\tilde{{K}_{22}}$ , which applies to the degrees of freedom of the interface $\Gamma$ , is written:

$$
{\tilde{K}}_{22}=\frac{1}{\beta \Delta {t}^{2}}({M}_{22}+{\Psi}_{2}^{1})+\frac{\gamma}{\beta \Delta t}({C}_{22}+{\Psi}_{1}^{1})+({K}_{22}+{\Psi}_{0}^{1})
$$

It will be noted that the terms ${\Psi}_{0}^{1}$ , ${\Psi}_{1}^{1}$ and ${\Psi}_{2}^{1}$ play a role in the conditioning of the Newmark operator $\tilde{K}$ and, consequently, their value has an impact on the convergence of the solution. Note also that $\tilde{{K}_{22}}$ , remaining constant during the analysis, is computed only once.

In code_aster, these instantaneous terms are assembled on the left-hand side via the operator MACR_ELEM_DYNA (cf. [U4.65.01](doc:u4.65.01)) and possibly a connection LIAISON_INTERF in the operator AFFE_CHAR_MECA (cf. [U4.44.01](doc:u4.44.01)) if a reduced modal basis of the interface has been used.

The term:math:{R}_{Sigma (n-1)} is modelled in code_aster during the non-linear dynamic analysis as a right-hand-side load obtained with the option “CHARGE_SOL” of the operator AFFE_CHAR_MECA (cf. [U4.44.01](doc:u4.44.01)).
