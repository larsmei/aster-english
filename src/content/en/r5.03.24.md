# r5.03.24 Large-strain model GDEF_LOG

**Summary:**

This document presents a hypoelastic large-strain formulation for constitutive laws of von Mises type, called GDEF_LOG in Code_Aster.

This model, due to C. Miehe, N. Apel and M. Lambrecht [13], is a large-strain model based on a logarithmic measure, with a particular dual stress tensor. It is valid whatever the small-strain constitutive behaviour, and it has the advantage of supplying a symmetric tangent matrix. No modification of the kinematic internal variables is required. It allows an incrementally objective integration of the constitutive laws (as does the SIMO_MIEHE model). However, as with all hypoelastic laws, the constitutive laws are strictly speaking limited to small elastic strains.

The capabilities of this model, and its advantages relative to the PETIT_REAC approximation, are illustrated in this document.

## 1 Introduction

Most of the constitutive laws of Code_Aster can be used under the small-perturbation hypothesis (HPP), which makes it possible to identify the initial and current geometric configurations. However, when the strains become large (the limit is generally set at 5%), this hypothesis is no longer satisfied. The notions of material derivative and partial derivative are then different and, as a result, the incrementally formulated constitutive laws lose their objective character (independence of the mechanical state with respect to the observer); an inconvenient consequence is the possible evolution of the stresses under a rigid-body motion, contrary to physics.

In order to recover objectivity, which is therefore indispensable if a reliable result is to be guaranteed, a large-strain strategy is possible. The purpose of this document is to present the formalism implemented in Code_Aster to treat constitutive laws with isotropic and kinematic hardening and a von Mises criterion.

The GDEF_LOG formalism is presented, due to C. Miehe, N. Apel and M. Lambrecht [13], which is a large-strain model based on a logarithmic measure, with a particular dual stress tensor. It is valid whatever the small-strain constitutive behaviour, and it has the advantage of supplying a symmetric tangent matrix. No modification of the kinematic internal variables is required.

## 2 Formulation and assumptions

This algorithm, due to C. Miehe, N. Apel and M. Lambrecht [13], is based on an energetic formulation, and the stiffness matrix is supplied in [13].

### 2.1 Elements of kinematics

The kinematic elements in the continuous case can be found, for example, in [3]. Interest here is in the case discretised directly in time, which makes it possible to define the quantities used in the formalism presented in this document. Consider a closed initial continuous domain ${\Omega}_{0}\subset {ℝ}^{3}$ , each point of which is located by its coordinates $X\in {\Omega}_{0}$ , subjected to a deformation field $\varphi$ which takes it into the configuration $\Omega$ :
$$
\varphi :{\Omega}_{0}\to \Omega \subset {ℝ}^{3}
$$

The coordinates of this point in the current configuration will be denoted $x\in \Omega$.

The deformed configuration evolving over time, a family of fields ${\varphi}_{n}$ is in fact defined, by means of the time discretisation, each corresponding to a time instant ${t}_{n}$ of the evolution history of the domain.

In the case of the large-strain formalism treated here, it is necessary to introduce four configurations for the domain and its evolution (cf. Figure 1): the initial reference configuration ${\Omega}_{0}$ (that is to say the one for which the strains are zero), the configuration ${\Omega}_{n}$ at the beginning of the current time step ${t}_{n+1}={t}_{n}+\Delta t$ , the configuration ${\Omega}_{n+1}$ at the end of this time step, and a mid-step configuration, ${\Omega}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}$ , the formalism being integrated with a midpoint rule.

The quantities taking the initial configuration into a given configuration are denoted in capitals $(U,\mathrm{F})$ and the quantities relating two deformed configurations to each other are denoted in lower case $(u,\mathrm{f})$ . From these deformation gradients, it is possible to define the strain rates $\mathrm{L}$ :

$$
{\mathrm{L}}_{°}={\dot{\mathrm{F}}}_{°}{\dot{\mathrm{F}}}_{°}^{-1}
$$

The spin-rate tensors $\omega$ :

$$
{\mathrm{d}}_{°}=\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathrm{L}}_{°}+{\mathrm{L}}_{°}^{T}\right)
$$

and the strain-rate tensors $\mathrm{d}$ :

$$
{\omega}_{°}=\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathrm{L}}_{°}-{\mathrm{L}}_{°}^{T}\right)
$$

with ${}_{°}$ denoting the configuration $n$ , $n+1$ or $n+\frac{1}{2}$ . The Eulerian strain tensor between the configurations ${\Omega}_{n}$ and ${\Omega}_{n+1}$ is deduced from these definitions:

$$
{\mathrm{e}}_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}\left[{\mathrm{I}}_{d}-{\left({\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}\cdot {\mathrm{f}}_{n+1}^{T}\right)}^{-1}\right]
$$

The strain rate is then indeed related to the Eulerian strain:

$$
{\mathrm{d}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{\Delta t}{\stackrel{̃}{\mathrm{f}}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{-1}\cdot {\mathrm{e}}_{n+1}\cdot {\stackrel{̃}{\mathrm{f}}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}
$$

The last quantity to be introduced for the algorithm is the incremental displacement gradient, relative to the configuration ${\Omega}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}$ and defined by:

$$
{\mathrm{h}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}={\mathrm{grad}}_{n}u\cdot {\mathrm{f}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{-1}
$$

The latter makes it possible to determine the spin rate in the same configuration by the relation:

$$
{\omega}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\left[{\mathrm{h}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}-{\mathrm{h}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}^{T}\right]
$$

From these kinematic elements, it is possible to define hypoelastic constitutive laws whose integration is objective in large strains. The following section presents this type of formulation of the constitutive laws.

### 2.2 Hypo-elastoplastic constitutive laws

In this section, the class of phenomenological plasticity models (here time-independent) with hypoelasticity is considered. It constitutes an *ad hoc* extension of the writing of the small-strain laws, which allows a certain generality and represents an advantage in the context of an analysis code: it will be seen in the following chapter that its numerical integration can be carried out in a manner equivalent to that of small strains.

This class of models is to be contrasted with the hyperelastic class, based on the thermodynamic approach of continua. In this context, a free energy, which may for example be considered as a function of the temperature and of the Green-Lagrange strain, is defined; the evolutions of the stresses and possibly of the internal variables follow from it. One may cite, for example, the case of the Signorini hyperelastic law (cf. [4]) in elasticity and the Simo-Miehe formalism in hyperelastoplasticity (cf. [3]).

A hypo-elastoplastic constitutive law is generally constructed in five steps.

1. In the same way as the additive split of the small strains, the strain rate $\mathrm{d}$ is first split into an elastic part and a plastic part:

$$
\mathrm{d}={\mathrm{d}}^{e}+{\mathrm{d}}^{p}
$$

1. A derivative of the Kirchhoff stress $\tau =det(\mathrm{F})\sigma$ is then determined by an incremental relation depending on the elastic strain rate, with $\stackrel{˚}{x}$ an objective derivative to be defined and $\mathrm{C}$ the elasticity tensor.:

$$
\stackrel{̊}{\tau}=\mathrm{C}:\left[\mathrm{d}-{\mathrm{d}}^{p}\right]
$$

1. A convex reversibility domain defining the admissible stress space is constructed from a function $f$ , with $S$ the stress space and $\mathrm{q}$ the set of the $m$ internal variables representing the kinematic hardening of the material and $\alpha$ the scalar variables (including the isotropic hardening):

$$
{{\rm E}}_{\tau}=\lbrace (\tau ,\mathrm{q},\alpha )\in S\times {ℝ}^{m+1}\mid f(\tau ,\mathrm{q},\alpha )⩽0\rbrace
$$

The evolution laws of these internal variables follow a normality principle (only associated constitutive laws are considered here), with $\gamma \ge 0$ the plastic multiplier, $\frac{\partial f}{\partial \tau }(\tau ,\mathrm{q},\alpha )$ defining the direction of plastic flow and $g(\tau ,\mathrm{q},\alpha )$ the evolution of the other internal variables:

$$
{\mathrm{d}}^{p}=\gamma \frac{\partial f(\tau ,\mathrm{q},\alpha )}{\partial \tau }
\stackrel{̊}{q}=-\gamma g(\tau ,\mathrm{q},\alpha )
$$

The writing of the loading/unloading conditions, classically represented by Kuhn-Tucker and the consistency condition:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\gamma \ge 0\\ f(\tau ,q,\alpha )\le 0\\ \gamma f(\tau ,q,\alpha )=0\end{array}
$$

This class of model is therefore characterised by a strong analogy with the small-strain formalisms, with an incremental writing of the stresses which is not without raising some difficulties of numerical integration: indeed, in order to prevent stress evolutions under a rigid-body motion, an objective integration of equation () is required.

### 2.3 Strain and stress tensors

The model is based on the logarithmic strain defined by:

$$
\mathrm{E}=\frac{1}{2}\log\left[{\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{T}}.\mathrm{F}\right]
$$

The definition of this expression is supplied in appendix 2. The stress $\mathrm{T}$ is defined in the logarithmic space as dual to $\mathrm{E}$ , so that the mechanical power density ${p}_{m}$ is expressed by their product $\mathrm{T}:\dot{\mathrm{E}}$ . It is not a classical stress tensor, but it can be related to the usual tensors. Indeed, the mechanical power being written:

$$
{p}_{m}=\mathrm{T}:\dot{\mathrm{E}}=\Pi :\dot{\mathrm{F}}
$$

One obtains:

$$
{p}_{m}=\mathrm{T}:\dot{\mathrm{E}}=\mathrm{T}:\frac{\partial \mathrm{E}}{\partial \mathrm{F}}:\dot{\mathrm{F}}=\Pi :\dot{\mathrm{F}}=\mathrm{T}:{\mathrm{P}}_{\Pi}:\mathrm{F}
$$

With $\Pi$ the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor. This defines the projection tensor (of order four in 3D) ${\mathrm{P}}_{\Pi}$ :

$$
{\mathrm{P}}_{\Pi}=\frac{\partial \mathrm{E}}{\partial \mathrm{F}}
$$

One therefore has:

$$
\Pi =\mathrm{T}:{\mathrm{P}}_{\Pi}
$$

The Cauchy tensor $\sigma$ and the Kirchhoff tensor $\tau$ will be written in the usual way:

$$
J\sigma =\tau =\Pi {\mathrm{F}}^{T}
$$

The second Piola-Kirchhoff tensor $\mathrm{S}$ can also be computed as a function of $\mathrm{T}$ :

$$
{p}_{m}=\mathrm{T}:\dot{\mathrm{E}}=\mathrm{S}:\dot{\Delta}=\mathrm{S}:\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathrm{C}}
$$

With $\Delta$ the Green-Lagrange strain tensor such that:

$$
\Delta =\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{C}-\mathrm{I}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left({\mathrm{F}}^{T}\mathrm{F}-\mathrm{I}\right)
$$

There is a new form of the mechanical power:

$$
{p}_{m}=\mathrm{T}:\dot{\mathrm{E}}=\mathrm{T}:\frac{\partial \mathrm{E}}{\partial \mathrm{C}}:\dot{\mathrm{C}}=\mathrm{S}:\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathrm{C}}
$$

One obtains for the second Piola-Kirchhoff tensor $\mathrm{S}$ :

$$
\mathrm{S}=\mathrm{T}:\mathrm{P}
$$

If the chosen physics is particular, the model nevertheless makes it possible to retain the additive split of the elastic and plastic strains which is classical under HPP, with:

$$
{\mathrm{E}}^{p}=\frac{1}{2}\log({\mathrm{F}}^{p,T}.{\mathrm{F}}^{p})
$$

Such a choice is always admissible. It simply amounts to adopting a definition for the elastic strain. Now, this definition proves consistent with a multiplicative split, in the absence of rotation (coaxial situation). Furthermore, plastic incompressibility is ensured because:

$$
\mathit{tr}{\mathrm{E}}^{p}=\log{J}^{p}
$$

The elastic energy ${\psi}^{e}$ of the model also takes the same form as that of small strains but, adopting the notions of stress and strain specific to this formalism, one has:

$$
{\psi}^{e}=\frac{1}{2}{\parallel \mathrm{E}-{\mathrm{E}}^{p}\parallel }_{E}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{T}:{\mathrm{C}}^{-1}:\mathrm{T}
$$

This formulation has certain advantages:

- the kinematic dimension of the model is confined upstream and downstream of the integration of the constitutive behaviour; this was one of the main factors in the choice of the formalism; all the constitutive models available in small strains are *a priori* available, provided of course that this has a physical meaning (hypoelastic large strains are well suited to metallic constitutive behaviours, and not to the constitutive behaviours of concrete);
- if the HPP model admits an energetic expression, the same will be true of the large-strain model: the tangent matrix is therefore symmetric;
- the only difficulty seems *a priori* to be concentrated in the definition of the logarithmic strain, but article [13] supplies a computation algorithm distinguishing the difficult cases (multiple eigenentities);
- the model, according to the examples presented by the authors, gives results very close to those obtained by a classical formalism with a multiplicative split;
- the model can be extended to cases of anisotropy (initial or induced).

Moreover, article [13] supplies an expression of the tangent matrix in the configuration using $\Pi$ (called nominal); however, since it is based on a formulation in terms of the first Piola-Kirchhoff stresses, which is non-symmetric, which is not classical and is never carried out in *Code_Aster* , it is preferred here to use the second Piola-Kirchhoff tensor to compute the internal forces and the tangent matrix on the initial configuration, referring to [15] for example.

## 3 Algorithm

### 3.1 Pre-processing

> The logarithmic strain tensor computed by spectral decomposition:

$$
{\mathrm{E}}_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}\log\left[{\mathrm{F}}_{n+1}^{T}\cdot {\mathrm{F}}_{n+1}\right]=\frac{1}{2}\log{\mathrm{C}}_{n+1}
$$

That is to say that, if the ${\lambda}^{(i)}$ are the eigenvalues of ${\mathrm{C}}_{n+1}$ and ${N}^{(i)}$ the associated eigenvectors, then the chosen strain measure is written:

$$
{\mathrm{E}}_{n+1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1,3}\log({\lambda}^{(i)}){N}^{(i)}\otimes {N}^{(i)}
$$

This measure makes it possible to obtain an additive split:

$$
\mathrm{E}={\mathrm{E}}^{e}+{\mathrm{E}}^{p}
$$

With:

$$
{\mathrm{E}}^{p}=\frac{1}{2}\log\left[{\mathrm{F}}^{p,T}\cdot {\mathrm{F}}^{p}\right]
$$

One may also write, between time instant $n$ and time instant $n+1$ :

$$
{\mathrm{E}}_{n+1}={\mathrm{E}}_{n}+\Delta \mathrm{E}
$$

### 3.2 Branching to the HPP constitutive law

The HPP constitutive law must supply the stress tensor $\mathrm{T}$ , defined by:

$$
{\mathrm{T}}_{n+1}=\widehat{\mathrm{T}}\left(\Delta \mathrm{E};{\mathrm{E}}_{n},{\mathrm{T}}_{n},{\beta}_{n}\right)
$$

where ${\beta}_{n}$ represents the set of internal variables at time instant $n$ and ${\mathrm{T}}_{n}$ the stresses at time instant $n$ . ${\mathrm{T}}_{n}$ must therefore be recomputed as a function of the Cauchy stresses ${\sigma}_{n}$ stored at time instant $n$ . These stresses are written ${\mathrm{T}}_{n}={\mathrm{S}}_{n}:{\mathrm{P}}_{n}^{\text{-1}}$ . However, this requires the transformation of ${\sigma}_{n}$ into ${\mathrm{S}}_{n}$ and the computation of ${\mathrm{P}}_{n}^{\text{-1}}$ , which can be costly. It is therefore chosen to store the tensors $\mathrm{T}$ as internal variables.

*Note*

The tensors $\mathrm{T}$ being stored as internal variables, the user wishing to impose an initial stress state shall use the operands VARI and DEPL of the factor keyword ETAT_INIT of the command STAT_NON_LINE. Indeed, the stress tensor defined in the logarithmic space $\mathrm{T}$ (and not the Cauchy stress $\sigma$ ) must be given as input. The user wishing to use the GDEF_LOG formalism with an initial stress field (ETAT_INIT) is advised to refer to the test case *ssnp159b (V6.03.159).*

### 3.3 Post-processing

The second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor is obtained by:

$$
{\mathrm{S}}_{n+1}={\mathrm{T}}_{n+1}:{\mathrm{P}}_{n+1}
$$

With ${\mathrm{P}}_{n+1}={\partial}_{\mathrm{C}}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{n+1}\right)$ . This quantity is computed *via* an algorithm presented in [13] and recalled in appendix 2. It is also recalled that the Cauchy stress tensor is obtained by:

$$
J\sigma =\tau =\mathrm{F}\cdot \mathrm{S}\cdot {\mathrm{F}}^{T}=\mathrm{F}\cdot (\mathrm{T}:\mathrm{P})\cdot {\mathrm{F}}^{T}
$$

The tangent modulus, in the so-called "Lagrangian" configuration, is obtained by differentiation of ${\mathrm{S}}_{n+1}$ :

$$
{\dot{\mathrm{S}}}_{n+1}={\mathrm{C}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ep}}:\frac{1}{2}{\dot{\mathrm{C}}}_{n+1}
$$

${\mathrm{E}}_{n+1}^{\mathit{ep}}=\frac{\partial {\mathrm{T}}_{n+1}}{\partial {\mathrm{E}}_{n+1}}$ represents the tangent operator coming from the constitutive law and ${\mathrm{L}}_{n+1}$ is the sixth-order tensor (in 3D) defined by:

$$
{\mathrm{L}}_{n+1}=4{\partial}_{\mathrm{C}\mathrm{C}}^{2}\left({\mathrm{E}}_{n+1}\right)
$$

The internal forces and the tangent matrix can then be computed on this configuration, on the initial configuration, as in [14]. Objectivity is preserved (stresses invariant under rotation for isotropic and kinematic hardening) and the accuracy is good (results identical to SIMO_MIEHE in the isotropic case).

## 4 Validity of the large-strain models

It must also be stated that the formalism presented and studied here does not extend the validity of the constitutive laws into the large-strain domain; it only proposes an objective derivation of them. To clarify this statement, the case of an elastoplastic law with linear isotropic hardening may be considered. This type of plasticity law is physically valid in small strains; its use is extended to large strains, but its physical validity may rightly be called into question.

Furthermore, an identification carried out on small-strain tests must potentially be reconsidered; Figure 2 presents a tensile curve modelled by a linear isotropic hardening: the tangent modulus must necessarily be defined with respect to the strain range considered. In small strains, it seems more appropriate to use ${E}_{\mathrm{T1}}$ ; if the strains are larger, it seems more appropriate to use ${E}_{\mathit{T2}}$ as the value of the hardening slope. But it is clear that it is the physical validity of the law itself that should be called into question.

## 5 Comparison with PETIT_REAC

### 5.1 Approximation of large strains by PETIT_REAC

The principle of the PETIT_REAC formulation simply consists in updating the geometry of the problem during the Newton iterations (and not at the end of each time step). This means that all the quantities appearing in the equations of the problem are evaluated on the current configuration. Nothing else is modified relative to the small-perturbation case.

#### 5.1.1 Kinematic description

The kinematic description is the same as that of the small perturbations. This means that a strain increment is computed by:

$$
\mathrm{\Delta \epsilon }=\frac{1}{2}({\nabla}_{{\Omega}_{i}}(\mathrm{\Delta u})+{\nabla}_{{\Omega}_{i}}^{T}(\mathrm{\Delta u}))
$$

${\Omega}_{i}$ being the updated configuration. The total strain is then the sum of each of these linearised strain increments, computed on different configurations. It is therefore delicate to give it a physical meaning, and it is better to use it as an indicator of the strain level reached. The hypothesis of an additive split of the strains is applied.

#### 5.1.2 Elastoplastic constitutive relation

In the expression of the elastic stress–strain relation, the necessity of using an objective derivative has been seen: $\stackrel{̊}{\sigma}=\mathrm{C}:[\dot{\epsilon}-{\dot{\epsilon}}^{p}]$ . With PETIT_REAC the objective derivative is replaced by the simple time derivative: it is therefore not objective. Consequently, the use of PETIT_REAC is not appropriate for large rotations, but it is appropriate for large strains, under certain conditions [10]:

- very small increments;
- very small rotations (which implies a quasi-radial loading);
- elastic strains small compared with the plastic strains;
- isotropic constitutive behaviour.

#### 5.1.3 Equilibrium and tangent matrix

In terms of finite elements, the solution by PETIT_REAC implies, at each load step, the solution of the same non-linear system as in small strains [11]:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{L}^{int}\left({u}_{i},{t}_{i}\right)+{B}^{T}.{\lambda}_{i}={L}^{\mathit{ext}}({t}_{i})\\ B.{u}_{i}={u}^{d}({t}_{i})\end{array}
$$

With the sole difference that the internal forces are formally computed by:

$$
{L}^{int}({u}_{i},{t}_{i})={Q}^{T}({u}_{i}):\sigma
$$

where the operator $Q$ depends on the displacements. In this framework, the computation of the tangent matrix leads to:

$$
{K}_{i}^{n}=\frac{\partial {L}^{int}}{\partial u}{\mid}_{({u}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}=Q(u):\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial u}{\mid}_{({u}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}+\frac{\partial Q(u)}{\partial u}{\mid}_{({u}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}:\sigma
$$

The first term is the contribution of the constitutive behaviour, similar to what was presented in small transformations, with the difference that this contribution is here evaluated in the current configuration. The second term is the geometric contribution, which is not present in small transformations.

In the framework of the PETIT_REAC solution, this term is not present in the computation of the tangent matrix. One therefore has:

$$
{K}_{i}^{n}=Q(u):\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial u}{\mid}_{({u}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}
$$

The absence of the geometric contribution in the tangent matrix can sometimes make convergence difficult.

### 5.2 Comparison on an example

The PETIT_REAC formalism (cf. [6]) is based on an update of the geometry for the computation of the strain increment, before integrating the constitutive behaviour in the same way as for small strains. This allows a simple treatment of large strains, but in a very approximate and non-objective manner, which can generate large errors.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.24/10000200000004530000015FCDEB098627D618FE.png)

To be convinced of this, consider the alternating tension–rotation of a cube; for more details on the test, refer to [7] for example.

Figure 3: Example of tension–rotation of a cube

During the rotation phases, the stress must remain constant: a rigid-body motion does not generate stresses (in statics at least, and without viscosity).

If the response obtained with a VMIS_ISOT_LINE constitutive behaviour is considered for the PETIT_REAC and GDEF_LOG strains, the PETIT_REAC strain type is shown to be inadequate whereas GDEF_LOG is valid (and supplies a response identical to SIMO_MIEHE).

## 8 Appendix 1: Computation of the logarithmic strains

### 8.1 Notation:

$\mathrm{A}$ denotes a second-order tensor, and $\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{A}}$ a fourth-order tensor. The Voigt notation is adopted, (see for example [16]) defined for a second-order tensor:

$$
\mathrm{A}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{A}_{11}\\ {A}_{22}\\ {A}_{33}\\ \sqrt{2}{A}_{12}\\ \sqrt{2}{A}_{13}\\ \sqrt{2}{A}_{23}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

And for a fourth-order tensor:

$$
\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{A}}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{cccccc}{A}_{1111}& {A}_{1122}& {A}_{1133}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{1112}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{1123}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{1113}\\ {A}_{2211}& {A}_{2222}& {A}_{2233}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{2212}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{2223}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{2213}\\ {A}_{3311}& {A}_{3322}& {A}_{3333}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{3312}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{3323}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{3313}\\ \sqrt{2}{A}_{1211}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{1222}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{1233}& 2{A}_{1212}& 2{A}_{1223}& 2{A}_{1213}\\ \sqrt{2}{A}_{1311}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{1322}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{1333}& 2{A}_{1312}& 2{A}_{1323}& 2{A}_{1313}\\ \sqrt{2}{A}_{2311}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{2322}& \sqrt{2}{A}_{2333}& 2{A}_{2312}& 2{A}_{2323}& 2{A}_{2313}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

Where the components relating to the Voigt notation will be denoted by a Greek letter:

$$
\parallel {A}_{ij}\parallel =\parallel {A}_{\alpha}\parallel
$$

The following properties then hold:

$$
\mathrm{A}:\mathrm{B}={A}_{ij}{B}_{ij}={A}_{\alpha}{B}_{\alpha}
\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{A}}:\mathrm{B}={A}_{ijkl}{B}_{kl}={A}_{\alpha \beta }{B}_{\beta}
\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{A}}:\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{B}}={A}_{ijkl}{B}_{\mathit{klmn}}={A}_{\alpha \beta }{B}_{\beta \gamma }
$$

The inverse of a fourth-order tensor having minor symmetries (${A}_{\mathit{ijrs}}={A}_{\mathit{jirs}}={A}_{\mathit{ijsr}}$ ) is written:

$$
\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{A}}:{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{A}}}^{-1}={\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{I}}}_{d}
{A}_{\mathit{ijrs}}{A}_{\mathit{rskl}}^{-1}={I}_{ijkl}=\frac{1}{2}\left({\delta}_{\mathit{ik}}{\delta}_{\mathit{jl}}+{\delta}_{\mathit{il}}{\delta}_{\mathit{jk}}\right)
{A}_{\alpha \gamma }{A}_{\gamma \beta }^{-1}={I}_{\alpha \beta }={\delta}_{\alpha \beta }
$$

### 8.2 Expression of the stresses in the Lagrangian configuration

The power of the internal forces is written:

$$
{p}_{int}=\mathrm{T}:\dot{\mathrm{E}}=\mathrm{S}:{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}}^{-1}\dot{\mathrm{E}}
$$

with $\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}=2\frac{\partial \mathrm{E}}{\partial \mathrm{C}}$ which makes it possible to compute $\mathrm{S}=\mathrm{T}:\mathrm{P}$ (or ${S}_{ij}={T}_{kl}:{P}_{\mathit{klij}}$ ). To compute the Cauchy stress tensor, it is sufficient to write:

$$
\sigma =\frac{1}{det\mathrm{F}}\mathrm{F}\cdot \mathrm{S}\cdot {\mathrm{F}}^{T}
$$

**Particular case of plane stresses:**

In this case, $det\mathrm{F}$ is not entirely known. Indeed, the $zz$ component of the logarithmic strain tensor $\mathrm{E}$ is unknown, because it depends on the constitutive law. Restricting attention to constitutive behaviours such that $det{\mathrm{F}}^{p}=0$ (plastic incompressibility), one then has $det\mathrm{F}=det{\mathrm{F}}^{e}$ .

Following [16] this expression can be computed:

$$
det{\mathrm{F}}^{e}={e}^{{E}_{xx}^{e}+{E}_{yy}^{e}+{E}_{zz}^{e}}
$$

Where ${\mathrm{E}}^{e}$ represents the elastic part of the logarithmic strains, known for any elastoplastic or elastoviscoplastic constitutive law by Hooke's law ${\mathrm{E}}^{e}={\Lambda}^{-1}\cdot \mathrm{T}$ .

This relation will be proved. The definition of the logarithmic strains is recalled:

$$
{E}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1,3}\log({\lambda}^{(k)}){N}_{i}^{(k)}\otimes {N}_{j}^{(k)}
$$

Which leads to this expression of the determinant:

$$
det{\mathrm{F}}^{e}=\sqrt{det{\mathrm{F}}^{T}\mathrm{F}}=\sqrt{{\lambda}_{1}{\lambda}_{2}{\lambda}_{3}}
$$

${\lambda}_{i}$ being the eigenvalues of ${\mathrm{F}}^{T}\cdot \mathrm{F}$ . Hence:

$$
\log(det{\mathrm{F}}^{e})=\frac{1}{2}\left\lbrace \log({\lambda}_{1})+\log({\lambda}_{2})+\log({\lambda}_{3})\right\rbrace
$$

By applying the exponential function, the result () is obtained.

### 8.3 Expression of the tangent operator in the Lagrangian configuration

By differentiating the expression $\mathrm{S}=\mathrm{T}:\mathrm{P}$ with respect to time:

$$
\dot{\mathrm{S}}=\dot{\mathrm{T}}:\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}+\mathrm{T}:\dot{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}}=\left(\frac{\partial \mathrm{T}}{\partial \mathrm{E}}:\dot{\mathrm{E}}\right):\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}+\mathrm{T}:\left(\frac{\partial \stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}}{\partial \mathrm{C}}:\dot{\mathrm{C}}\right)=\left[\frac{\partial \mathrm{T}}{\partial \mathrm{E}}:\left(\frac{\partial \mathrm{E}}{\partial \mathrm{C}}:\dot{\mathrm{C}}\right)\right]:\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}+\mathrm{T}:\left(\frac{\partial \stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}}{\partial \mathrm{C}}:\dot{\mathrm{C}}\right)
$$

That is:

$$
\dot{\mathrm{S}}=\left({\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}}^{T}:{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{E}}}^{p}:\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}+\mathrm{T}:\stackrel{̄}{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{L}}}\right):\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathrm{C}}
$$

with $\stackrel{̄}{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{L}}}=4\frac{{\partial}^{2}\mathrm{E}}{\partial \mathrm{C}\partial \mathrm{C}}$ and ${\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{E}}}^{p}=\frac{\partial \mathrm{T}}{\partial \mathrm{E}}$ . Which defines the tangent operator ${\stackrel{̄}{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{C}}}}^{\mathit{ep}}=\left({\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}}^{T}\cdot {\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{E}}}^{p}\cdot \stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}+\mathrm{T}:\stackrel{̄}{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{L}}}\right)$ which satisfies $\dot{\mathrm{S}}={\stackrel{̄}{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{C}}}}^{\mathit{ep}}:\frac{1}{2}\dot{\mathrm{C}}$ . Or again, as a function of the Green-Lagrange strains $\Delta =\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathrm{C}-{\mathrm{I}}_{d}\right)$ :

$$
\frac{\partial \mathrm{S}}{\partial \Delta }=\frac{\partial \mathrm{S}}{\partial \mathrm{C}}:\frac{\partial \mathrm{C}}{\partial \Delta }=2\frac{\partial \mathrm{S}}{\partial \mathrm{C}}={\stackrel{̄}{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{C}}}}^{\mathit{ep}}
$$

The expression of this tangent operator as well as of the stress tensor, both in the Lagrangian configuration, make it possible, for the computation of the internal forces, to use a variational formulation in the initial configuration, as in [R5.03.20](doc:r5.03.20) for example. Equilibrium is written in variational form on the initial configuration:

$$
\delta {W}_{int}\cdot \delta \mathrm{v}+{\mathit{SW}}_{\text{ext}}\cdot \delta \mathrm{v}=0
\forall \delta \mathrm{v}
$$

Under the hypothesis that the loading does not depend on the geometric transformation, the virtual work of the external forces is written as a linear form:

$$
\delta {W}_{\text{ext}}\cdot \delta \mathrm{v}=\underset{{\Omega}_{0}}{\int}{\rho}_{0}{F}_{i}\delta {v}_{i}d{\Omega}_{0}+\underset{{\partial}_{F}{\Omega}_{0}}{\int}{T}_{i}^{d}\delta {v}_{i}{\mathit{dS}}_{o}
$$

With $F$ the body loading and ${\mathrm{T}}^{d}$  a surface loading acting on the boundary ${\partial}_{F}{\Omega}_{0}$ . Here again, the initial configuration is chosen as the reference configuration, in order to express the work of the internal forces[R5,03,20].[R7.02.03]:

$$
{\mathit{SW}}_{int}\cdot \delta \mathrm{v}=-\underset{{\Omega}_{0}}{\int}{F}_{\mathit{ik}}{S}_{kl}\delta {v}_{i,l}d{\Omega}_{o}
$$

With a view to a solution by a Newton method, it is also important to express the second variation of the virtual work of the internal forces, namely the geometric stiffness:

$$
{d}^{2}{W}_{int}\cdot \delta \mathrm{u}\cdot \delta \mathrm{v}=-\underset{{\Omega}_{0}}{\int}\delta {u}_{i,k}S{r}_{kl}\delta v{r}_{i,l}d{\Omega}_{0}
$$

And the elastic stiffness:

$$
-\underset{{\Omega}_{0}}{\int}\delta {u}_{i,q}{F}_{\mathit{ip}}{\left(\frac{\partial \mathrm{S}}{\partial \Delta }\right)}_{\mathit{pqkl}}{F}_{\mathit{jk}}\delta {v}_{j,l}d{\Omega}_{0}
$$

### 8.4 Effective computation of the logarithmic strains

The logarithmic strains are defined by:

$$
\mathrm{E}=\frac{1}{2}\log(\mathrm{C})=\frac{1}{2}\log({\mathrm{F}}^{T}\cdot \mathrm{F})
$$

Strictly speaking, the metric tensor should be added in the case of an initial configuration defined in a space different from Euclidean space (the case of shells for example). To simplify the expressions, the case of a Euclidean initial configuration will be considered, the components of the vectors and tensors being written in a 3D orthonormal frame. The restriction to 2D is immediate. The computation of the logarithmic strain can only be carried out in the principal frame. The three eigenvalues ${\lambda}^{(i)}$ and eigenvectors ${N}^{(i)}$ which are solutions of the following eigenvalue problem must therefore be determined:

$$
\mathrm{C}{N}^{(i)}={\lambda}^{(i)}{N}^{(i)}
$$

The three values in the "principal" space can then be computed:

$$
{e}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{2}\log({\lambda}^{(i)})
$$

The logarithmic strains are then transported into the original space by:

$$
{E}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1,3}\log({\lambda}^{(k)}){N}_{i}^{(k)}\otimes {N}_{j}^{(k)}
$$

Because the logarithmic function is an isotropic function of the tensor $\mathrm{C}$ [16]. For the post-processing (see § 3.3  ), that is to say the computation of the stress tensor and of the tangent operator, the quantities $\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}=2\frac{\partial \mathrm{E}}{\partial \mathrm{C}}$ must be computed:

$$
\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{P}}=\sum_{i=1,3}\frac{1}{2}{d}^{(i)}{N}^{(i)}\otimes {N}^{(i)}\otimes {M}^{(ii)}+\sum_{i=1,3}\sum_{j\ne i}^{3}{\theta}_{ij}{N}^{(i)}\otimes {N}^{(j)}\otimes {M}^{(ij)}
$$

And the quantity $\mathrm{T}:\stackrel{̄}{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{L}}}$ :

$$
\begin{array}{c}\mathrm{T}:\stackrel{̄}{\stackrel{̄}{\mathrm{L}}}=\sum_{i}^{3}\frac{1}{4}{f}^{(i)}{\zeta}^{(ii)}{\mathrm{M}}^{(ii)}\otimes {\mathrm{M}}^{(ii)}+\sum_{i}^{3}\sum_{j\ne i}^{3}\sum_{k\ne i,k\ne j}^{3}2\eta {\zeta}^{(ij)}{\mathrm{M}}^{(\mathit{ik})}\otimes {\mathrm{M}}^{(ij)}\\ +\sum_{i}^{3}\sum_{j\ne i}2{\xi}^{(ij)}[{\zeta}^{(ij)}({\mathrm{M}}^{(ij)}\otimes {\mathrm{M}}^{(jj)}+{\mathrm{M}}^{(jj)}\otimes {\mathrm{M}}^{(ij)})+{\zeta}^{(jj)}{\mathrm{M}}^{(ij)}\otimes {\mathrm{M}}^{(ij)}]\end{array}
$$

With ${d}^{(i)}=\frac{1}{{\lambda}^{(i)}}$ , ${f}^{(i)}=\frac{-2}{{({\lambda}^{(i)})}^{2}}$ , ${\zeta}^{(ij)}=\mathrm{T}:{N}^{(i)}\otimes {N}^{(j)}$ , ${\mathrm{M}}_{\mathit{ab}}^{(ij)}={N}_{a}^{(i)}{N}_{b}^{(j)}+{N}_{a}^{(j)}{N}_{b}^{(i)}$ . ${\theta}^{(ij)}$ , ${\xi}^{(ij)}$ and ${\eta}^{(ij)}$ are defined by:

- if all the eigenvalues are different:

$$
{\theta}^{(ij)}=\frac{({e}^{(i)}-{e}^{(j)})}{({\lambda}^{(i)}-{\lambda}^{(j)})}
{\xi}^{(ij)}=\frac{({\theta}^{(ij)}-\frac{1}{2}{d}^{(j)})}{({\lambda}^{(i)}-{\lambda}^{(j)})}
\eta =\sum_{i}^{3}\sum_{j\ne i}^{3}\sum_{k\ne i,k\ne j}^{3}\frac{{e}^{(i)}}{2({\lambda}^{(i)}-{\lambda}^{(j)})({\lambda}^{(i)}-{\lambda}^{(k)})}
$$

- if two eigenvalues are equal ${\lambda}^{(i)}={\lambda}^{(j)}\ne {\lambda}^{(k)}$ :

$$
{\theta}^{(ij)}={\theta}^{(ji)}=\frac{1}{2}{d}^{(j)}
\textrm{pour}
n=k,m\in \lbrace i,j\rbrace
{\theta}^{(\mathrm{mn})}=\frac{({e}^{(m)}-{e}^{(n)})}{({\lambda}^{(m)}-{\lambda}^{(n)})}
$$

- if the three eigenvalues are equal ${\lambda}^{(i)}={\lambda}^{(j)}={\lambda}^{(k)}$ :

$$
{\theta}^{(ij)}=\frac{1}{2}{d}^{(j)}
$$
