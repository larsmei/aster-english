# r3.01.00 The isoparametric finite-element method

**Summary:**

This document presents the foundations of the isoparametric finite elements introduced in *Code_Aster* for the modelling of 2D and 3D continuous media. One first recalls the passage from a strong formulation to a variational formulation, then one details the discretisation by finite elements: use of a reference element, computation of the shape functions and evaluation of the elementary terms. One also briefly describes the principle of the assembly of these terms and of the imposition of the boundary conditions, and one mentions the matrix solution methods used. Finally, the principal stages of a finite-element analysis as it is designed and implemented in *Code_Aster* are set out.

## 1 Introduction

The finite-element method is used in many scientific fields to solve partial differential equations. It makes it possible to construct a simple approximation of the unknowns in order to transform these continuous equations into a system of equations of finite dimension, which one may write schematically in the following form:

$$
[A].\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace L\right\rbrace
$$

where $\left\lbrace U\right\rbrace$ is the vector of the unknowns, $[A]$ a matrix and $\left\lbrace L\right\rbrace$ a vector.

In a first stage, one transforms the partial differential equations into an integral formulation (or **strong** formulation of the problem); often this first integral form is modified (weakened) by using Green's formula (one then obtains a **weak** formulation). The approximate solution is sought as a linear combination of given functions. These functions must be simple but general enough to be able to approximate the solution “well”. In particular, they must make it possible to generate a space of finite dimension which is as close as one wishes to the space of functions in which the solution lies. Starting from this old idea (method of weighted residuals), the various ways of choosing these functions give rise to different numerical methods (collocation, spectral methods, finite elements, etc.).

The originality of the finite-element method is to take as approximation functions polynomials which vanish over almost the whole domain, and therefore take part in the computation only in the neighbourhood of a particular point. Thus, the matrix $[A]$ is very sparse, containing only the terms of interaction between “neighbouring points”, which reduces the run time and the memory space required for storage. Moreover, the matrix $[A]$ and the vector $\left\lbrace L\right\rbrace$ can be constructed by assembly of elementary matrices and vectors, computed locally.

## 2 Obtaining a variational formulation

One may obtain the variational formulation of a problem from the partial differential equations, by multiplying the latter by test functions and integrating by parts. In solid mechanics, the weak formulation then obtained is identical to that given by the Principle of Virtual Work and, in the conservative case, to the minimisation of the total potential energy of the structure. Note, however, that for certain problems the equations of the model are easier to establish in the variational framework (the case of plates and shells, for example).

### 2.1 Modelling of the physical problem – Principles and notation

A physical system is most often modelled by partial differential equations which act on unknowns $u$ which may be:

- A scalar, such as the temperature in thermal problems;
- A vector, such as the displacements in mechanics problems;
- A tensor, such as the stresses in mechanics problems;

One may also use several fields of unknowns simultaneously, related by partial differential equations. These are *coupled* problems. In Code_Aster, one may cite as an example the thermo-hydro-mechanical problems, which couple displacements, pressure and temperature.

The fields of unknowns are parametrised by:

- Space, which may be described by a Cartesian coordinate system or any other type of parametrisation. In the remainder of the document, it will be denoted $x$ ;
- Time, denoted $t$ ;

### 2.2 Equations of the system

A continuous physical system may be represented by a *system* of partial differential equations which one will write in the domain $\Omega$ :

$$
\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{u})+\mathrm{f}=0\text{dans}\Omega
$$

This system is associated with the boundary conditions on the boundary $\Gamma$ of the domain $\Omega$ :

$$
C(u)=h\text{sur}\Gamma =\partial \Omega
$$

The differential operator may be expressed over several partial differential equations. One could write:

$$
\begin{array}{}{L}_{1}(u)+{f}_{1}=0\\ {L}_{2}(u)+{f}_{2}=0\\ \dots \end{array}
$$

${L}_{i}(u)$ is a differential operator acting on the vector of the unknowns $u$ . More generally, the differential operator ${L}_{i}(u)$ is written as a function of the unknowns and of their partial derivatives:

$$
{L}_{i}(u,\frac{\partial u}{\partial {x}_{1}},\dots ,\frac{{\partial}^{2}u}{\partial {x}_{1}.\partial {x}_{2}},\dots ,\frac{{\partial}^{m}u}{\partial {x}_{\alpha}^{m}},t,\frac{\partial u}{\partial t},\dots ,\frac{{\partial}^{p}u}{\partial {t}^{p}},)
$$

Such an operator is said to be of order $m$ in space and of order $p$ in time. If it does not depend on time (and on its derivatives), one says that the problem is stationary. In the remainder of the document one will consider only stationary problems.

### 2.3 Method of weighted residuals – Strong integral formulation

One will define the residual $R(u)$ as the quantity which vanishes when $u$ is the solution of the physical problem:

$$
R(u)=L(u)-f=0\text{dans}\Omega
$$

The method of weighted residuals consists in:

1/ Constructing an approximate solution $u$ by the linear combination of judiciously chosen functions

$$
u(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}{c}_{i}.{\phi }_{i}(x)
$$

where ${\phi }_{i}(x)$ are the shape functions of the approximation and ${c}_{i}$ coefficients to be identified.

2/ Solving the system in integral form:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\text{Trouver}\mathrm{u}\in {E}_{u}\text{tel que}\forall \mathrm{P}\in {E}_{P}\\ \text{Avec}W=\underset{\Omega}{\int}\mathrm{R}(\mathrm{u}).\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{u}).d\Omega +\underset{\Gamma}{\int}[\mathrm{C}(\mathrm{u})-\mathrm{h}].\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{u}).d\Omega =0\end{array}
$$

We have used the same weighting functions for the principal system and for the boundary conditions, but this is not compulsory. $P(u)$ are the weighting functions belonging to a set of functions ${E}_{P}$ . The solution $u$ belongs to the space ${E}_{u}$ of functions which are “sufficiently” regular (differentiable up to order *m* ).

The choice of the weighting functions $P(u)$ makes it possible to distinguish several methods:

- If the function $P(u)$ is a Dirac distribution, one obtains the point-collocation method.
- If the function $P(u)$ is constant over subdomains, one obtains the subdomain-collocation method.
- If the weighting functions $P(u)$ use the same shape functions ${\phi }_{i}(x)$ as the approximation of the solution (), one obtains the Galerkin method.

One thus obtains the **strong integral form** .

### 2.4 Weak integral formulation

> The integral formulation () requires spaces of functions differentiable to order $m$ for ${E}_{u}$ . The weak formulation consists in carrying out an integration by parts (by application of Green's formula) of the system (). In return, one increases the regularity requirements on the weighting functions $P(u)$ . Green's formula is stated as follows:

$$
\underset{\Omega}{\int}\mathrm{u}.\nabla .\mathrm{P}.d\Omega =-\underset{\Omega}{\int}\mathrm{P}.\nabla .\mathrm{u}.d\Omega +\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\mathrm{u}.\mathrm{P}.\mathrm{n}.d\Gamma
$$

where $n$ is the outward normal to the boundary $\Gamma$ of the domain.

## 3 Finite-element method

### 3.1 General principles

The search for a suitable approximate function over the whole domain becomes difficult in the general case of a geometry of arbitrary shape. The idea of the finite-element method is therefore to construct this approximation in two stages:

- Identify geometrically simple subdomains ${\Omega}_{e}$ which tile the domain;
- Define an approximate function on each subdomain;

One therefore anticipates a certain number of characteristics of this construction:

- The tiling of the domain ${\Omega}_{e}$ by the subdomains ${\Omega}_{e}$ must be as accurate as possible;
- The approximate function on the subdomain must satisfy continuity conditions between the various subdomains;
- The approximate function on the subdomain must have properties consistent with the differentiability conditions and related to the physical description of the solution (which may imply using a weakened formulation, for example).

### 3.2 Approximation of the geometry

#### 3.2.1 Principle

One identifies the ${N}_{e}$ subdomains (or *elements* ) ${\Omega}_{e}$ which tile the space $\Omega$ of the solid:

$$
\Omega =\sum_{e=1}^{{N}_{e}}{\Omega}_{e}
$$

Let ${x}_{\alpha =1,3}$ denote the coordinates of a point $x$ in the absolute frame. The geometry of the subdomain is constructed with a nodal approximation, that is, for an *element* with ${N}_{\mathrm{nd}}$ nodes:

$$
{x}^{e}=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathit{nd}}}{x}_{i}^{e}.{\stackrel{ˉ}{N}}_{i}^{e}
$$

This tiling (mesh) is an operation which may be complex, especially in 3D. General algorithms exist for meshing. One uses triangles or quadrangles in 2D and tetrahedra or hexahedra in 3D (plus a few elements serving as connections). Triangles and tetrahedra give what are called *free* meshes; quadrangles and hexahedra form *mapped* meshes. Free meshes are relatively easy to construct thanks to widely proven techniques: Voronoi cells constructing a Delaunay triangulation, or propagation methods (so-called frontal methods); mapped meshes are much more delicate to generate. The mesh necessarily induces a geometric discretisation error. For example, in figure (), one sees that a curved boundary is only imperfectly approximated by linear elements.

Likewise, the mesh must be conforming: no holes or overlaps (see figure ()).

| ![Figure](images/Cadre1.gif)Illustration 2: Non-conformity of the mesh |

To satisfy this *conformity* condition, two rules suffice:

1. Each element must be defined in a unique manner from the coordinates of its geometric nodes (and not those of its neighbours!);
2. The boundary of an element must be defined in a unique manner from the nodes of these boundaries, these nodes being common to the elements sharing this boundary.

These conformity conditions are an important difference with respect to finite volumes, which do not have these requirements. The tiling of the domain makes it possible to apply the finite-element method to complex geometries, unlike finite-difference methods. The geometric tiling of the domain induces a first error: it is not possible, in the general case, to represent a real geometry by a mesh of regular polygons, in particular on the boundary of the domain.

> « **A beautiful mesh is a good mesh»**

#### 3.2.2 Reference elements

The computation of the shape functions for an arbitrary element may be rather complicated. This is why one often prefers to reduce the problem to a so-called reference element, from which one can generate all the elements of the same family by a geometric transformation. The shape functions are then computed on this generic element, denoted ${\Omega}_{r}$ , and the transport of the quantities onto the real element ${\Omega}_{e}$ is carried out thanks to the knowledge of the geometric transformation.

| ![Figure](images/Cadre3.gif)Illustration 3: Passage from the reference space to the real space |
| --- |

The points of the reference element will be described in terms of parametric coordinates ${\xi}_{\alpha =1,3}$ . The transformation $\tau$ must be bijective and transform the vertices and sides of the reference element into vertices and sides of the real element:

$$
{\xi}_{\alpha}\stackrel{\tau}{\to }{x}_{\alpha}
$$

#### 3.2.3 Geometric interpolation functions

The geometry of the element is therefore approximated by means of geometric interpolation functions. These functions, denoted $\stackrel{ˉ}{N}(\xi )$ , are defined on the reference element; they make it possible to know the coordinates ${x}_{\alpha}$ of any point of the real element from the coordinates ${\xi}_{\alpha}$ of its antecedent in the reference element and from the coordinates ${x}_{\alpha}^{i}$ of the nodes (of local number $I$ ) of the real element:

$$
{x}^{e}=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathrm{nd}}}{x}_{i}^{e}.{\stackrel{ˉ}{N}}_{i}^{e}
$$

#### 3.2.4 Jacobian matrix of the transformation

The Jacobian of the transformation is the matrix of the partial derivatives of the real coordinates ${x}_{\alpha}$ with respect to the coordinates ${\xi}_{\alpha}$ in the reference element:

$$
{J}_{\alpha \beta }=\frac{\partial {x}_{\alpha}}{\partial {\xi}_{\beta}}
$$

Taking into account the definition of the coordinates ${x}_{\alpha}$ as a function of the coordinates ${x}_{\alpha ,i}$ of the nodes, one obtains an equivalent expression of the Jacobian matrix:

$$
{J}_{\alpha \beta }=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathrm{nd}}}\frac{\partial \stackrel{ˉ}{{N}_{i}}}{\partial {\xi}_{\beta}}.{x}_{\alpha ,i}
$$

where $\frac{\partial \stackrel{ˉ}{{N}_{i}}}{\partial {\xi}_{\beta}}$ are the terms of the matrix ${\left[\frac{\partial \stackrel{ˉ}{N}}{\partial \xi }\right]}^{T}$ , whose number of rows is the number of directions of space, and whose number of columns is the number of nodes of the element. Note that the matrix ${\left[\frac{\partial \stackrel{ˉ}{N}}{\partial \xi }\right]}^{T}$ depends only on the definition of the reference element and not on that of the real element. The determinant of the Jacobian matrix, useful in the computations which follow, is called the Jacobian of the geometric transformation. It is non-zero when the transformation $\tau$ which takes one from the reference element to the real element is bijective, and positive when $\tau$ preserves the orientation of space.

$$
J=\det\left[\frac{\partial \stackrel{ˉ}{N}}{\partial \xi }\right]\ge 0
$$

### 3.3 Representation of the unknowns

To solve the problem, one considers a finite-element approximation of an unknown field. The spaces ${E}_{P}$ and ${E}_{u}$ are represented by discrete spaces ${E}^{h}$ . There are two equivalent ways of representing the unknowns in an element: by the coefficients of their polynomial approximation, or by their nodal values. These two possibilities correspond to the two complementary ways of defining an element: by the data of a basis of monomials, or by the data of the shape functions associated with the nodes. In general, one constructs the approximate function by writing the following linear relation on each element:

$$
{u}^{e}(\xi )=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathrm{nd}}}{a}_{i}^{e}.{\phi }_{i}^{e}(\xi )
$$

where the ${\phi }_{i}^{e}(\xi )$ are independent linear functions. They constitute the **basis** of the approximation, the general parameters of the approximation being the coefficients ${a}_{i}$ .

#### 3.3.1 Nodal approximation

The first idea of the finite-element method is to construct an approximation of nodal type for which the coefficients ${u}_{i}={a}_{i}$ correspond to the solution at these nodes:

$$
{u}^{e}(\xi )=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathrm{nd}}}{u}_{i}^{e}.{N}_{i}^{e}(\xi )
$$

One then obtains a nodal approximation with ${N}_{i}^{e}(\xi )$ the interpolation functions on the reference element. On each of these subdomains one constructs an approximate function which differs from one subdomain to another. The finite-element approximation is *elementary* because the function depends only on the nodal values constituting the element:

$$
{u}^{e}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathrm{nd}}}{u}_{i}^{e}.{N}_{i}^{e}(x)
$$

An element is *isoparametric* when it is based on identical interpolations for its geometry and its unknowns: $\stackrel{ˉ}{N}(\xi )=N(\xi )$ .

To ensure the continuity of the solution on the element and, possibly, the continuity of its derivatives, the functions ${N}_{i}^{e}(\xi )$ must be continuous and, possibly, have continuous derivatives.

Likewise, if one wishes to ensure the continuity of the solution and of its derivatives at the boundaries of the elements (conformity of the approximation), the solution and its derivatives must depend in a unique manner on the nodal variables of the nodes of the boundary.

#### 3.3.2 Polynomial basis

The simplest way of defining an element is to choose a polynomial basis composed of a certain number of independent monomials. For a given unknown, the number of monomials used must be equal to the number of nodal variables, that is, to the number of nodes used to represent the unknown. One generally defines the polynomial basis on the reference element; it contains monomials of the form ${\xi}_{1}^{\gamma}.{\xi}_{2}^{\delta}.{\xi}_{3}^{\varepsilon}$ , where $\gamma$ , $\delta$ and $\varepsilon$ are positive or zero integer exponents. The degree of such a monomial is the integer $\gamma +\delta +\varepsilon$ . The basis is said to be complete of degree $n$ when all the monomials of degree $n$ are present. In certain cases, one uses incomplete bases. One denotes by ${P}_{p}(\xi )$ the ${p}^{\mathit{ième}}$ monomial of the basis (which comprises $m$ of them). The components of the displacement vector $u(\xi )$ in the element are then given by the formula:

$$
{u}_{\alpha}(\xi )=\sum_{p=1}^{m}{a}_{\alpha ,p}.{P}_{p}(\xi )
$$

One will denote by $\Pi$ the matrix giving the values taken by the monomials of the polynomial basis at the nodes of the reference element:

$$
{\Pi}_{\mathrm{Ip}}={P}_{p}({\xi}_{I})
$$

where $p$ is the order number of the monomial in the basis, $I$ the number of the node local to the element and ${\xi}_{I}$ the coordinates of the node $I$ in the reference element. This matrix is square; its dimension is the square of the number of nodes of the element.

At the node $I$ the displacement ${u}_{\alpha}^{I}$ is equal to:

$$
{u}_{I,\alpha }={a}_{\alpha ,p}.{\Pi}_{\mathrm{Ip}}
$$

One distinguishes three main types of finite elements frequently used:

- Lagrange finite elements, which rest on complete polynomial bases and various types of geometries (simplex for triangles and tetrahedra, with a tensor-product structure for quadrangles and hexahedra, or of prismatic type);
- Serendipity finite elements, which are Lagrange finite elements with incomplete bases;
- Hermite finite elements, of high accuracy, which use the nodal unknowns and their derivatives;

**Simplex Lagrange finite elements**

To determine whether a polynomial basis is complete with the simplex elements, it suffices to use Pascal's triangle:

A complete polynomial basis of order two comprises six monomials: $\lbrace  1;{\xi}_{1};{\xi}_{2};{\xi}_{1}^{2};{\xi}_{2}^{2};{\xi}_{1}.{\xi}_{2}\rbrace$ and therefore the supporting geometric element will be a six-node triangle.

Such an element contains terms of order 3 and 4.

Serendipity elements, for a polynomial of order $s$ , exclude the cross terms of degree higher than $s+1$ so as not to have nodes in the interior of the elements. For example, for a Serendipity element of order two, the monomials will be $\lbrace  1;{\xi}_{1};{\xi}_{1}^{2};{\xi}_{2};{\xi}_{2}^{2};{\xi}_{1}.{\xi}_{2};{\xi}_{1}.{\xi}_{2}^{2};{\xi}_{1}^{2}.{\xi}_{2}\rbrace$ , that is, eight nodes.

#### 3.3.3 Shape functions

An equivalent way of defining a finite element is to give, for each unknown, the expression of the shape functions of the element. For a given scalar unknown (the displacement component along y, for example), there are as many of them as there are nodes at which the unknown must be computed. In many cases, one uses the same shape functions for all the components of an unknown vector, but this is not compulsory. In what follows, one will nevertheless assume, in order to simplify the writing, that this is the case.

The shape functions may be defined on the real element ${\Omega}_{e}$ : one then denotes them ${N}^{e}(x)$ ; they depend on the geometry of the real element, and are therefore different from one element to another. It is simpler to express them on the reference element, which gives the functions $N(\xi )$ independent of the geometry of the real element. Recall that these functions are polynomial on the element, and that the shape function associated with a given node takes the value one there, whereas it vanishes at all the other nodes of the element. The unknowns are then expressed as a linear combination of the shape functions, the coefficients ${u}_{\alpha ,i}$ of the combination being called the nodal variables:

$$
{u}_{\alpha}(\xi )=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathrm{nd}}}{u}_{\alpha ,i}.{N}_{i}(\xi )
$$

Using the transformation $\tau$ between the reference element and the real element:

$$
{\xi}_{\alpha}\stackrel{\tau}{\to }{x}_{\alpha}
$$

One has:

$$
{u}_{\alpha}(\xi )=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathrm{nd}}}{u}_{\alpha ,i}.{N}_{i}({\tau}^{\text{-1}}(x))
$$

#### 3.3.4 Correspondence between the polynomial basis and the shape functions

One has two relations. The first comes from the approximation of the solution by a polynomial basis:

$$
{u}_{\alpha}(\xi )=\sum_{p=1}^{m}{a}_{\alpha ,p}.{P}_{p}(\xi )
$$

The second is the nodal approximation:

$$
{u}_{\alpha}(\xi )=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathrm{nd}}}{u}_{\alpha ,i}.{N}_{i}(\xi )
$$

The matrix giving the values taken by the monomials of the polynomial basis at the nodes of the reference element:

$$
{\Pi}_{\mathrm{Ip}}={P}_{p}({\xi}_{I})
$$

At a node $I$ , one has written the following polynomial approximation;

$$
{u}_{I,\alpha }={a}_{\alpha ,p}.{\Pi}_{\mathrm{Ip}}
$$

By injecting equation () into the nodal expression (), one obtains:

$$
{u}_{\alpha}(\xi )=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathrm{nd}}}{a}_{\alpha ,p}.{\Pi}_{\mathrm{Ip}}.{N}_{i}(\xi )
$$

By comparison with the polynomial approximation (), one deduces the following relation between the polynomial basis and the shape functions:

$$
{\Pi}_{\mathrm{Ip}}.{N}_{i}(\xi )={P}_{p}(\xi )
$$

In practice, one will find in the literature the expressions of the nodal shape functions for the most common elements, according to the choice of the polynomial basis.

### 3.4 Existence and uniqueness results

One may write the problem in a more abstract manner:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\text{Trouver}\mathrm{u}\in {E}_{u}\text{tel que}\forall \mathrm{v}\in {E}_{v}\\ a(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{v})=f(\mathrm{v})\end{array}
$$

${E}_{u}$ and ${E}_{v}$ are vector spaces of functions defined on $\Omega$ . They are Hilbert spaces.

$a(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{v})$ is a bilinear form on ${E}_{u}\times {E}_{v}$ (one has assumed that $\mathrm{L}(\mathrm{u})$ represents a physical problem linear with respect to $\mathrm{u}$ ).

$f(\mathrm{v})$ is a linear and continuous form on ${E}_{v}$ .

To establish the existence and uniqueness conditions, one applies the Lax–Milgram theorem. In a first stage, one assumes that the solution belongs to the same space as the test functions ${E}_{u}={E}_{v}$

If the form $a(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{v})$ is coercive, that is:

$$
\forall \mathrm{u}\in {E}_{u}a(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{u})\ge \mathit{c.}{∥\mathrm{u}∥}_{{E}_{u}}^{2}\text{avec}c>0
$$

Then the problem:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\text{Trouver}\mathrm{u}\in {E}_{u}\text{tel que}\forall \mathrm{v}\in {E}_{u}\\ a(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{v})=f(\mathrm{v})\end{array}
$$

admits one and only one solution.

## 4 Ritz method

The Galerkin method, in certain cases, is equivalent to making a functional stationary. This is the case if the bilinear form $a(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{v})$ is symmetric and positive:

$$
\forall \mathrm{u},\mathrm{v}\in {E}_{u}a(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{v})=a(\mathrm{v},\mathrm{u})\text{et}a(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{u})\ge 0
$$

In this case the problem () admits one and only one solution $\mathrm{u}$ which minimises on ${E}_{u}$ the following functional:

$$
\pi (\mathrm{u})=\frac{1}{2}.a(\mathrm{u},\mathrm{u})-f(\mathrm{u})
$$

From the mechanical point of view, this means that the principle of virtual power may also be written as the minimisation of a scalar quantity: the total energy of the structure. This way of writing equilibrium is very frequently used. We shall present here a few results.

We first recall that a functional is a function of a set of functions (and of its derivatives). One will write $\pi$ for this functional. One will restrict oneself to displacement formulations, knowing that others exist. In this case, the functional $\pi$ will be written:

$$
\pi (u)=\pi (u,\frac{\partial u}{\partial x})
$$

For conservative problems, one can show that writing that the first variation of $\pi$ is zero (stationarity condition of the functional) is equivalent to applying the principle of virtual work, or again to using the Galerkin method by taking the virtual displacements as the weighting function. We recall that the Galerkin method consists in starting from the partial differential problem establishing the equilibrium of the structure, namely:

$$
L(u)+f=0\text{dans}\Omega \text{avec}\sigma .n=g\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{N}\text{et}u={u}^{D}\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{D}
$$

One then seeks to solve the problem in integral form by using weighting functions which are of the same nature as the approximate solution:

$$
\begin{array}{}W=\underset{\Omega}{\int}[L(u)+f].\psi (u).d\Omega =0\\ \text{Avec}\sigma .n=g\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{N}\text{et}u={u}^{D}\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{D}\end{array}
$$

If one chooses as weighting function the variation of the unknowns $\psi =\delta u$ and after having integrated by parts once, one obtains:

$$
\delta \pi (u)=W(u)=0\text{avec}u={u}^{D}\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{D}
$$

Finding the exact form of the functional is not immediate in the general case. In mechanics, for the conservative cases, it turns out that this functional is equivalent to the total potential energy of the system. After discretisation of the functional (by a finite-element approximation), one is left with a matrix system strictly equivalent to that of the Galerkin method (or its mechanical equivalent, the method of virtual power).

Intuitively, one understands that a small variation $\delta u$ of the solution is a field which may be kinematically admissible and which therefore corresponds well to the assumptions of the method of virtual power.

## 5 Construction of the matrix system

We shall now present the various ingredients leading to the construction of the matrix system which will make it possible to solve the problem.

### 5.1 New notation (Voigt notation)

In order to understand clearly the construction of the discrete terms in the finite-element method, we shall use a more compact notation:

> $\langle V\rangle$  is a row vector
> $\left\lbrace V\right\rbrace$  is a column vector
> $\left[A\right]$  is a matrix

Thus the geometric interpolation is written along the three dimensions of space:

$$
{x}^{e}={x}_{1}^{e}=\langle {x}_{1,i}^{e}\rangle .\left\lbrace {N}_{i}^{e}\right\rbrace =\langle {N}_{i}^{e}\rangle .\left\lbrace {x}_{1,i}^{e}\right\rbrace
{y}^{e}={y}_{1}^{e}=\langle {y}_{1,i}^{e}\rangle .\left\lbrace {N}_{i}^{e}\right\rbrace =\langle {N}_{i}^{e}\rangle .\left\lbrace {y}_{1,i}^{e}\right\rbrace
{z}^{e}={z}_{1}^{e}=\langle {z}_{1,i}^{e}\rangle .\left\lbrace {N}_{i}^{e}\right\rbrace =\langle {N}_{i}^{e}\rangle .\left\lbrace {z}_{1,i}^{e}\right\rbrace
$$

Or again, more compactly, in vector form:

$$
\left\lbrace {x}^{e}\right\rbrace =\left[{N}_{i}^{e}\right].\left\lbrace {x}_{i}^{e}\right\rbrace =\langle {x}_{i}^{e}\rangle .{\left[{N}_{i}^{e}\right]}^{T}
$$

With the matrix $N$ of the shape functions. Considering an element with two nodes, one obtains in expanded form:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{x}^{e}\\ {y}^{e}\\ {z}^{e}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left[\begin{array}{cccccc}{N}_{1}^{e}& 0& 0& {N}_{2}^{e}& 0& 0\\ 0& {N}_{1}^{e}& 0& 0& {N}_{2}^{e}& 0\\ 0& 0& {N}_{1}^{e}& 0& 0& {N}_{2}^{e}\end{array}\right].\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{x}_{1}^{e}\\ {y}_{1}^{e}\\ {z}_{1}^{e}\\ {x}_{2}^{e}\\ {y}_{2}^{e}\\ {z}_{2}^{e}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

### 5.2 Discretised system

One places oneself in the hyperelastic case in small strains; the mechanics problem to be solved is written more compactly:

$$
\textrm{Trouver}
u\in {E}^{h}
\textrm{avec}
a(u,\tilde{u})+l(\tilde{u})=0
$$

With $a(u,\tilde{u})$ a bilinear, symmetric form which represents the potential energy of the structure and $l(\tilde{u})$ the potential 1
of the body forces and surface forces:

$$
\begin{array}{}a(u,\tilde{u})=\underset{{\Omega}^{h}}{\int}\varepsilon (\tilde{u}):\sigma (u).d{\Omega}^{h}\\ l(\tilde{u})=\underset{{\Omega}^{h}}{\int}f.\tilde{u}.d{\Omega}^{h}+\underset{{\Gamma}_{N}^{h}}{\int}g.\tilde{u}.d{\Gamma}^{h}\end{array}
$$

The discretisation consists in choosing a basis of the space ${\Omega}^{h}$ and in computing numerically the terms of the matrix $A$ and of the vector $L$ . To this end, one expresses the bilinear form $a(.,.)$ and the linear form $l(.)$ as a sum over elements, defined by subdivision of the base domain:

$$
\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}a({u}_{i},{\tilde{u}}_{j})& =\sum_{\text{éléments}{\Omega}^{e}}\underset{{\Omega}^{e}}{\int}{\sigma}_{kl}({u}_{i}).{\varepsilon}_{kl}({\tilde{u}}_{j}).d{\Omega}^{e}\\ l({\tilde{u}}_{i})& =\sum_{\text{éléments}{\Omega}^{e}}\underset{{\Omega}^{e}}{\int}{f}_{i}.{\tilde{u}}_{i}.d{\Omega}^{e}+\underset{{\Gamma}_{N}^{e}}{\int}{g}_{i}.{\tilde{u}}_{i}.d{\Gamma}_{N}^{e}\end{array}
$$

The terms ${A}_{ij}$ , which represent the interaction between two degrees of freedom $i$ and $j$ , are constructed by *assembling* ( the operation denoted $\sum_{\text{éléments}{\Omega}^{e}}(\mathrm{...})$ ) the contributions coming from each of the elements which contain the corresponding nodes; one proceeds in the same way to construct the right-hand-side vector ${L}_{i}$ . These contributions, called elementary terms, are computed during a loop over the elements and depend only on the variables of the element ${\Omega}^{e}$ alone:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}{a}^{e}& =\underset{{\Omega}^{e}}{\int}{\sigma}_{kl}.{\varepsilon}_{kl}.d{\Omega}^{e}\\ {l}^{e}& =\underset{{\Omega}^{e}}{\int}{f}_{i}.{w}_{i}.d{\Omega}^{e}+\underset{{\Gamma}_{N}^{e}}{\int}{g}_{i}.{w}_{i}.d{\Gamma}_{N}^{e}\end{array}
$$

The relation between the Cauchy stress tensor $\sigma$ and the displacements $u$ is given by the constitutive relation, and is independent of the writing of the variational formulation. In the elastic case, one has:

$$
{\sigma}_{ij}({w}_{i})={\Lambda}_{ijkl}.{\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{ku}}({w}_{i})
$$

${\Lambda}_{ijkl}$ is the Hooke elasticity tensor. This tensor form is not very practical; one preferentially uses the Voigt notation, which makes it possible to write:

$$
\sigma :\varepsilon =\langle \sigma \rangle .\lbrace \varepsilon \rbrace
$$

In Cartesian coordinates, one has:

$$
\langle \sigma \rangle =\langle \begin{array}{cccccc}{\sigma}_{xx}& {\sigma}_{yy}& {\sigma}_{zz}& {\sigma}_{xy}& {\sigma}_{xz}& {\sigma}_{yz}\end{array}\rangle
$$

And a modified form of the strain components in order to make it possible to express the contracted product, namely:

$$
\langle \varepsilon \rangle =\langle \begin{array}{cccccc}{\varepsilon}_{xx}& {\varepsilon}_{yy}& {\varepsilon}_{zz}& 2.{\varepsilon}_{xy}& 2.{\varepsilon}_{xz}& 2.{\varepsilon}_{yz}\end{array}\rangle
$$

**Important note:**

*In the integration of the constitutive laws, the shear components of the stresses and of the strains used by* Code_Aster *are:*

$\langle \sigma \rangle =\langle \begin{array}{cccccc}{\sigma}_{xx}& {\sigma}_{yy}& {\sigma}_{zz}& \sqrt{2}.{\sigma}_{xy}& \sqrt{2}.{\sigma}_{xz}& \sqrt{2}.{\sigma}_{yz}\end{array}\rangle$

$\langle \varepsilon \rangle =\langle \begin{array}{cccccc}{\varepsilon}_{xx}& {\varepsilon}_{yy}& {\varepsilon}_{zz}& \sqrt{2}.{\varepsilon}_{xy}& \sqrt{2}.{\varepsilon}_{xz}& \sqrt{2}.{\varepsilon}_{yz}\end{array}\rangle$

*The product of these two vectors does indeed give the same result as the double contracted product (* *).*

With this new notation, in elasticity we have:

$$
\lbrace \sigma \rbrace =[A].\lbrace \varepsilon \rbrace
$$

We start again from the finite-element writing of the displacement field:

$$
\left\lbrace {u}^{e}\right\rbrace =\left[{N}_{i}^{e}\right].\left\lbrace {u}_{i}^{e}\right\rbrace =\langle {u}_{i}^{e}\rangle .{\left[{N}_{i}^{e}\right]}^{T}
$$

And, in an analogous manner, the virtual displacement field:

$$
\left\lbrace {\tilde{u}}^{e}\right\rbrace =\left[{N}_{i}^{e}\right].\left\lbrace {\tilde{u}}_{i}^{e}\right\rbrace =\langle {\tilde{u}}_{i}^{e}\rangle .{\left[{N}_{i}^{e}\right]}^{T}
$$

For the sake of simplifying the notation, one will omit the reference to the element. It is necessary to express the strain tensor (virtual or real):

$$
\left\lbrace \varepsilon \right\rbrace =\left[B\right].\left\lbrace u\right\rbrace =\langle u\rangle .{\left[B\right]}^{T}
$$

One then obtains, for the matrix relative to the bilinear form:

$$
\begin{array}{cc}[a]& =\langle \tilde{u}\rangle .\underset{{\Omega}^{e}}{\int}{\left[B\right]}^{T}.[\Lambda ].\left[B\right].d{\Omega}^{e}.\left\lbrace u\right\rbrace \end{array}
$$

The matrices $\left[B\right]$ and $[\Lambda ]$ contain the possible non-linearity of the behaviour and will depend on the displacements:

1. $\left[B\right]$ is a function of the displacements if one is in the situation of large strains or of large transformations (large rotations and/or large displacements).
2. $[\Lambda ]$ is the constitutive matrix. It becomes dependent on the displacements (and on other variables) in the case of non-linear and/or non-elastic constitutive behaviours.

In these two cases, the process of solving the equations will involve a specific treatment (most often, a linearisation of Newton–Raphson type). In an analogous manner, one will easily obtain the elementary form for the right-hand side.

### 5.3 Computation of the elementary terms

> The elementary terms to be computed are of the form:

$$
\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}f(u(x),\frac{\partial u(x)}{\partial x}).dx
$$

Three types of operations are to be carried out:

1. the transformation of the derivatives with respect to $x$ into derivatives with respect to $\xi$ ;
2. the passage from an integration over the real element to an integration over the reference element,
3. the numerical realisation of this integration, which is generally carried out by a quadrature formula.

#### 5.3.1 Transformation of the derivatives

The transformation of the derivatives is carried out thanks to the Jacobian matrix $J$ , according to the chain rule:

$$
\frac{\partial {u}_{\alpha}}{\partial {x}_{\beta}}=\frac{\partial {\xi}_{\gamma}}{\partial {x}_{\beta}}.\frac{\partial {u}_{\alpha}}{\partial {\xi}_{\gamma}}={J}^{\text{-1}}.{\left[\frac{\partial N}{\partial \xi }\right]}^{T}.{u}_{\alpha}^{\text{nod}}
$$

where ${u}_{\alpha}^{\text{nod}}$ is the vector of the nodal values of the component $\alpha$ of the displacement.

#### 5.3.2 Change of the integration domain

The passage to the integration over the reference element is carried out by multiplying the integrand by the determinant of the Jacobian matrix, called the Jacobian:

$$
\underset{{\Omega}_{e}}{\int}f(u(x),\frac{\partial u(x)}{\partial x}).dx=\underset{{\Omega}_{r}}{\int}f(u(\xi ),\frac{\partial u(\xi )}{\partial \xi }).\det(J(\xi )).d\xi
$$

The passage from the reference element to the real element implies the bijectivity of the transformation $\tau$ . One must therefore have $\det(J(\xi ))\ne 0$ , which implies that the element must not turn inside out or degenerate (for example, the quadrangle must not degenerate into a triangle).

#### 5.3.3 Numerical integration

In certain particular cases, one can compute the integrals analytically. For example, for a triangle in two dimensions, the Jacobian is constant over the triangle, and the integrands reduce to monomials which one knows how to integrate exactly thanks to the numerical integration formula known as “Gauss's” 2
:

$$
\underset{0}{\overset{1}{\int}}\underset{0}{\overset{1-\xi }{\int}}{\xi}_{1}^{\alpha}.{\xi}_{2}^{\beta}.d{\xi}_{1}.d{\xi}_{2}=\frac{\alpha !\beta !}{(\alpha +\beta +2)!}
$$

However, these particular cases are rare, and one prefers to evaluate the integrals numerically by calling upon quadrature formulae. These give an approximation of the integral in the form of a weighted sum of the values of the integrand at a certain number of points of the element called integration points:

$$
\underset{{\Omega}_{r}}{\int}g(\xi ).d\xi \approx \sum_{g=1}^{r}{\omega}_{g}.g({\xi}_{g})
$$

The scalars ${\omega}_{g}$ are called the integration weights, and the coordinates ${\xi}_{g}$ are the coordinates of the $r$ integration points in the reference element.

In the Gauss integration methods, the integration points and weights are determined so as to integrate exactly polynomials of a given order. It is this type of method that one uses in *Code_Aster* ; the integration points are then called Gauss points.

The number of Gauss points chosen makes it possible to integrate exactly in the reference element. In fact, because of the possible non-linearity of the geometric transformation or of the spatial dependence of the coefficients (for example for deformed elements or elements of second order), the integration is not exact in the real element.

For each element ${\Omega}_{e}$ , one has been able to compute the so-called elementary terms: elementary matrix ${\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{e}}$ and elementary vector ${L}^{e}$ . The matrix $A$ and the vector $L$ are obtained by a procedure which one calls the assembly of the elementary terms.

If one takes up again the elementary stiffness form:

$$
[a]=\underset{{\Omega}^{e}}{\int}\lbrace  \sigma \rbrace  .\langle \varepsilon \rangle .d{\Omega}^{e}
$$

Numerical integration implies that one evaluates the stresses and the strains at the integration points:

$$
[a]=\underset{{\Omega}^{e}}{\int}\lbrace \sigma \rbrace .\langle \varepsilon \rangle .d{\Omega}^{e}\approx \sum_{g=1}^{r}{\omega}_{g}.\lbrace \sigma ({\xi}_{g})\rbrace .\langle \varepsilon ({\xi}_{g})\rangle
$$

Which means that the stresses and the strains are the most exact (or the least wrong) at the integration points (fields known as “ELGA” in *Code_Aster* ). The mere fact of extrapolating these values to the nodes for display introduces an error. This is, moreover, a method of error evaluation, called the Zhu–Zienkiewicz error indicator.

In 2D elasticity, a triangle exhibiting a constant Jacobian, a single Gauss point is sufficient to integrate exactly the terms of the matrix and of the right-hand side (if it is constant).

The cost of the analysis increases with the number of integration points, particularly for non-linear constitutive laws. For example, a 27-node hexahedron needs 27 Gauss points to integrate the quantities. It therefore frequently happens that one “under-integrates”, that is, that one uses fewer integration points than the required minimum, thus committing an error which one will possibly compensate by a finer mesh. In addition to this systematic error, this under-integration must be done with care because it may produce rank deficiencies of the matrix and thus make the linear system non-invertible.

The potential of the external forces does not depend on the displacement of the structure; this is what one calls a dead or non-follower loading. In the case of large strains, loadings of pressure type cannot satisfy this assumption.

By an abuse of language, one frequently calls the numerical integration schemes “Gauss schemes” although several kinds exist (Hammer for the triangle, Gauss–Radau, Newton–Cotes, etc.).

## 6 Solution of the matrix system

One therefore obtains a linear system to be solved:

$$
\langle \tilde{u}\rangle .[A].\left\lbrace u\right\rbrace +\langle \tilde{u}\rangle .\left\lbrace L\right\rbrace =0
$$

Whatever the virtual displacement field, hence:

$$
[A].\left\lbrace u\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace L\right\rbrace
$$

### 6.1 Imposition of the kinematic boundary conditions

> The treatment of kinematic boundary conditions of the type $u={u}^{D}$ is carried out in two different ways:

1. The “kinematic” method (AFFE_CHAR_CINE in Code_Aster) consists in modifying the matrix and the right-hand side. This method is fast and does not introduce additional variables. On the other hand, it is not general and does not make it possible to apply complex boundary conditions of the type $\sum{u}_{i}.{a}_{i}={u}^{D}$ .
2. The method by dualisation (AFFE_CHAR_MECA in Code_Aster) consists in introducing a vector of Lagrange multipliers (or parameters) $\lambda$ , which increases the number of unknowns but makes it possible to treat all cases.

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}[A].\left\lbrace u\right\rbrace +{[Q]}^{T}\left\lbrace \lambda \right\rbrace =\left\lbrace L\right\rbrace \\ [Q].\left\lbrace u\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {u}^{D}\right\rbrace \end{array}
$$

### 6.2 Solution

The linear system may be solved by a certain number of numerical methods. The methods used in *Code_Aster* are a block ${\mathit{LDL}}^{T}$ factorisation, a multifrontal method (or its equivalent with pivoting, MUMPS), a preconditioned conjugate gradient, as well as the collection of iterative solvers PETSC.

The solution methods divide into two categories:

- Direct methods, which solve exactly (to within numerical errors)
- Iterative methods, which construct a sequence of vectors converging towards the solution

The matrices arising from the finite-element method are very sparse (they contain a majority of zero terms). In practice, on systems of standard size (a few tens of thousands of equations), the density of non-zero terms rarely exceeds 0.01%. They are therefore stored in sparse form (or “sparse”) and take up little space in memory. Conversely, the matrices are not constructed to be used efficiently with the optimised mathematical program libraries dedicated to full matrices (BLAS libraries, for example). Solvers are therefore developed specifically for these problems.

A direct solver has as its principle the decomposition of the matrix into a product of matrices of particular form. For example, the ${\mathit{LDL}}^{T}$ decomposition:

$$
[A]=[L].[D].{[L]}^{T}
$$

where the matrix $D$ is diagonal and the matrix $L$ is lower triangular. This decomposition is valid only for symmetric matrices. If this is not the case, one must use other decompositions.

The principle is the following:

- From the initial matrix (very sparse), one constructs a product of remarkable matrices. This is the *factorisation* operation.
- These remarkable matrices make it possible to solve the problem very rapidly. This is the *forward and back substitution* phase.

The factorisation phase is the most costly. For the most widespread decompositions, the machine cost scales as ${n}^{3}$ where $n$ is the number of equations. The memory cost will depend on the profile of the matrix (on the numbering of the finite elements). Automatic procedures seek to optimise this numbering in order to have a structure which is as compact as possible. Even with this optimisation, it is frequent that the factorised matrix takes several hundred times, or even several thousand times, more memory than the initial matrix. Direct solvers therefore consume a great deal of memory, and this becomes prohibitive from several hundred thousand degrees of freedom onwards, even on the most powerful machines. On the other hand, these direct methods are particularly robust. Problems in structural and solid mechanics very often lead to matrices with poor conditioning (this is particularly the case of all the latest numerical innovations which use mixed methods with many Lagrange multipliers).

Whenever it is possible, one preferentially uses iterative methods, whose principle consists in finding an approximation of the inverse of the matrix and then proceeding to an iterative solution, step by step, which uses only matrix–vector products, which are very efficient and inexpensive in memory.

However, these iterative methods have several defects:

- They are less robust than the direct methods, particularly when the conditioning is poor
- The preconditioning methods are very numerous and there exist as many of them as there are different problems (or even several possible ones per problem). This obliges the user to juggle the various methods, without ever being assured of obtaining a result at the end.
- They are iterative methods, which implies a stopping criterion for the process, and therefore a parameter to be managed, but also problems of accumulation of round-off errors.

## 7 Organisation of a finite-element analysis in Code_Aster

One describes very briefly how and where the aspects mentioned in this document are implemented in *Code_Aster* .

### 7.1 Notion of a finite element in Code_Aster

A finite-element type is defined by:

- a mesh-cell type
- a list of nodes
- shape functions
- calculation options

An element in the mesh is defined by a mesh-cell type, a geometry (coordinates of the nodes) and a topology (ordered list of the nodes). It is the type of modelling chosen in the command file which makes it possible to assign to each mesh cell of the mesh a finite-element type. The command AFFE_MODELE [U4.22.01](doc:u4.22.01) assigns to each mesh cell a finite-element type corresponding to the modelling specified for that mesh cell.

**Important note:**

*One must not forget to assign finite elements to the boundary mesh cells which one needs in order to impose the boundary conditions and loadings, and which one will have taken care to create during the construction of the mesh.*

The operator AFFE_CHAR_MECA [U4.44.01](doc:u4.44.01), which assigns boundary conditions and loadings, will also create finite elements, for example the finite elements which will carry the LAGRANGE degrees of freedom used in the dualisation of the boundary conditions [R3.03.01](doc:r3.03.01).

The operator AFFE_CARA_ELEM [U4.42.01](doc:u4.42.01) makes it possible to define additional characteristics for certain types of elements: for example, the thickness of the shells, the orientation of the beams, the mass and stiffness matrices of the discrete elements.

A calculation option indicates the type of elementary computation which the element is capable of computing. For example RIGI_MECA concerns the computation of the elementary mechanical stiffness matrix:

$$
{A}_{\alpha \beta }^{e}=\underset{{\Omega}^{e}}{\int}{\Lambda}_{ijkl}.{\varepsilon}_{ij}({N}_{\alpha}^{e}(\mathrm{x})).{\varepsilon}_{kl}({N}_{\beta}^{e}(\mathrm{x})).d{\Omega}^{e}
$$

The “data” of this option are the geometry ${\Omega}^{e}$ and the material $\Lambda$ , supplemented by the temperature if the material depends on it.

Recall that, in order to apply the boundary loadings, one uses particular boundary finite elements, and not the boundaries of the volume (3D) or surface (2D) finite elements.

**Note:**

*A developer may sometimes have the choice between creating a new finite element or adding a calculation option to an existing element; the choice between these two solutions generally takes into account criteria of programming convenience (e.g. under-integrated elements).*

### 7.2 Initialisation of the elements

The use of reference elements makes it possible to carry out a certain number of computations once and for all at the beginning of the execution. One defines, for each type of reference element:

- The number of nodes and their coordinates;
- The number of families of GAUSS points;
- The number of GAUSS points;
- The integration weights ${\omega}_{g}$ ;
- The values of the shape functions at the Gauss points ${N}_{i}({\xi}_{g})$ ;
- The values of the derivatives of the shape functions at the Gauss points $\frac{\partial {N}_{i}({\xi}_{g})}{\partial \xi }$ .

For a given element, one does not necessarily integrate all the elementary terms with the same number of Gauss points: for example, one generally uses more Gauss points for the mass matrix than for the stiffness matrix, because the products of shape functions are of higher degree than the products of their derivatives. Another example is the under-integration used in certain cases. One calls a family of Gauss points each set of Gauss points liable to be used.

### 7.3 Computation of the elementary terms

During the computation of the elementary terms (in the routines TE....), one carries out, for each Gauss point, the following operations:

- Computation of the derivatives of the shape functions on the real element from the coordinates of the nodes of the element and from the derivatives of the shape functions on the reference element;
- Computation of the Jacobian matrix;
- Retrieval of the integration weight multiplied by the Jacobian at the GAUSS point considered;
- Evaluation of the integrand (according to the option computed).

The elementary term is computed by summation over the Gauss points, weighting by the integration weights.

### 7.4 Global solution

The global solution takes place in the high-level routines OP.... corresponding to the user commands (MECA_STATIQUE [U4.51.01](doc:u4.51.01), STAT_NON_LINE [U4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03), THER_LINEAIRE [U4.54.01](doc:u4.54.01), etc.).
