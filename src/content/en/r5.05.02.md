# r5.05.02 Direct integration algorithms of the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’PHYS’

**Summary:**

This document describes the time-integration schemes which are used to solve, in a direct manner, dynamics problems in **linear transient mechanics**. The NEWMARK and WILSON- $\theta$  schemes are detailed, as well as the "central differences with a constant step" and "adaptive time step" schemes.

## 1 Introduction

The aim of transient dynamic analysis is to determine, as a function of time, the response of a structure, given an external loading, or boundary conditions which are functions of time, when the inertia effects cannot be neglected.

In a certain number of physical configurations, a transient analysis cannot be dispensed with in favour of a modal or harmonic analysis alone:

- if the history of the phenomenon is important in the study,
- if the external loading is complex (earthquake, multi-component excitations...),
- if the system is non-linear (plasticity, impacts, friction ...).

The methods of transient dynamic analysis which can then be used are divided into two broad categories:

- the so-called direct integration methods,
- the Ritz methods, including among others modal projection recombination.

The direct integration methods are so named because no transformation is carried out on the dynamic system after the discretisation into finite elements.

A presentation will be given of the direct integration algorithms used to solve a dynamic problem in mechanics for linear structures. These algorithms are employed in the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’PHYS’

The Ritz methods, on the contrary, carry out a transformation of the initial dynamic system, very often a projection onto a subspace of the initial discretisation space. The dynamic solution is then performed on a modified system, which only gives access to an approximation of the response of the initial system. They are presented in another document [r5.06.04](doc:r5.06.04).

## 2 Implicit and explicit direct time-integration methods for a dynamic problem

It is assumed that the structure under study has a linear constitutive behaviour and that the equations governing its dynamic equilibrium have been discretised by finite elements. A discrete system of second-order differential equations is obtained, which is to be integrated in time.

In general, these equations take the following form:

$M.{\ddot{X}}_{t}+C.{\dot{X}}_{t}\text{}+K.{X}_{t}={R}_{t}$

- $M$ is the mass matrix of the system,
- $C$ is the viscous damping matrix of the system,
- $K$ is the elastic stiffness matrix of the system,
- $R$ is the vector of the external forces applied to the viscous system.

The system is of second order.

Two classes of integration methods can be distinguished for integrating the equilibrium equations step by step: these are the explicit and implicit integration methods.

Let us see what distinguishes them by examining the time integration of the following linear system:

$M.{\ddot{X}}_{t}+C.{\dot{X}}_{t}\text{}+K.{X}_{t}={R}_{t}$

This second-order differential system can be reduced to a first-order system:

$$
A.\dot{u}=B.u+F
$$

where:

$u=\left[\begin{array}{c}X\\ \dot{X}\end{array}\right]u=\left[\begin{array}{c}\dot{X}\\ \ddot{X}\end{array}\right]A=\left[\begin{array}{cc}I& 0\\ 0& M\end{array}\right]B=\left[\begin{array}{cc}0& I\\ -K& -C\end{array}\right]F=\left[\begin{array}{c}0\\ R\end{array}\right]$

To integrate this differential equation, a discretisation ${t}_{i}$ of the interval under study is used, together with a finite-difference formula to express the derivative $\dot{u}$ .

The methods in which, in the form written at time ${t}_{i}$ , only the derivative $\dot{u}$ involves the variable $u$ at time ${t}_{i+1}$ are called **explicit integration** methods. In this way, the determination of the quantities sought at the time instant ${t}_{i+1}$ does not result from an inversion of a system involving the operator $K$ . If, in addition, a "mass-lumping" is carried out so as to make the matrix $M$ diagonal, the determination of ${u}_{i+1}$ is particularly simple. These are the main characteristics of the explicit integration methods.

The implicit or semi-implicit methods involve the discretisation of $U$ in the form at a time instant later than ${t}_{i}$ , generally ${t}_{i+1}$ , so as to determine the variables of the problem at ${t}_{i+1}$ . Their determination therefore requires the solution of a system involving the operator $K$ .

Two notions concerning the integration schemes are important: consistency and stability.

The approximations used to obtain the differential operators define the **consistency** or the **order of the integration scheme**. One may indeed consider that the approximation with which the displacement is obtained at each time step is related to the order of approximation of the first and second derivatives with respect to time.

The **stability** study of a scheme consists in analysing the propagation of the numerical perturbations over time. A stable scheme keeps a finite solution, in spite of the perturbations, whereas an unstable scheme leads to a numerical blow-up or a divergence of the solution.

To carry out a stability study of an integration scheme, the latter is put in the form of a linear recursive system and the eigencharacteristics of this system are determined. If all the eigenvalues of the recursion operator are smaller than 1 in modulus, the scheme is stable; otherwise it is unstable (cf. ).

Explicit integration schemes are generally conditionally stable, which means that the time step must be sufficiently small to ensure the stability of the scheme.

Certain implicit algorithms have the particular feature of being unconditionally stable, depending on the choice of certain parameters, which is what makes them of interest and makes it possible to integrate the dynamic phenomenon with an arbitrarily large time step.

The Wilson- $\theta$  scheme and the NEWMARK scheme can be explicit for certain choices of their parameters. They are used for their unconditional stability properties, which are specific to implicit schemes. They will therefore be classified here in the category of implicit schemes, and the conditions under which they give the desired stability properties will be seen.

Two explicit integration schemes have also been introduced. These are the DIFF_CENTRE and ADAPT schemes, both of which are based on the central-difference method. They are conditionally stable and, in order to be efficient, require a diagonalised mass matrix. Conditional stability leads to a control of the time step which, exploited in the case of the ADAPT scheme, allows the time step to be adapted according to the rapidity of the phenomena being modelled.

## 3 The WILSON-\theta scheme [bib1]_

### 3.1 Presentation of the scheme

It will be assumed in what follows that the solids are linear elastic. This method starts from the hypothesis that the acceleration is linear between $t$ and $t+\theta .\Delta t$ :

$$
{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{\mathrm{t}+\tau }={\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+\frac{\tau}{\theta \Delta t}.\text{}\left({\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\theta .\Delta t}-{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}\right)
$$

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.02/10000200000001B8000000E14DAC8384ED959649.png)

By integrating with respect to the variable $t$ , one obtains:

$$
{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\tau }={\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+\tau {\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+\frac{{\tau}^{2}}{2\theta \Delta t}\left({\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\theta .\Delta t}-{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}\right)
$$

$$
{\mathrm{X}}_{t+\tau }=\text{}{\mathrm{X}}_{t}+\tau {\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+\frac{{\tau}^{2}}{2}\ddot{\mathrm{X}}+\frac{{\tau}^{3}}{6\theta \Delta t}\left({\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\theta .\Delta t}-{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}\right)
$$

The equilibrium equations are written at time $t+\theta .\Delta t$ with $\theta \ge 1$ :

$$
M.{\ddot{X}}_{t+\theta \Delta t}+\text{}C.{\dot{X}}_{t+\theta \Delta t}+\text{}K.{X}_{t+\theta \Delta t}\text{}=\text{}{R}_{t+\theta \Delta t}
$$

by expressing ${\dot{X}}_{t+\theta .\Delta t }$ and ${\ddot{X}}_{t+\theta .\Delta t }$ as functions of ${\ddot{X}}_{t+\theta .\Delta t }$ and of ${X}_{t}$ , ${\dot{X}}_{t}$ and ${\ddot{X}}_{t}$ by the system , , and substituting into , one obtains:

$\tilde{K}.{X}_{t+\theta .\Delta t }=\tilde{R}$

where

$\tilde{K}=K+\frac{3}{(\theta .\Delta t)}.C+\frac{6}{{(\theta .\Delta t)}^{2}}.M$

$\tilde{R}={R}_{t}+\theta .({R}_{t+\Delta t}-{R}_{t})+M.({a}_{0}.{X}_{t}+{a}_{2}.{\dot{X}}_{t}+2.{\ddot{X}}_{t})+C.({a}_{1}.{X}_{t}+2.{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{3}.{\ddot{X}}_{t})$

$\begin{array}{ccccccc}{a}_{0}=\frac{6}{{(\theta .\Delta t )}^{2}}& & {a}_{1}=\frac{3}{(\theta .\Delta t )}& & {a}_{2}=2.{a}_{1}& & {a}_{3}=\frac{\theta .\Delta t }{2}\end{array}$

The displacements, velocities and accelerations at step $t+\Delta t$ are recovered by the relations:

$\begin{array}{c}{\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}={a}_{4}.({X}_{t+\theta .\Delta t}-{X}_{t})+{a}_{5}.{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{6}.{\ddot{X}}_{t}\\ {\dot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}={\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{7}.({\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}+{\ddot{X}}_{t})\\ {X}_{t+\Delta t}={X}_{t}+\Delta t.{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{8}.({\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}+2.{\ddot{X}}_{t})\end{array}$

$\begin{array}{ccccccccc}{a}_{4}=\frac{{a}_{0}}{\theta}& & {a}_{5}=\frac{-{a}_{2}}{\theta}& & {a}_{6}=1-\frac{3}{\theta}& & {a}_{7}=\frac{\Delta t }{2}& & {a}_{8}=\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{6}\end{array}$

### 3.2 Complete algorithm of the WILSON-$\theta$ method

**a) initialisation:**

1. initial conditions ${X}_{0},{\dot{X}}_{0}$ and ${\ddot{X}}_{0}$
2. choice of $\Delta t$ and $\theta$ and computation of the coefficients ${a}_{1}$ ,… ${a}_{8}$ (cf. above)
3. assemble the stiffness matrix $K$ and the mass matrix $M$
4. form the effective stiffness matrix $\tilde{K}=K+{a}_{0}.M+{a}_{1}.C$
5. factorise $\tilde{K}$

**b) at each time step:**

|  | compute the effective loading $\tilde{R}$ |
| --- | --- |
|  | $\tilde{R}={R}_{t}+\theta .({R}_{t+\Delta t}-{R}_{t})+M.({a}_{0}.{X}_{t}+{a}_{2}.{\dot{X}}_{t}+2.{\ddot{X}}_{t})+C.({a}_{1}.{X}_{t}+2.{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{3}.{\ddot{X}}_{t})$ |
|  | solve $\tilde{K}.{X}_{t+\theta .\Delta t}=\tilde{R}$ |
|  | compute the displacements at time $t+\Delta t$ $\begin{array}{c}{\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}={a}_{4}.({X}_{t+\theta .\Delta t}-{X}_{t})+{a}_{5}.{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{6}.{\ddot{X}}_{t}\\ {\dot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}={\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{7}.({\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}+{\ddot{X}}_{t})\\ {X}_{t+\Delta t}={X}_{t}+\Delta t.{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{8}.({\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}+2.{\ddot{X}}_{t})\end{array}$ |
|  | computation of the next time step: return to the beginning |

### 3.3 Stability condition of the WILSON-$\theta$ scheme

The method is unconditionally stable for WILSON- $\theta$  > 1.37, a value commonly used for $\theta$ being 1.4. Furthermore, the method exhibits numerical dissipation for $\theta >1$ , all the greater as $\theta$ increases; but it can also generate spurious oscillatory amplifications at high frequencies.

The factor keyword WILSON makes it possible to specify the use of this algorithm and the choice of the value of $\theta$ . By default, the value of $\theta$ is taken as 1.4.

For the value of $\theta$ equal to 1, the Wilson- $\theta$  scheme is equal to the scheme known as " *linear acceleration* ", that is to say the Newmark scheme with $\gamma =1/2;\beta =1/6$ which is conditionally stable.

## 4 The Newmark scheme [bib1]_, [bib2]_

### 4.1 Presentation of the scheme

Newmark introduced two parameters $\gamma$ and $\beta$ for the computation of the positions and velocities at step $t+\Delta t$ :

${\dot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}={\dot{X}}_{t}+\Delta t.\left[(1-\gamma ).{\ddot{X}}_{t}+\gamma .{\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}\right]$

${X}_{t+\Delta t}=\text{}{X}_{t}+\Delta t.{\dot{X}}_{t}+\Delta {t}^{2}.\left[(\frac{1}{2}-\beta ).{\ddot{X}}_{t}+\beta .{\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}\right]$

Consider the equilibrium equations at time $t+\Delta t$ :

$M.{\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}+C.{\dot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}+\text{}K.{X}_{t+\Delta t}={R}_{t+\Delta t}$

substituting the previous relations and eliminating ${\dot{X}}_{t+\Delta t }$ and ${\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t }$ , one obtains, in the " *D-form* " parametrisation:

$\tilde{K}.{X}_{t+\Delta t}=\tilde{R}\text{où :}\tilde{K}=K+{a}_{0}.M+{a}_{1}.C$

$\tilde{R}={R}_{t+\Delta t}+C.\left\lbrace {a}_{1}.{X}_{t}+{a}_{4}.{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{5}.{\ddot{X}}_{t}\right\rbrace +M.({a}_{0}.{X}_{t}+{a}_{2}.{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{3}.{\ddot{X}}_{t})$

with: $\begin{array}{cccc}{a}_{0}=\frac{1}{(\beta .\Delta {t}^{2})}& {a}_{1}=\frac{\gamma}{(\beta .\Delta t)}& {a}_{2}=\frac{1}{(\beta .\Delta t)}& {a}_{3}=\frac{1}{2\beta }-1\\ {a}_{4}=\frac{\gamma}{\beta}-1& {a}_{5}=\frac{\Delta t}{2}(\frac{\gamma}{\beta}-2)& {a}_{6}=\Delta t.(1-\gamma )& {a}_{\mathrm{²7}}=\gamma .\Delta t\end{array}$

### 4.2 Complete algorithm of the Newmark scheme

**a) initialisation:**

1. initial conditions ${X}_{0}$ , ${\dot{X}}_{0}$ and ${\ddot{X}}_{0}$
2. choice of $\Delta t$ and computation of the coefficients ${a}_{1}$ ,… ${a}_{8}$ (cf. above)
3. assemble the stiffness matrix $K$, the mass matrix $M$ and the damping matrix $\mathrm{C}$
4. form the effective stiffness matrix $\tilde{K}=K+{a}_{0}.M+{a}_{1}.C$
5. factorise $\tilde{K}$

**b) at each time step:**

|  | compute the effective loading $\tilde{R}$ |
| --- | --- |
| 2 | $\tilde{R}={R}_{t+\Delta t}+M.({a}_{0}.{X}_{t}+{a}_{2}.{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{3}.{\ddot{X}}_{t})+C.\left\lbrace {a}_{1}.{X}_{t}+{a}_{4}.{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{5}.{\ddot{X}}_{t}\right\rbrace$ |
|  | solve $\tilde{K}.{X}_{t+\theta .\Delta t}=\tilde{R}$ |
|  | compute the velocities and accelerations at time $t+\Delta t$ $\begin{array}{c}{\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}={a}_{0}.({X}_{t+\Delta t}-{X}_{t})-{a}_{2}.{\dot{X}}_{t}-{a}_{3}.{\ddot{X}}_{t}\\ {\dot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}={\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{6}.{\ddot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{7}.{\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}\end{array}$ |
| 5 | computation of the next time step: return to the beginning |

### 4.3 Stability conditions of the Newmark scheme

The Newmark method is unconditionally stable if:

$\gamma >0.5$ et $\beta >\frac{{(2\gamma +1)}^{2}}{4}$

A positive numerical damping is introduced if $\gamma >0.5$ and a negative one if $\gamma <0.5$ . When $\gamma =0.5$ and $\beta =0$ , the Newmark formula reduces to the central-difference scheme. A very frequently used combination is $\gamma =0.5$ and $\beta =\frac{1}{4}$ , because it leads to a second-order scheme, unconditionally stable and without numerical damping.

This integration scheme is used quite widely in mechanics, because it makes it possible to choose the order of the integration, to introduce numerical damping or not, and it has a very good accuracy. It is integrated in the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’PHYS’. The factor keyword NEWMARK makes it possible to specify the use of this integration scheme and the choice of the value of $\beta$ and $\gamma$ . By default, the value of $\beta$ is taken as $0.25$ and the value of $\gamma$ is taken as $0.5$ .

## 5 Numerical damping of the implicit schemes

The numerical advantage of the direct implicit integration schemes lies in the fact that the time step can be substantially large relative to the smallest natural period of the system without risking causing an instability of the results.

However, if the content of the response lies in a set of eigenmodes, the highest of which has a natural frequency ${F}_{\max}$ , a criterion on the time step of the following form must still be satisfied:

$\Delta t<\frac{0.1}{{F}_{\max}}\text{à}\frac{0.01}{{F}_{\max}}$

For modes whose natural period is of the order of the time step or smaller than the time step, the integration algorithms introduce a strong damping which contributes to erasing the contribution of these high modes.

The graph below shows the decrease in amplitude of a single-degree-of-freedom system, without damping, when it is integrated by different methods (Wilson- $\theta$  and Newmark $\gamma =\frac{1}{2},\beta =\frac{1}{4}$ )

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.02/10000200000002F4000002342FFB6DF2F94A690B.png)

It is verified here that the Newmark algorithm with these parameters exhibits no numerical damping.

On the other hand, the implicit algorithms also have a rather significant effect of elongation of the natural periods contained in the response of the structure, which leads to a phase shift of the computed solution. The graph below presents percentages of elongation of the natural period of a single-degree-of-freedom system without damping.

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.02/100002000000026D0000023511572BD2913D7C1D.png)

On these two graphs, it is observed that, in order to guarantee an accuracy on the amplitude and the phase of the computed displacements, a criterion close to:

$\Delta t<\frac{0.1}{{F}_{\max}}\text{à}\frac{0.01}{{F}_{\max}}$

where ${F}_{\max}$ is the highest frequency of the motion that one wishes to capture correctly in the numerical analysis.

## 6 Central-difference scheme with a constant step

### 6.1 Principle

The explicit central-difference scheme with a constant step is written:

$\begin{array}{c}{\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}={\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\Delta t{\ddot{X}}_{n}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n})+o(\Delta {t}^{2})\\ {X}_{n+1}={X}_{n}+\Delta t{\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n})+o(\Delta {t}^{2})\end{array}$

in an acceleration parametrisation ( *one*  does not propose the displacement parametrisation), with the following notation:

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.02/Object_110.svg)

.

The velocity is expressed at half-integer indices of the time discretisation, whereas the displacements and accelerations are expressed at integer indices.

Written in this way, the scheme is of order two. It can also be written in the following manner with the values over three successive steps:

$\begin{array}{c}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}={\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\Delta t{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n}\left({t}_{n},{\mathrm{X}}_{n},{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n}\right)+o\left(\Delta {t}^{2}\right)\\ {\mathrm{X}}_{n+1}={\mathrm{X}}_{n}+\Delta t{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\left({t}_{n},{\mathrm{X}}_{n},{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n}\right)+o\left(\Delta {t}^{2}\right)\end{array}\phantom{\rule{4em}{0ex}}\Rightarrow \phantom{\rule{4em}{0ex}}\begin{array}{c}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n+1}={\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t\left({\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n}+{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n+1}\right)\\ {\mathrm{X}}_{n+1}={\mathrm{X}}_{n}+\Delta t\left({\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n}+\frac{1}{2}\Delta t{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{n}\right)\end{array}$

The acceleration at ${t}_{n}$ is not immediately computable because the velocity is known only at the previous half time step (at ${t}_{n-1/2}$ ), which poses a problem for evaluating the damping terms. To circumvent this difficulty, the acceleration at ${t}_{n}$ is computed by the following approximation of the equation of motion:

${\ddot{X}}_{n}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n})\stackrel{~}{=}{\ddot{X}}_{n}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n-})={M}^{-1}(F({t}_{n})-\mathrm{K.}{X}_{n}-C.{\dot{X}}_{n-})$

which constitutes a valid approximation if the damping is sufficiently weak (${\dot{X}}_{n}={\dot{X}}_{n-1/2}+o(1)$ ). The scheme loses its second-order accuracy if the damping of the structure is significant.

Other methods of approximating the acceleration can be envisaged. The one chosen has proved a good compromise between simplicity and stability, as shown by the study described in the reference on the accuracy and the stability of several methods.

The fields are archived at the time instants ${t}_{n},{t}_{;+1},\mathrm{...}$ , the velocity being approximated at these time instants by the following formula:

${\dot{X}}_{n+1}={\dot{X}}_{n+1/2}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\ddot{X}}_{n+1}({t}_{n+1},{X}_{n+1},{\dot{X}}_{n+1/2})$

The central-difference scheme "shortens" the periods (frequency distortion). It conserves the angular momentum. The total energy of the system oscillates over the time steps.

### 6.2 Stability

The central-difference scheme is **conditionally stable**. In the case of a system without damping, the scheme is stable for a time step satisfying $\Delta t <\frac{2}{{\omega}_{\max}}$ where ${\omega}_{\max}$ is the largest natural angular frequency of the system, that is $\Delta t <\frac{{T}_{\min}}{\pi}$ . A minimum number of time steps is required to describe the smallest period of the system ${T}_{\min}$ .

The limiting value for the time step decreases slowly as the damping increases. The stability criterion then requires:

$\Delta t<\frac{2}{{\omega}_{\max}}\left(\sqrt{1+{\xi}^{2}}-\xi \right)$

where $\xi$ denotes the reduced damping.

For example, for a damping of 0.5%, the condition becomes $\Delta t <\frac{{T}_{\min}}{5}$ .

### 6.3 Algorithm

In summary, the scheme as it is introduced in *Code_Aster*  takes the following form:

| 0 | initialisation: $\Delta t,{X}_{0},{\dot{X}}_{0}$ given ${\ddot{X}}_{0}={M}^{-1}(F(t=0))-K.{X}_{0}-C.{\dot{X}}_{0}$ ${\dot{X}}_{-1/2}={\dot{X}}_{0}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\ddot{X}}_{0}$ |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | At each time step ${X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n-1/2},{\ddot{X}}_{n}$ known $\begin{array}{c}{\dot{X}}_{n+1/2}={\dot{X}}_{n+1/2}+\Delta t{\ddot{X}}_{n}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n+1/2})\\ {X}_{n+1}={X}_{n}+\Delta t{\dot{X}}_{n+1/2}\\ {\ddot{X}}_{n+1}={M}^{-1}(F({t}_{n})-\mathrm{K.}{X}_{n+1}-C.{\dot{X}}_{n-1/2})\\ {\dot{X}}_{n+1}={\dot{X}}_{n+1/2}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\ddot{X}}_{n+1}\end{array}$ |
| 2 | possible archiving of ${X}_{n+1},{\dot{X}}_{n+1},{\ddot{X}}_{n+1}$ then return to step 1) for the next step. |

### 6.4 Diagonal mass matrix

The computation of the acceleration requires the inversion of the mass matrix. This explicit scheme becomes more efficient if a lumped mass matrix ("mass lumping") is used, so that it is diagonal. The inversion then no longer requires a factorisation and is immediate.

This is why the central-difference scheme is admissible only with mass matrices built in a diagonal manner, by the option ‘MASS_MECA_DIAG’ of the operator CALC_MATR_ELEM.

### 6.5 Verification of the time step

It has been seen that the central-difference scheme is stable provided that the time step, in the absence of damping, is smaller than a limiting value, equal to $\Delta t <\frac{2}{{\omega}_{\max}}$ . In practice a time step equal to 5% to 20% of the critical time step is used. A test on the time step has therefore been introduced, which verifies that:

$\Delta t <0,05\frac{2\pi }{\underset{1\le i\le \text{nddl}}{\max}(\sqrt{\frac{{k}_{ii}}{{m}_{ii}}})}$

where ${k}_{ii}$ and ${m}_{ii}$ are the diagonal terms of the stiffness and mass matrices.

If this condition is not satisfied, the user is stopped with a message indicating the maximum time step that can be used.

### 6.6 Computation of the acceleration

The computation of the acceleration is carried out as follows:

for each degree of freedom, a test is made of whether the corresponding diagonal term of the mass matrix is zero.

- if it is not zero, the acceleration term is computed according to the formula: ${\ddot{X}}_{n+1}={M}^{-1}(F({t}_{n})-K.{X}_{n+1}-\mathrm{C.}{\dot{X}}_{n-})$
- if it is zero, the acceleration term is not computed. This is the case for the so-called Lagrange degrees of freedom. If they correspond to fixed degrees of freedom, it is admissible not to take the line in question into account and not to compute its acceleration. In the case where the Lagrange degree of freedom has been introduced to define a link between two degrees of freedom, this no longer makes sense. The scheme is then unusable and a test stops the execution with an explicit message.

## 7 Adaptive time-step scheme

### 7.1 Principle

Explicit analysis methods are particularly indicated for the simulation of rapid phenomena, such as the propagation of waves in solids. On the other hand, they are less well suited to slower phenomena, since the stability condition of the scheme imposes a time step of the order of the smallest natural period of the system.

The adaptive scheme, based on the central-difference scheme, has been developed to allow the computation of transient responses in which "rapid" and "slow" phenomena occur. For example, during an impact, high-frequency waves first propagate and dissipate in the structure. Then, the structure responds only on its low-frequency modes, the high frequencies having been damped. The idea is therefore to adapt the time step progressively according to the phenomena involved, by setting an accuracy criterion on the solution.

### 7.2 Scheme

The explicit central-difference scheme with variable steps is written:

$\begin{array}{c}{\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}={\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\Delta {t}_{n-1}+\Delta {t}_{n}}{2}{\ddot{X}}_{n}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n})+o(\Delta {t}^{2})\\ {X}_{n+1}={X}_{n}+\Delta t{\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n})+o(\Delta {t}^{2})\end{array}$

with the following notation:

![Figure](/figures/r5.05.02/Object_135.svg)

.

It is observed that the time step varies. It is indexed: $\Delta {t}_{n}$ .

The consequence is that the scheme is no longer strictly of second order, since it is no longer "centred". The more $\Delta {t}_{n-1}$ and $\Delta {t}_{n}$ differ, the closer the order of the scheme is to one. Large variations of the time step are therefore accompanied by a loss of accuracy. The velocity formula used leads to good results when the time step decreases, but lowers the stability limit when the time step increases. This is why it is constrained to increase only very gradually.

Finally, the same approximations as for the central differences are used as regards the computation of the accelerations and of the velocities at "integer" time steps:

- the acceleration is estimated by ${\ddot{X}}_{n}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n})\stackrel{~}{=}{\ddot{X}}_{n}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n-})$ and ${\ddot{X}}_{n}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n-})={M}^{-1}.(F({t}_{n})-\mathrm{K.}{X}_{n}-C{\dot{X}}_{n-})$ ;
- and the stored velocity is evaluated by ${\dot{X}}_{n+1}={\dot{X}}_{n+}+\frac{\Delta t }{2}{\ddot{X}}_{n+1}$ .

As for the central-difference scheme, from which it is derived, the adaptive-step scheme requires the inversion of the mass matrix. This is why the diagonalisation of the mass matrix is required, as well as the same restrictions on the Lagrange degrees of freedom as for the central-difference scheme.

### 7.3 Estimation of the time step as a function of the required accuracy

In order to define a criterion on the time step as a function of the accuracy required on the solution, the notion of **perturbed apparent frequency** is introduced:

${f}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}=\frac{1}{2\pi }\sqrt{\mid \frac{{\ddot{X}}_{x}-{\ddot{X}}_{x-1}}{{X}_{x}-{X}_{x-1}}\mid }$

This quantity can be interpreted as the "instantaneous frequency" of the system. It is indeed an approximation of the local slope of the force/displacement curve. It is related to the error due to truncation in the truncated expansions of the scheme. It also makes it possible to take into account the external forces and their fluctuations in frequency.

In the case of a system with several degrees of freedom, it is necessary to compute an apparent frequency for each degree of freedom. The maximum over all the computed frequencies is then used to determine the time step.

If the denominator tends to zero, the apparent frequency can become very large and lose its physical meaning. An unjustified refinement of the time step is then obtained when the velocity vanishes. In the case of sinusoidal oscillations, this occurs twice per period. The criterion is then modified by introducing the following condition:

$\frac{\mid {X}_{x}-{X}_{x-1}\mid }{\Delta t }\le {\dot{X}}_{\min}\Rightarrow {f}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}=\frac{1}{2\pi }\sqrt{\mid \frac{{\ddot{X}}_{x}-{\ddot{X}}_{x-1}}{{\dot{X}}_{\min}\Delta t }\mid }$

An intermediate is obtained between the perturbed apparent frequency and the truncation error. The value of ${\dot{X}}_{\min}$ is not easy to determine *a priori*  and a poorly chosen value can lead to an artificial moderation of the apparent frequency.

Two methods are proposed.

#### 7.3.1 Influences of the neighbouring nodes

In the case of a system with several degrees of freedom, use can be made of the information given by the $1\le j\le \text{nv}$ neighbouring nodes of the node $i$ :

${f}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}=\underset{\text{DX},\text{DY},\text{DZ},\text{DRX},\text{DRY},\text{DRZ}}{\max}(\underset{1\le i\le \text{nb}\text{noeud}}{\max}\frac{1}{2\pi }\sqrt{\mid \frac{{\ddot{X}}_{x}^{i}-{\ddot{X}}_{x-1}^{i}}{{b}_{n}^{i}}\mid })$

where ${b}_{n}^{i}=\Delta {t}_{n}\max({10}^{-15}{\text{ms}}^{-1},{\dot{X}}_{n+}^{i},\frac{1}{100}\underset{1\le j\le \text{nv}}{\max}({\dot{X}}_{n+}^{j}))$

This method requires the enumeration of the neighbouring nodes and the estimation of the "velocities" according to each type of degree of freedom (translation DX, DY, DZ, and possibly rotation DRX, DRY and DRZ) for these neighbouring nodes.

The programmed method simplifies this formula somewhat and consists, for a given degree of freedom, $i$ , in carrying out from this position an ascending search and a descending search over the degrees of freedom in their numbering order defined by NUME_DDL. The first two degrees of freedom, $k$ and $l$ , of the same nature found respectively before and after the degree of freedom $i$ are considered as the "neighbours". In order to limit the cost of this technique, the search is carried out once and for all at the beginning of the transient analysis and the "neighbours" are recorded in two integer arrays.

The use of this method is started by the keyword VITE_MIN=‘NORM’.

#### 7.3.2 Use of the information at the previous time

One can also rely on the information provided by the previous time steps to estimate the minimum velocity. It is then estimated by the following formula:

${\dot{X}}_{\min}=\underset{k<n}{\max}(\frac{\mid {\dot{X}}_{k}^{i}\mid }{100},{10}^{-15}{\text{ms}}^{-1})$

One then has:

${f}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}=\underset{\text{DX},\text{DY},\text{DZ},\text{DRX},\text{DRY},\text{DRZ}}{\max}(\underset{1\le i\le \text{nb}\text{noeud}}{\max}\frac{1}{2\pi }\sqrt{\mid \frac{{\ddot{X}}_{x}^{i}-{\ddot{X}}_{x-1}^{i}}{{b}_{n}^{i}}\mid })$

with ${b}_{n}^{i}=\Delta {t}_{n}\max({10}^{\text{-15}}{\text{ms}}^{\text{-1}},{\dot{X}}_{n+}^{i},\frac{1}{100}\underset{k<n}{\max}({\dot{X}}_{k}^{j}))$

This method is triggered by the keyword VITE_MIN=‘MAXI’.

This method cannot be used if the velocity varies too much during the analysis, because in that case one would have at each step:

$\frac{\mid {X}_{n}-{X}_{n-1}\mid }{\Delta t }\le {\dot{X}}_{\min}^{i}$

### 7.4 Choice of the number of steps per apparent period, $N$

The error computations and the stability criteria established for a single-degree-of-freedom system (see ) have made it possible to estimate the number of steps $N$ required per apparent period in order to obtain a good accuracy. These tests have shown that a minimum of 20 steps per period is necessary. This number can be set by the user in the command file by means of the keyword ‘NB_POIN_PERIODE’. Its default value, set at 50, leads to an accuracy on the time integration of the order of 1 to 2%.

The initial time step serves as the absolute maximum time step: $\Delta {t}_{\text{mac}}=\Delta {t}_{\text{initial}}$ . Weighted by a coefficient which can be set by PAS_LIMI_RELA, it serves as the minimum time step: $\Delta {t}_{\min}=\text{PLR}\ast \Delta {t}_{\text{initial}}$

### 7.5 Heuristic for the evolution of the time step

An indicator, called the "error", is defined on the choice of the time step:

$\text{erreur}=\Delta {t}_{n}{\mathit{Nf}}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}$

This indicator must be smaller than 1 if one hopes to guarantee a good time integration of the smallest natural period. However, the adaptive scheme must at the same time avoid the use of a time step that is too small, which would then cause an extra analysis cost, or even the appearance of spurious "noise".

Depending on the indicator, the algorithm will increase or decrease the time step. Two coefficients are defined for this purpose, $\text{CDP}$ , the time-step refinement coefficient (keyword ‘COEF_DIVI_PAS’, default value: 1.334) and $\text{CMP}$ , the time-step amplification coefficient (keyword ‘COEF_MULT_PAS’, default value: 1.1).

During this search for the optimal time step, a maximum number of time-step reduction iterations, ${\text{iter}}_{\max}$ , is defined, in order to prevent the time step from evolving too abruptly, which is detrimental to the order of the scheme, and in order not to launch an optimisation that is too costly.

- if the error indicator is greater than its limit value, if the limit number of refinements for a time step has not been exceeded and if the time step remains larger than its minimum value fixed *a priori* , the time step is refined: $\Delta {t}_{n}>\frac{1}{{\mathit{Nf}}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}},\text{iter}<{\text{iter}}_{\max}\text{et}\Delta {t}_{n}>\Delta {t}_{\min}\Rightarrow \frac{\Delta {t}_{n}}{\text{CDP}}\to \Delta {t}_{n}$ ,
- if the indicator shows that for five consecutive steps the time step proves too fine, i.e. $\Delta {t}_{n}<\frac{0,75}{{\mathit{Nf}}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}}$ , then $\min\left(\Delta {t}_{\min},\text{CMP}\Delta {t}_{n}\right)\to \Delta {t}_{n}$

## 8 Conclusion

The operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’PHYS’ allows the choice between several time-integration methods. In their default parameter setting, the Wilson and Newmark schemes are unconditionally stable implicit schemes. They therefore require an inversion of a linear system at each time step, but in return they offer a choice of time step which is restricted only by the fineness with which one wishes to describe the time evolution of the phenomena being modelled.

The DIFF_CENTRE and ADAPT schemes are explicit, which saves them, in the case of a diagonal mass matrix, a costly matrix inversion. But the conditional stability of this type of scheme generally leads to the use of small time steps, conditioned by the smallest natural period of the system. It is therefore not guaranteed that the explicit schemes are systematically faster. This depends on the phenomena being simulated. If the physics of these phenomena requires a fine time discretisation, the time step used is naturally within the stability interval. Otherwise, the numerical stability constraints lead to a very costly inflation in the number of time steps.

The ADAPT scheme takes advantage of information on the frequency content of the response in order to adapt the time step. The discretisation of time is therefore no longer imposed by the smallest natural period of the system but by its response. This can be an advantage when the frequency of the response evolves in time, as in the case of impacts.
