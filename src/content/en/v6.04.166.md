# v6.04.166 SSNV166 – Cylinder cracked under multiple loadings

**Summary**

The purpose of this test is to calculate stress intensity factors along the crack bottom for a cylinder with axisymmetric crack.

The influence of the degree of elements and the type of the method is studied through various modellings.

1. $A$ Modelling tests $\mathrm{K1}$ and $\mathrm{K3}$ with a mesh linear $\mathrm{3D}$ and a elements classic finished method ($\mathrm{FEM}$).
2. $B$ Modelling tests $\mathrm{K1}$ and $\mathrm{K3}$ with a mesh quadratic $\mathrm{3D}$ (elements from Barsium) around the crack bottom and an $\mathrm{FEM}$ .
3. $C$ Modelling tests $\mathrm{K1}$ and $\mathrm{K3}$ with a mesh linear $\mathrm{3D}$ with a classic resolution but intensity factor extraction based on energy calculation.

In addition, for each model, various cases of loadings are studied:

- traction (solicitation in $I$ mode);
- twist (solicitation in $\mathrm{III}$ mode);
- flexion (opening one side, closing the other) with and without taking into account the contact.

Tensile and twisting cases do not involve contact.

Although symmetries exist in some cases (axisymetry for case 1, plane symmetry for the 2nd) the representation is made in $\mathrm{3D}$ to make the test generalizable under multiple loading.

## 2 reference solution

### 2.1 Calculation method used for reference solution

For axisymmetric cracks in a cylinder of infinite length, the Singular Integral Equations and Asymptotical Developments [bib1] method allows the calculation of stress intensity factor values.

1. Case 1: Traction and Torsion

Tensile induces an opening in mode 1. $\mathrm{K1}$ is given by the following formula:

${K}_{I}=\frac{P}{\pi {a}^{2}}\sqrt{\pi a}{F}_{1}(a/b)$

where $P$ is the force applied to the upper and lower faces and ${F}_{1}$ is a given function [Figure 2.1-a].

The twist induces an opening in mode 3. $\mathrm{K3}$ is given by the following formula:

${K}_{\mathrm{III}}=\frac{\mathrm{2T}}{\pi {a}^{3}}\sqrt{\pi a}{F}_{3}(a/b)$

where T is the time applied to the upper and lower faces and ${F}_{3}$ is a given function [Figure2.1-a].

1. Case 2: Contactless flexion

The bending induces an opening in $1$ mode. The value of $\mathrm{K1}$ to the point maximum opening $A$ is given by the following formula:

${K}_{{I}_{A}}=\frac{\mathrm{4M}}{\pi {a}^{3}}\sqrt{\pi a}{F}_{2}(a/b)$

where $M$ is the time applied to the upper and lower faces and ${F}_{2}$ is a given function [Figure2.1‐a].

1. Case 3: Flexion with contact

There is no analytical solution to this problem. $\mathrm{K1}$ is expected to be close to the contactless case on the opening part of the crack, and $\mathrm{K1}$ is expected to be zero on the closing part of the crack.

These three functions are derived from [bib1].

It is noted that in the case where the contact is taken into account (see [Figure 5.4-b *]),* $\mathrm{K1}$ *n't really zero on the crack bottom segments where it is closed. ## 6 Summary of the results

The objectives of this test are achieved:

- The aim is to validate the consideration of contact on the lips of the crack with quadratic elements (and Barsium elements). The results are better, compared to those obtained with a linear mesh.
- This test shows the value of the $«G-\mathit{thêta}»$ method for the calculation of stress intensity factors. This energy method has the advantage of being more general than the one using displacement jump (POST_K1_K2_K3) because it can be applied to any geometry cracks, while POST_K1_K2_K3 is restricted to flat cracks. In addition, the $«G-\mathit{thêta}»$ method gives better results (compared to the analytical solution) than POST_K1_K2_K3 for the same linear mesh.
