# u2.04.03 Choice of the elasto-(visco)-plastic constitutive behaviour

> **Summary.**

The aim of this note is to give advice to a user wishing to carry out analyses with non-linear constitutive behaviours of elastoplastic or elastoviscoplastic type, in order to choose a law suited to the modellings envisaged. The materials concerned are mainly metals. For the other types of material, the first paragraph refers to the appropriate references.

The specific features and capabilities of the elastoviscoplastic laws are described. A description is then given of the particularities of the different types of hardening, which makes it possible to put forward a few recommendations.

Some general advice is given on the identification of the parameters of the laws.

The effects of viscosity and of temperature are also addressed. Finally, some elements are given for checking the validity of the choices made concerning the constitutive behaviour and its parameters.

## 1 Introduction

The choice of the constitutive law is of course a function of the material that one is modelling, but also of the phenomena to be treated: for example, the same steel will be elastoplastic at low temperature, and viscoplastic at high temperature.

### 1.2 Which elastoplastic laws to choose: what are their capabilities?

This document provides elements for choosing the constitutive laws, according to their capabilities, and the phenomena to be modelled.

Advice for the identification of the parameters will be given, with emphasis on the domain of validity of the models: the parameters are identified for quite specific strains, rates and temperatures, which must correspond to the studies envisaged.

Moreover, if the modellings envisaged require it, it may be necessary to carry out the identifications in the large-strain domain. Suitable formalisms may be used for this purpose:

- SIMO_MIEHE for the Von Mises constitutive behaviours with isotropic hardening, the laws with the effect of the metallurgical phases, the Rousselier law,
- GDEF_LOG for most of the constitutive behaviours,
- GROT_GDEP for the hyperelastic laws of MOONEY-RIVLIN type.

### 2.3 The choice of the type of hardening

#### 2.3.1 Isotropic hardening

The elastoplastic laws with isotropic hardening make it possible to model an increase in the size of the elastic domain with the plastic strain that is identical in all directions. Although certain materials may correspond to this type of law, for most metals, which exhibit strong kinematic hardening, these laws are suited to modellings in which the global loadings are monotonic, or possibly with unloadings of small amplitude, so as to remain in the elastic regime.

This is a necessary condition for the response of the model to conform to reality (a complete model, with non-linear kinematic and isotropic hardenings, would in this case give the same result). But it is not a sufficient condition: there may exist structures in which a global monotonic loading produces local unloadings.

The validity of the approach with an isotropic hardening can be checked a posteriori: it is sufficient that, at every point, no unloading has given rise to an entry into plasticity. This check is detailed in § 5.2

To define the parameters of a law with isotropic hardening, the constitutive behaviour must be identified on a tensile curve, checking that the identification is indeed carried out over the range of strains likely to be encountered during the structural analysis envisaged.

The different types of hardening proposed (curve, power law) generally make it possible to reproduce the tensile test well (see
:ref:`r5.03.02` and the training documents:
`15-Constitutive laws <https://www.code-aster.org/V2/spip.php?action=dw2_out&id=1457>`_
`  <[https://www.code-aster.org/V2/spip.php?action=dw2_out&id=1457](https://www.code-aster.org/V2/spip.php?action=dw2_out&id=1457)>`_).

**Figure** 2.3.1-a **. Von Mises criterion, isotropic hardening**

In certain cases (fracture mechanics), it is necessary to approximate the elastoplastic constitutive behaviour by an equivalent non-linear elastic constitutive behaviour: these are the Hencky laws ( ELAS_VMIS_LINE, ELAS_VMIS_TRAC, ELAS_VMIS_PUIS ). Here again, these laws are valid only with a monotonic loading, and this time without any unloading, because they do not make it possible to model the plastic strain.

A very simplified way of using an isotropic hardening is to consider that it is linear (VMIS_ISOT_LINE). This may be valid over one range of strains, and not valid over another. Let us take for example tensile curves of a stainless steel:

**Figure** 2.3.1-b **. Tensile curves up to 20% strain**

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.03/10000000000014F700000BC08EC32D101E1EF902.png)

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.03/10000000000014F700000BC071D85748664BE550.png)

**Figure** 2.3.1-c **. Tensile curves up to 3% strain**

It can be seen from the figure that it is possible to model the tensile curve by a linear hardening, in large strains, if one is not interested with any precision in the small strains (lower than 1%). In the opposite case (figure ), it seems quite tricky to construct a linear hardening that is valid right from the entry into plasticity.

Moreover, this type of hardening (like all the models with linear hardening) risks **overestimating** the stresses in the case of large strains (or underestimating the strains at imposed stress), because nothing limits the hardening curve. A remedy for this difficulty is described in paragraph 5.1.

By using the constitutive behaviour VMIS_ISOT_TRAC, the risks are smaller: the tensile curve is defined by a function DEFI_FONCTION, and the maximum value of the abscissa (strain) makes it possible to define the domain of validity and thus to avoid exceeding this value in the structural analysis (take care to leave the default value PROL_DROITE='EXCLU' in DEFI_FONCTION).

#### 2.3.2 Linear kinematic hardening

The elastoplastic laws with linear kinematic hardening are suited to modellings in which the global loadings contain a few unloadings, and for which the approximation of the hardening curve by a straight line is **acceptable**.

They make it possible to represent, in a very simplified way, the Bauschinger effect, which is present for most metals. Let us examine the law VMIS_CINE_LINE:

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.03/10000000000002720000008FB551583D9F220D06.png)

1. Evolution of the 3D elastic domain (section)

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.03/1000000000000193000001D4C87DE2C8783AF505.png)

b) Evolution of the elastic domain in 1D

**Figure** 2.3.2-a **. Von Mises criterion, kinematic hardening**

**Advantage**:

- The interest of this model lies in its simplicity; it makes it possible in particular to test rapidly the effect of the kinematic hardening, because the identification and the solution are very rapid.

**Limitations**:

1. This model presents **no** isotropic hardening.
2. The approximation of the real tension–compression curve is often **poor** (cf. previous paragraph)
3. This model (like all the models with linear hardening) risks **overestimating** the stresses in the case of large strains (or underestimating the strains at imposed stress), because nothing limits the hardening curve.
4. Finally, if the loading comprises cycles, this model tends very quickly towards a stabilised cycle (in the uniaxial case, it is reached in a single cycle), which does not correspond to reality.

To remove the first limitation, it is possible to combine the linear kinematic hardening with an isotropic hardening: these are the models VMIS_ECMI_LINE (which nevertheless presents the 3 other drawbacks), VMIS_ECMI_TRAC (which also makes it possible to answer the second limitation).

Great care must be taken when identifying VMIS_ECMI_TRAC (cf.
[r5.03.16](doc:r5.03.16)
[r5.03.16](doc:r5.03.16)): indeed, the share of kinematic hardening, over the strain range studied, must remain smaller than the share of isotropic hardening; otherwise a negative isotropic hardening may be obtained.

#### 2.3.3 Non-linear kinematic hardening: laws of J.L. Chaboche

These laws make it possible at the same time to represent the Bauschinger effect (kinematic hardening), its non-linear evolution, and the isotropic hardening, as well as other phenomena (memory effect of the maximum plastic strain, recovery).

In their simplest form ( VMIS_CIN1_CHAB ) they lead to a particular form of the hardening curve, with a determined asymptote. The idea underlying these models is to reproduce the tension–compression cycles well, in size and in shape. To improve the description of the real curves, several independent kinematic variables can be introduced, each having a specific role in representing a given strain level. In Code_Aster, one has limited oneself to two kinematic variables ( VMIS_CIN2_CHAB ).

Their identification is more complex than for the previous models: the number of parameters increases, and at a minimum a cyclic test (tension–compression over several cycles) is required in order to identify them correctly. Moreover, tests at several strain levels are often necessary (and difficult to represent completely).

Here again it is indispensable to target properly the range of strain expected in the studies, so that the parameters are adjusted to that strain level. If parameters coming from an earlier identification are used, one must at a minimum check (via SIMU_POINT_MAT, for example, on a modelling of the tension–compression test) the response of the model for these parameters.

To illustrate the advantage of using a non-linear kinematic hardening beyond a few loading cycles, let us consider an example of tension–compression cycles at imposed strain:

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.03/10000000000007F1000008231E16304DBB9194D0.png)

This curve is in fact a numerical curve (simulated with VMIS_CIN2_CHAB) but it correctly reproduces the experimental curves for the stainless steel considered. It will serve as a reference for the illustrations below.

The approximation of this curve by a linear kinematic hardening (with an isotropic component, adjusted on the first tensile curve) shows that the response is very far from it:

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.03/10000000000014F700000BC0EF2CEAD69735C1E2.png)

The representation of the very first cycles can be improved by choosing VMIS_ECMI_TRAC, and by readjusting the values of the Prager coefficient. It is found that, although the first 2 cycles are well represented, the model VMIS_ECMI_TRAC tends towards a stabilised state with a stress amplitude very much higher than the real curve.

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.03/10000000000007F1000008231C94B0F6FB938AC7.png)

If the cycles are continued, this model would moreover tend towards an adapted cycle, of amplitude $1600mathit{MPa}$!

If the modelling aims to predict a progressive-strain phenomenon, the use of such models is delicate: indeed, they lead to a constant ratcheting at a non-zero mean stress, of a value very much higher than the experimental ratcheting (unless the parameters are chosen so that one of the kinematic hardenings is linear, in which case a stabilised accommodated cycle is recovered (too) rapidly).

It is preferable in these situations to use the TAHERI model.

If the situation studied involves a pre-hardening, it may be useful to identify the model VMIS_CIN2_MEMO on cyclic tests with pre-hardening. (see for example
[v6.08.105](doc:v6.08.105)
[v6.08.105](doc:v6.08.105)).

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.03/10000000000014F700000BC0D19F8F13413439B9.png)

Other aspects can be taken into account, in particular the over-hardening due to non-proportional cyclic loadings. This is modelled in VMIS_CIN2_CHAB (without memory effect) or VMIS_CIN2_MEMO (with memory effect) via the parameters DELTA1, DELTA2.

#### 2.3.4 Conclusions on the choice of the type of elastoplastic hardening

The previous paragraphs show that this choice is essential:

- for a monotonic loading, the tensile curve should be well approximated over the target strain range, and it should be checked that the structural analysis remains within this interval
- to model one or two loading–unloading cycles, a model with linear kinematic hardening may be used, provided that the response is carefully checked at one or several points.
- To simulate several loading cycles, a model of Chaboche (or Taheri) type is necessary.

### 2.4 Influence of the rate

For purely elastoplastic materials, the time used in the simulations is a mere parameter of the loading (even if it has a physical meaning in the thermo-mechanical cases) and has no direct influence on the constitutive laws.

But it is necessary to take it into account in the constitutive behaviour in the following cases:

- high loading rate: elastoplastic law of Johnson-Cook
- viscosity: elastoviscoplastic laws.

#### 2.4.1 Johnson-Cook law

This law makes it possible to take the strain rate and the temperature directly into account in the evolution of the isotropic hardening (cf.
r5.03.02` page 11). It makes it possible to treat impact problems, and to implement the thermo-mechanical coupling (see for example
:ref:`v7.20.105
[v7.20.105](doc:v7.20.105)).

#### 2.4.2 Elastoviscoplasticity with isotropic hardening

The elastoviscoplastic model of Lemaître makes it possible to take into account secondary creep (at constant rate – for certain particular values of the parameters it can reduce to a Norton constitutive relation) and primary creep. (cf.
[r5.03.08](doc:r5.03.08) ).

The loading surface remains isotropic (no kinematic hardening). Creep tests, relaxation tests, or tensile tests at different strain rates are necessary for the identification of the parameters.

Here again, it must be checked that the values thus obtained are valid for the studies envisaged, that is to say that the strain rates encountered in the studies do indeed lie in the range of those that were used for the identification.

Where one wishes to go further, that is to say to model tertiary creep (taking large strains into account is often necessary), the following models may be used, which incorporate a creep damage:

- VISC_ENDO_LEMA, VENDOCHAB

**cf.**

[r5.03.15](doc:r5.03.15)
[r5.03.15](doc:r5.03.15)

- HAYHURST

**cf.**

[r5.03.13](doc:r5.03.13)
[r5.03.13](doc:r5.03.13)

#### 2.4.3 Elastoviscoplasticity with non-linear kinematic hardening

The following constitutive behaviours make it possible to take kinematic hardening into account:

- VISC_CIN1_CHAB, VISC_CIN2_CHAB, VISC_CIN2_MEMO

**cf.**

[r5.03.04](doc:r5.03.04)

These laws are extensions of the elastoplastic laws of J.L. Chaboche to the viscoplastic case. The various components of the Chaboche laws described previously are present, and the viscosity must in addition be included (of Lemaître type, that is to say making it possible to reproduce primary and secondary creep). This means that their identification will have to take the strain rate into account (for example on the cyclic tests).

Other phenomena can be represented (the hardening linked to the non-proportionality of the loading, the recovery of hardening), by the following model:

- VISCOCHAB

**cf.**

[r5.03.12](doc:r5.03.12)

The complete identification of this model requires a large number of different tests: cyclic tests at different rates and at different strain levels, with pre-hardening, tension–torsion tests, relaxation tests.

#### 2.4.4 Viscosity law as a hyperbolic sine and isotropic hardening

Another form of viscosity law is proposed in the following models:

- VISC_ISOT_LINE, VISC_ISOT_TRAC

**cf.**

[r5.03.21](doc:r5.03.21)

They have isotropic hardening, and require the use of SIMO_MIEHE.

## 3 Identifying the parameters: which tests are necessary?

The identification of the parameters of the models quickly becomes difficult to carry out manually, except for the simplest models ( VMIS_CINE_LINE, VMIS_ISOT_LINE, VMIS_ISOT_TRAC ).

One therefore resorts to an optimisation procedure, available in the command MACR_RECAL
[u4.73.02](doc:u4.73.02)
[u4.73.02](doc:u4.73.02).

There are several advantages in using this command:

- the simulation that makes it possible to find the numerical curves (which will be compared with the experimental curves) is a classical Code_Aster command file, which can be launched on its own, and which represents any analysis (not necessarily on a material point);
- the recalibrated coefficients are directly usable in the studies, since they are parameters of the simulation file;
- numerous algorithms are available, as well as analysis modes making it possible to use multi-processor architectures if necessary.

Details of the algorithms used may be consulted in the document
[r4.03.06](doc:r4.03.06)
[r4.03.06](doc:r4.03.06).

But the tools do not do everything! Indeed, before seeking to identify the parameters of a model, several questions must be asked:

- is the number of tests available sufficient with respect to the number of parameters to be recalibrated;
- do the tests bring out the physical phenomena simulated by the constitutive law (already mentioned previously): loading–unloading, cycles, memory effects, recovery, non-radiality, high rate, viscosity, …);
- can these effects be separated, so as to identify the parameters successively, which will lighten the optimisation task and make it possible to grasp the results better.

To go further into the detail of the identification, documents specific to the different constitutive behaviours remain to be written; as regards the cyclic elastoviscoplastic constitutive behaviours, a more detailed user's guide is in the course of being written, drawn from EDF/R&D work [ 4 ] and [ 5 ].

Moreover, a fairly general methodology is proposed in [ 1 ] page 617 and [ 3 ].

## 4 The anisothermal simulations

During anisothermal simulations, the variation of the parameters with temperature must most of the time be taken into account. Care must therefore be taken that these parameters are properly identified.

In this paragraph, a few classical errors induced by the interpolation or the extrapolation of the values as a function of the temperature are illustrated.

The tests are carried out with the laws VMIS_ISOT_TRAC and VMIS_CIN1_CHAB. The conclusions retained are not, however, exclusive to one particular law.

### 4.1 Dangers of extrapolation:

To carry out a thermo-mechanical study with a constitutive law whose coefficients depend on the temperature, the user may wish to extrapolate the curves in order to carry out the study at a given temperature. This is strongly discouraged. An example:

In the case of an isotropic hardening, it is common to use experimental tensile curves for a few temperatures, varying for example between $20°$ and $350°$. The tensile curves are entered in the command file for different temperatures with the command ‘DEFI_NAPPE’. Suppose that the extensions have been defined by PROL_DROITE='LINEAIRE' and PROL_GAUCHE='LINEAIRE'.

Suppose that the user wishes to carry out an analysis at a temperature which exceeds the maximum temperature at which the identifications of the tensile curves were made, say 1000°C for example (this is a deliberately exaggerated example, but it makes it possible to illustrate the point). The material coefficients of the law would thereby be obtained at $1000°C$ by extrapolation.

This can lead to aberrant results ( Figure 4.1-a ): the tensile curve obtained at the extrapolated temperature of $1000°C$ presents a concavity and a hardening slope that contradict the other curves and reality.

To avoid this kind of error, **any extrapolation with respect to the temperature must be avoided.**

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.03/100000000000034A000002530FB04E30AD635559.png)

**Figure** 4.1-a **. Tensile curves as a function of temperature – result at** $1000°C$

### 4.2 Error in the interpolation of the temperature

This example brings out a possible error in the interpolation of the temperature, generally due to a non-monotonic evolution of the material coefficients with temperature. It is sufficient that a single one of the coefficients does not evolve in a monotonic manner for the interpolation between two tensile curves to lead to a curve which does not lie between the two extremes.

To exhibit this kind of error, with a constitutive law of type ‘VMIS_CIN1_CHAB’, the following test was set up:

Suppose that three tensile curves identified experimentally at 3 different temperatures are known: $20°$, $100°$ and $200°C$. One seeks to identify the parameters of the law ‘VMIS_CIN1_CHAB’ at these three temperatures. In order to understand the point, let us briefly recall the form of the hardening of the law ‘VMIS_CIN1_CHAB’:

- Criterion: ${(sigma -Calpha )}_{text{eq}}-R(p)le 0$
- kinematic hardening: $dot{alpha}=dot{{varepsilon}^{p}}-gamma alpha dot{p}$
- isotropic hardening: $R(p)={R}_{infty}+({R}_{0}-{R}_{infty}){e}^{-text{bp}}$

Suppose that the results of 3 identifications at three different temperatures are:

- at $T=20°$; the identified material parameters are: ${C}_{1}$, ${gamma}_{1}$, ${R}_{0}$, non-zero, and ${R}_{infty}mathrm{simeq }{R}_{0}$ $bmathrm{simeq }0$, (that is, an almost purely kinematic constitutive behaviour);
- at $T=100°$; the identified material coefficients are: ${C}_{2}mathrm{simeq }0$, ${R}_{0}$, ${R}_{infty}$, ${b}_{2}$, non-zero, (that is, an almost purely isotropic constitutive behaviour)
- at $T=200°$; the material coefficients are: ${C}_{3}$, ${gamma}_{3}$, ${R}_{0}$, non-zero, ${R}_{infty}mathrm{simeq }{R}_{0}$, ${b}_{3}mathrm{simeq }0$, (that is, once again a purely kinematic constitutive behaviour).

Each of these identifications is sufficiently accurate, and makes it possible to recover, for each temperature, numerical curves very close to the experimental curves.

The simulation of the tensile curve at the temperature of $50°C$ is shown in the figure )

It is found that the curve obtained by interpolation at $50°C$ is erroneous:

![Figure](/figures/u2.04.03/1000000000000318000002646D45D55E96271702.png)

**Figure** 4.2-a **. Tensile curves as a function of temperature – result at 50°**

This comes from the fact that the identifications were carried out independently, without checking the consistency of the results. The variations of each coefficient with temperature are enormous: for example

| Temperature °C | C | b |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 20 | {C}_{1} | mathrm{simeq }0 |
|  | mathrm{simeq }0 | {b}_{2} |
|  | {C}_{3} | mathrm{simeq }0 |

This example is of course extreme, but it makes it possible to put forward a recommendation:

- either check the monotonic evolution of the material parameters as a function of temperature, and carry out the identification again for the suspect values,
- or, if possible, carry out the identification in one go for all the temperatures.

## 5 The domain of validity

Several checks are possible in order to verify that the constitutive law chosen, and the values of the parameters used, are valid for the simulation.

In addition to the advice given previously, so as to choose the constitutive law properly according to what one wants to model, certain additional checks can be carried out with the aid of specific tools.

### 5.1 Validity of the parameters over the strain and rate range.

The parameters of the chosen model having been identified over a certain strain range, it is important to check that, in the studies using these parameters, these strains do indeed remain within the identification interval.

The tensile curves defined by DEFI_FONCTION incorporate a "safeguard": the maximum value of the abscissa ( EPSI ) cannot be exceeded in the study. But if this should nevertheless occur, instead of defining a constant extension (or worse: a linear one) the identification should be taken up again in order to define additional points on the tensile curve.

Linear hardenings ( ECRO_LINE ), or those defined by an analytical function ( ECRO_PUIS , VMIS_CINx*_CHAB , etc.), are much more dangerous. Nothing will prevent the studies from greatly exceeding the strain level of the identification. This is why a safeguard ought to be put in place in a future version.

In any event, it is relatively easy, in the post-processing of a study, to compute ( CALC_CHAMP ) the norm of the strain field ( EPEQ_ELGA ) and to extract its maximum from it ( POST_ELEM / MINMAX , or graphical post-processing in SALOME_MECA ).

If the study leads one to use a large-strain formalism, the identification must use it as well.

As regards the strain rate, a check is again necessary here. Its automatic computation ought to be proposed in a future version.

### 5.2 Unloading: validity of the isotropic hardening (and of the Hencky laws)

How can one check that the unloadings are small enough for the analysis with an isotropic hardening to be valid? There exists in CALC_CHAMP an unloading indicator DERA_ELGA (cf.
[u4.81.04](doc:u4.81.04)
[u4.81.04](doc:u4.81.04)).

- The components DCHA_V, DCHA_T indicate whether there exist unloadings on the stresses (either on the Von Mises stress, or on the total tensor), thus invalidating the analysis with a non-linear elastic law.
- The component IND_DCHA supplies an indicator which indicates whether there is a risk of entering plasticity on unloading, thus invalidating the analysis with isotropic hardening.

For further details of their computation, see
[r4.20.01](doc:r4.20.01) ).

### 5.3 Radiality: effects of non-proportionality

In the case of strongly non-proportional cyclic loadings, the over-hardening effect may be ignored by the constitutive behaviour chosen. By using, in CALC_CHAMP, the indicator of unloading and of radiality of the loading, DERA_ELGA (cf.
[u4.81.04](doc:u4.81.04)), the component ERR_RADI measures the error committed through the rotation of the normal to the loading surface. If this value is large, a model making it possible to take this effect into account must then be used (for example VISCOCHAB).
