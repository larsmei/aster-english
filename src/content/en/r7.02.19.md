# r7.02.19 Cohesive elements with X-FEM

**Summary:**

This document presents the various cohesive elements available with the extended finite element method (X-FEM). Three types of cohesive law are available. The law introduced first is a regularised law, available for the linear elements, and a function of the displacement jump alone. Following the limitations of the latter, two mixed laws were introduced: one for the linear elements, the other for the quadratic elements. A crack-propagation procedure with cohesive elements is implemented. It is based on the linear mixed elements.

The cohesive elements are implemented in *Code_Aster* in 2D and in 3D. They can be used with two types of discontinuity: the emerging interfaces (keyword TYPE_DISCONTINUITE='INTERFACE' in DEFI_FISS_XFEM) and the cohesive-type cracks (keyword TYPE_DISCONTINUITE='COHESIF'), which are used for propagation studies on an unknown path. The cohesive law is defined in STAT_NON_LINE, and the solution is carried out with the command STAT_NON_LINE [U4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03). For a propagation study on an unknown path, the cracked surface is updated by the command PROPA_FISS, and the directional criterion is computed by the command CALC_G.

## 1 Introduction

Among the classical approaches of fracture mechanics, one counts the damage laws, for which the loss of mechanical strength is described by the degradation of the volumetric properties of the material, and two approaches which concentrate this degradation on a surface: Griffith's approach [bib1], and the cohesive-zone models (CZM). Griffith's model consists in carrying out a mechanical analysis on a cracked domain, the crack being free of stress, and in computing energy quantities (energy release rate $G$ and stress intensity factors ${K}_{I},{K}_{\mathit{II}}$ , see the documents [R7.02.01](doc:r7.02.01) and [R7.02.05](doc:r7.02.05)) which trigger propagation if a threshold quantity ${G}_{c}$ (which is a material property) is reached.

> Despite this simplicity, this method has drawbacks *for certain applications:*

- If the size of the crack-process zone becomes comparable to the characteristic lengths of the model (size of the structure, initial crack length...), the response of the structure exhibits a scale effect which Griffith's model fails to reproduce. This is generally the case for *concrete and geomaterials,* as well as for *short cracks* .
- Griffith's propagation criterion being implicit, it is very difficult to determine the advance of the front as a curve $G={G}_{c}$ . As explained by Fries [bib11], very empirical explicit criteria must be used instead.

In the CZMs, cohesive forces ${t}_{c}$ act between the crack lips. They obey a softening relation in the displacement jump $⟦u⟧$ , so that they are equal to $0$ when the crack is fully open (see fig.).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/1000020000000170000000E00A16B64884841619.png)

Figure 1-1: Cohesive-zone model.

In this context, the extended finite element method (XFEM) is a numerical technique which makes it possible to insert discontinuity surfaces through the elements [bib2], and therefore to free oneself from the constraint of meshing them. This is particularly useful for crack-propagation studies on an unknown path: the remeshing issues are avoided.

Three types of cohesive law are available for the X-FEM elements:

- the regularised cohesive laws (that is, with a finite initial stiffness) for linear finite elements CZM_LIN_REG and CZM_EXP_REG. These are the first laws introduced and the simplest, but they present convergence difficulties as soon as the adherent zone becomes large.
- the mixed cohesive laws (that is, with an infinite initial stiffness) for quadratic finite elements CZM_OUV_MIX and CZM_TAC_MIX. They are more robust and more accurate, but reserved for quadratic elements.
- the mixed cohesive law CZM_LIN_MIX, for linear finite elements. *Propagation studies on an unknown path can be carried out only with the law* CZM_LIN_MIX.

In this document, we begin by writing the strong formulation of the problem and by presenting the cohesive-force/displacement-jump relations for the three cohesive laws (cf. [§2]). We then write the variational formulation of the problem (cf. [§3]). We explain the solution of the problem by a Newton–Raphson method (cf. [§4]), and present the linearisation of the variational formulation. We write the finite-element discretisation of the problem, giving the matrix expressions of the tangent matrix and of the right-hand side vectors (cf. [§5]). Finally, we detail the procedure for studying the propagation of cohesive cracks on an unknown path (cf. [§6]).

## 2 Strong formulation of the cohesive problem

### 2.1 Formulation of the equations of the general problem

Let $\Omega \subset {ℝ}^{d},d\in \lbrace 1,2\rbrace$ denote the uncracked computational domain. Its boundary $\partial \Omega$ is split into two parts ${\Gamma}_{u}$ and ${\Gamma}_{g}$ , on which displacements and a surface-force density $t$ are imposed respectively (see fig.). We denote by $\Gamma$ the *potential cracking zone* on which the cohesive law is defined, composed of an upper crack lip ${\Gamma}^{+}$ and a lower lip ${\Gamma}^{-}$ . If ${u}^{i}$ is the displacement on ${\Gamma}^{i}$ , then the displacement jump is introduced as $⟦u⟧\left(x\right)={u}^{+}\left(x\right)-{u}^{-}\left(x\right)$ . Let $n$ denote the outward normal vector to ${\Gamma}^{-}$ , ${n}^{+}$ the outward normal vector to ${\Gamma}^{+}$ , and $-{t}_{c}^{+}={t}_{c}^{-}={t}_{c}$ the cohesive force that is applied on ${\Gamma}^{-}$ (see fig.). On $\Gamma$ , we therefore have $-\sigma \cdot {n}^{+}=\sigma \cdot n={t}_{c}$ . With these conventions, we have $\mathrm{〚}{u}_{n}\mathrm{〛}=\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}\cdot n$ positive in opening and negative in interpenetration (see fig.).

| Constitutive law | $\sigma =C:\epsilon \text{dans}\Omega$ |
| --- | --- |
| Equilibrium | $\nabla \cdot \sigma =f\text{dans}\Omega$ |
| Imposed surface forces | $\sigma \cdot {n}_{\mathit{ext}}=t\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{t}$ |
| Cohesive-force density | $\sigma \cdot n={t}_{c}\text{sur}\Gamma$ |
| Imposed displacements | $u=0\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{u}$ |

### 2.2 Cohesive laws

#### 2.2.1 Regularised cohesive laws

The first type of law that we may consider is a law in which the initial adhesion is not perfect: the initial slope is finite. Two cohesive laws of this kind are available in Code_Aster, the laws CZM_EXP_REG and CZM_LIN_REG, whose characteristics are detailed in [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11). We recall here the main points; the reader may refer to that document for further details. We detail here the extension to XFEM for the law CZM_EXP_REG, based on the reference article [bib 10 ]. The extension to the law CZM_LIN_REG is carried out by following exactly the same paradigm.

The opening of a crack in mixed mode is characterised by a damage criterion defined by means of the equivalent displacement jump and of the internal variable $\alpha$ . The material remains in the elastic domain as long as the following inequality is satisfied:

$f({\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\mathit{eq}},\alpha )={\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\mathit{eq}}-\alpha \le 0$

- ${⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}=\sqrt{{\langle ⟦{u}_{n}⟧\rangle }_{+}^{2}+{⟦{u}_{\tau}⟧}^{2}}$ is the equivalent displacement jump,
- $\mathrm{〚}{u}_{\tau}\mathrm{〛}=\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}-\mathrm{〚}{u}_{n}\mathrm{〛}n$ is the tangential displacement jump,
- $\alpha (t)=max\lbrace  {\alpha}_{0},\underset{v\in [0,t]}{max}{\mathrm{〚}u(v)\mathrm{〛}}_{\mathit{éq}}\rbrace$ is the internal variable of the cohesive law,
- ${\alpha}_{0}$ is the initial value of $\alpha$ . This value is given by the user through the material parameter PENA_ADHERENCE, so that ${\alpha}_{0}=\frac{{G}_{c}}{{\sigma}_{c}}\text{PENA\_ADHERENCE}$ .

The cohesive stress is then written as the sum of an elastic stress, a dissipative stress and a penalisation stress which accounts for contact:

${t}_{c}=H({〚u〛}_{\text{èq}}-\alpha ){\sigma}_{\mathrm{lin}}+(1-H({〚u〛}_{\text{èq}}-\alpha )){\sigma}_{\mathrm{dis}}+{\sigma}_{\mathrm{pen}}$

where $H$ is the indicator function of ${\mathrm{\Re }}^{+}$

- ${\sigma}_{\mathit{pen}}=C{\langle ⟦{u}_{n}⟧\rangle }_{-}n$ is the penalisation stress.

where $C$ is a penalisation coefficient made explicit in [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11), determined from the material parameter PENA_CONTACT.

- ${\sigma}_{\mathit{lin}}={\sigma}_{\mathit{dis}}=\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{\alpha}\exp(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}\alpha )⟦u⟧$ is the expression common to the linear and dissipative stresses, with $\alpha ={\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\mathit{èq}}$ for ${\sigma}_{\mathit{dis}}$ .

where ${\sigma}_{c}$ is the critical stress at failure and ${G}_{c}$ is the energy toughness of the material. It corresponds in fact to the energy required for the complete opening of the interface over a unit length. A quick computation with the previous expressions makes it possible to confirm:

$\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{t}_{c,\tau }\cdot d⟦{u}_{\tau}⟧+\underset{0}{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}\left({t}_{c}\cdot n\right)d⟦{u}_{n}⟧={G}_{c}$

The cohesive stress for a pure mode $I$ loading is represented in the figure as a function of the normal displacement jump.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/100000000000019700000164A6C12195D857A9DE.png)

**Figure** 2.2.1-1 **: Evolution of the normal cohesive force as a function of the displacement jump.**

For $\mathrm{〚}{u}_{n}\mathrm{〛}<0$ , it is also usual to define an equivalent cohesive force ${t}_{c,\mathrm{eq}}$ by means of the energetic equivalence condition:

${t}_{c,\mathit{eq}}{\dot{⟦u⟧}}_{\mathit{eq}}={t}_{c,n}\dot{⟦{u}_{n}⟧}+{t}_{c,\tau }\cdot \dot{⟦{u}_{\tau}⟧}$

In order to recover its value, one differentiates ${\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\mathit{èq}}$ with respect to time.

$\dot{{\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\mathit{èq}}}=\frac{\dot{\mathrm{〚}{u}_{n}\mathrm{〛}}\mathrm{〚}{u}_{n}\mathrm{〛}+\dot{\mathrm{〚}{u}_{\tau}\mathrm{〛}}\cdot \mathrm{〚}{u}_{\tau}\mathrm{〛}}{{\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\mathit{èq}}}$

Whence one identifies: ${t}_{c,\mathit{èq}}={t}_{c,n}\frac{{⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}}{⟦{u}_{n}⟧}=\parallel {t}_{c,\tau }\parallel \frac{{⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}}{\parallel ⟦{u}_{\tau}⟧\parallel }=\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{\alpha}\exp\left(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}\alpha \right){⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}$

#### 2.2.2 Mixed cohesive laws for quadratic elements

The second type of law that we may consider is, conversely, a law in which the initial adhesion is perfect: the initial slope is infinite. Two cohesive laws of this kind are available with XFEM in Code_Aster, the laws CZM_OUV_MIX and CZM_TAC_MIX, whose characteristics are detailed in [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11). We recall here the law CZM_OUV_MIX, represented in the figure.

Denoting by $\delta$ the displacement jump (in order to conform to the notation of [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11)), the material remains in the elastic domain as long as:

$f(\delta ,\alpha )={\delta}_{n}-\alpha \le 0$

This time, however, the internal variable has a rigorously zero initial value, so that the stress is no longer explicit as a function of the displacement, as the figure shows.

#### 2.2.3 Mixed cohesive laws for linear elements

The law also has an infinite initial slope, but unloading is linear (see fig.). It is available under the name CZM_LIN_MIX. In order to lighten the notation, let $w=⟦u⟧$ . In the augmented-Lagrangian formalism (see the documents [R5.03.52](doc:r5.03.52) and [R5.03.54](doc:r5.03.54)), a general expression for the surface-energy density is:

$\Pi (w,\lambda )=\varphi (\lambda +rw)-\frac{\lambda \cdot \lambda }{2r}$

where $\varphi$ is a differentiable function, and $r$ an augmentation parameter.

> The interface stress is then classically given by a derivative with respect to the primal variable ${t}_{c}=\frac{\partial \Pi }{\partial w}$ . An additional equation is required in order to determine whether one is in the adherent regime, and to impose the corresponding Dirichlet relation if that is the case. In the augmented-Lagrangian formalism, this additional interface law is determined by differentiation with respect to the dual variable $\frac{\partial \Pi }{\partial \lambda }=0$ . The advantage of the augmented-Lagrangian method is that there are no other inequalities to be checked during the solution: the interface behaviour is entirely contained in these two *equalities* , which allows a Newton method to be used for the solution.

The cohesive force is then:

> ${t}_{c}\left(\lambda +rw\right)=\frac{\partial \Pi }{\partial w}=r\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial (\lambda +rw)}$

The interface law is then written simply as $\frac{\partial \Pi }{\partial \lambda }=\frac{1}{r}\left[{t}_{c}\left(\lambda +rw\right)-\lambda \right]=0$ , which can be rewritten more simply as $\lambda ={t}_{c}\left(\lambda +rw\right)$ .

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/Cadre3.gif)

Figure 2.2.3-1: Mixed cohesive law for linear elements.

An equivalent cohesive force is defined by ${\left(\lambda +rw\right)}_{\mathit{eq}}:=\sqrt{{\langle {\lambda}_{n}+r{w}_{n}\rangle }_{+}^{2}+{\left({\lambda}_{\tau}+r{w}_{\tau}\right)}^{2}}$ . A threshold function $\phi$ is introduced by $\phi \left({\left(\lambda +rw\right)}_{\mathit{eq}}\right):=\frac{{\left(\lambda +rw\right)}_{\mathit{eq}}-{\sigma}_{c}}{r{w}_{c}-{\sigma}_{c}}$ . We can then define a *dimensionless* internal variable $\alpha$ as the maximum of the threshold function over time, projected onto the interval $[0,1]$ :

$\tilde{\alpha}(t)=\underset{[0,t]}{\max}\phi \left({\left(\lambda +rw\right)}_{\mathit{eq}}\right)$

$\alpha ={P}_{[0,1]}(\tilde{\alpha})$

The value $\alpha =0$ (or $\tilde{\alpha}\le 0$ ) corresponds to a sound material (adherent zone), and the value $\alpha =1$ (or $\tilde{\alpha}\ge 1$ ) to a fully cracked material (cracked zone). For loading conditions, that is if $\alpha =\phi \left({\left(\lambda +rw\right)}_{\mathit{eq}}\right)$ , the function $\varphi$ is defined by:

> $\varphi \left(\lambda +rw\right)=2{G}_{c}\left(1-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{r{w}_{c}}\right)\alpha \left(1-\frac{\alpha}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2r}{\langle {\lambda}_{n}+r{w}_{n}\rangle }_{-}^{2}$

An expression for the cohesive force ${t}_{c}=\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial w}$ which derives from this energy can then be obtained. Defining an equivalent cohesive force as ${t}_{c,\mathit{eq}}=\sqrt{{\langle {t}_{c,n}\rangle }_{+}^{2}+{t}_{c,\tau }^{2}}$ , the force/opening law can be expressed in terms of equivalent quantities as ${t}_{c,\mathit{eq}}=\left(1-{T}_{d}\right){\left(\lambda +rw\right)}_{\mathit{eq}}$ , where ${T}_{d}$ is the damage function which corresponds to linear softening:

${T}_{d}=\frac{\alpha}{\left(1-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{w}_{c}r}\right)\alpha +\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{w}_{c}r}}$

The vector relation between the cohesive force and the displacement jump is then expressed in terms of the normal component ${t}_{c,n}=\left(1-{T}_{d}\right){\langle {\lambda}_{n}+r{w}_{n}\rangle }_{+}+{\langle {\lambda}_{n}+r{w}_{n}\rangle }_{-}$ (the last term having been added in order to account for unilateral contact) and of the tangential component ${t}_{c,\tau }=\left(1-{T}_{d}\right)\left({\lambda}_{\tau}+r{w}_{\tau}\right)$ .

## 3 Variational formulations

Let us transform the strong form of the problem into a weak formulation, better suited to the finite elements. The field $u$ must belong to the set ${V}_{0}$ of kinematically admissible displacement fields:

${V}_{0}=\left\lbrace v\in {H}^{1},v\text{discontinu}à\text{travers}{\Gamma}_{c},v=0\text{sur}{\Gamma}_{u}\right\rbrace$

### 3.1 Formulation for the regularised cohesive law

We denote $H={H}^{-1/2}(\Gamma )$ for the cohesive laws. The weak formulation of the cohesive problem is written as follows:

Find $\left(u,{t}_{c}^{+},{t}_{c}^{-}\right)\in {V}_{0}\times H\times H$ such that:

${\int}_{\Omega}\sigma (u):\epsilon ({u}^{\text{*}})d\Omega ={\int}_{\Omega}f\cdot {u}^{\text{*}}d\Omega +{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{t}}t\cdot {u}^{\text{*}}\mathit{d\Gamma }+{\int}_{{\Gamma}^{+}}{t}_{c}^{+}\cdot {u}^{\text{*}+}{\mathit{d\Gamma }}^{+}+{\int}_{{\Gamma}^{-}}{t}_{c}^{-}\cdot {u}^{\text{*}-}{\mathit{d\Gamma }}^{-}\forall {u}^{\text{*}}\in {V}_{0}$

Since $⟦u⟧\left(x\right)={u}^{+}\left(x\right)-{u}^{-}\left(x\right)$ and ${t}_{c}={t}_{c}^{-}=-{t}_{c}^{+}$ , the weak formulation is written equivalently as follows:

Find $u\in {V}_{0}$ such that:

${\int}_{\Omega}\sigma (u):\epsilon ({u}^{\text{*}})d\Omega ={\int}_{\Omega}f\cdot {u}^{\text{*}}d\Omega +{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{t}}t\cdot {u}^{\text{*}}d\Gamma -{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{t}_{c}\cdot ⟦{u}^{\text{*}}⟧d{\Gamma}_{c}\forall {u}^{\text{*}}\in {V}_{0}$

The current implementation in fact includes a post-processing part, in order to assign a value to the unused Lagrange multipliers:

Find $\left(u,{\lambda}_{n},{\lambda}_{\tau}\right)\in {V}_{0}\times H\times H$ such that:

$\forall \left({u}^{\text{*}},{\lambda}_{n}^{\text{*}},{\lambda}_{\tau}^{\text{*}}\right)\in {V}_{0}\times H\times H$

| Equilibrium equation | $\begin{array}{c}{\int}_{\Omega}\sigma (u):\epsilon ({u}^{\text{*}})d\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}f\cdot {u}^{\text{*}}d\Omega -{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{t}}t\cdot {u}^{\text{*}}\mathit{d\Gamma }\\ +{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{t}_{c,n}\cdot {⟦{u}^{\text{*}}⟧}_{n}{\mathit{d\Gamma }}_{c}+{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}{t}_{c,\tau }\cdot {⟦{u}^{\text{*}}⟧}_{\tau}{\mathit{d\Gamma }}_{c}=0\end{array}$ |
| --- | --- |
| Post-processing, normal part | ${\int}_{\mathit{\Gamma c}}\left({\lambda}_{n}-{t}_{c,n}\cdot n\right){\lambda}_{n}^{\text{*}}{\mathit{d\Gamma }}_{c}=0$ |
| Post-processing, tangential part | ${\int}_{\mathit{\Gamma c}}\left({\lambda}_{\tau}-{t}_{c,\tau }\right){\lambda}_{\tau}^{\text{*}}{\mathit{d\Gamma }}_{c}=0$ |

Thus, the multipliers ${\lambda}_{n}$ and ${\lambda}_{\tau}$ do not enter the solution. They serve only to store the cohesive stresses in an explicit manner.

The normal displacement jump and the force deduced from it are plotted in the figures, for a loading ${u}_{0}=0.04\mathit{mm}$ , for meshes conforming or not to the cracked surface, and for penalisation parameters ${\alpha}_{0}={10}^{-2}$ and ${\alpha}_{0}={10}^{-6}$ . As we can see in the figures, the regularised cohesive laws present three difficulties:

1. if the penalisation is too weak, the displacement jump along the inclusion is wrong: one observes an opening which is not physical (mark 1 in the fig.);
2. if, on the contrary, the penalisation is too high, *spurious oscillations* of the cohesive stress appear in the adherent zone (mark 2 in the fig.). This problem has been widely reported in the literature (see for example [bib3]). Originally noticed in the context of contact for Code_Aster, and referred to as the “LBB condition”, it is treated in the documentation [R5.03.54](doc:r5.03.54), §6. In a word, it comes from the fact that the discretisation space of the cohesive forces becomes too rich in comparison with that of the displacement, when the stiffness of the law becomes such that one approaches a Dirichlet condition;
3. *a poor evaluation of the operating regime* (adherent or dissipative) can occur as a consequence of these oscillations (mark 3 in the fig.).

To conclude, the penalised laws fail to give a faithful representation of the adherent zones, since a penalisation parameter ${\alpha}_{0}^{-1}$ that is too small implies a non-physical opening of the adherent zone, which leads to a wrong solution, whereas a parameter ${\alpha}_{0}^{-1}$ that is too large generates spurious oscillations because of a stability problem, as soon as the penalisation parameter begins to become large enough to describe the adhesion properly.

Considering the pull-out test of the figure (this test is numerical; it does not correspond to any physical reality), it is possible to highlight the oscillation phenomenon even more clearly.

### 3.3 Reduced space for the discretisation of the interface stress

For a detailed description of the discretisation of the contact unknowns, the reader may refer to the documentation [R5.03.54](doc:r5.03.54), §5. In short, the *initial components* of the multiplier are defined on the vertex nodes $K$ of the intersected parent elements (see fig.). The implementation of such contact elements is detailed in [R5.03.54](doc:r5.03.54), §4. Equality relations are then imposed between some of these *initial components* in order to arrive at a smaller number ${N}_{\lambda}$ of effectively independent *degrees of freedom*. These relations are carried by certain intersected edges $V$ , called vital edges: a truly independent degree of freedom $I$ is shared by a group of nodes of $K$ (see fig.), which produces an extended contact shape function ${\psi}_{I}:=\sum_{i\in I}{N}_{i}$ (see fig.). The algorithm for selecting such vital edges, and therefore for constructing the reduced space, is detailed in the documentation [R5.03.54](doc:r5.03.54), §6. The multiplier field is then obtained by interpolation on the parent elements, and the discrete multiplier is the trace of this field on the interface:

${M}_{h}:=\left\lbrace \sum_{I}{\mu}_{I}{\psi}_{I}{\text{|}}_{\Gamma},{\mu}_{I}\in {ℝ}^{d}\right\rbrace$

### 3.4 Formulation for a mixed cohesive law for quadratic elements

In contrast to the previous formulation, the treatment of such a law will require a genuine multi-field formulation, in the sense that a dual vector field $\lambda$ will actually enter the formulation, instead of being a post-processing device as in § 3.1 . This formulation follows an energy argument, explained in detail in the documentation [R3.06.13](doc:r3.06.13). Let us summarise its main points:

One writes that the opening of the crack costs an energy proportional to the surface to be opened, that is:

${E}_{\text{fr}}(\delta )=\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\Pi (\delta )\text{dS}$

where $\Pi (\delta )$ is the cohesive-energy density. For the law CZM_OUV_MIX, we have for example $\Pi (\delta )={\int}_{0}^{{\delta}_{n}}{t}_{c,n}(\delta ')d\delta '$ .

The discontinuity field $\delta$ appearing in the previous expressions is then defined as a field in its own right, introduced into the formulation as a new unknown. The total energy is then written:

$E(u,\delta )=\underset{\Omega \mathrm{\setminus }\Gamma }{\int}\Phi (\epsilon (u))d\Omega -{W}_{\mathit{ext}}(u)+\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\Pi (\delta )\mathit{d\Gamma }$

The solution of the problem then consists in the minimisation of this total energy under the constraint that $\delta$ correspond to the displacement jump. One seeks:

$\underset{\begin{array}{c}u,\delta \\ \left[\left[u\right]\right]=\delta \end{array}}{\min}E\left(u,\delta \right)$

In order to solve this, we introduce the Lagrangian associated with the problem, to which we add an augmentation term whose usefulness will appear later:

${L}_{r}\left(u,\delta ,\lambda \right)\underset{\mathit{déf.}}{=}E\left(u,\delta \right)+\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\lambda \cdot \left(⟦u⟧-\delta \right)\mathit{d\Gamma }+\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\frac{r}{2}{\left(⟦u⟧-\delta \right)}^{2}\mathit{d\Gamma }$

We can then write the first optimality condition of this Lagrangian:

$\forall {\delta}^{\text{*}}\underset{\Gamma}{\int}\left[{t}_{c}-\lambda +r\left(\delta -⟦u⟧\right)\right]\cdot {\delta}^{\text{*}}=0\text{avec}{t}_{c}\in \partial \Pi \left(\delta \right)$

This equation involves the cohesive stress ${t}_{c}$ . Now, the only expression available for ${t}_{c}$ is a local constitutive law. This first equation must therefore, in order to make sense, be discretised in a way which makes it possible to return to a local expression. This is possible if $\delta$ is discretised by collocation at the Gauss points of the interface, of coordinates ${x}_{g}$ .

Indeed, with such a discretisation, the solution of the first optimality condition amounts to satisfying the cohesive law at each collocation point:

${t}_{c}\left({\delta}_{g},{\alpha}_{g}\right)={\lambda}_{g}+r\left(⟦{u}_{g}⟧-{\delta}_{g}\right)$

where we have denoted ${\lambda}_{g}=\lambda ({x}_{g})$ , for example, the values of a field at the Gauss points, and where ${t}_{c}({\delta}_{g},{\alpha}_{g})$ follows the law. The graphical translation of this constitutive law is the following: the solution corresponds to the intersection of the linear function $\delta \to {\lambda}_{g}+r⟦{u}_{g}⟧-r\delta$ (with a negative slope given by the penalty coefficient $r$ ) with the graph ${t}_{c}(\delta ,\alpha )$ . We then see that for $r$ large enough, the solution is unique, whence the advantage of having augmented the Lagrangian.

The field $\delta$ is therefore written locally as a function of $\lambda +r⟦u⟧$ , which we shall call the augmented multiplier and denote $p$ . Consequently, it disappears from the unknown fields of the problem. The formulation is then given by the two remaining optimality conditions of the Lagrangian:

Find $(u,\lambda )\in {V}_{0}\times H$ such that:

$\forall ({u}^{\text{*}},{\lambda}^{\text{*}})\in {V}_{0}\times H$

| Equilibrium equation | $\begin{array}{c}{\int}_{\Omega}\sigma (u):\epsilon ({u}^{\text{*}})d\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}f\cdot {u}^{\text{*}}d\Omega -{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{t}}t\cdot {u}^{\text{*}}d\Gamma \\ +{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\left[\lambda +r\left(⟦u⟧-\delta \left(p\right)\right)\right]\cdot ⟦{u}^{\text{*}}⟧d\Gamma =0\text{avec}p=\lambda +r⟦u⟧\end{array}$ |
| --- | --- |
| Interface law | ${\int}_{{\Gamma}_{c}}\left[⟦u⟧-\delta (p)\right]\cdot {\lambda}^{\text{*}}d\Gamma =0$ |

As regards the discretisation of these two unknown fields, a simple discretisation respecting the inf-sup stability condition, and consistent with the discretisation of $\delta$ by collocation, consists in discretising the displacement with P2 elements and the multiplier in a P1 manner on a reduced space adapted to X-FEM, as detailed in § 3.3 or in [R5.03.54](doc:r5.03.54), §6.

### 3.5 Formulation for a mixed cohesive law for linear elements

As demonstrated previously, the quantities at the interface must be defined on a reduced space ${M}_{h}$ in order to prevent spurious oscillations of numerical origin in the adherent phases. This applies to the cohesive stresses $\lambda$ and ${t}_{c}$ . In fact, ${t}_{c}$ depends on $\lambda +rw$ where $w$ is the displacement jump. As a consequence of this augmentation, which is present in particular during the adherent phases, $w$ must itself also be written on the consistent space ${M}_{h}$ .

**Note:**

*Another way of convincing oneself of this consists in considering a problem of pure adhesion, the adhesion being described by a regularised law. This problem does indeed describe the behaviour of the augmentation term during the adherent phases. In order to avoid oscillations, we have seen that a penalised law must be written properly on the reduced space, so that the system to be solved is:*     $\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{K}_{u}^{1}& 0& 0\\ 0& {K}_{u}^{2}& {A}^{T}\\ 0& A& \frac{1}{r}C\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\lbrace a\rbrace \\ \lbrace b\rbrace \\ \lbrace \lambda \rbrace \end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\lbrace {L}_{\text{méca}}^{1}\rbrace \\ \lbrace {L}_{\text{méca}}^{2}\rbrace \\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace$

*where:*

- *we group in* $\lbrace {L}_{\text{méca}}\rbrace$ *and* $[{K}_{u}]$ *the discretisation of all the terms which are not interface terms;*
- ${C}_{IK}={\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{I}{\psi}_{K}d\Gamma$ *;*
- ${A}_{Ij}={\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{I}{\varphi}_{j}d\Gamma$ *;*
- $\lbrace a\rbrace$ *is the vector grouping the classical displacement degrees of freedom;*
- $\lbrace b\rbrace$ *that of the enriched degrees of freedom;*
- $\lbrace \lambda \rbrace$ *groups the degrees of freedom of the multiplier at the interface, a discretisation which is carried out on the reduced space* ${M}_{h}$ *;*
- $r$ *is the penalisation parameter.*

*In a consistent penalised formulation, we can interpret the interface stress as proportional to a consistent jump* $\lbrace w\rbrace$ *:* $\lbrace \lambda \rbrace =r\lbrace w\rbrace$ *. With the previous matrix expression, we can then identify* $\lbrace w\rbrace ={[C]}^{-1}[A]\lbrace b\rbrace$ *.*

*We could then condense the previous matrix system in order to eliminate* $\lbrace \lambda \rbrace$ *:*

$[{K}_{u}^{2}]\lbrace b\rbrace +{[A]}^{T}\lbrace \lambda \rbrace =[{K}_{u}^{2}]\lbrace b\rbrace +{[A]}^{T}\left(r{[C]}^{-1}[A]\lbrace b\rbrace \right)$

We then see that the solution would require the assembly and the computation of a matrix ${[A]}^{T}{[C]}^{-1}[A]$ .

*Such a matrix product is not feasible in an elementary computation, all the more so given the non-local character of* ${M}_{h}$ *. Such a global operation therefore runs counter to the software architecture of Code_Aster, and must be ruled out as far as possible.*

In order to remedy the problem, $w$ is introduced as a new unknown of the problem, which is not discretised as $⟦u⟧$ but is a projection of it onto a reduced space ${M}_{h}$ . Decomposed with the help of this field, which is now available in the formulation, the previous stabilisation term can be determined by assembling an elementary computation. The total energy of the problem is written:

$E(u,\lambda ,w)=\frac{1}{2}{\int}_{\Omega}ϵ(u):C:ϵ(u)d\Omega -{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{g}}g\cdot ud{\Gamma}_{g}+{\int}_{\Gamma}\Pi \left(w,\lambda \right)d\Gamma$

The solution of the continuous problem consists in a minimisation under equality constraints $\left(u,w,\lambda \right)=\underset{{w}^{\text{*}}=⟦{u}^{\text{*}}⟧}{\mathit{argmin}}E\left({u}^{\text{*}},{\lambda}^{\text{*}},{w}^{\text{*}}\right)$ . We can write the associated Lagrangian as:

$L(u,w,\lambda ,\mu )=\frac{1}{2}{\int}_{\Omega}ϵ(u):C:ϵ(u)d\Omega -{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{g}}g\cdot ud{\Gamma}_{g}+{\int}_{\Gamma}\Pi (w,\lambda )d\Gamma +{\int}_{\Gamma}\mu \cdot \left(⟦u⟧-w\right)d\Gamma$

Writing the optimality conditions of this Lagrangian leads to the following variational formulation:

| Equilibrium equation | $\forall {u}^{\text{*}}\in {V}_{h},{\int}_{\Omega}\sigma (u):ϵ({u}^{\text{*}})d\Omega -{\int}_{{\Gamma}_{g}}g\cdot {u}^{\text{*}}d{\Gamma}_{g}+{\int}_{\Gamma}\mu \cdot ⟦{u}^{\text{*}}⟧d\Gamma =0$ |
| --- | --- |
| Projection of the displacement jump | $\forall {\mu}^{\text{*}}\in {M}_{h},{\int}_{\Gamma}\left(⟦u⟧-w\right)\cdot {\mu}^{\text{*}}d\Gamma =0$ |
| Expression of the cohesive force | $\forall {w}^{\text{*}}\in {M}_{h},-{\int}_{\Gamma}\left[\mu -{t}_{c}(\lambda +rw)\right]\cdot {w}^{\text{*}}d\Gamma =0$ |
| Interface law | $\forall {\lambda}^{\text{*}}\in {M}_{h},-{\int}_{\Gamma}\frac{\left[\lambda -{t}_{c}(\lambda +rw)\right]}{r}\cdot {\lambda}^{\text{*}}d\Gamma =0$ |

## 4 Solution strategy

### 4.1 Newton–Raphson method

The solution strategy is nothing other than a simple Newton method. Unlike the case of frictional contact [R5.03.52](doc:r5.03.52) or [R5.03.54](doc:r5.03.54), there are neither fixed-point loops nor sign fields. The only operation to be carried out in addition to the Newton iterations is therefore the updating of the internal variable.

- For a time step:

  - Newton iterations. Computation of the tangent matrix and of the right-hand side
  - End of the Newton iterations

Updating of the internal variable $\alpha$

One might legitimately wonder why the internal variable is not updated during the Newton iterations. In fact, since it is a parameter measuring irreversibility, and determined by a maximum over time, it must be updated only at each converged time step. Indeed, otherwise, if this parameter exceeds its equilibrium value during a Newton iteration, the Newton algorithm will then be unable to decrease it in order to find the equilibrium value.

In the one-dimensional case, the Newton method is an iterative process making it possible to approach the zeros of a continuous and differentiable function. One reduces the problem to the solution of $F(x)=0$ . One constructs a sequence of points ${x}^{k}$ by carrying out a Taylor expansion of $F$ in the neighbourhood of ${x}^{k}$ , which gives, to first order:

$F({x}^{k+1})\approx F({x}^{k})+{F}^{'}({x}^{k})({x}^{k+1}-{x}^{k})$

Denoting by ${\mathrm{\delta x}}^{k}={x}^{k+1}-{x}^{k}$ the increment between two successive iterations, the linearised equation at iteration $k+1$ is then the following:

${F}^{'}({x}^{k}){\mathrm{\delta x}}^{k}=-F({x}^{k})$

In the case of the finite element method, ${F}^{'}({x}^{k})$ corresponds to the tangent matrix, which can be computed at each iteration if necessary, ${\mathrm{\delta x}}^{k}$ is the vector of the increments of the nodal unknowns, and $F({x}^{k})$ is the right-hand side. One notes that ${F}^{'}({x}^{k})$ and $F({x}^{k})$ involve only quantities from iteration $k$ , which are therefore known quantities.

### 4.2 Differentiation of the cohesive law

To give an example of differentiation of the cohesive law coupling the modes, let us consider the regularised cohesive law CZM_EXP_REG. The differential of ${t}_{c}(\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛})$ will be $\frac{\partial {t}_{c}}{\partial \mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}\cdot \mathrm{〚}\delta u\mathrm{〛}$ with:

Now $\frac{\partial {t}_{c}}{\partial \mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}=H({\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\text{èq}}-\alpha )\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{\mathit{lin}}}{\partial \mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}+(1-H({\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\text{èq}}-\alpha ))\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{\mathit{dis}}}{\partial \mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}+\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{\mathit{pen}}}{\partial \mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}$

We reuse the expressions of these three partial derivatives, which are given in [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11), with $\delta =⟦u⟧$ . In the expression for ${\sigma}_{\mathit{dis}}$ , one must write $\alpha ={\mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}_{\mathit{èq}}$ , which thus becomes a variable to be taken into account in the differentiation. In practice, in the code, four cases are distinguished for the sake of readability:

  - ${⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}\ge \alpha \mathrm{et}⟦{u}_{n}⟧\ge 0$ (dissipative, non-contacting). We then have: $\frac{\partial {t}_{c}}{\partial ⟦u⟧}={\sigma}_{c}\exp\left(-\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}{⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}\right)\left(\frac{\text{Id}}{{⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}}-\frac{⟦u⟧}{{⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}}\otimes \frac{⟦u⟧}{{⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}}\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}+\frac{1}{{⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}}\right)\right)$
- ${⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}<\alpha \mathrm{et}⟦{u}_{n}⟧<0$ (elastic, contacting). With $({\tau}_{1},{\tau}_{2})$ a basis of the tangent plane, we have: $\frac{\partial {t}_{c}}{\partial ⟦u⟧}=\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{\mathit{lin}}(⟦{u}_{\tau}⟧)}{\partial ⟦u⟧}+\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{\mathit{pen}}(⟦{u}_{n}⟧)}{\partial ⟦u⟧}=Cn\otimes n+\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{\alpha}\exp(\frac{-{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}\alpha )\left({\tau}_{1}\otimes {\tau}_{1}+{\tau}_{2}\otimes {\tau}_{2}\right)$
- ${⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}\ge \alpha \mathrm{et}⟦{u}_{n}⟧<0$ (dissipative, contacting). By a similar argument, replacing ${\sigma}_{\mathit{lin}}$ by ${\sigma}_{\mathit{dis}}$ , we obtain: $\frac{\partial {t}_{c}}{\partial ⟦u⟧}=Cn\otimes n+\exp\left(\frac{-{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}{⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}\right)\left[\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}}\left({\tau}_{1}\otimes {\tau}_{1}+{\tau}_{2}\otimes {\tau}_{2}\right)-\left(\frac{{\sigma}_{c}^{2}}{{G}_{c}}+\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{{⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}}\right)\frac{{⟦u⟧}_{\tau}\otimes {⟦u⟧}_{\tau}}{{{⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}}^{2}}\right]$
- ${⟦u⟧}_{\mathit{èq}}<\alpha \mathrm{et}⟦{u}_{n}⟧\ge 0$ (elastic, non-contacting). $\frac{\partial {t}_{c}}{\partial ⟦u⟧}=\frac{{\sigma}_{c}}{\alpha}\exp(\frac{-{\sigma}_{c}}{{G}_{c}}\alpha )\text{Id}$

## 5 Matrix expression of the problem

### 5.1 Regularised cohesive law

#### 5.1.1 Matrix writing of the linearised problem

Taking up the notation of [R5.03.54](doc:r5.03.54), and considering the unified writing adopted for the frictional-contact laws and the regularised cohesive laws, the linearised system as it is solved at Newton iteration $k+1$ can be put in matrix form:

| Equilibrium equation | $\begin{array}{c}\left\lbrace {u}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace \left[{K}_{\text{méca}}\right]\left(\mathit{\delta u}\right)+\left\lbrace {u}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace \left[{K}_{\mathit{coh}}^{u}\right]\left(\mathit{\delta u}\right)\\ =\left\lbrace {u}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace \left({L}_{\text{méca}}^{1}\right)+\left\lbrace {u}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace \left({L}_{\text{coh}}^{1}\right)\\ \end{array}$ |
| --- | --- |
| Post-processing of the normal cohesive stress | $\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{n}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace \left[C\right]\left({\text{δλ}}_{n}\right)=\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{n}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace \left({L}_{\text{post}}^{1}\right)+\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{n}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace \left({L}_{\text{coh}}^{2}\right)$ |
| Post-processing of the tangential cohesive stress | $\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{\tau}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace \left[{F}_{r}\right]\left({\mathit{\delta \lambda }}_{\tau}\right)=\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{\tau}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace \left({L}_{\text{post}}^{2}\right)+\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{\tau}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace \left({L}_{\text{coh}}^{3}\right)$ |

where the column vectors are denoted $(x)$ and the row vectors $\left\lbrace x\right\rbrace ={(x)}^{T}$ . This system can be put in the following matrix form:

$\left[\begin{array}{c}{{\rm K}}_{\text{méca}}+{K}_{\text{coh}}^{u}\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\begin{array}{c}0\\ C\\ 0\end{array}\begin{array}{c}0\\ 0\\ F\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}{c}\mathit{\delta u}\\ {\text{δλ}}_{n}\\ {\mathit{\delta \lambda }}_{\tau}\end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}{c}{L}_{\text{méca}}^{1}+{L}_{\text{coh}}^{1}\\ {L}_{\text{post}}^{1}+{L}_{\text{coh}}^{2}\\ {L}_{\text{post}}^{2}+{L}_{\text{coh}}^{3}\end{array}\right)$

The unknown is the increment with respect to the previous Newton iteration. The reference to the number of the Newton iteration has deliberately been omitted.

${K}_{\text{méca}}$ is the mechanical stiffness matrix defined in paragraph [§3.2] of [R7.02.12](doc:r7.02.12).

${K}_{\mathit{coh}}^{u}$ is the stiffness matrix due to the cohesive forces.

$C$ is the post-processing matrix for the normal direction.

$F$ is the post-processing matrix for the tangential direction(s).

${L}_{\text{méca}}^{1}$ is the right-hand side representing the internal forces and the loading increments.

${L}_{\text{post}}^{1}$ and ${L}_{\text{post}}^{2}$ are the post-processing right-hand sides.

${L}_{\text{coh}}^{1}$ , ${L}_{\text{coh}}^{2}$ and ${L}_{\text{coh}}^{3}$ are the right-hand sides due to the cohesive forces.

**Note:**

*It is recalled that the system solved by Code_Aster is not of the type* $[K][U]=[F]$ *but of the type* $[K][U]+[F]=0$ *. There is therefore a minus sign between the right-hand sides given in this document and those coded in the Fortran files.*

#### 5.1.2 Expression of the elementary matrices and vectors

The matrix $C$ required for the post-processing has the expression (the indices $n$ on $\lambda$ , indicating the normal component of the cohesive stress, are omitted):

> ${\left\lbrace {\lambda}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace }_{i}{\left[C\right]}_{ij}{(\text{δλ})}_{j}={\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{i}{\lambda}_{i}^{\text{*}}{\psi}_{j}{\text{δλ}}_{j}\mathit{d\Gamma }$

The vector ${L}_{\text{post}}^{1}$ required for the post-processing has the expression (indices $n$ omitted on $\lambda$ ):

${\left\lbrace {\lambda}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace }_{i}{\left({L}_{\text{post}}^{1}\right)}_{i}={\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{i}{\lambda}_{i}^{\text{*}}{\lambda}^{k-1}\mathit{d\Gamma }$

> The matrix $F$ required for the post-processing has the expression:
> ${\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{\tau}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace }_{i}{\left[F\right]}_{ij}{\left(\delta {\lambda}_{\tau}\right)}_{j}={\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{i}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{\tau ,i}^{1\text{*}}& {\lambda}_{\tau ,i}^{2\text{*}}\end{array}\right\rbrace \cdot \left[\begin{array}{cc}{\tau}_{i}^{1}{\tau}_{j}^{1}& {\tau}_{i}^{1}{\tau}_{j}^{2}\\ {\tau}_{i}^{2}{\tau}_{j}^{1}& {\tau}_{i}^{2}{\tau}_{j}^{2}\end{array}\right]\cdot {\psi}_{j}\left(\begin{array}{c}{\lambda}_{\tau ,j}^{1}\\ {\lambda}_{\tau ,j}^{2}\end{array}\right)\mathit{d\Gamma }$
> The vector ${L}_{\text{post}}^{2}$ required for the post-processing has the expression:

$\begin{array}{c}{\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{\tau}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace }_{i}{\left({L}_{\text{post}}^{2}\right)}_{i}={\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{i}\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{\tau ,i}^{1\text{*}}{\tau}_{i}^{1}+{\lambda}_{\tau ,i}^{2\text{*}}{\tau}_{i}^{2}\right\rbrace {\lambda}_{\tau}^{k-1}\mathit{d\Gamma }\end{array}$

Let us now seek to write ${K}_{\mathit{coh}}^{u}$ . Let $X$ and $Y$ be two directions of the fixed basis, with unit vectors ${e}_{X}$ and ${e}_{Y}$ . Let us introduce the tangent matrix of the cohesive law in the fixed basis ${K}^{\mathit{gl}}$ , with coefficients ${K}_{XY}^{\mathit{gl}}={e}_{X}\cdot \frac{\partial {t}_{c}}{\partial ⟦u⟧}\cdot {e}_{Y}$ . With the expression for $\frac{\partial {t}_{c}}{\partial \mathrm{〚}u\mathrm{〛}}$ given in [§ 4.2 ], we have the tangent matrix of the cohesive law ${K}^{\mathit{loc}}$ in the local basis (see doc. [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11)). We then obtain ${K}^{\mathit{gl}}$ by ${K}^{\mathit{gl}}={Q}^{T}\cdot {K}_{\mathit{loc}}\cdot Q$ , where $Q$ is an orthonormal change-of-basis matrix defined by:

$Q=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{n}_{X}& {n}_{Y}& {n}_{Z}\\ {\tau}_{X}^{1}& {\tau}_{Y}^{1}& {\tau}_{Z}^{1}\\ {\tau}_{X}^{2}& {\tau}_{Y}^{2}& {\tau}_{Z}^{2}\end{array}\right]$

Let $i$ and $j$ be two enriched nodes. Let $\Gamma$ be the intersection of the supports of $i$ and $j$ . The matrix $[{K}_{\mathit{coh}}^{u}]$ is then given by:

${\lbrace {u}^{\text{*}}\rbrace }_{i}{[{K}_{\mathit{coh}}^{u}]}_{ij}{\lbrace \delta u\rbrace }_{j}={\int}_{\Gamma}2{\varphi}_{i}{b}_{i}^{\text{*}}\cdot {K}^{\mathit{gl}}\cdot {b}_{j}2{\varphi}_{j}d\Gamma$

The cohesive right-hand sides have the following expressions:

${\lbrace {u}_{i}\rbrace }^{\text{*}}{\left({L}_{\mathit{coh}}^{1}\right)}_{i}=-{\int}_{\Gamma}2{\varphi}_{i}{b}_{i}^{\text{*}}\cdot {t}_{c}^{k-1}d\Gamma$

where ${t}_{c}$ can be expressed in the global basis by ${t}_{c}^{\mathit{glo}}={Q}^{T}\cdot {t}_{c}^{\mathit{loc}}$ , and:

${\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{n}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace }_{i}{\left({L}_{\text{coh}}^{2}\right)}_{i}=-{\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{i}{\lambda}_{n,i}^{\text{*}}\left({t}_{c,n}^{k-1}\right)\mathit{d\Gamma }$

$\begin{array}{c}{\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{\tau}^{\text{*}}\right\rbrace }_{i}{\left({L}_{\text{coh}}^{3}\right)}_{i}=-{\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{i}\left\lbrace {\lambda}_{\tau ,i}^{1\text{*}}{\tau}_{i}^{1}+{\lambda}_{\tau ,i}^{2\text{*}}{\tau}_{i}^{2}\right\rbrace \cdot {t}_{c,\tau }^{k-1}\mathit{d\Gamma }\end{array}$

where $k-1$ represents the index of the previous Newton iteration.

### 5.2 Mixed cohesive law for quadratic elements

#### 5.2.1 Matrix writing of the problem with a mixed cohesive law

The matrix system as it is solved at Newton iteration $k+1$ can be put in the following matrix form:

$\left[\begin{array}{c}{{\rm K}}_{\text{méca}}+{{\rm A}}_{u}\\ {\rm A}\end{array}\begin{array}{c}{{\rm A}}^{T}\\ C\end{array}\right](\begin{array}{c}\mathit{\delta u}\\ \text{δλ}\end{array})=(\begin{array}{c}{L}_{\text{méca}}^{1}+{L}_{\text{coh}}^{1}\\ {L}_{\text{coh}}^{2}\end{array})$

##### 5.2.1.1 Expression of the elementary cohesive matrices:

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two directions of the fixed basis, with unit vectors ${e}_{X}$ and ${e}_{Y}$ . We introduce, as previously, the tangent matrix of the cohesive law in the fixed basis ${K}^{\mathit{gl}}$ , with coefficients ${K}_{\mathit{XY}}^{\mathit{gl}}={e}_{X}\cdot \frac{\partial \delta }{\partial p}\cdot {e}_{Y}$ . We have, from the cohesive constitutive law, the tangent matrix ${K}_{\mathit{loc}}$ in the local basis (see doc. [R7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11)). We then obtain ${K}_{\mathit{gl}}$ by ${K}^{\mathit{gl}}={Q}^{T}\cdot {K}^{\mathit{loc}}\cdot Q$ , where $Q$ is an orthonormal change-of-basis matrix defined by:

$Q=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{n}_{X}& {n}_{Y}& {n}_{Z}\\ {\tau}_{X}^{1}& {\tau}_{Y}^{1}& {\tau}_{Z}^{1}\\ {\tau}_{X}^{2}& {\tau}_{Y}^{2}& {\tau}_{Z}^{2}\end{array}\right]$

Having introduced this notation, we have:

${\lbrace {u}^{\text{*}}\rbrace }_{i}{[{A}_{u}]}_{ij}{\lbrace \delta u\rbrace }_{j}={\int}_{\Gamma}2{\varphi}_{i}{b}_{i}^{\text{*}}r\left(1-r{K}^{\mathit{gl}}\right)2{\varphi}_{j}{b}_{j}d\Gamma$

Furthermore, we choose to discretise $\lambda$ in the local basis. The coefficient $\left(1,1\right)$ of the matrix $\lbrace {\lambda}_{n}^{\text{*}}\rbrace {[{A}_{u}]}_{ij}{\lbrace \delta {u}_{X}\rbrace }_{j}$ is then ${\int}_{\Gamma}{\varphi}_{i}{\lambda}_{i}^{\text{*}}n\cdot \left(1-r\frac{\partial \delta }{\partial p}\right)\cdot {e}_{X}2{\varphi}_{j}{b}_{j}d\Gamma$ . For the coefficients $\left(2,1\right)$ and $\left(3,1\right)$ , the formula is the same, replacing $n$ by ${\tau}_{1}$ and ${\tau}_{2}$ , respectively. Exploiting the notation introduced in the previous paragraph, we deduce:

${\lbrace {\lambda}^{\text{*}}\rbrace }_{i}{[A]}_{ij}{\lbrace \delta u\rbrace }_{j}={\int}_{\Gamma}{\varphi}_{i}{\lambda}_{i}^{\text{*}}\left(1-r{K}^{\mathit{loc}}\right)\cdot Q2{\varphi}_{j}{b}_{j}d\Gamma$

As for the matrix $[C]$ , it is written simply:

${\lbrace {\lambda}^{\text{*}}\rbrace }_{i}{[C]}_{ij}{\lbrace \delta \lambda \rbrace }_{j}=-{\int}_{\Gamma}{\varphi}_{i}{\lambda}_{i}^{\text{*}}{K}^{\mathit{loc}}{\varphi}_{j}{\lambda}_{j}d\Gamma$

##### 5.2.1.2 Expression of the elementary cohesive vectors:

The coefficient along $X$ of ${\left({L}_{\mathit{coh}}^{1}\right)}_{i}$ has the expression ${\int}_{\Gamma}{\varphi}_{i}\left(-\lambda \cdot {e}_{X}-r\left(⟦u⟧-\delta \right)\cdot {e}_{X}\right)d\Gamma$ . With the notation introduced in the previous part, we deduce:

${\lbrace {u}_{i}\rbrace }^{\text{*}}{\left({L}_{\mathit{coh}}^{1}\right)}_{i}={\int}_{\Gamma}2{\varphi}_{i}{b}_{i}^{\text{*}}\cdot \left(-{Q}^{T}\cdot \lambda -r\left(⟦u⟧-{Q}^{T}\cdot \delta \right)\right)d\Gamma$

where $\delta$ and $\lambda$ are given in the local basis, and $⟦u⟧$ in the fixed basis.

The coefficient $1$ of ${\left({L}_{\mathit{coh}}^{2}\right)}_{i}$ is written ${\int}_{\Gamma}\left(-⟦u⟧\cdot n+\delta \cdot n\right)d\Gamma$ . For the coefficients $2$ and $3$ , the formula is the same, replacing $n$ by ${\tau}_{1}$ and ${\tau}_{2}$ , respectively. We deduce:

${\lbrace \lambda \rbrace }_{i}^{\text{*}}{\left({L}_{\mathit{coh}}^{2}\right)}_{i}={\int}_{\Gamma}{\lambda}_{i}^{\text{*}}\cdot \left(-Q⟦u⟧+\delta \right)d\Gamma$

where $\delta$ is given in the local basis, and $⟦u⟧$ in the fixed basis.

### 5.3 Mixed cohesive law for linear elements

The components of the unknowns $u$ and $\mu$ are defined in a fixed basis $\left({e}_{X},{e}_{Y},{e}_{Z}\right)$ , whereas the components of $w$ and $\lambda$ are defined in the local basis $\left(n,{\tau}_{1},{\tau}_{2}\right)$ to the cracked surface $\Gamma$ at each point $x\in \Gamma$ , so that *:*

> $w(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\lambda}}{\psi}_{I}(x)\left({w}_{I,n}n(x)+{w}_{I,\tau 1}{\tau}_{1}(x)+{w}_{I,\tau 2}{\tau}_{2}(x)\right)$

An analogous definition holds for $\lambda$ . For a degree of freedom $I$ of the reduced space (see § 3.3 ), it is possible to determine the components ${t}_{c,n}^{I},{t}_{t,\tau 1}^{I},{t}_{c,\tau 2}^{I}$ of the cohesive force from $\left({w}_{I,n},{w}_{I,\tau 1},{w}_{I,\tau 2}\right)$ , $\left({\lambda}_{I,n},{\lambda}_{I,\tau 1},{\lambda}_{I,\tau 2}\right)$ and from the cohesive law of § 2.2.3 . These components are not intended to be associated with a particular direction $I$ of the degree of freedom, but intended to be related in the weak sense to the global stress $\mu$ written in the fixed basis.

The matrix system can be put in the following form:

> $\left[\begin{array}{cccc}{K}^{\mathit{uu}}& {\left({K}^{\mu u}\right)}^{T}& 0& 0\\ {K}^{\mu u}& 0& {\left(-{K}^{w\mu }\right)}^{T}& 0\\ 0& {-K}^{w\mu }& {D}^{\mathit{ww}}& {\left({D}^{\lambda w}\right)}^{T}\\ 0& 0& {D}^{\lambda w}& {D}^{\lambda \lambda }\end{array}\right]\left(\begin{array}{c}\delta u\\ \delta \mu \\ \delta w\\ \delta \lambda \end{array}\right)=\left(\begin{array}{c}{L}_{\text{méca}}+{L}_{\mu}^{1}\\ {L}_{u}-{L}_{w}\\ -{L}_{\mu}^{2}+{L}_{\mathit{coh}}^{1}\\ -{L}_{\lambda}+{L}_{\mathit{coh}}^{2}\end{array}\right)$

where:

- ${K}^{uu}$ is the volumetric stiffness matrix;
- ${K}^{\mu u}$ and ${K}^{w\mu }$ are matrices discretising the “mortar” operators, the latter also handling the change of basis;
- the matrices $D$ are all block-diagonal: for $I$ and $J$ two distinct Lagrange degrees of freedom, they satisfy ${D}_{\mathit{IJ}}=0$ .

Let us give the expressions of the matrices and right-hand sides which do not depend on the cohesive law:

${\lbrace u\rbrace }_{i}^{\text{*}}{\left[{K}^{\mu u}\right]}_{iJ}{(\delta \mu )}_{J}={b}_{i}^{\text{*}}\cdot (\delta {\mu}_{J}){\int}_{\Gamma}2{\psi}_{J}{\varphi}_{i}d\Gamma$

Introducing the orthonormal change-of-basis matrix $Q$ defined as previously, we have:

${\lbrace w\rbrace }_{I}^{\text{*}}{\left[{K}^{w\mu }\right]}_{IJ}{(\delta \mu )}_{J}={w}_{I}^{\text{*}}\cdot \left({\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{I}{\psi}_{J}Qd\Gamma \right)\cdot (\delta {\mu}_{J})$

${\lbrace u\rbrace }_{i}^{\text{*}}{\left({L}_{\mu}^{1}\right)}_{i}=-{b}_{i}^{\text{*}}\cdot {\int}_{\Gamma}2{\varphi}_{i}\mu d\Gamma$

${\lbrace \mu \rbrace }_{I}^{\text{*}}{\left({L}_{u}\right)}_{I}=-{\mu}_{I}^{\text{*}}\cdot {\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{I}⟦u⟧d\Gamma$

${\lbrace \mu \rbrace }_{I}^{\text{*}}{\left({L}_{w}\right)}_{I}=-{\mu}_{I}^{\text{*}}\cdot {\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{I}{Q}^{T}\cdot wd\Gamma$

${\lbrace w\rbrace }_{I}^{\text{*}}{\left({L}_{\mu}^{2}\right)}_{I}={w}_{I}^{\text{*}}\cdot {\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{I}Q\cdot \mu d\Gamma$

Let us now detail the discretisation of the interface quantities. The matrices are block-diagonal, and have the expressions:

${\lbrace w\rbrace }_{I}^{\text{*}}{\left[{D}^{ww}\right]}_{II}{(\delta w)}_{I}={w}_{I}^{\text{*}}(\delta {w}_{I})r\frac{\partial {t}_{c}}{\partial (\lambda +rw)}({\lambda}_{I}+r{w}_{I}){\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{I}d\Gamma$ ${\lbrace \lambda \rbrace }_{I}^{\text{*}}{\left[{D}^{\lambda w}\right]}_{II}{(\delta w)}_{I}={\lambda}_{I}^{\text{*}}(\delta {w}_{I})\frac{\partial {t}_{c}}{\partial (\lambda +rw)}({\lambda}_{I}+r{w}_{I}){\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{I}d\Gamma$

${\lbrace \lambda \rbrace }_{I}^{\text{*}}{\left[{D}^{\lambda \lambda }\right]}_{II}{(\delta \lambda )}_{I}={\lambda}_{I}^{\text{*}}(\delta {\lambda}_{I})\frac{1}{r}\left(\frac{\partial {t}_{c}}{\partial (\lambda +rw)}({\lambda}_{I}+r{w}_{I})-1\right){\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{I}d\Gamma$

The elementary vectors have the expressions:

${\lbrace w\rbrace }_{I}^{\text{*}}{\left({L}_{\mathit{coh}}^{1}\right)}_{I}={w}_{I}^{\text{*}}{t}_{c}({\lambda}_{I}+r{w}_{I}){\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{I}d\Gamma$

${\lbrace w\rbrace }_{I}^{\text{*}}{\left({L}_{\mathit{coh}}^{2}\right)}_{I}=\frac{{w}_{I}^{\text{*}}}{r}{t}_{c}({\lambda}_{I}+r{w}_{I}){\int}_{\Gamma}{\psi}_{I}d\Gamma$

## 6 Propagation of a cohesive crack

In this method, cohesive-zone models are introduced on extended potential cracking surfaces. Thus, the cohesive law will naturally separate the adherent and open domains, which requires a formulation of the cohesive law that allows a faithful representation of adherent zones of large size. The problem was discussed in § 3 . An implicit updating of the propagation front can then be carried out, which is the originality of the method proposed here. For the propagation algorithms, we use a mixed cohesive law with linear elements CZM_LIN_MIX, which is introduced into the model by the command DEFI_CONTACT, with the keywords ZONE/RELATION.

Suppose a structure presenting a cracked surface, described by a cohesive law, for which the cohesive front separating the effective cohesive zone from the potential cracking surface is known, that is the surface within which the crack will be able to propagate at the next loading step (see fig.). Carrying out one iteration of the propagation computation then comprises, in order, the following steps (see fig.):

1. carrying out the mechanical analysis at the next time step (command STAT_NON_LINE),
2. detection of the new cohesive front (command PROPA_FISS, operation DETECT_COHESIF),
3. determination of the propagation direction along this new front (command CALC_G),
4. construction of a new potential cracking zone beyond this front, from the directions determined in c), according to an extension of fixed length (command PROPA_FISS, operation PROPA_COHESIF),
5. updating of the model according to this new zone (command MODI_MODELE_XFEM), and if necessary, projection of the current cohesive state onto this new model (keyword ETAT_INIT/COHE in STAT_NON_LINE). This state will serve as the initial state for the mechanical analysis of step a) of the next iteration.

### 6.1 Description of a cohesive crack

In order to be able to locate the cohesive front, one defines a discontinuity of type 'COHESIF' in DEFI_FISS_XFEM. Compared with a discontinuity of type 'INTERFACE', it must contain additional information in order to *locate the cohesive front:* the data structures relating to the description of a front (coordinates of the points, covariant basis...) and an additional level-set. Indeed, given that the position of the potential cracking surface is described with a normal level-set by $[{\varphi}_{n}=0]$ , the cohesive front is classically located by an additional “tangent” level-set, as $[{\varphi}_{n}=0]\cap [:ref:`{\varphi}_{t}=0 <{\varphi}_{t}=0>$]` (see fig.). For a detailed description of the use of level-sets with XFEM, see the documentation [R7.02.12](doc:r7.02.12), §2.

### 6.2 Carrying out the mechanical analysis

The law CZM_LIN_MIX and the formulation used have been detailed in the first five parts of this documentation. The length of the cohesive zone is estimated by ${l}_{c}=\frac{E{G}_{c}}{(1-{\nu}^{2}){\sigma}_{c}^{2}}$ . It is recommended to choose a mesh-cell size $h$ such that ${l}_{c}\sim \text{quelques}h$ so that the cohesive zone is well resolved.

It is recommended to use load piloting, which makes it possible to control the crack advance at each propagation step. Indeed, in general, when load piloting is used, the displacement increment $\Delta u$ is related to the time step $\Delta t$ by the relation (see documentation [R5.03.80](doc:r5.03.80)):

$f(\Delta u)=\frac{\Delta t}{\text{COEF\_MULT}}$

where:

- $f$ is the piloting function, which depends on the quantity that one seeks to control,
- COEF_MULT is the value supplied under the keyword of the same name, under the factor keyword PILOTAGE of the command STAT_NON_LINE.

More particularly for the cohesive law CZM_LIN_MIX, the piloting function is:

$f(\Delta u)=\frac{⟦\Delta u⟧}{{w}_{c}}$ where ${w}_{c}=\frac{2{G}_{c}}{{\sigma}_{c}}$ is the critical displacement jump.

The crack length created over a propagation step is therefore approximately $\Delta l=\frac{{l}_{c}}{2}\frac{\Delta t}{\text{COEF\_MULT}}$ . Once again, it is recommended to choose $\Delta l\sim \text{quelques}h$ . If possible, $\Delta l$ must be much smaller than the radius of curvature expected for the cracked surface (in order to capture the geometry of the crack accurately). Failing that, this criterion must be checked a posteriori.

### 6.3 Detection of the front

In order to determine the crack front from the result of the cohesive analysis, we shall assign to the nodes of the mesh a status indicating whether they are sound or already damaged. This status must be a scalar and must not be modified in unloading situations. Consequently, it seems natural to use the internal variable $\alpha$ of the cohesive law. However, like any internal variable measuring an irreversible process, $\alpha$ does not evolve during the adherent phases ($\alpha =0$ , see § 2.2.3 ). As it stands, it therefore does not make it possible to determine a scalar field whose zero iso-value would define the crack front. On the other hand, one can use for this purpose the quantity $\tilde{\alpha}\text{}$ , such that $\alpha ={P}_{[0,1]}(\tilde{\alpha})$ . As a reminder (cf. § 2.2.3 ), one has $\tilde{\alpha}(t)=\underset{[0,t]}{\max}\phi \left({\left(\lambda +rw\right)}_{\mathit{eq}}\right)$ with $\phi \left({\left(\lambda +rw\right)}_{\mathit{eq}}\right):=\frac{{\left(\lambda +rw\right)}_{\mathit{eq}}-{\sigma}_{c}}{r{w}_{c}-{\sigma}_{c}}$ . From then on, $\tilde{\alpha}\le 0$ corresponds to a sound material, and $\tilde{\alpha}\ge 0$ to a cracked material.

> The construction of a crack front from the previous relevant field is carried out by assimilating $\tilde{\alpha}\text{}$ to a tangent level-set ${\varphi}_{t}$ . The procedure then consists in:

1. recovering the field $\tilde{\alpha}\text{}$ , already defined as a field at the nodes for the mixed formulation with linear elements;
2. locating the elements intersected (see fig., steps a and b) by the zero iso-value of $\tilde{\alpha}\text{}$ . For the detail, see the documentation [R7.02.12](doc:r7.02.12), §3.2.5.1 and §3.2.5.2;
3. determining the intersection points of the zero iso-value of the field with the faces (see fig.c). For a detail of the procedure, see the Code_Aster documentation [R7.02.12](doc:r7.02.12), §2.4.1.

As highlighted by the references to the Code_Aster documentation, all the routines carrying out these operations already exist, since the field acts as a tangent level-set, which makes the implementation particularly rapid.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/Cadre24.gif)

**Figure** 6.3-a **: Computation of the level sets at the nodes projected onto the crack front.**

A prerequisite for the detection phase is the production of a regular tangent level-set ${\varphi}_{t}$ : it must be regular enough to be used by the crack-propagation algorithms at the subsequent propagation steps. The field ${\varphi}_{t}=\tilde{\alpha}\text{}$ and the front which follows from it are still too irregular for this use.

In order to smooth out these irregularities, a smoothing procedure must be applied to the front.

To do this, we use the knowledge of the old front, assumed to be more regular. The crack advance which produces the newly detected front from the old one is determined a posteriori (fig.a-b). The direction being known from the previous propagation, a level-set updating algorithm is applied in order to produce a more regular tangent level-set (fig.c-d).

In detail, the steps of this procedure are given below.

1. For each point $P$ of the *old* front, one determines a propagation distance by computing the distance separating $P$ from the intersection point between the plane $\left(P,{n}_{P},{t}_{P}\right)$ and the newly detected “raw” front (see fig., step a). Given an influence length $d$ , this distance is computed in the following way:

${A}_{P}=\frac{\sum_{i}\omega ({d}_{i}){A}_{i}}{\sum_{i}\omega ({d}_{i})}$ with $\omega ({d}_{i})=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\exp\left(\frac{-{d}_{i}^{2}}{2{d}^{2}}\right)\text{si}{d}_{i}\le \mathrm{2d}\\ 0\text{sinon}\end{array}\right\rbrace$

*Warning.* Careful: this is to be distinguished from a 3D projection of $P$ onto the new front, which would not give the same result. In our case, one does indeed seek the advance in the plane normal to the old front passing through $P$ .

1. One smooths the field of velocity norms thus obtained (see fig., step b).

*Notes.* If the field is sufficiently regular, this step may be omitted. The propagation direction having been determined at the previous time step (see part 6.4 ), the propagation velocity on the old front is completely known (see fig., step c), which allows:

1. The implementation of a level-set updating algorithm (see part 6.5 ), for which one carries out only the operations concerning the tangent level-set (see fig., step d).

*Conclusion.* The updated tangent level-set provides the position of a regular front and the associated covariant basis.

### 6.4 Determination of the propagation direction

In the literature, the propagation direction can be deduced from two types of information: either the stress fields in the neighbourhood of the tip, or the equivalent stress intensity factors.

> The reuse of directional criteria based on the stress intensity factors even for cohesive analyses has been suggested by [bib4, bib5]. The authors explain there that this is a reasonable assumption for concrete, since the experiments show that the cracking path, unlike the force/displacement response curve, is almost insensitive to the size of the cohesive zone. One could compute these stress intensity factors from an additional elastic analysis of a free crack — one removes the cohesive forces from the model — as in [bib6, bib7].

> One can nevertheless do without this additional step: for example, Moës and Belytschko [bib8] compute them directly from the result with cohesive forces. The argument justifying this simplification is the following (Planas and Elices [bib9]): far from the cohesive zone, the mechanical fields are close to those of an “equivalent” free crack. *Equivalent* stress intensity factors can then be defined by contour integrals which surround the whole of the cohesive zone.
> We define a virtual crack-extension field $\theta$ , such that $\parallel \theta \parallel =1$ , tangent to the cracked surface at each point of it and directed along $t$ along the front (see figures). Let $C$ be a contour which surrounds the whole of the cohesive zone, and ${\Gamma}_{C}$ the part of $\Gamma$ which lies between the crack front and the ends of $C$ (see fig.), so that $C\cup {\Gamma}_{C}^{+}\cup {\Gamma}_{C}^{-}$ is a closed contour (fig.). As detailed in [bib8], a J-integral can be defined on this closed contour as $J={J}_{\text{coh}}+{J}_{\text{ext}}$ with ${J}_{\text{ext}}:=-{\int}_{C}\theta \cdot E\cdot nd\Gamma$ and ${J}_{\text{coh}}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{C}}\theta \cdot ⟦E⟧\cdot nd\Gamma$ , the Eshelby tensor being denoted $E={\nabla u}^{T}\cdot \sigma -\frac{1}{2}\left(\sigma :ϵ\right)1$ and $⟦E⟧={E}^{+}-{E}^{-}$ . Because ${\sigma}^{+}\cdot n={\sigma}^{-}\cdot n={t}_{c}$ and $\theta \cdot n=0$ (as a reminder, $n$ is oriented from $-$ towards $+$ ), the expression for ${J}_{\text{coh}}$ reduces to:

${J}_{\text{coh}}={\int}_{{\Gamma}_{C}}{t}_{c}\cdot \nabla ⟦u⟧\cdot \theta d\Gamma$

The integrand of ${J}_{\text{coh}}$ vanishes outside the cohesive zone, since ${t}_{c}=0$ on the *open zone* and $⟦u⟧=0$ on the *adherent zone* . Thus, ${J}_{\text{coh}}$ is independent of the contour $C$ provided that the latter surrounds the cohesive zone, and is written as:

> ${J}_{\text{coh}}={\int}_{\Gamma}{t}_{c}\cdot \nabla ⟦u⟧\cdot \theta d\Gamma$

Moreover, since there is no singularity in the neighbourhood of the front in the presence of cohesive forces, we have $J=0$ , which confirms that ${J}_{\text{ext}}$ does not depend on $C$ as long as the latter encompasses the cohesive zone.

> By Irwin's formula, we have furthermore ${J}_{\text{ext}}=-{J}_{\text{coh}}=\frac{1-{\nu}^{2}}{E}\left({K}_{I,\mathit{eq}}^{2}+{K}_{\mathit{II},\mathit{eq}}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2\mu }{K}_{\mathit{III},\mathit{eq}}^{2}$ . Decomposing the cohesive force by ${t}_{c}={t}_{c,n}n+{t}_{c,t}t+{t}_{c,b}b$ and adopting the notation $⟦\nabla u⟧\cdot \theta =\frac{\partial {⟦u⟧}_{n}}{\partial \theta }n+\frac{\partial {⟦u⟧}_{t}}{\partial \theta }t+\frac{\partial {⟦u⟧}_{b}}{\partial \theta }b$ , the equivalent stress intensity factors can be written:
> ${K}_{I,\mathit{eq}}^{2}=-\frac{E}{1-{\nu}^{2}}{\int}_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial {⟦u⟧}_{n}}{\partial \theta }{t}_{c,n}d\Gamma$
> ${K}_{\mathit{II},\mathit{eq}}^{2}=-\frac{E}{1-{\nu}^{2}}{\int}_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial ⟦{u}_{t}⟧}{\partial \theta }{t}_{c,t}d\Gamma$
> ${K}_{\mathit{III},\mathit{eq}}^{2}=-2\mu {\int}_{\Gamma}\frac{\partial ⟦{u}_{b}⟧}{\partial \theta }{t}_{c,b}d\Gamma$

Physically, these expressions quantify the energy dissipated according to each of the three fracture modes, if one makes the assumption of a homothetic propagation, in the direction $\theta$ , and *that the size of the cohesive zone remains small compared with that of the specimen* .

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/100002000000015F000000D495F4FDF4012A4654.png)

**Figure** 6.4-a **: Definitions of invariant integrals in the presence of a cohesive zone.**

The criterion then adopted is the maximum circumferential stress criterion of Erdogan and Sih [bib12] (see also [R7.02.13](doc:r7.02.13), §2.1):

$\beta =2\arctan\left[\frac{1}{4}\left({K}_{I}^{\mathit{èq}}/{K}_{\mathit{II}}^{\mathit{èq}}-\mathit{sign}({K}_{\mathit{II}}^{\mathit{èq}})\sqrt{{\left({K}_{I}^{\mathit{èq}}/{K}_{\mathit{II}}^{\mathit{èq}}\right)}^{2}+8}\right)\right]$

**Note – correction of the field** $\theta$ **in order to make it tangent to the cracked surface:**

*In order to ensure* $\theta \cdot \nabla {\varphi}_{n}=0$ *at every point* $M$ *of the cracked surface (see figures), the field* $\theta$ *is corrected in the following manner (see fig.):*

*- Initialisation* $\theta \leftarrow \nabla {\varphi}_{t}$ *;*

*- Vector* $\theta$ *normal to the propagation plane:* $b\leftarrow \nabla {\varphi}_{n}\times \nabla {\varphi}_{t}$ *, then* $b\leftarrow b/\parallel b\parallel$ *;*

*- Correction in order to ensure that* $\theta$ *is tangent to the crack surface:* $\theta =\nabla {\varphi}_{n}\times b$ *, then* $\theta \leftarrow \theta /\parallel \theta \parallel$ *.*

**Note –** **field** $\theta$ **at the ends of the front:**

*In a classical* $\left(G,\theta \right)$ *method, a correction is applied to the values of* $\theta$ *on the free surfaces, in order to ensure* $\theta \cdot \nu =0$ *, where* $\nu$ *is the normal to the free surface (see fig.), so as to avoid negative values of G. This correction is no longer applied in the case of a cohesive analysis (see fig.), because this risk is excluded by the nature of the computation (surface dissipation integrals).*

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/Cadre36.gif)

**Figure** 6.4-c **: Field** θ on the cracked surface for a classical (G, θ) computation, and for a cohesive computation.

**Note –** **choice of the basis** $\theta$ **for the decomposition of the modes:**

*If the cohesive zone is of large size, it is distributed over a curved surface. The choice of the basis for the decomposition of the modes is then not obvious: one may choose:*

*-either the basis at the crack front (figure, solution a),*

*-or the basis at the point under consideration (fig., solution b),*

*-or the basis linked to the polar coordinates at the crack front (fig., solution c).*

*Whereas the first solution underestimates the bifurcation angle and the second overestimates it, the third solution c) is the one which produces the most balanced results, and therefore the one which has been implemented. The computation of the basis therefore proceeds as follows:*

- *Position vector in the crack-front frame:* $\mathit{PM}=x(M)-x(P)$ *;*

*-Projection into the plane:* $t\leftarrow \mathit{PM}-(\mathit{PM}\cdot b)b$ *, then* $t\leftarrow t/\parallel t\parallel$ *;*

*-Construction of a normal vector* $n$ *:* $n\leftarrow t\times b$ *, then* $n\leftarrow n/\parallel n\parallel$ *.*

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/Cadre27.gif)

**Figure** 6.4-d **:** Choice of possible bases for the decomposition of the modes.

### 6.5 Extension of the potential cracking surface

For the updating of the cracked surface, we use the 'GEOMETRIQUE' algorithm described in [R7.02.13](doc:r7.02.13), §6. In the detection phase described in § 6.3 , only the tangent level-set is updated (OPERATION='DETECT_COHESIF' in PROPA_FISS). In the phase of extension of the potential cracking surface detailed here (OPERATION='PROPA_COHESIF' in PROPA_FISS), the normal level-set is also updated, and the extension is carried out over a fixed length DA_MAX, which must be chosen much greater than $\Delta l$ .

The various methods for updating level-sets are listed in the documentation [R7.02.13](doc:r7.02.13). Whereas the classical methods are based on a centred-difference discretisation of Hamilton–Jacobi equations (METHODE_PROPA='SIMPLEXE'/'UPWIND' in PROPA_FISS), simpler methods have appeared since (METHODE_PROPA='GEOMETRIQUE' in PROPA_FISS), for which the only remaining step is the updating proper. This technique takes advantage of the fact that the crack surface created at the previous instant is frozen: the problem of the propagation of a surface therefore reduces to the propagation of a front from which distances can be evaluated directly.

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/100002000000028E0000009FF3F02F5CAE01451A.png)

**Figure** 6.5-a **:** Direct computation, plane by plane, of the new level-sets by the geometric propagation algorithm.

From the evolution equations, one arrives at showing that for each node $M$ , *provided that one manages to project* $M$ *onto a point* $P$ *of the front such that* $M\in (P,{n}_{P},{t}_{P})$ *,* the updating reduces to a problem in this plane $(P,{t}_{P},{n}_{P})$ . One therefore computes the new values, *plane by plane* , by plane translations and rotations, as follows:

For each point M of the parent mesh:

- One projects this point onto the old crack front (fig., step a).
- One deduces from it the projection onto the new front (fig., step b).
- One computes the corresponding level-sets. If one had ${\varphi}_{t}\left(M\right)\le 0$ , one does not modify ${\varphi}_{n}\left(M\right)\le 0$ , in order to “freeze” the existing cracked surface (fig., step c).

**Note – correction of the basis** $({n}_{p},{t}_{p})$ **at the end points of the front:**

*An important assumption of the geometric propagation method is that the point* $M$ *belongs to the plane* $(P,{n}_{p},{t}_{p})$ *(as a reminder,* $M$ *is the point of space at which one seeks to update the level-sets, and* $P$ *is its projection onto the crack front). If this is not the case, the basis* $({n}_{p},{t}_{p})$ *must be corrected in order to ensure this property before applying the propagation algorithm.*

*There are three situations for which this property is not satisfied, which have in common the fact that the point* $M$ *projects onto an end of the crack front. In* **chronological order of treatment:**

*1. The case of a curved crack front*

*In this case, it is necessary to replace* ${t}_{P}$ *by a* ${t'}_{P}$ *tangent to the edge of the structure:* ${t'}_{P}\cdot \nu =0$ *where* $\nu$ *is the normal to the edge of the structure.*

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/Cadre30.gif)

**Figure** 6.5-b **:** Case of a curved crack front.

*We shall consider that* ${t}_{P}$ *has been corrected according to this procedure in what follows:* ${t'}_{P}$ *will simply be denoted* ${t}_{P}$ *.*

*2. The case of a cracked surface inclined with respect to the edge of the structure*

*In this case, it is necessary to correct* ${n}_{P}$ *by an* ${n}_{P}^{\text{*}}$ *, so that* $M\in (P,{n}_{P}^{\text{*}},{t}_{P})$ *. It is not necessary to correct* ${t}_{P}$ *. This correction is written:*

${n}_{P}^{\text{*}}\leftarrow \left(1-{t}_{P}\otimes {t}_{P}\right)\cdot \mathit{PM}$ *, and* ${n}_{P}^{\text{*}}\leftarrow \frac{{n}_{P}^{\text{*}}}{\parallel {n}_{P}^{\text{*}}\parallel }$ *.*

*For a crack which would emerge perpendicularly, one does indeed recover* ${n}_{P}^{\text{*}}={n}_{P}$ *.*

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/Cadre31.gif)

**Figure** 6.5-c **:** Case of a surface inclined with respect to the edge of the structure.

*We shall consider that* ${n}_{P}$ *has been corrected according to this procedure in what follows:* ${n}_{P}^{\text{*}}$ *will simply be denoted* ${n}_{P}$ *.*

*3. The case of a concave edge of the structure*

*In this case, it is necessary to correct* ${t}_{P}$ *by a* ${t}_{P}^{\text{*}}$ *, so that* $M\in (P,{n}_{P},{t}_{P}^{\text{*}})$ *. It is not necessary to correct* ${n}_{P}$ *. This correction is written:*

${t}_{P}^{\text{*}}\leftarrow \left(1-{n}_{P}\otimes {n}_{P}\right)\cdot \mathit{PM}$ *, and* ${t}_{P}^{\text{*}}\leftarrow \frac{{t}_{P}^{\text{*}}}{\parallel {t}_{P}^{\text{*}}\parallel }$

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/100002000000010900000084BC3D646614F59204.png)

**Figure** 6.5-d **:** Case of a concave edge of the structure.

### 6.6 Extension of the multiplier space and initial internal variables

As illustrated by the figure, steps a-b, the updating of the cracked surface implies that the latter has rotated upstream of the front. Consequently, the edges which are intersected by the new cracked surface are not the same as those which were intersected by the old one, in particular upstream of the front (see fig., steps a-b).

> In the fig., step d, we denote by $Q$ the set of edges intersected by the *new* cracked surface, $V$ the subset of the vital edges (the edges which carry an equality relation), and $K$ the set of enriched nodes. We denote by ${Q}_{0}$ , ${V}_{0}$ and ${K}_{0}$ these various sets if the *previous* cracked surface is considered (fig., step a). The set of vital edges $V$ — which defines the new reduced space of multipliers ${M}_{h}$ — is not a subset of ${Q}_{0}$ but of $Q$ .

> A reconstruction of $V$ starting from scratch will probably lead to very different groups of vital edges. Since the internal variables are defined on the previous space ${M}_{h}$ , they would have to be projected between two rather different spaces, which would lead to a poorer conservation of energy. It is therefore preferable to construct the new space while respecting the existing combinations on the near side of the front, and to extend it by new groups only in the zone not previously intersected, located beyond the crack front (see fig., steps a-c).

> One initialises the set of edges given to the restriction algorithm to the set $Q$ of edges intersected by the *new* cracked surface. Then, one removes from this set (see fig., step b) the edges of ${Q}_{0}\setminus {V}_{0}$ whose ends $n$ are both such that:

- for every edge $v\in {V}_{0}$ of which $n$ is an end, $v\in Q$ .

The algorithm for selecting the vital edges detailed in the documentation [R5.03.54, §6] is then implemented: by construction, it will produce a set $V$ which preserves the previous groups of nodes (see fig., step c).

> The new reduced space involves nodes in $K\setminus {K}_{0}$ which had no values assigned for the internal variables (empty circles in the fig., step c). For such a node $n$ , the initial value is determined as follows (see fig., steps c-d):

- If there exists an edge of $V$ connecting $n$ to a node $m\in {K}_{0}$ , the value of $m$ is assigned to $n$ .
- Otherwise, the initial internal variable is set to 0 (sound material).

![Figure](/figures/r7.02.19/100002000000025F000002A8F600597A80072A87.png)

**Figure** 6.6-a **: Extension of the multiplier space and of the initial internal variables** .

There are also a few nodes of ${K}_{0}$ whose value must be changed, because they are connected to a different group between $V$ and ${V}_{0}$ (grey circles in the fig., step c). Once again, if there exists an edge connecting it to a node $m\in {K}_{0}$ , the value of $m$ is assigned to $n$ . Note that the presence of non-zero, although small, values of the internal variables upstream of the front (such as ${\alpha}_{5}$ and ${\alpha}_{6}$ in the figure, step a) comes from the fact that the propagation front was smoothed during its detection (see § 6.3 ).
