# r5.03.15 Viscoplastic behaviour with CHABOCHE damage

**Summary:**

The viscoplastic model coupled to the isotropic damage of Chaboche (originally developed in order to predict successfully the lifetime and the cracking of the blades of modern turbojets) is used for analyses predicting the time to failure of structures at high temperatures.

This model is implemented in *Code_Aster* under the name VENDOCHAB; the rate equations are integrated numerically by an explicit Runge-Kutta scheme of order 2 with automatic subdivision into local sub-steps according to an estimate of the integration error (embedded Runge-Kutta method, see [R5.03.14](doc:r5.03.14)), or by an implicit integration method [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01).

The tests SSNV126 and SSNV183 validate the integration of this model. The validation document [V6.04.126](doc:v6.04.126) supplies the analytical solution for an isothermal uniaxial creep test.

## 1 Introduction

The finite-element analyses carried out in the framework of the studies on severe accidents of nuclear reactors have highlighted the need to use damage models in order to predict the failure of a structure such as the vessel subjected to the severe and complex thermal conditions (high temperatures going as far as melting, strong thermal gradients in space or in time, etc.) that the corium would impose on it [bib1].

The major interest of this choice lies in the fact that the value of the damage variable at rupture (or at cracking) can be regarded as an intrinsic parameter of the material which is accessible, although this is difficult and delicate, by physical measurements (ultrasonics, $X$ diffraction, etc.). The rupture criterion with damage theory is then more "physical" than the maximum-strain criteria sometimes used in viscoplastic analyses without damage, or the uncoupled damage criteria (cumulative rule of the time actually spent under certain conditions $(\sigma ,T)$ divided by the time to failure for those same conditions).

The model implemented in *Code_Aster* is a model of viscoplastic constitutive behaviour with multiplicative viscosity hardening coupled to isotropic damage (model due to Chaboche [bib2]).

**Nota Bene:**

A detailed description of the capabilities of the model, a methodology for the identification of the parameters and the values of these parameters for steel 22MoNiCr37 will be found in reference [bib3].

## 2 Formulation of the model

### 2.1 Theoretical framework

In this sub-chapter, the specificity of the law VENDOCHAB (that is to say the damage) with respect to the usual viscoplastic models is emphasised. For further details, the reader is referred to [bib2].

Damage theory describes the evolution of the phenomena between the virgin state and the initiation of the macroscopic crack in a material by means of a continuous variable (scalar or tensorial) describing the progressive deterioration of this material. This idea, due to Kachanov, who was the first to use it to model rupture by creep of metals under uniaxial loading, was taken up in France in the 1970s by Lemaitre and Chaboche. The evolution of the material from its virgin state to its damaged state is not always easy to distinguish from the strain phenomenon that most often accompanies it, and is due to several different mechanisms, of which creep is one. Viscoplastic creep damage corresponds to the intergranular decohesions accompanying the viscoplastic strains for metals at medium and high temperatures.

In order to define what this damage variable is, consider the area $S$ of one of the faces of a volume element $\Omega$ located by its outward normal $\text{n}$ . On this section, the microcracks and the cavities that constitute the damage leave traces of various shapes. Let $\tilde{S}$ be the effective resisting area and ${S}_{D}$ the total area of all the traces.

One has:

${S}_{D}=S-\tilde{S}$

and the damage variable is defined by:

${D}_{n}=\frac{{S}_{D}}{S}$

${D}_{n}$ is the measure of the local damage relative to the direction $n$ . From a physical point of view, the damage variable ${D}_{n}$ is therefore the relative area of the cracks and cavities cut by the plane normal to the direction $\overrightarrow{n}$ . From a mathematical point of view, by letting $S$ tend to 0, the variable ${D}_{\overrightarrow{n}}$ is the surface density of the discontinuities of the matter in the plane normal to $n$ . ${D}_{n}=0$ corresponds to the undamaged virgin state. ${D}_{n}=1$ corresponds to the volume element broken into two parts along a plane normal to $n$ .

The isotropy assumption implies that the cracks and cavities are uniformly distributed in orientation at a point of the material. In this case, the damage variable becomes a scalar which no longer depends on the orientation and is denoted $D$ . One has:

$D={D}_{n}\forall n$

Only the isotropic damage variable will be considered here.

The global mechanical measurements (modification of the elasticity, plasticity or viscoplasticity characteristics) are easier to interpret in terms of a damage variable thanks to the notion of effective stress introduced by Rabotnov. The effective stress represents the stress related to the section that effectively resists the forces. In the case of isotropic damage, it is written:

$\tilde{\sigma}=\frac{\sigma}{(1-D)}$

And one has:

1. $\tilde{\sigma}=\sigma$ for a virgin material
2. $\tilde{\sigma}\to +\infty$ at the moment of rupture

The principle of equivalence in strain implies that any strain behaviour, unidimensional or three-dimensional, of a damaged material is expressed by the constitutive laws of the virgin material in which the usual stress is replaced by the effective stress.

Two types of variables are distinguished in order to characterise the medium:

The observable (measurable) variables:

- the temperature $T$
- the total strain $\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}$ which is decomposed as indicated below:

$\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}={\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{e}+{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{\text{vp}}+{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{\text{th}}$

The internal variables:

- the viscoplastic strain ${\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{\text{vp}}$
- the isotropic hardening variable $r$
- the isotropic damage variable $D$

Let $\Psi =\Psi (\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}},{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{\text{vp}},T,r,D)$ , the state potential; the state laws describing this potential are:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}\underline{\underline{\sigma}}=\rho \frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial \underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}\\ R=-\rho \frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial r}\\ s=-\rho \frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial T}\\ Y=-\rho \frac{\partial \Psi }{\partial D}\end{array}$

According to the normality law, one has, with $\Phi$ , the dissipation potential:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}{\underline{\underline{\dot{\varepsilon}}}}^{\text{vp}}=\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial \underline{\underline{\sigma}}}\\ \dot{r}=\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial R}\\ \dot{D}=\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial Y}\end{array}$

The modelling of the hardening and of the damage of the material is done by means of internal (or hidden) variables. In the case of the model VENDOCHAB, the internal variables introduced in *Code_Aster* are:

- ${\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{\text{vp}}$ : tensor of the inelastic strains
- $p$ : cumulative plastic strain
- $r$ : viscosity hardening variable
- $D$ : scalar variable of isotropic damage

### 2.2 Equations of the model

The equations of the model are then written:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}={\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{e}+{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{\text{th}}+{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{\text{vp}}\\ \underline{\underline{\sigma}}=(1-D)\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\Lambda}}}}{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{e}\\ {\underline{\underline{\dot{\varepsilon}}}}^{\text{vp}}=\frac{3}{2}\dot{p}\frac{{\underline{\underline{\sigma}}}^{'}}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}}\\ \dot{p}=\frac{\dot{r}}{(1-D)}\\ \dot{r}={\langle \frac{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}-{\sigma}_{y}(1-D)}{(1-D)K{r}^{}}\rangle }^{N}\\ \dot{D}={\langle \frac{\chi (\underline{\underline{\sigma}})}{A}\rangle }^{R}(1-D{)}^{-k(\chi (\underline{\underline{\sigma}}))}\end{array}$

with:

$\begin{array}{}\chi (\underline{\underline{\sigma}})=\alpha {J}_{0}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}})+\beta {J}_{1}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}})+(1-\alpha -\beta ){J}_{2}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}})\\ \text{où :}{J}_{0}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}})\text{est la contrainte principale maximale}\\ \text{}{J}_{1}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}})=\text{Tr}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}})\\ \text{}{J}_{2}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}})={\sigma}_{\text{eq}}=\sqrt{\frac{3}{2}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{ij}^{'}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{ij}^{'}}\\ \langle x\rangle \text{partie positive de}x\end{array}$

where:

| $\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}},{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{e},{\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{\text{th}}$ et ${\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}}^{\text{vp}}$ | are respectively the total, elastic, thermal and plastic strains, |
| --- | --- |
| $\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\Lambda}}}}=({\Lambda}_{ijkl})$ | is the elastic stiffness tensor, |
| ${\underline{\underline{\sigma}}}^{'}=\underline{\underline{\sigma}}-\frac{1}{3}\text{Tr}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}})\underline{\underline{\text{Id}}}$ | is the deviatoric part of the stress tensor, |
| $p$ | is the cumulative plastic strain, |
| $r$ | is the viscoplastic isotropic hardening variable |
| $D$ | is the scalar variable of isotropic damage |

**Nota Bene:**

*The whole set of parameters of the model* $\alpha ,\beta ,N,M,K,A,R\text{et}k$ may be functions of the temperature (in $°C$ ). $k$ may be constant, depend on the temperature or on $\chi (\underline{\underline{\sigma}})$ (in $\mathit{MPa}$ ) and on the temperature.

Moreover, it is seen that this model considers that there may exist a viscoplastic threshold ${\sigma}_{y}$ which depends on the temperature.

It is seen that this model reduces to the viscoplastic model of Lemaitre if one considers that $D=0$ and if the evolution equation of $D$ is neglected. $M,N,\text{et}K$ are coefficients characteristic of the purely viscoplastic constitutive behaviour of the material.

The evolution of the damage is governed by a law with three parameters: $A,R,\text{et}k$ *.* The equivalent stress $\chi (\underline{\underline{\sigma}})$ makes it possible to take into account a possible effect of the spherical part of the stress tensor on the damage (somewhat as in the cavity-growth laws at the basis of the models of Gurson and of Rousselier). The fact that the maximum principal stress can play a role in $\chi (\underline{\underline{\sigma}})$ is difficult to imagine for materials such as steel, but makes the model more general.

## 3 Computation of the material parameters

The parameters of the constitutive law can be computed from creep tests carried out for different levels of stress and of temperature. For this purpose a unidimensional constitutive law is used, because the loading of a cylindrical specimen in tension can be modelled in dimension 1. The stress tensor reduces to its axial component.

$\underline{\underline{\sigma}}=(\begin{array}{ccc}{\sigma}_{0}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array})$ and ${\underline{\underline{\sigma}}}^{'}={\sigma}_{0}(\begin{array}{ccc}\frac{2}{3}& 0& 0\\ 0& -\frac{1}{3}& 0\\ 0& 0& -\frac{1}{3}\end{array})$

One therefore has: ${J}_{0}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}})={J}_{1}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}})={J}_{2}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}})={\sigma}_{0}$

$\chi (\underline{\underline{\sigma}})={\sigma}_{0}\forall (\alpha ,\beta )$

The system of equations to be solved is then:

$\begin{array}{}{\underline{\underline{\dot{\varepsilon}}}}^{\text{vp}}=\dot{p}(\begin{array}{ccc}1& 0& 0\\ 0& -\frac{1}{2}& 0\\ 0& 0& -\frac{1}{2}\end{array})\\ \dot{p}=\frac{\dot{r}}{(1-D)}\\ \dot{r}={\langle \frac{{\sigma}_{0}-{\sigma}_{y}(1-D)}{(1-D)K{r}^{\frac{1}{M}}}\rangle }^{N}\\ \dot{D}={\langle \frac{{\sigma}_{0}}{A}\rangle }^{R}(1-D{)}^{-k({\sigma}_{0})}\end{array}$

This system of equations is integrable, which makes it possible to have a single equation for the cumulative viscoplastic strain rate (which can be assimilated to the total strain by neglecting the elastic strains).

This expression can then be correlated with the experimental data in order to adjust the coefficients, but the number of parameters and the non-linearities make this difficult (moreover there is no uniqueness).

A correlation method calling upon "physical" assumptions on the creep phenomenon, whose curve is shown below, must therefore be used.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.15/Object_58.svg)

**Figure** 3 **-a: The different phases of creep on a creep curve**

The strain-versus-time curve obtained after a creep test is decomposed into three parts:

- a so-called primary-creep part, in which the damage is negligible.
- a so-called secondary-creep part, in which the strain rate is essentially constant.
- a so-called tertiary-creep part, in which the hardening is saturated and in which the damage phenomena are predominant.

A method for computing the parameters using the experimental data $(e,t)$ (one also uses $(\dot{\varepsilon},t)$, which is deduced from them by a numerical procedure) for different stress levels and different temperatures has been developed at the CEA. It uses the expressions found above in the case of a homogeneous and unidimensional stress, by making assumptions according to the part of the curve from which the data are taken. For example, in the primary-creep phase, the assumption $D=0$ is made and, in the secondary-creep phase, the fact that $\dot{\varepsilon}$ is constant is used.

The complete description and examples of computations carried out on the German vessel steel 22MoNiCr37 will be found in references [bib3] and [bib4].

## 4 Implementation in Code_Aster

### 4.1 Solution algorithm

The algorithm used is of global-local type.

The global iterations use the elastic stiffness matrix computed from the damaged Hooke matrix:

$\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\Lambda}}}}=(1-D){\underline{\underline{\underline{\underline{\Lambda}}}}}^{0}$

At the level of the local iterations (that is to say at each GAUSS point), the numerical integration of the rate equations can be carried out either by an explicit Runge-Kutta scheme of order 2 with automatic subdivision into local sub-steps according to an estimate of the integration error (embedded Runge-Kutta method), or by an implicit Euler scheme solved by a Newton method. The reader is referred to references [bib3] for all the details concerning the numerical methods, and to [R5.03.14](doc:r5.03.14) for the explicit algorithms employed and their computer programming.

### 4.2 Implicit integration of the constitutive relation

At each global iteration of the solution of the variational equilibrium problem and for each elementary integration point, the equations of the model described in [§3] must be integrated in order to obtain the stress tensor and, where applicable, to compute the tangent constitutive operator.

The problem written in a generic form at time instant t consists of the following four systems of non-linear equations:

> $\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}\underline{\text{Rb}}(\underline{\beta},\underline{p},\underline{\varepsilon},{\underline{\nu}}_{\text{etat}}^{t-1})=\underline{0}\\ \underline{\text{Rp}}(\underline{\beta},\underline{p},\underline{\varepsilon},{\underline{\nu}}_{\text{etat}}^{t-1})=\underline{0}\end{array}$                         eq 4.2-1

with

> $\lbrace \begin{array}{}\underline{\Omega}(\underline{\underline{\sigma}},\underline{\beta},\underline{p},\underline{\varepsilon},{\underline{\nu}}_{\text{etat}}^{t-1})=\underline{0}\\ \underline{\Theta}({\underline{\nu}}_{\text{etat}},\underline{\beta},\underline{p},\underline{\varepsilon},{\underline{\nu}}_{\text{etat}}^{t-1})=\underline{0}\end{array}$                         eq 4.2-2

$\underline{\text{Rb}}$ is a system of six equations (six unknowns) describing the unknowns associated with the stresses. $\underline{\beta}$ denotes the 6-component vector of these unknowns. The link between $\underline{\beta}$ and $\underline{\underline{\sigma}}$ is realised by means of the system of equations $\underline{\Omega}$ and the vector $\underline{p}$ contains the variables $r$ and $D$ .

$\underline{\text{Rp}}$ is a system of equations describing the internal unknowns. A system of 2 equations is chosen, with $\dot{D}$ and $\dot{r}$ as internal unknowns. The evolution of the state variables is described by the system of equations $\underline{\Theta}$ .

The implicit Euler scheme is used and the algorithm takes the following form:

| Initialisation of the unknowns of the discretised problem and recovery of the values of the state variables obtained at the previous step |
| --- |
| iterations of Newton's method (maximum number of iterations predefined by the user): 1. Recovery of the values of the parameters involved in the material law (the elasticity operator) 2. Computation of the stress criteria and of their derivatives with respect to the stresses 3. Recovery of the value of the parameter k involved in the evolution of the damage and of its derivative 4. Computation of the current value of the state variables, of the equations describing the internal unknowns and of the equations describing the stresses 5. Computation of the derivatives of the equations with respect to the unknowns 6. Solution of the linear system $\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\frac{\partial \underline{\text{Rb}}}{\partial \underline{\beta}}}^{n}& {\frac{\partial \underline{\text{Rb}}}{\partial \underline{p}}}^{n}\\ {\frac{\partial \underline{\text{Rp}}}{\partial \underline{\beta}}}^{n}& {\frac{\partial \underline{\text{Rp}}}{\partial \underline{p}}}^{n}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}\underline{d\beta }\\ \underline{\mathrm{dp}}\end{array}\right]=-\left[\begin{array}{c}{\underline{\text{Rb}}}^{n}\\ {\underline{\text{Rp}}}^{n}\end{array}\right]$ eq 4.2-3 * Convergence test |
| Evaluation of the tangent operator |

#### 4.2.1 Implicit discretisation of the equations of the model

Considering that a time increment characterises a new state of the system [eq 4.2-1] and [eq 4.2-2] solved by a Newton algorithm, one chooses to identify the state of a quantity at the previous time instant by the exponent t-1, whereas its current state is denoted without an exponent. Thus the variation of a quantity for the time increment considered is presented by $U={U}^{t-1}+\Delta U={U}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{U}(\theta \Delta t)$

For $\theta =0$ , an explicit scheme is obtained and for $\theta =1$ , a purely implicit scheme is obtained.

With these notations, the discretised form of the vector system is written:

$\underline{\text{Rb}}\equiv \underline{\beta}-(1-({D}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{D}))\underline{\underline{\Lambda}}(\underline{\varepsilon}-{\underline{\varepsilon}}_{\text{th}}-({\underline{\varepsilon}}_{\text{vp}}^{t-1}+\Delta t\frac{3}{2}\dot{r}\frac{\underline{{\sigma}^{'}}(\underline{\beta})}{(1-({D}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{D})){\sigma}_{\text{eq}}(\underline{\beta})}))=\underline{0}$         eq 4.2.1-1

or more simply $\underline{\text{Rb}}\equiv \underline{\beta}-(1-({D}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{D}))\underline{\underline{\Lambda}}{\underline{\varepsilon}}_{\text{el}}=\underline{0}$

$\underline{\Omega}\equiv \underline{\sigma}=\underline{\beta}$

where $\underline{\beta}$ is the 6-component vector arising from the stress tensor $\underline{\underline{\sigma}}$ .

> $\underline{\text{Rp}}\equiv \lbrace \begin{array}{}\dot{r}-{\langle \frac{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}(\underline{\beta})-{\sigma}_{y}(1-({D}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{D}))}{(1-({D}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{D}))K{({r}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{r})}^{1/M}}\rangle }^{N}=0\\ \dot{D}-{\langle \frac{\chi (\underline{\beta})}{A}\rangle }^{R}{(1-({D}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{D}))}^{-k(\chi (\underline{\beta}))}=0\end{array}$         eq 4.2.1-2

The evolution of the state variables is described by the system of equations $\underline{\Theta}$ :

> $\underline{\Theta}\equiv \lbrace \begin{array}{}D={D}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{D}\\ {\underline{\varepsilon}}_{\text{vp}}={\underline{\varepsilon}}_{\text{vp}}^{t-1}+\Delta t\frac{3}{2}\frac{\dot{r}}{(1-({D}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{D}))}\frac{\underline{{\sigma}^{'}}}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}}={\underline{\varepsilon}}_{\text{vp}}^{t-1}+\Delta t\frac{\dot{r}}{(1-({D}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{D}))}{\dot{\sigma}}_{\text{eq}}\\ r={r}^{t-1}+\Delta t\dot{r}\end{array}$     eq 4.2.1-3

where $D$ , ${\underline{\varepsilon}}_{\text{vp}}$ and $r$ are the state variables whose history is retained.

The strains $\underline{\underline{\varepsilon}}$ and the state variables are not unknowns of the problem. These quantities (grandeur) will be archived at each converged time increment in order to be reused at the following increment.

#### 4.2.2 Numerical solution

The solution of the non-linear system $\lbrace \begin{array}{}\underline{\text{Rb}}=\underline{0}\\ \underline{\text{Rp}}=\underline{0}\end{array}$ uses the Newton-Raphson method associated with a tangential approximation technique in order to seek the solutions in a domain in which the functions are correctly conditioned.

Following the Newton-Raphson algorithm, this system is solved iteratively on the following sequence:

1. Initialisation of the unknowns
2. Search for a descent direction by the solution of the system [eq 4.2-3]
3. Convergence test $\text{err}=\frac{\sum\mid \Delta x\mid }{\sum\mid x\mid }$

#### 4.2.3 Tangent constitutive operator

The tangent operator is obtained by differentiating the stresses with respect to the total strains according to the rules of composite differentiation:

$\frac{d\underline{\sigma}}{d\underline{\varepsilon}}=\frac{\partial \Sigma }{\partial \underline{\beta}}\frac{\partial \underline{\beta}}{\partial \underline{\varepsilon}}+\frac{\partial \Sigma }{\partial \underline{p}}\frac{\partial \underline{p}}{\partial \underline{\varepsilon}}+\frac{\partial \Sigma }{\partial \underline{\varepsilon}}$                             eq 4.2.3-1

where the stress function $\Sigma (\underline{\beta},\underline{p},\underline{\varepsilon},\underline{{v}_{\text{etat}}^{t-1}})=\underline{\beta}$ . The derivatives of the unknowns with respect to the total strains are obtained by differentiating the system [eq 4.2-1], that is:

$\left[\begin{array}{cc}\frac{\partial \underline{\text{Rb}}}{\partial \underline{\beta}}& \frac{\partial \underline{\text{Rb}}}{\partial \underline{p}}\\ \frac{\partial \underline{\text{Rp}}}{\partial \underline{\beta}}& \frac{\partial \underline{\text{Rp}}}{\partial \underline{p}}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial \underline{\beta}}{\partial \underline{\varepsilon}}\\ \frac{\partial \underline{p}}{\partial \underline{\varepsilon}}\end{array}\right]=-\left[\begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial \underline{\text{Rb}}}{\partial \underline{\varepsilon}}\\ \frac{\partial \underline{\text{Rp}}}{\partial \underline{\varepsilon}}\end{array}\right]$                         eq 4.2.3-2

#### 4.2.4 Particular case of plane stresses

The 2D elements in plane stress having to be usable for this constitutive model, an additional treatment is carried out as an overlay of the general treatment carried out in 3D.

A positive test on the plane-stress case means:

1. At the solution of the system [eq 4.2-1], the additional equations ${\sigma}_{33}={\sigma}_{13}={\sigma}_{23}=$ 0 are added
2. The tangent operator is modified in order to ensure energy equilibrium.

If the derivative is negative, the value of the bound is decreased in order to obtain $f>0$ . The chord method is then applied.

The precision chosen for the solution is $\text{RESI\_INTE\_RELA}\ast f(0)$ .

Once the value of $\Delta r$ has been obtained, that of $\Delta D$ follows immediately, then the stress deviator is computed by:

${\sigma}^{'}=(1-D){(1+\frac{3\mu \Delta r}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}})}^{-1}{s}^{e}$

and the trace by:

$\text{tr}(\Delta \varepsilon -\Delta {\varepsilon}^{\text{th}})+(\frac{\text{tr}{\sigma}^{-}}{{(3\lambda +2\mu )}^{-}(1-{D}^{-})})=\frac{\text{tr}(\sigma )}{(3\lambda +2\mu )(1-D)}$

## 5 Meaning of the internal variables

The internal variables of the model at the Gauss points (keyword VARI_ELGA) are accessible via:

1. $\mathit{V1}={\varepsilon}_{\text{vp}}^{11}$
2. $\mathit{V2}={\varepsilon}_{\text{vp}}^{22}$
3. $\mathit{V3}={\varepsilon}_{\text{vp}}^{33}$
4. $\mathit{V4}={\varepsilon}_{\text{vp}}^{12}$
5. $\mathit{V5}={\varepsilon}_{\text{vp}}^{13}$
6. $\mathit{V6}={\varepsilon}_{\text{vp}}^{23}$
7. $\mathit{V7}=p$ , the cumulative plastic strain
8. $\mathit{V8}=r$ , the viscoplastic isotropic hardening variable
9. $\mathit{V9}=D$ , the damage variable
10. $\mathit{V10}=\mathit{ind}$ , indicator equal to 0 if the current point remained elastic at the current step, 1 otherwise.
