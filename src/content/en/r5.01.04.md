# r5.01.04 Eigenvalue counting procedure

**Summary:**

Whether one is studying the **vibrations of a structure** or looking for its **buckling modes**, the engineer must often solve a modal problem: either generalised (GEP) [r5.01.01](doc:r5.01.01), or quadratic (QEP) [r5.01.02](doc:r5.01.02). To do so, *code_aster* offers several algorithms and methodologies through the operator CALC_MODES, not counting the supporting operators: INFO_MODE and NORME_MODE.

**These operators often use, in pre- or post-processing, a counting procedure.** For some of their functionalities require the number of eigenvalues contained inside an interval (if they are real) or a disc (if they are complex). In the first case, concerning only the **real symmetric GEPs**, one has the traditional Sturm method. In the second case, looking at **arbitrary GEPs** and **QEPs**, the situation is much less favourable. This numerical problem is still the subject of active research and no completely satisfactory solution has yet emerged.

After testing several variants of these methods, we finally industrialised one of them in the code: the **APM variant** $\mathrm{LDLT}$. It is for the moment available only in INFO_MODE [u4.52.01](doc:u4.52.01) (METHODE='APM'), in addition to the Sturm method (METHODE='STURM'). This method is still the subject of research. It is to be reserved for simplified problems of small size (< ${10}^{4}$ degrees of freedom).

In the most frequent case of real symmetric GEPs, **it is strongly advised to pre-calibrate one’s modal analysis** **via estimates** **from prior INFO_MODE** [U4.52.01]. Thanks to their **two levels of parallelism** 1
, these INFO_MODE can be “almost free”! On a hundred processors, one can obtain accelerations in time of the order of 70 and reductions of the memory peak of the order of 2.

In the first part of this document we summarise the problem of eigenvalue counting. Then, before detailing the various existing solutions, we specify their main numerical ingredients. A specific chapter details the implementation of multi-level parallelism. Finally, we conclude with a summary of the algorithms actually available in the code and of their parameter settings.

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Context

Whether one is studying **the vibrations of a structure**, possibly damped and/or rotating, or looking for its buckling modes, the engineer must often solve **a modal problem**. To do so, code_aster* offers various operators  1
which treat two types of modal problems: the **Generalised** ones (GEP for ‘Generalized Eigenvalue Problem’) and the **Quadratic** ones (QEP for ‘Quadratic Eigenvalue Problem’):

Find $(\lambda, \mathrm{u}) \text{ tel que :}$

Depending on the problem, the matrices $\mathrm{A}$, $\mathrm{B}$ and $\mathrm{C}$ mentioned above are linear combinations of the various usual mechanical matrices:

- Mass $\mathrm{M}$,
- Stiffness $\mathrm{K}$,
- Geometric stiffness ${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{g}}$,
- Damping induced by dissipative forces ${\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{visq}}$ or by the structure ${\mathrm{E}}_{\mathrm{hyst}}$,
- Gyroscopic effect $\mathrm{G}$.

We have deliberately placed ourselves in the most general framework, but often one can nevertheless make the identification:

with $\xi$ a real parameter representing the rotation speed (in the case of a gyroscopic effect).

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.04/100000000000042B000001A7676EBECF03151B19.png)

*Two distinct problems: counting of the eigenvalues contained strictly in a segment of the real axis and in a finite portion of the complex plane.*

These matrices are often real (except in the presence of hysteretic damping), but not always symmetric (for example in the presence of gyroscopic effects) and rarely positive definite (because of the Lagranges in particular). **This departure from the norm of the usual modal problems** (symmetric and positive definite SEP) and **this variability of the problems undoubtedly complicates their algorithmic treatment in the code.**

### 1.2 Counting and modal analysis in code_aster

Indeed, **the solution of a modal problem, whether standard, generalised or quadratic, remains a difficult problem**. Even if, in the literature, certain categories of modal problems seem clearly solved long ago (for example the standard GEPs). It remains no less true that the numerical implementation remains difficult.

One therefore systematically adds 2, as a verification of a modal analysis, an eigenvalue counting procedure. **For the standard GEPs (real symmetric) this counting is carried out in a segment of the real axis** (cf. r5.01.04-fig-1 a). For example, one checks that, in a given frequency band, the number of “theoretical” eigenvalues does indeed correspond to the number of eigenvalues identified by the modal solver. To do so, one uses an old and classical algorithm (Sturm test, cf. r5.01.04-theoreme-sturm/ r5.01.04-methode-sturm), which, although costly 3
, remains fairly reliable.

But the extension of this criterion to the QEPs and to the atypical GEPs (non-symmetric or complex), for which **one seeks to estimate the number of eigenvalues contained in a finite zone of the complex plane** (for example a disc centred on the value of interest to the user 4, cf. r5.01.04-fig-1 b), has been seriously conceivable only for a few years (cf. r5.01.04-tab-procedures).

The passage from a counting on the real axis to a counting in the complex plane is indeed not trivial. The algorithms used share common tools (cf. r5.01.04-prerequis-mathematique-numerique), but their complexity, their risk of failure and their computational cost are considerably amplified by this change of dimension.

In the modal-analysis functionalities of *code_aster*, one adds this eigenvalue counting test to the verifications on the modal residuals 5

The counting of the eigenvalues is also indispensable to four other functionalities of the modal operators:

- **Operator INFO_MODE:** This operator provides a prior estimate of the number of eigenvalues contained in a given frequency band in order to gauge a future dynamic analysis.
- **Operator CALC_MODES + OPTION='BANDE':** The same problem but this time in order to set the parameters of the main modal solvers. One must indeed fix for them a maximum number of frequencies to compute, whereas the user may supply only the frequency band of interest. From this band, one must therefore estimate the number required.
- **Operator CALC_MODES + OPTION='BANDE' with subdivision into several sub-bands** 6: When one seeks to compute a substantial part of the spectrum ($>40$ eigenvalues), it is much more efficient and reliable to decompose one’s global analysis into several independent sub-analyses. This decomposition is based on a partition of the search zone. In order to organise this partition, one relies in particular on this counting procedure.
- **Operator CALC_MODES + OPTION='PROCHE' or 'AJUSTE' or 'SEPARE':** This is an operator which serves to compute only a few modes of the spectrum and whose initial heuristic 7 (activated with OPTION=’AJUSTE’/’SEPARE’) is based on a counting criterion.

### 1.3 Scope of use

In practice, **these five counting needs are covered by the Sturm method in the framework of the standard GEPs**. When one moves on to atypical GEPs or to QEPs, this functionality is no longer available. This restriction of the scope of use is protected by dedicated alarms and it is mentioned in the User documentation.

**Only the operator INFO_MODE** [u4.52.01](doc:u4.52.01) **has recently benefited** **from this extension of the scope to the complex plane** (*via* the METHODE='APM'). After prototyping and testing several families of counting methods (cf. r5.01.04-methode-formule-quadrature/ r5.01.04-methode-recherche-polynome), only the “APM method, LDLT variant” seems sufficiently mature (cf. r5.01.04-methode-argument-principal), despite its computational cost, to be industrialised. As needs arise, **numerous functional improvements may moreover be brought to it:**

- Various optimised contours (semicircle, quarter-circle…),
- User contour,
- Contour-tracking method in order to make the discretisation more reliable/to optimise it,
- Optimisation of the determinant computations,
- Performance gain by pooling argument computations in the counting heuristic,
- Parallel distribution of these independent computations,
- Near the eigenvalues, a process of recomputation of the determinant similar to that of the Sturm test (cf. parameters PREC_SHIFT and NMAX_ITER_SHIFT)….

## 2 Mathematical and numerical prerequisites

### 2.1 Ingredient no. 1: The characteristic polynomial

The GEP and QEP modal problems may be reformulated in the form of a search for the roots of particular polynomials, called **characteristic polynomials**

Find $\lambda$ such that:

with $n$ the size of the matrices considered. These polynomials are characteristic of the modal problems concerned. They contain, via their coefficients, important information: the eigenvalues, the determinants and the traces of the matrices considered.

The zeros of these characteristic polynomials are the eigenvalues sought. Its roots are distinct or not; they may be real (standard GEPs) or complex (in the case of the QEPs and of the atypical GEPs). Unlike the GEPs, the QEPs comprise more roots than the size of the problem $(\mathrm{2n})$ and these may be infinite.

In general **one does not use this polynomial formulation in order to compute the eigenmodes** of the problems (). Besides the computation of the polynomial coefficients (cf. r5.01.04-methode-calcul-polynome), there arises the acute problem of their manipulation. The latter is often costly and very unstable 1 (cf. r5.01.04-evaluation-polynomiale). One **prefers the matrix representation in the form of a factorisation** ${\mathrm{LDL}}^{\mathrm{T}}$; the computation is then more stable:

Considering, for simplicity and as is often the case in *code_aster*, that the matrices are symmetric. One denotes by $\sigma$ (an integer equal to $\pm 1$) the signature of the permutation matrix $\mathrm{P}$ ensuring the decomposition sought

When one moves to the non-symmetric case, this changes nothing in the procedure. One then speaks of an $\mathrm{LU}$ decomposition and the diagonal terms manipulated will be those of $\mathrm{U}$ rather than those of $\mathrm{D}$.

Thus, after having factorised in ${\mathrm{LDL}}^{\mathrm{T}}$ form the working matrices $\mathrm{A}-\lambda \mathrm{B}$ (for the GEPs) or $\mathrm{A}+\lambda B+{\lambda}^{2}C$ (for the QEPs), the computation of the determinant 2 becomes simply a product of the $n$ diagonal terms of $\mathrm{D}$.

### 2.3 Ingredient no. 3: The Cauchy integral formula

Before addressing this notion, one must define the zone of the complex plane in which one is going to be interested. This zone will be chosen by the user in the form of a simple geometric shape (disc, square…). It will be determined, either explicitly, in pre-processing for an estimate prior to a modal analysis, or implicitly, in post-processing for the verification of a modal analysis. *A priori*, this shape can be more complicated (half-disc, union of simple shapes, user contour…) or even constructed in real time (contour-tracking method cf. ). In order to simplify the presentation, one will sometimes restrict oneself, and without loss of generality, to the simplest case (and the one actually coded in *code_aster*) of the disc of radius ${R}_{c}$ centred at ${\Omega}_{c}$.

The essential point is to **manipulate this zone of the complex plane** **via its boundary** $\Gamma$. The latter must be a **Jordan curve**. That is, it must be parametrisable by a function $\Gamma :=[\alpha ,\beta ]\to C,T\to z(t)$ and it must comprise only one multiple point at the ends of the interval: $z(\alpha )=z(\beta )$.

One now has all the ingredients for addressing the subject of this paragraph, the Cauchy integral formula (cf. portrait opposite). One thus considers, on the one hand an analytic (or holomorphic) function $f:C\to C,z\to f(z)$, and on the other hand a Jordan curve $\Gamma$. One adds the supplementary condition that $f$ does not vanish on this curve. Then one has the following corollary 7 of the “residue theorem”. ### 2.6 Ingredient no. 6: Reliable polynomial evaluation

**Developing reliable numerical algorithms for polynomial evaluation remains a challenge**. Especially when one seeks a strategy with a moderate computational cost.

**A classical answer for overcoming this problem is to increase the precision of the computations** 14. Various solutions exist:

- Put the computations and the variables concerned into **quadruple precision**. Unfortunately this solution is not always sufficient and it is not portable.
- Use a **library emulating exact arithmetic** on the set of the integers and/or of the rationals. For example the GMP library. This is very costly in time and supposes an additional software dependency.
- Use **a multiple-precision arithmetic via an external library**: MP of P.Brent (1978), ARPREC of H.Bailey (2005) and above all that proposed by INRIA, MPFR (2000).
- Resort to **fixed arithmetic expansions** such as the double-doubles and the quad-doubles which simulate, respectively, a precision two times or four times higher than double precision. To do so, they redefine floating-point arithmetic as a (non-evaluated) sum of two or four floating-point numbers. Several libraries offer this type of approach: for example, QD of H.Bailey or XBLAS of W.Demmel (for ‘eXtended and mixed precision BLAS’).
- Rewrite the “critical” portions of algorithms so as to compensate (up to a certain threshold) the rounding errors. Indeed, with the help of algorithms known under the name of “**Exact Transformations**” (or ‘**Error-Free Transformation**’), it is possible to compute the rounding error generated by each floating-point operation. These “**compensated algorithms**” therefore have as their principle to adjoin, to each elementary computation carried out (sum, product…), a fine estimate of the associated rounding error. The objective being to use the latter in order to correct (“compensate”) the final result.

### 3.2 Standard Sturm method

In the most common case, that of the standard GEPs (real and symmetric matrices $A$ and $B$), the eigenvalues belong to the real axis. The problem of counting eigenvalues is thereby greatly **simplified.** One does not have to deal with a partitioning of the complex plane and one can rely on the following corollary of Sylvester’s law of inertia.

**Corollary 1**

Let $A$ and $\mathrm{B}$ be two real symmetric matrices, $B$ being moreover positive definite. The number of eigenvalues, strictly less than $\sigma$, of the generalised problem $\mathrm{A}\mathrm{u}=\lambda \mathrm{B}\mathrm{u}$ is then equal to the number of strictly negative diagonal coefficients of the matrix $D$ such that $\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{A}-\sigma \mathrm{B})=\mathrm{L}\mathrm{D}{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{T}}$ (with **P** a permutation matrix).

*Proof:*

Cf. paragraph no. 1 of the article by Y. Haugazeau.

Subsequently, one will call the modal position of $\sigma$, and one will denote it by $pm(\sigma )$, this number of strictly negative diagonal coefficients.

**Notes:**

- *This corollary extends to Hermitian matrices.*
- *Possible multiple eigenvalues are counted with their multiplicity.*

This corollary therefore makes it possible to determine easily the number of eigenvalues contained in an interval $[\sigma ,\mu ]$ and the modal position of these eigenvalues in the spectrum. It suffices to carry out two $\mathrm{L}\mathrm{D}{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{T}}$ decompositions, that of $(A-\sigma B)$ and that of $(A-\mu B)$, and to count the difference in the number of strictly negative terms between the two diagonal matrices. In the jargon of the code, one designates 4 this test under the name of **"Sturm test"**.

It must however be extended to the very particular forms of the matrices encountered in *code_aster*, and notably, one must be able to take buckling into account with or without Lagrange multipliers. To do so, one has **widened the criterion to an arbitrary matrix** $B$ and one has **generalised** it by taking into account the **dualised matrices**.

Recall that one usually defines the **signature** of a matrix (and that of the associated quadratic form) as the triplet of natural integers $(r,s,t)$ where $r$ designates the number of eigenvalues $>0$, $s$ the number of zero eigenvalues and $t$ those $<0$. This last integer is therefore what one denotes in our case.

### 3.3 Method of “Principal Argument” type

Taking up again the elements already cited (cf. r5.01.04-formule-cauchy /r5.01.04-bibliographie), one can schematise the operation of this method in the following form:

- Choose the zone of the complex plane and its characteristic dimensions according to the type of modal problem, the framework of use and the needs of the user.

⇒ $\Gamma$

- Choose the discretisation of the boundary of this zone (compromise computational cost/user needs/possibly prior spectral information) ⇒

⇒ $(\lambda_j)^k_{j=1}$

If one uses the APM+Rombouts variant, determination of the coefficients of $P$ *via* algorithm no. 1.

- At each of the discretisation points, compute ${(P({\lambda}_{j}))}_{j=1}^{k}$ then extract from it the argument.

⇒ ${({\theta}_{j})}_{j=1}^{k}$.

With the APM+LDLT version this computation is carried out *via* an ${\mathrm{LDL}}^{\mathrm{T}}$ factorisation (cf. r5.01.04-polynome-caractéristique)

⇒ $\frac{P({\lambda}_{j})}{\mid P({\lambda}_{j})\mid }=\prod_{l=1}^{n}\frac{{\mathrm{D}}_{ll}}{\mid {\mathrm{D}}_{ll}\mid }$

With the APM+Rombouts variant, one computes these values *via* its decomposition into monomials (+ standard or compensated Horner algorithm, cf. r5.01.04-evaluation-polynomiale)

⇒ $P({\lambda}_{j})={a}_{0}+{\lambda}_{j}({a}_{1}+{a}_{2}({\lambda}_{j}+\mathrm{...}+{a}_{n}{\lambda}_{j}))$

- Counting of the number of turns by an adapted algorithm (possibly coupled with a boundary-tracking method). Addition of supplementary discretisation points if necessary.

⇒ $\Delta \theta =\sum_{j=1}^{k}({\theta}_{j+1}-{\theta}_{j})$

- Estimation of the number of eigenvalues: possibly taking into account the conjugate values and the portions of numbers of turns.

⇒ ${N}^{\Gamma}=\frac{\Delta \theta }{2\pi }$

**Algorithm 6 Principle of the APM method.**

We are now going to discuss certain steps by crossing the elements picked from the literature and our small experience on *Aster* modal test cases.

#### 3.3.1 Choice of the counting domain

The methods of “Principal Argument” type therefore start from the discretisation of the boundary of the domain

$\Gamma \approx {({\lambda}_{j})}_{j=1,k}$

The simplest case is that of the disc of radius ${R}_{c}$ centred at ${\Omega}_{c}$ (cf. r5.01.04-fig-3-1 ab). But in order to save computational resources one can rely on certain properties of the modal problem in order to reduce the bill of the evaluations of ${(P({\lambda}_{j}))}_{j=1,k}$. Thus when one expects complex conjugate eigenvalues $(\lambda ,\stackrel{ˉ}{\lambda})$, a half-disc centred at the origin (+ the corresponding real straight segment, cf. r5.01.04-fig-3-1 c) may suffice. One can operate this strategy, for example, for a QEP without hysteretic damping.

On the other hand, with eigenvalues in pairs $(\lambda ,-\stackrel{ˉ}{\lambda})$, it is the “bow-tie” of quarter-circles which is optimal (+ the two corresponding purely real and imaginary segments, cf. r5.01.04-fig-3-1 d).

#### 3.3.2 Choice of the discretisation

In the literature, as in our tests, one has restricted oneself to a uniform discretisation. One could of course envisage more “adaptive” solutions based, for example, on criteria of evolution of the characteristic polynomial: if it evolves little one relaxes the discretisation, otherwise, on the contrary, one keeps the predefined step width or even one adds new observation points ${\lambda}_{i}$.

**This number of computation points is of course crucial. It completely conditions the robustness of the method**: if the ${\lambda}_{i}$ are not close enough, one risks missing information (cf. r5.01.04-fig-3-3) during the counting of the discretised turns ${\theta}_{j}=\mathit{arg}(P({\lambda}_{j}))$. On the other hand, this discretisation must not be too fine, because especially with the APM+LDLT method, each evaluation is fairly costly in computation time 12.

#### 3.3.3 Computation of the characteristic polynomial

In the literature, the Korean team tested both variants, APM+LDLT and APM+Rombouts, whereas that of INRIA tested only the first. We also tested both variants. **The one based on the Rombouts decomposition sometimes works**, but as the authors already pointed out, **it proves very unstable numerically.** The evaluation of the characteristic polynomial *via* its coefficients is constantly “on the verge of overflow” or it is greatly distorted by summations of terms of too different orders of magnitude.

#### 3.3.4 Heuristic for counting the number of turns

Transcribing so difficult a **linear-algebra problem** into a simple **geometric problem** may seem a great victory**. In fact, the counting of the turns,** in the plane $P(\lambda )$, of this sort of “snail shell” **is far from trivial!**

One has already emphasised the discretisation problems, the fact that the turns could reverse or that the shape can be more off-centre (lemniscate 14 or flower-petal type). And that is without counting the computations distorted by too great a proximity to a root. The examples of figure r5.01.04-fig-3-4 illustrate the problem.

The theoretical contours carried out on one and the same modal analysis of the *Aster* test case SDLL123a illustrate this problem. Between the two contours, only the diameter of the initial control domain changes. Yet the shapes are very different: in the first, the radii are concentric all in the same direction with a few failures near the right-hand edge due to the proximity of an eigenvalue; whereas in the second, the contour changes direction in the course of its path. One can conclude this example with a little test. How many turns should one have counted in the two cases? Answer 15.

### 3.4 Method of quadrature-formula type

These methods still rely on the Cauchy formula

They **seek to compute** ${N}^{\Gamma}$, not as for the APM methods by relying on the argument differences $\frac{\Delta \theta }{2\pi }$ of the characteristic polynomial, but by **approximating the integral by a quadrature formula** (applied to the function $\varphi :t\stackrel{\varphi (t)}{\to }(P\mathrm{°}z)(t)$),

For example, for the **trapezoidal method with constant step**, once the sequence of integration points ${({t}_{j})}_{j}$ has been determined, the computation is written

Numerous quadrature schemes are possible. The INRIA team has notably tested:

- The trapezoidal method with constant step,
- The trapezoidal method with variable step,
- The Adams method (cf. portrait opposite).

They seem to give as much satisfaction as the APM+LDLT method (with boundary tracking) but they suffer from the same ills:

- Computational cost of the numerous integration points (typically hundreds) which require as many ${\mathrm{LDL}}^{\mathrm{T}}$ factorisations,
- Lack of robustness when the contour passes “too close” to an eigenvalue and/or comprises singular points (corner, sharp edge),
- Better behaviour when one injects spectral information into the process (hence better suited to an *a posteriori* control).

On the other hand other difficulties are added which seem to us prohibitive in a first approach:

- Pointed parameter setting of the integration schemes, which imposes an adaptation case by case,
- The extra cost and the robustness problems imposed by the computation of the derivative of $P(z)$.

This last element is a major disadvantage of the method. **The reliable evaluation of the characteristic polynomial already poses enough problems; we did not wish to confront, in addition, those implied by the computation of its derivative.**

Another element corroborated our decision not to select this method in a first approach. It is the fact that the Korean team did not retain, nor even cite, this option. This strategy could nevertheless be the subject of a future evaluation by means of a collaboration or a placement.

### 3.5 Method of the type Search for the zeros of a polynomial

This method, called **“modified Sturm”** or **‘Modified Sturm Sequence Method’** by its authors, proceeds in two steps:

- Determination of the coefficients of the characteristic polynomial *via* **the Rombouts–Heyde algorithm** (cf. r5.01.04-methode-calcul-polynome, algorithm no. 1),
- Application to this particular polynomial of the zero-search **algorithm** of **Gleyse–Moflih** (cf. r5.01.04-methode-dénombrement-polynome, algorithm no. 2).

However **this combination**, if it can prove conclusive for testing a few canonical problems, **is not sufficient to treat all the modal problems in structural mechanics**. In particular in order to take into account the Lagranges commonly used in the *Aster* modellings or in order to count the eigenvalues of modulus greater than unity.

One must therefore also add two steps to it. These will try to free themselves from the limitations of the two combined algorithms:

- The transformation of the **initial modal problem** (GEP or QEP) into **an SEP of identical spectrum** (at least inside the contour), in order to be able to apply algorithm no. 1.
- The **dilation of the initial contour** of algorithm no. 2 (unit disc centred at the origin) in order to take into account, at least, the contours of circle type centred at the origin and of arbitrary radius.

As for the APM+Rombouts variant, we also tried to use compensated algorithmics (cf. r5.01.04-evaluation-polynomiale) in order to prevent the “algorithmic crashes” due mainly to overflows during the manipulations of monomials. But that was not enough to solve all the problems.

The Korean authors had already emphasised this type of problem without however explicitly proposing a concrete solution.

#### 3.5.1 Transformation into the form of an SEP

Firstly, in order to implement the first step, one must be able to transform one’s initial modal problem (GEP or QEP) into an SEP of the type

And of course one wishes the spectrum of this new working modal problem to be identical to that of the initial problem. **One therefore cannot use all the devices implemented during the modal analyses proper in order to transform the initial problems into standard modal problems**. For example, there is no question here of resorting to a shift $\sigma$ in order to be able to invert the system and thus transform it from GEP into SEP (technique called “shift and invert”, cf. §3.7):

For we are interested in the counting of the ${\lambda}_{i}$ inside a contour and not in that of the ${\mu}_{i}$. The linearisation techniques (making it possible to pass from a QEP to a GEP) and the spectral transformations (in order to pass from a GEP to the final SEP) are therefore to be used with discernment for this specific problem.

However, in order to treat **the GEPs of code_aster, one must free oneself from the fact that the mass matrix** $\mathrm{B}=\mathrm{M}$ **is often not invertible**. This is due to the blockings imposed by dualisations and to the new associated Lagrange variables. The Korean authors who promote this method did not have to confront this type of contingency. Their mass matrices are not dualised in order to take the Lagranges into account; they are therefore invertible.

In order to be able to construct an SEP which does not change the spectrum of the initial GEP

one must therefore modify the said matrices $\mathrm{A}/\mathrm{B}$ into $\tilde{\mathrm{A}}/\tilde{\mathrm{B}}$. A modification which we have proposed consists in “bluffing” the process and in making sure that the unknowns which are not “dynamically active” (Lagrange degrees of freedom and blocked physical degrees of freedom) do not take part in the eigenvalue counting computation.

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.04/10000000000004F5000001D00A0771D40B2CE4F4.png)

*Transformation of the initial Aster GEP into a GEP compatible with the Rombouts–Heyde algorithm.*

In order to implement this strategy, one can use the following device:

- “Make diagonal” the rows and columns of $\mathrm{A}$ and $\mathrm{B}$ corresponding to these degrees of freedom ${i}_{0}$
- Impose diagonal values ${A}_{{i}_{0}{i}_{0}}$ and ${B}_{{i}_{0}{i}_{0}}$ such that the eigenvalues associated with these degrees of freedom are pushed outside the control zone.

**In the end, this process makes it possible to construct the matrices** $\tilde{\mathrm{A}}/\tilde{\mathrm{B}}$ **sought: invertible and not modifying the spectrum (at least in the zone of interest).**

In our tests, we used the simplest contour: a circle of radius ${R}_{c}$ centred at ${\Omega}_{c}=0$. In order to handle this problem, it therefore sufficed to impose, for each of the “dynamically passive” degrees of freedom, the excitation of the eigenvalue ${\lambda}_{{i}_{0}}=2{R}_{C}+i\cdot 0$ (*via* for example ${A}_{{i}_{0}{i}_{0}}=2{R}_{C}$ and ${B}_{{i}_{0}{i}_{0}}=1$, cf. r5.01.04-fig-3-5). Of course, any of the values ${\lambda}_{{i}_{0}}=2{a}_{{i}_{0}}+i{b}_{{i}_{0}}$ with $\mid {\lambda}_{{i}_{0}}\mid =\sqrt{{({a}_{{i}_{0}})}^{2}+{({b}_{{i}_{0}})}^{2}}>{R}_{C}+\mathit{eps}$ and $\mathit{eps}=5\text{\%}$ of ${R}_{c}$ for example, would have worked.

This somewhat “cobbled” strategy nevertheless poses various problems:

- The substituted diagonal terms have orders of magnitude sometimes very different from the other matrix terms. This can pose numerical problems in the rest of the process. In particular, with the choice mentioned above, **they do not take into account the “scaling” coefficient** (‘scaling’ in English) which one imposes on the Lagrange degrees of freedom at the end of matrix assembly. And it is difficult to maintain the principal objective of filtering the eigenvalues associated with these degrees of freedom, while respecting the scalings of the two matrices.
- If the situation of the Lagranges of simple blocking (classical Dirichlet) seems well understood, **what is one to do for those associated with links between physical degrees of freedom** (generalised Dirichlet)?

## 4 Parallelism and intensive computation

### 4.1 First steps

**The counting procedures can prove costly in memory and above all in time**. This is particularly true when one seeks to count complex modes (non-symmetric and/or complex GEP or QEP), but it **can also be the case when one wishes to calibrate a simple standard GEP** (real symmetric) on the real axis.

This is in fact the most common case, when one seeks to parametrise better and/or to optimise 1 subsequent modal analyses. One then **calls intensively on the Sturm test in order to evaluate the number of modes contained in several contiguous intervals** (also called sub-bands). Typically about ten (for example the number of sub-bands is nb_sbande=10): $[{\mathit{freq}}_{1,}{\mathit{freq}}_{2}],[{\mathit{freq}}_{2,}{\mathit{freq}}_{3}],\mathrm{...}[{\mathit{freq}}_{10,}{\mathit{freq}}_{11}]$.

One then has about ten modal positions to compute $pm({\mathit{freq}}_{j})(j=1,11)$. One for each bound of the intervals considered. As detailed in chapter 3, each of these modal positions is computed by factorising the dynamic matrix associated with the shift ${\sigma}_{j}={(2\pi {\mathit{freq}}_{j})}^{2}$ in dynamics (resp. ${\sigma}_{j}={\mathit{freq}}_{j}$ in buckling).

One retains from these factorisations only the number of negative terms of the diagonal (in order to implement the Sturm test of corollary 3). With, possibly, a prior shift of the shift in order to manipulate only dynamic matrices “not too badly conditioned” (cf. algorithm 5): $\tilde{{\sigma}_{j}}={\sigma}_{j}+\epsilon$.

Hence finally, on this example, one can make the following **algorithmic analysis**:

- One needs 10 (or 11) independent computations (hence easily parallelisable) according to whether one adopts a view by sub-band (or by frequency);
- These computations produce 2 globally only an integer $pm({\mathit{freq}}_{j})$ and a real $\tilde{{\sigma}_{j}}$;
- The numerical factorisation can itself be optimised by keeping in memory none of the terms: ${\mathrm{L}}_{ij},{\mathrm{D}}_{ij}$. Just the number of ${\mathrm{D}}_{ij}<0$.

These elements, detailed on this small example, will guide the optimisation of the computational costs of the Sturm test, in sequential as in parallel. Concerning the first point, one retains, according to the cases:

- either a **view by sub-bands.** It is used in the Sturm test of INFO_MODE and in that of pre-processing of CALC_MODES with the option 'BANDE' subdivided into sub-bands;
- or a **frequency view.** It is used in the two preceding cases if nb_sbande=1 as well as in the Sturm test of post-processing of CALC_MODES with the option 'BANDE' subdivided into sub-bands.

As for the device of the third point, it is activated only with the linear solver MUMPS. The latter being the linear solver to be favoured when one wishes to do HPC in *code_aster*!

**These elements have therefore recently made it possible to optimise and to parallelise this type of analysis**. Thus, when one uses the standard Sturm method 3, the latter can benefit from notable accelerations. Judge for yourselves: on the test case perf013c parallelised on a hundred processors, the **gains in time are close to a factor 70** and those **in RAM memory peak are close to 2** (cf. r5.01.04-tab-performances).

Whence **“almost free” parallel Sturm tests!** Provided that one adapts one’s parameter setting and that one has numerous processors. And this, with a functional behaviour and result precisions unchanged relative to the sequential mode.

### 4.2 Principle

Let us come back to the parallelisation schemes. Up to now, the parallelism in *code_aster* was mainly a **parallelism in space**. It rested on a distribution of the mesh among the processors feeding the parallelism of two steps of the analysis:

- the construction of the matrix/right-hand side (elementary computation/assembly),
- the solution of the associated linear system (*via* the MUMPS or PETSc products).

In modal analysis, besides the fact that the parallelisation of the elementary computations/assemblies has practically no impact, that of the linear system procures gains in time which are often very moderate 5. Moreover, these gains fade rapidly from about ten processors 6.

In this very favourable case of the Sturm tests, one proposes to adjoin to this parallelism in space a more efficient parallelism based on the distribution of the sub-bands (or of the frequencies) over the processors. **Hence here, the union parallelism in frequency – parallelism in space makes the strength!** Implicitly, one reaches very good accelerations without revolutionising the code, nor modifying its behaviour or its results.

**This strategy of cumulating two levels of parallelism makes it possible:**

- To accelerate the performances more notably by taking the best advantage of the parallel efficiencies of each of the levels;
- To construct a strategy whose efficiency is less dependent on the case, on the numerical parameter setting and on the computing platform;
- And this, without real impact on the functional aspects, the robustness and the precision of the results.

**The natural splitting of the Sturm tests into nb_sbande** (or nb_freq) **independent computations therefore procures a first level of parallelism**. It is often very efficient in time, but it does not impact the memory consumptions.

Moreover, since the essential part of the cost of these computations is due to the numerical factorisation phase 7 of the linear solver, when one parallelises this step *via* **MUMPS** (SOLVEUR=_F(METHODE='MUMPS')), one adds notable gains in time and in memory. This is the **second level of parallelism**.

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.04/100002000000023C000001A38B0F01FF7F09E47B.png)

*Two levels of parallelism. INFO_MODE alone or in pre-processing of CALC_MODES with the option 'BANDE' subdivided into sub-bands. One uses here the linear solver MUMPS and the default parameter setting of the parallelism ('COMPLET').*

With the default value of the parameter setting (keyword **NIVEAU_PARALLELISME='COMPLET'**), when the analysis is parallel 8, the two levels of parallelism are cumulated. Since the second level is less efficient than the first, it is triggered only if the number of processors allows it (nb_proc>nb_sbande) and if the selected linear solver supports MPI parallelism (SOLVEUR=_F(METHODE='MUMPS')).

This parallelisation scheme is illustrated in figure r5.01.04-fig-4-1. It is an example of distribution of the Sturm test over nb_proc=40 processors with a splitting into 10 sub-bands (nb_sbande=10): **two-level parallelism said to be “10x4”.**

If one selects the other value of the keyword (**NIVEAU_PARALLELISME='PARTIEL'**), one benefits only from the parallelism of the linear solver (the second level). This can be of interest for testing methods or for focusing the gains on the reduction of the memory peak (after having tried the other levers proposed in the keyword SOLVEUR).

This parallelisation scheme is illustrated in figure r5.01.04-fig-4-2. It is an example of distribution of the Sturm test over nb_proc=40 processors with a splitting into 10 sub-bands: **single-level parallelism said to be “1x40”.**

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.04/100002000000012800000168C9F143F29D212E4A.png)

One uses here the linear solver MUMPS and one changes the default parameter setting of the parallelism ('PARTIEL').*

**The first level of parallelism can be qualified as “embarrassingly” or “pleasingly”** to take up classical denominations in HPC. It is so easy to conceive 9 that it would have been embarrassing not to have done it! It is all the more favourable in that its communications are very weak: an integer, the modal position of the shift considered 10 $pm(\sigma )$, and a real, the possibly shifted shift $\tilde{\sigma}$.

**The second level of parallelism is distinctly more difficult**. It comprises numerous interdependent tasks, of very variable granularity and requiring costly communications (pieces of matrix/vector). Moreover, certain tasks are managed dynamically. That is, they cannot be foreseen initially. It is therefore one of the most difficult parallel schemes. That is why we entrust this task to the ‘best-in-class’ product MUMPS.

Notes:

- When one activates the second level of parallelism, it is advised to reserve, in modal analysis, at least 105 degrees of freedom per processor in order to feed the linear solver MUMPS sufficiently.
- More precisely, the second level of parallelism is linked here to the analysis and numerical factorisation steps of MUMPS. The first analysis step is, for the moment, treated sequentially in the code_aster–MUMPS coupling. In order to optimise the parallel performances, it is therefore preferable to choose a numerical parameter setting of the linear solver making this analysis negligible relative to the factorisation. Often, in modal analysis, a “rough” renumberer of AMD or QAMD type meets this specification better than a more sophisticated renumberer of METIS, PORD or SCOTCH type.

### 4.3 Functional details

The parallelism implemented in the Sturm tests is managed differently according to the cases. These are of three types:

- Those integrated in operators benefiting from multi-level parallelism directly drivable by the user parameter setting: **INFO_MODE alone or in pre-processing of CALC_MODES with the option 'BANDE' subdivided into sub-bands.**
- Those benefiting from multi-level parallelism but which are not directly drivable by the user parameter setting. These are the verifications of **post-processing of CALC_MODES with the option 'BANDE' subdivided into sub-bands** (STURM='GLOBAL'/'LOCAL'). Plus the marginal case, but nevertheless taken into account from the software point of view, of the configurations of the first point on a single sub-band (nb_sbande=1).
- The other cases which possibly benefit only from the parallelism of MUMPS (hence single-level): **CALC_MODES with OPTION='BANDE', CALC_MODES with OPTION among** ['BANDE','CENTRE','PLUS_*','TOUT'] **and STURM='OUI', CALC_MODES with OPTION among** ['AJUSTE','SEPARE'].

In the paragraphs which follow one details the impact of the parallelism on each of these cases. For further details one may consult the User documentation associated with these operators [[u4.52.01](doc:u4.52.01), [u4.52.02](doc:u4.52.02), [u4.52.03](doc:u4.52.03), [u4.52.04](doc:u4.52.04)].

### 4.4 Load imbalances

#### 4.4.1 General points

From a functional and performance point of view, one takes certain load imbalances into account. Thus, the use of parallelism remains very simple and this does not penalise its performances too much.

Before detailing all the cases, one can summarise their causes:

- **Distribution of the sub-bands or of the frequencies over the processors**. Functional imbalance which can occur in all the cases (INFO_MODE alone, in pre- or post-processing of CALC_MODES with the option 'BANDE' subdivided into sub-bands). It is handled automatically and is not very prejudicial to the performances.
- Distribution of the frequencies by sub-band. Imbalance of a rather software nature (for INFO_MODE alone) and functional (in pre-processing of CALC_MODES with the option 'BANDE' subdivided into sub-bands). It is not too prejudicial to the performances of the studies and it can be improved (in the first case) easily.
- Imbalance of the numerical tasks at the level of the parallel linear solver (here MUMPS).

**One treats directly only the possible imbalances of the first level of parallelism (** by sub-bands or by frequencies).

**Those of the second level are managed directly by MUMPS** and are not parametrisable. Even if the numerical parameter setting 17 of the linear solver sometimes makes it possible to influence these imbalances, one cannot manipulate them directly. One must therefore trust the product. However, in practice, one rarely observes very bad imbalances at this level.

#### 4.4.2 INFO_MODE alone or in pre-processing of CALC_MODES with the option 'BANDE' subdivided into sub-bands

**An obvious cause of imbalance concerns the “sub-bands/processors” distribution**. When the number of processors of the batch class is not a multiple of nb_sbande

$\text{nb_proc}=\alpha \times \text{nb_sbande}+\beta (\beta <\text{nb_sbande})$ (cf. r5.01.04-fig-4-11),

one distributes the remainder of processors by giving priority to the first sub-bands. Each of the $\beta$ first processors manages one extra sub-band ($\alpha +1$). The following $\text{nb_sbande}-\beta$ manage, each, $\alpha$ sub-bands.

In order to limit the MPI communication costs, one reserves contiguously each packet of $\alpha$ (or $\alpha +1$ in the case of imbalance) processors treating a sub-band. Figure r5.01.04-fig-4-11 illustrates this type of imbalance with an analysis of 10 sub-bands distributed (nb_sbande=10) over 41 processors (nb_proc=41): **parallelism said to be “1x5 + 9x4”.**

Notes:

- Since here the master processor works more than the others (cf. following point), this redistribution tries to compensate a little for this imbalance.
- However, concerning CALC_MODES with the option 'BANDE' subdivided into sub-bands, the imbalance is mainly due to the modal analyses proper (even if these treat sub-bands homogeneous in number of modes). In this case, our corrective strategies around the simple Sturm test compensate this imbalance only partially. To do it properly, one would need a parallel modal solver with dynamic load balancing (inserted between the first and the second level of parallelism, cf. ERAM strategy of C.Calvin).

![Figure](/figures/r5.01.04/100002000000021F00000235134B65BFE057D9E2.png)

*Load imbalance “sub-bands/processors”. INFO_MODE alone or in pre-processing of CALC_MODES with the option 'BANDE' subdivided into sub-bands. Example of distribution of the Sturm test over nb_proc=41 processors with a splitting into nb_sbande=10 sub-bands (parallelism said to be “1x5 + 9x4”).*

In fact, in order not to rewrite the programming of the Sturm test and to keep the same source in parallel and in sequential, **one has kept an unbalanced organisation of the computations of the first level**. The first sub-band treats the first two frequencies (freq1 and freq2) whereas the others treat only a single frequency (freq *j*). Figure r5.01.04-fig-4-12 illustrates this imbalance.

Whence a theoretical imbalance of the order of 2, equal to the “frequencies/sub-bands” imbalance. In practice, one observes that this imbalance is less severe and often leads to a parallel efficiency close to 0.6 (instead of 0.5).

**In practice, it is not truly prejudicial** because the global gains procured are already potentially important. Moreover, often one of the frequencies proves close enough to an eigenmode for one to activate the shift algorithm (cf. algorithm 5). ### 4.5 Gains procured by the parallelism

#### 4.5.1 INFO_MODE alone or in pre-processing of CALC_MODES with the option 'BANDE' subdivided into sub-bands

In terms of parallel efficiency in time, the first level of parallelism is close to 0.6 over a wide range of processors. One can thus distribute the analysis of several tens of sub-bands over as many packets of processors with a good efficiency.

Whereas that of the second level is of the order of 0.4, on condition however that the problem treated be large enough (>0.5M degrees of freedom). Above all, this efficiency fades from 4, 8 or 16 processors devoted to the treatment of a sub-band. Everything depends on the case, on the numerical parameter setting of MUMPS (renumberer, pre/post-processings...) and on the characteristics of the machine (memory architecture, networks...).

**In short, the first level of parallelism is more efficient in terms of time consumptions and above all more stable than the second. On the other hand, the first level procures no gain in RAM memory peak, whereas the second makes it possible to gain on this element which often dimensions the analysis.**

Hence, by activating only the first level of parallelism, an analysis split into nb_sbande and parallelised over nb_proc=nb_sbande can gain in time at least a factor 0.6* nb_sbande (without extra cost/memory gain and without loss of precision).

If one distributes the analysis over more processors (nb_proc>nb_sbande) by using the solver MUMPS (one thus adds the 2nd level), the gains in time will be improved by a factor close to 2 as soon as one adds 5* nb_bande additional processors.

And this, with gains in memory which can go up to a factor 2 18.

Thus, the analysis over nb_sbande=10 illustrated in figure r5.01.04-fig-4-1, if it costs $\mathrm{1000s}$ sequentially, consumes only $1000/(0.6\times 10)=\mathrm{166s}$ over nb_proc=10.

And when one adds 30 new processors (nb_proc=40) in order also to activate the second level 19, the total time is close to $166/(0.4\times 4)=\mathrm{104s}$.

Whence a global acceleration in time, over 40 processors, of $1000/104=9.6$. That is almost a gain of an order of magnitude over 40 processors!

### 4.6 Software safeguards and methodological advice

The introduction of MPI parallelism into a rich, generic code with “sustained development 20” such as *code_aster* could pose certain problems: quality-assurance flaws, restriction of scope, ergonomics problem, and above all, the risks of wrong results!

In order to circumscribe these problems, a certain number of software safeguards and of methodological pieces of advice have therefore been put in place for some ten years:

- **Restrict oneself to simple MPI operations**. They are software-wise confined in dedicated “wrapper” routines in order not to be sprinkled through the code.
- In particular, carry out only **simple collective communications** (less risk of 'deadlocks'). Leave the subtle implementations of “asynchronisation” or “bufferisation” type to the MPI library itself and limit, as far as possible, the point-to-point communications.
- **Communicate as early as possible and simple quantities**: vectors of integers, of reals or of complex numbers.
- **Type the data structures potentially impacted by the parallelism** (MPI_(IN)COMPLET). They can be complete or incomplete. One completes them if a particular numerical treatment requires it: matrix–vector product, archiving...
- **Limit the parallelism to the most costly, the most transverse and the most modular tasks**: mainly construction of the matrices/vectors and solution of linear systems.
- All **the operators of the code read their input data and write their output data** (*via* the global and volatile databases) **in a centralised manner and sequentially**. At the end of an operator, one therefore completes, if necessary, the distributed data structures in order to be able to hand over without hindrance to the following operator.

The introduction of a multi-level parallelism into the modal analyses has somewhat complicated the situation... but by procuring the notable counterpart of large benefits in performance. **The principal novelty concerns the simultaneous management of several MPI sub-communicators of the current communicator** 21.

In order to continue to ensure the maintainability, the developability and the quality assurance of the code, the list of software safeguards and of methodological pieces of advice has been enriched:

- In order not to pass the sub-communicator concerning “a given task/group of processors” as an input parameter of the routines, one stores it in a dedicated object of the global database. **At the beginning of each routine manipulating MPI**, one interrogates this object **in order to know the current sub-communicator** 22. One moreover never manipulates this object directly. One always goes through an access “method” 23.
- One treats only **nested sub-communicators** in order not to forget any processor (equivalent of the deadlock for the sub-communicators).
- One **changes sub-communicator only in circumscribed zones** of the code; and not in low-level routines (of elementary-computation or system-solution type), rather conductor routines. One reinitialises, as soon as possible, the current communicator to the classical MPI_COMM_WORLD.
- Before **using an external parallel library in restart mode** (e.g. the solve of MUMPS), one checks that the communicator associated with it is indeed the same as the current communicator.
- **Destroy a sub-communicator** only after having made sure that the occurrences of external products with which it was associated (if that is the case) have indeed been destroyed. Otherwise the destruction of this occurrence risks posing a problem: memory leak, erratic behaviour, hang...
- **At each change of sub-communicator,** provide a transitional barrier step on MPI_COMM_WORLD. This makes it possible to make sure that all the active processors are present at this step of the analysis.
- During the communications of large volumes of data (of eigenvector-group type), one can play on the sub-communicators in order to avoid collapsing the networks! One can, for example, **communicate in cascade between various levels of** nested **sub-communicators**. Beyond the robustness aspects, this is often beneficial in terms of performance.
