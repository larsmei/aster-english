# r7.01.08 MAZARS damage model

**Summary:**

This documentation presents the MAZARS constitutive model, which makes it possible to describe the elastic damageable behaviour of concrete. This model is 3D and isotropic, and is based on a damage criterion written in strain and describing the tension–compression asymmetry. The initial model does not account for the recovery of stiffness in the event of “crack closure” and does not take into account the possible plastic strains or viscous effects that may be observed during the straining of a concrete. The version implemented in Code_Aster takes the latest improvements into account. This reformulation of the Mazars model of the 1980s makes it possible to describe better the behaviour of concrete in biaxial compression and in pure shear.

The $\mathrm{1D}$ version of the model makes it possible to account for the recovery of stiffness in the event of crack closure; it is used only with multifibre beams [R5.03.09](doc:r5.03.09).

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 An elastic damageable constitutive law

The MAZARS] constitutive model is a simple model, reputed to be robust, based on damage mechanics, which makes it possible to describe the decrease in the stiffness of the material under the effect of the creation of microcracks in the concrete. It is based on a single scalar internal variable $D$ describing the damage in an isotropic manner, while nevertheless distinguishing tensile damage and compressive damage. **The version implemented in Aster corresponds to the 2012 reformulation**. The major modification with respect to the original model ] is the introduction of a new internal variable, denoted $Y$ , corresponding to the maximum reached during the loading by the equivalent strain defined in the 1980s. Consequently, damage is no longer the internal variable in the revisited model. Moreover, its evolution law is simplified in order to eliminate the notions of tensile and compressive damage.

Unlike the ENDO_ISOT_BETON model, this model does not make it possible to represent the phenomenon of crack closure (stiffness recovery). Furthermore, the Mazars model does not take into account the possible plastic strains or viscous effects that may be observed during the straining of a concrete.

The $\mathrm{1D}$ version of the Mazars model is described in the document [R5.03.09](doc:r5.03.09) “Non-linear constitutive relations”. In this specific case, the model is capable of accounting for the phenomenon of crack closure. The $\mathrm{1D}$ version of the model can be used only with multifibre beams.

### 1.2 Limits and regularisation methods

Like all softening laws, the Mazars model raises difficulties related to the phenomenon of strain localisation.

Physically, the heterogeneity of the microstructure of concrete induces interactions at a distance between the cracks formed ]. Thus, the strains localise in a thin band, called the localisation band, to form the macrocracks. The stress state at a material point can no longer be described solely by the characteristics at the point, but must also take its environment into consideration. In the case of this (local) model, no indication is included concerning the scale of the cracking. Consequently, no information is given on the width of the localisation band, which then becomes zero. This leads to a mechanical behaviour with failure without energy dissipation, which is physically unacceptable.

Mathematically, localisation makes the problem to be solved ill-posed, because softening causes a loss of ellipticity of the differential equations that describe the straining process ]. The numerical solutions do not converge towards physically acceptable solutions despite the mesh refinements.

### 1.3 Coupling with thermal analysis

For certain studies, it may be of interest to be able to take into account the modification of the material parameters under the effect of temperature. This is possible in *Aster* (MAZARS_FO combined or not with ELAS_FO). The assumptions made for the coupling with thermal analysis are the following:

- thermal expansion is assumed to be linear, that is:

$$
{\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}=\alpha (T-{T}_{\text{ref}}){I}_{d}
$$

with $\alpha$ = constant or a function of temperature,

- thermo-mechanical interactions are not taken into account, that is, the effect of the mechanical stress state on the thermal strain of the concrete is not modelled,
- concerning the evolution of the material parameters with temperature, they are considered to depend not on the current temperature but on the maximum temperature $\mathit{Tmax}$ seen by the material during its history (an effect cited in the literature),
- only the elastic (mechanical) strain induces damage.

**Note:**

*Because of data-processing constraints, the initial value of* $\mathrm{Tmax}$ *is initialised to 0. Consequently, material parameters defined for negative temperatures cannot be used (if necessary, this problem can nevertheless be circumvented by entering all temperatures in Kelvin instead of* $°C$ *).*

### 1.4 Mazars law in the presence of a drying or hydration field

The use of ELAS_FO and/or MAZARS_FO under the operator DEFI_MATERIAU makes it possible to make the material parameters depend on drying or on hydration.

Furthermore, the strains related to endogenous shrinkage ${\varepsilon}_{\text{re}}$ and to desiccation shrinkage ${\varepsilon}_{\text{rd}}$ are taken into account in the model, in the following (linear) form (cf. [R7.01.12](doc:r7.01.12)):

$$
{\varepsilon}_{\text{re}}=-\beta \xi {I}_{d}
$$

$$
{\varepsilon}_{\text{rd}}=-\kappa ({C}_{\text{ref}}-C){\mathrm{I}}_{\mathrm{d}}
$$

where $\xi$ is the hydration, $C$ the water concentration (drying field in the *Code_Aster* terminology), ${C}_{\mathit{ref}}$ the initial water concentration (or reference drying). Finally $\beta$ is the endogenous shrinkage coefficient and $\kappa$ the desiccation shrinkage coefficient, to be supplied in DEFI_MATERIAU, factor keyword ELAS_FO, operands B_ENDO and K_DESSIC. As stated in the previous paragraph, the choice made in the implementation of the MAZARS model is that only the elastic strain induces damage. Consequently, if a concrete specimen that dries or hydrates freely and uniformly is modelled, a non-zero strain field and a perfectly zero stress field will indeed be obtained.

The writing of the model is presented first, then a few data on the identification of the parameters. Finally, the principles of the numerical integration in *Code_Aster* are set out.

## 2 The MAZARS models

### 2.1 Original Mazars model

The MAZARS model was developed within the framework of damage mechanics. This model is detailed in the thesis of MAZARS ]

The stress is given by the following relation:

$$
\sigma =(1-D)\text{E}{\epsilon}^{\mathrm{e}}
$$

with:

- $E$ the Hooke matrix,
- $D$ the damage variable
- ${\varepsilon}^{e}$ the elastic strain ${\varepsilon}^{e}=\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}-{\varepsilon}^{\text{rd}}-{\varepsilon}^{\text{re}}$

*•*     ${\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}=\alpha (T-{T}_{\text{ref}}){I}_{d}$ thermal expansion

*•*     ${\varepsilon}^{\text{re}}=-\beta \xi {\mathrm{I}}_{\mathrm{d}}$ endogenous shrinkage (related to hydration)

*•*     ${\varepsilon}_{\text{rd}}=-\kappa ({C}_{\text{ref}}-C){I}_{d}$ desiccation shrinkage (related to drying)

$D$ is the damage variable. It lies between $0$ , sound material, and $1$ , failed material. The damage is driven by the equivalent strain ${\epsilon}_{\text{eq}}$ , which makes it possible to translate a triaxial state into an equivalence with a uniaxial state. Since extensions are of prime importance in the cracking phenomenon of concrete, the equivalent strain introduced is defined from the positive eigenvalues of the strain tensor, that is:

$$
\epsilon_{\text{eq}} = \sqrt{\langle \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \rangle_+ : \langle \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \rangle_+} \\
\text{où dans le repère principal du tenseur de déformations :} \\
\epsilon_{\text{eq}} = \sqrt{\langle \epsilon_1 \rangle_+^2 + \langle \epsilon_2 \rangle_+^2 + \langle \epsilon_3 \rangle_+^2}
$$

knowing that the positive part ${\langle \rangle }_{+}$ is defined so that if ${\varepsilon}_{i}$ is the principal strain in direction $i$ :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\langle {\epsilon}_{i}\rangle }_{+}={\epsilon}_{i}\text{si}{\epsilon}_{i}\ge 0\\ {\langle {\epsilon}_{i}\rangle }_{+}=0\text{si}{\epsilon}_{i}<0\end{array}
$$

**Note:**

*In the case of a thermo-mechanical loading, only the elastic strain* ${\varepsilon}^{e}=\varepsilon -{\varepsilon}^{\text{th}}$ *contributes to the evolution of the damage, whence:* ${\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}=\sqrt{{\langle {\varepsilon}^{e}\rangle }_{+}:{\langle {\varepsilon}^{e}\rangle }_{+}}$ *.*

${\epsilon}_{\text{eq}}$ is an indicator of the state of tension in the material that generates the damage. This quantity defines the loading surface $f$ such that:

$$
f={\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}-K(D)=0
$$


$K(D)={\epsilon}_{\text{d0}}$ si $D=0$ . ${\epsilon}_{\text{d0}}$ la déformation seuil d'endommagement.



When the equivalent strain reaches this value, the damage is activated. $D$ is defined as a combination of two damage modes defined by ${D}_{\text{t}}$ and ${D}_{\text{c}}$ , varying between 0 and 1 according to the associated damage state, and corresponding respectively to the damage in tension and in compression. The relation linking these variables is the following:

$$
D={\alpha}_{t}^{\beta}{D}_{t}+{\alpha}_{c}^{\beta}{D}_{c}
*(5)*
$$

$$
D={\alpha}_{t}^{\beta}{D}_{t}+{\alpha}_{c}^{\beta}{D}_{c}
$$

$\beta$ is a coefficient that was introduced subsequently to improve the behaviour in shear. Its value is usually set to 1.06. The coefficients ${\alpha}_{\text{t}}$ and ${\alpha}_{\text{c}}$ establish a link between the damage and the state of tension or of compression. When tension is activated ${\alpha}_{\text{t}}=1$ whereas ${\alpha}_{\text{t}}=0$ and conversely in compression.

A particular feature of this model is its explicit writing, which implies that all the quantities are computed directly without using a linearisation algorithm such as that of Newton–Raphson. Thus, the evolution laws of the damages ${D}_{\text{t}}$ and ${D}_{\text{c}}$ are expressed solely from the equivalent strain ${\epsilon}_{\text{eq}}$

$$
{D}_{t}=1-\frac{(1-{A}_{t}){\varepsilon}_{\mathit{d0}}}{{\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}}-{A}_{t}\exp(-{B}_{t}({\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}-{\varepsilon}_{\mathit{d0}}))
$$

$$
{D}_{c}=1-\frac{(1-{A}_{c}){\varepsilon}_{\mathit{d0}}}{{\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}}-{A}_{c}\exp(-{B}_{c}({\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}-{\varepsilon}_{\mathit{d0}}))
$$

with ${A}_{t}$ , ${A}_{c}$ , ${B}_{t}$ , and ${B}_{c}$ , material parameters to be identified. These parameters make it possible to modulate the shape of the post-peak curve. They are obtained by means of tension tests and a compression test.

### 2.2 Revisited Mazars model

Although commonly used, the original Mazars model has shortcomings in the modelling of the behaviour of concrete under shear and biaxial-compression loadings. A comparison between the loading surfaces of the two models is given in .

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.08/1000000000000395000001DC17F7AAC17446E432.jpg)

> **a) Original Mazars model** **b) Revisited Mazars model**

Figure 2.2-1: Comparison of the damage-initiation and failure surfaces of the Mazars models in the plane ${\sigma}_{3}=0$ and a C30 concrete

Thus, a new formulation is proposed through 2 major modifications:

1. improvement of the behaviour in biaxial compression,
2. simplification and improvement of the behaviour in shear.

The original Mazars model of the 1980s ] greatly underestimates the strength of concrete in biaxial compression. The first modification introduced by the revisited model therefore improves the behaviour in biaxial compression. This aim is achieved by correcting the equivalent strain when at least one principal stress is negative, by means of a variable $\gamma$ :

$$
{\epsilon}_{\mathit{eq}}^{\mathit{corrigée}}=\gamma {\epsilon}_{\text{eq}}=\gamma \sqrt{{\langle \epsilon \rangle }_{+}:{\langle \epsilon \rangle }_{+}}
$$

with:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\gamma =-\frac{\sqrt{\sum_{i}{\langle {\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}\rangle }_{-}^{2}}}{\sum_{i}{\langle {\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}\rangle }_{-}}\text{si au moins une contrainte effective est négative}\\ \gamma =1\text{sinon}\end{array}
$$

The effective stress in the sense of damage mechanics is defined by:

$$
\underline{\underline{\tilde{\sigma}}}=\frac{\underline{\underline{\sigma}}}{1-D}
$$

The definition of < > - is similar to :

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\langle {\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}\rangle }_{-}={\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}\text{si}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}\le 0\\ {\langle {\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}\rangle }_{-}=0\text{si}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}>0\end{array}
$$

where ${\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}$ is a principal effective stress.

The improvement of the behaviour in shear is achieved by the introduction of a new internal variable: $Y$ . It corresponds to the maximum reached during the loading of the equivalent strain. Its initial value ${Y}_{0}$ is ${\epsilon}_{\mathit{d0}}$ . $Y$ is defined by the following equation:

$$
Y=\max\left({\epsilon}_{\mathit{d0}}\text{,max}\left({\epsilon}_{\mathit{eq}}^{\mathit{corrigée}}\right)\right)
$$

The loading function is:

$$
f={\epsilon}_{\mathit{eq}}^{\mathit{corrigée}}-Y
$$

The evolution of the damage is given by:

$$
D=1-\frac{(1-A){Y}_{0}}{Y}-A\exp(-B(Y-{Y}_{0}))
$$

In this expression, it is the variables $A$ and $B$ that make it possible to reproduce the quasi-brittle behaviour of concrete in tension and the hardened behaviour in compression. In order to represent the experimental results as well as possible, the following evolution laws were chosen for $A$ and $B$ :

$$
A={A}_{t}({\mathrm{2r}}^{2}(1-\mathrm{2k})-r(1-\mathrm{4k}))+{A}_{c}({\mathrm{2r}}^{2}-\mathrm{3r}+1)
$$

and

$$
B={r}^{2}{B}_{t}+(1-{r}^{2}){B}_{c}
$$

where the expression of $r$ is:

$$
r=\frac{\sum_{i}{\langle {\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}\rangle }_{+}}{\sum_{i}\mid {\tilde{\sigma}}_{i}\mid }
$$

There appears in these equations a new variable $r$ which informs us about the stress state. When $r$ is equal to 1 (corresponding to the tension sector), the variables $A$ and $B$ are equivalent to the parameters ${A}_{t}$ and ${B}_{t}$ . Thus, is identical to . Conversely, if $r$ is zero (corresponding to the compression sector), then $A={A}_{c}$ , $B={B}_{c}$ and is identical to .

It gives, in the plane ${\sigma}_{3}=0$ , the evolution, as a function of the sign of the principal stresses, of the variables $A$ , $B$ , $r$ and $\gamma$ .

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.08/100000000000024E000001AD016D3A52FE7FC172.jpg)

Figure 2.2-2: Evolution of the variables $A$ , $B$ , $r$ and $\gamma$ in the plane ${\sigma}_{3}=0$

In the equation a new parameter appears: $k$ . It introduces an asymptote to the $\sigma -\epsilon$ curve in shear and it is defined by:

$$
k=\frac{{A}_{\text{cisaillement}}}{{A}_{t}}
$$

where ${A}_{\text{cisaillement}}$ defines the residual stress in pure shear. It is similar to ${A}_{t}$ for this loading case. The recommended value for $k$ is $0.7$ . A value of $k$ lower than 1 is very useful in the modelling of friction effects between the concrete and the reinforcement in reinforced-concrete structures, because it induces a residual shear stress. For the value $k=1$ the behaviour of the original model is recovered ().

The original model underestimates the strength of concrete in pure shear. This new formulation makes it possible to increase this strength in pure shear from $2.5\mathit{MPa}$ to $3.5\mathit{Mpa}$ for a C30 concrete. This value depends on those of the material parameters entered (${A}_{t}$ , ${A}_{c}$ , ${B}_{t}$ , and ${B}_{c}$ ).

## 3 Identification

In addition to the thermo-elastic parameters $E,\nu ,\alpha$ , the revisited MAZARS model involves 6 material parameters: ${A}_{c},{B}_{c},{A}_{t},{B}_{t},{\varepsilon}_{\mathit{d0}},k$ .

- ${\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{d0}}$ is the damage threshold. It obviously acts on the peak stress but also on the shape of the post-peak curve. Indeed, the drop in stress is the less abrupt the smaller ${\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{d0}}$ is. In general ${\varepsilon}_{\mathrm{d0}}$ lies between $0.5$ and $1.5{10}^{-4}$ .

The coefficients $A$ and $B$ make it possible to modulate the shape of the post-peak curve. They are defined by the equations and and which depend on the parameters of the original Mazars model (${A}_{t}$ , ${B}_{t}$ , ${A}_{c}$ and ${B}_{c}$ ) and on $r$ :

- $A$ introduces a horizontal asymptote which is the $\varepsilon$ axis for $A=1$ and the horizontal line passing through the peak for $A=0$ (cf.[]). In the tension domain, $A$ is equivalent to ${A}_{t}$ (and conversely in the compression domain $A={A}_{c}$ ). In general, ${A}_{c}$ lies between 1 and 2. and ${A}_{t}$ between 0.7 and 1.
- $B$ , according to its value, may correspond to an abrupt drop in stress ($B>10000$ ) or to a preliminary phase of increase in stress followed, after passing through a maximum, by a more or less rapid decrease, as can be seen in the []. In the tension domain, $B$ is equivalent to ${B}_{t}$ (and conversely in the compression domain $B={B}_{c}$ ). In general ${B}_{c}$ lies between $1000$ and $2000$ and ${B}_{t}$ between $9000$ and $21000$ .
- $k$ introduces a horizontal asymptote in pure shear on the stress–strain curve if its value is different from 1 for ${A}_{t}=1$ , . The recommended value is $0.7$ .

One way of obtaining a set of parameters is to have the results of uniaxial tests in compression and tension (for tension, other types of tests may be used, “Brazilian” splitting tests for example).

If strain-gradient regularisation is used (see § 1.2 ), it is recommended to fit the parameters of the law at the same time as the characteristic length ${L}_{c}$ . A few authors (confer ) also suggest calibrating ${L}_{c}$ by using experimental tests on several specimen sizes; indeed, the characteristic length is related to the size of the energy-dissipation zone, which could be the origin of structural size effects.

## 4 Numerical solution

### 4.1 Evaluation of the internal variable Y

The computation of $Y$ is very simple and follows an explicit scheme. The steps are the following:

- Computation of the elastic and thermal strains
- Computation of the principal elastic stresses and evaluation of $\gamma$  .
- Computation of the equivalent strain (  and  ).
- Computation of the variables $r$ *,* $A$ *and* $B$
- Computation of the internal variable $Y$ .

  - If $Y\le {Y}^{-}$ then ${Y}^{+}={Y}^{-}$ .

*If* $Y>{Y}^{-}$ *then* ${Y}^{+}=Y$ *.*

> Note: At present the variable stored during the analyses is ${\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}$ so as not to modify the existing couplings with UMLV. A condition on the strictly increasing evolution of the damage allows this simplification in the case where $\gamma$ varies.

### 4.2 Evaluation of the damage

> The damage is computed in all cases with the equation .

$$
D=1-\frac{\left(1-A\right){Y}_{0}}{Y}-A\exp\left(-B\left(Y-{Y}_{0}\right)\right)
$$

It is important to specify that $D$ is imposed to lie between 0 and 1, because it is possible to have values outside this range depending on the choice of the material parameters, as for the original model.

### 4.3 Computation of the stress

After evaluation of $D$ , one simply computes:

$$
\sigma =(1-D)\mathrm{A}{\varepsilon}^{\mathrm{e}}
$$

### 4.4 Computation of the tangent matrix

One disadvantage of the Mazars model is the absence of a tangent matrix. It is not possible to compute this matrix because of the use of the MacCauley operator in the computation of the equivalent strain , of $\gamma$  and $r$ . However, it is possible to use an approximation for radial loadings.

The tensor $\mathrm{M}$ such that $\dot{\sigma}=\mathrm{M}\dot{\varepsilon}$ is sought, knowing that $\sigma =(1-D)\mathrm{A}\varepsilon$ . The matrix is therefore the sum of two terms, one at constant damage, the other due to the evolution of the damage:

$$
\dot{\sigma}=(1-D)\mathrm{A}\dot{\varepsilon}-\mathrm{A}\varepsilon \dot{D}
$$

The first term is straightforward: it is the Hooke operator, multiplied by the factor $1-D$ .

The second requires the evaluation of the damage increment $\dot{D}$ .

If a radial loading is imposed, the variables $\gamma$ , $r$ , $A$ and $B$ are constant. Setting:

$$
\dot{D}=\frac{\partial D}{\partial Y}\frac{\partial Y}{\partial (\gamma {\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}})}\frac{\partial (\gamma {\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}})}{\partial \varepsilon }\dot{\varepsilon}
$$

With

$$
\frac{\partial Y}{\partial (\gamma {\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}})}\frac{\partial (\gamma {\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}})}{\partial \varepsilon }=\frac{\gamma {\langle \varepsilon \rangle }_{+}}{{\epsilon}_{\text{eq}}}
$$

Under this radial-loading condition, the strain increment is written:

$$
\dot{D}=\left[\frac{(1-A){Y}_{0}}{{Y}^{2}}+\text{AB}\exp(B(Y-{Y}_{0}))\right]\frac{\gamma {\langle \varepsilon \rangle }_{+}}{{\varepsilon}_{\text{eq}}}\dot{\varepsilon}
$$

| *1.* | *Given the simplifications made, in the general case the tangent matrix is not consistent. Also, it may happen that updating the tangent matrix during the Newton iterations does not help convergence. In that case, it is sufficient to use only the secant matrix by imposing* *STAT_NON_LINE(NEWTON = _F(REAC_ITER =0 )).* |
| --- | --- |
| *2.* | *In the general case, the tangent matrix is non-symmetric. It is* *possible to do so by means of the keyword* *SOLVEUR=_F(SYME = ‘OUI’)* *of* *STAT_NON_LINE* *.* |
| 3 *.* | *The analytical expression of the tangent matrix is valid only for radial loadings (* $\mathit{dr}=d\gamma =0$ *). In the other cases, the quadratic convergence of the method is no longer guaranteed.* |
