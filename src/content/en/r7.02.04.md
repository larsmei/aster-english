# r7.02.04 Beremin model

**Summary**

The foundations of the local-approach models of fracture, which make it possible to model brittle fracture, are first recalled.

It is described how, with the Beremin model, the fracture probability of a structure is computed from knowledge of the mechanical fields acting on it. Placing oneself in the general case of a non-monotonic thermomechanical loading path, and assuming that certain parameters of the models do not depend on temperature, the general expression for the cumulative fracture probabilities is established. The Beremin model also makes it possible to include a plastic-strain correction.

The industrial version of the Beremin model is briefly described.

Finally, indications concerning the numerical implementation of the Beremin model are summarised.

## 1 Introduction

The integrity of the structures of nuclear power plants, for all loading modes, normal or accidental, must be constantly ensured. In particular, for the most important structures such as the vessel or the primary circuit, the aim is to assess the mechanical strength, from the design stage, with respect to brittle and ductile fracture.

From the point of view of continued operation, the aim is also to justify the mechanical strength of the structures in the presence of a crack when defects have been detected during an inspection. This approach makes it possible to establish a schedule for repair or replacement of the components. Furthermore, certain components cannot be inspected, in which case a demonstration of the mechanical integrity in the presence of hypothetical defects must be carried out.

In this context, fracture mechanics provides the tools required for the analysis of cracked components. Its objective is to characterise the damage (crack, damage) in order to model each type of fracture, so as to be able to establish fracture criteria making it possible to judge the loading margins under normal or accidental operating conditions.

From a physical point of view, a metallic structure subjected to thermomechanical loading is considered. This document is concerned only with the brittle fracture of a structure, that is, when the material fails suddenly by cleavage.

The Beremin model is used. It relies on knowledge of the mechanical fields around the crack front in order to obtain a local fracture criterion representative of the physical mechanisms involved (instability of the cleavage microcracks).

### 1.1 Industrial context

From an engineering point of view, the resistance of a material to the propagation of a crack is measured by means of the toughness. Depending on the temperature and the strain rate, certain steels (in particular the ferritic steels of PWR vessels, or carbon-manganese steel for secondary piping) exhibit characteristics which are either brittle or ductile. At low temperatures, these steels fail in a brittle manner by cleavage, sometimes exhibiting intergranular decohesion, whereas at higher temperatures ductile tearing appears. The transition between these two mechanisms is characterised by the brittle/ductile transition temperature. Resilience measurements make it possible to establish criteria, such as the transition temperature, in order to define the domain of use of the material.

In the case of the ferritic steels of PWR vessels under normal operating conditions, the material is loaded in the ductile domain. However, during its operation, under the effect of neutron irradiation, the vessel will “age” and may become embrittled. Moreover, very exceptional accidental conditions, such as the cold thermal shock (Pressurized Thermal Shock, PTS) under pressure in the event of a loss of primary coolant (APRP), could cause the component to be loaded in the ductile-brittle transition domain. This is why it is important to determine the degree of embrittlement of the material by means of the ductile-brittle transition curve, and to know precisely the toughness of the material in the transition domain, in order to prevent all risks of sudden fracture.

Concerning the piping of the secondary circuit, the C-Mn steels which are used in the various backup circuits (ASG, Emergency Feedwater of the Steam Generators, for example) are sensitive to (static) strain ageing, which induces a shift of their ductile-brittle transition towards high temperatures. In order to predict the brittle fracture of these components in the presence of static ageing, several earlier studies have been carried out, making it possible to model the mechanical constitutive behaviour of the material taking strain ageing into account (see ,  ,  ).

These works have already shown that the cleavage fracture probability can be correctly described on the brittle shelf by a local approach to fracture such as that proposed by Beremin.

### 1.2 Value of the local approach

To justify the mechanical integrity of nuclear-power-plant components, the global approach is used first. The global approach aims to describe the loading conditions of a cracked component leading to fracture by means of a single parameter which depends on the geometry and on the loading. The parameter most commonly used in the domain of cleavage fracture (linear elastic case) is the mode I stress intensity factor, ${K}_{I}$ . Failure of the structure is obtained when this parameter reaches a critical value, the toughness ${K}_{\mathit{Ic}}$ . The safety analysis compares the stress intensity factor with minimum values of the toughness of the steel, which are established by numerous mechanical tests and gathered in the form of an RCC-M Appendix Z.G. reference curve. Throughout the operating life of the vessel, it is verified that the toughness properties, deduced from exploitation of the surveillance programme, are higher than those provided by the reference curve, indexed on a conventional transition temperature RTNDT (Reference Temperature for Nil Ductility Transition) .

The global approach is well validated and accepted, but it remains simple and conservative. It can therefore be too enveloping in certain particular cases, such as for example in the case of a “small-defect effect”, a “triaxiality effect”, a “warm pre-loading effect”, or in the case of non-proportional loading. This is why one may seek to use a finer local approach, which makes it possible to establish a link between the toughness of a material and the local fracture stress for a macroscopically homogeneous material.

An additional reason for using the Beremin-type local-approach method is explained by the toughness values, which exhibit a large scatter in the ductile-brittle transition domain. By introducing a statistical model, this approach makes it possible to explain and quantify the scatter inherent in these tests, via knowledge of local metallurgical parameters .

The industrial version of the Beremin model is, for its part, a deterministic approach which relies on the surface density of Weibull stress in order to characterise the stress field at the crack tip  .

## 2 Brittle fracture

### 2.1 Mechanisms

Brittle fracture is a sudden mode of fracture. It is generally associated with the phenomenon of cleavage. Cleavage is the complete decohesion of a crystal along one of its crystallographic planes. The theoretical local stress required for this separation of the crystal lattice ${K}_{\mathit{Ic}}$ is very high, and is expressed as a function of the interplanar spacing and of the surface energy:

However, the theoretical stress for separation of the atomic planes is never reached globally, but it can become so locally thanks to structures acting as stress amplifiers. In the case of steels, it is the numerous defects which play this role, whether they are due to plasticity at the crack tip (dislocations) or to the composition of the steel (inclusions, cracks) .

Fracture of steels by cleavage generally distinguishes three successive phases: (1) nucleation, (2) propagation and (3) the crossing of the microstructural barriers (grain boundaries, bainite-packet boundaries). Nucleation corresponds to the development of a microcrack inside the sound metal; it is generally accepted that this stage requires a fairly small prior plastic strain, which produces a pile-up of dislocations and a stress singularity, and the reaching of a limit fracture stress ${\sigma}_{c}$ (critical threshold). The phenomenon of nucleation of a microcrack can be explained by the various mechanisms of plasticity (Zener-Stroh, Cottrell, Smith, twinning) listed in .

Unstable propagation of the cleavage cracks is obtained, at high speed, which can sometimes be slowed by a relaxation mechanism by glide of the dislocations (blunting of cleavage by emission of dislocations) .

In order to be able to propagate in a polycrystal without being arrested by blunting, the crack must cross certain microstructural barriers (grain boundaries, interphases …) in order to lead to failure of the structure. At the microscopic level, the microstructural entity which controls fracture is not unique and depends strongly on the type of material.

All three stages contribute to the description of brittle fracture. However, depending on the test temperature and the geometry of the specimen (notched or cracked), one of these stages is predominant and controls the triggering of brittle fracture. Consequently, within the framework of taking the cleavage mechanisms into account in a fracture model, only one of these three stages is used to define a fracture criterion .

In the case of low-alloy steels at very low temperature ($T<-160°C$ ) fracture is controlled by the initiation phase, i.e. the nucleation of the microcracks. At higher temperature ($T>-160°C$ ) fracture is controlled by the propagation phase of the microcracks initiated on brittle second-phase particles (carbides, manganese sulphides, titanium nitrides) (,   ). The amplification of the stresses results from the strain incompatibilities between these particles and the matrix. This amplification of the stresses at the head of the dislocation pile-up can cause: fracture of the brittle second-phase particle (figure r7.02.04-amorcageFissure) or, in the case of a weak particle/matrix interface, decohesion of the interface (figure r7.02.04-decohesion).

Studies on the ferritic steels of vessels (16MND5) show that at low temperatures (negative temperatures), it is indeed the stage of propagation of the micro-defects (created during the initiation phase) into the surrounding ferritic matrix which constitutes the critical stage for propagating cleavage (see ,  and ). On the other hand, the origin of cleavage cannot be attributed solely to a single microstructural element (a chemical species). The influence of the microstructure of vessel steels is complex, since it can involve numerous parameters whose variation induces several consequences . Among these parameters, one thinks in particular of the grain size, the inclusion sizes and the distribution of defect sizes. One can therefore conclude that there exists a statistical aspect in terms of the population of defects leading to brittle fracture.

For more details on the influence of the microstructure on brittle fracture in low-alloy steels of 16MND5 type, one may refer to the works ,  and .

### 2.2 Statistical aspect of brittle fracture

Cleavage is a mode of fracture of a probabilistic nature, because the cleavage stress depends on the size distribution of the defects and the local mechanical fields depend on the local microstructure. Fracture initiates on the defect for which the critical stress is reached first. However, Ritchie, Knott and Rice  (RKR model) postulate that fracture occurs only if the critical stress at the crack tip is reached over a characteristic distance which represents approximately the size of two grains. Thus cleavage fracture is conditioned by a characteristic distance and a critical defect size. The RKR model represents the first attempt to relate the resistance to brittle fracture, expressed by the toughness, to a local fracture criterion based on the nucleation mechanisms . This model opened the way towards probabilistic models.

Subsequently, the works of Curry and Knott  demonstrated that the characteristic distance of the RKR model must not be correlated with the grain size but with the distribution of the carbides and with their sizes. Thus, for a fixed grain size, at a given test temperature, the toughness of the material increases when the inter-particle distance decreases. These works made possible the introduction of the notion of statistics into the brittle-fracture models . However, in both models the scale effect and the scatter of the experimental measurements associated with the brittle fracture mode are not taken into account.

In order to describe the scale effect and the scatter of the toughness measurements, statistical models were developed from the 1980s onwards. Among these models, the most commonly used are those of Beremin  and of Wallin . These models rest on the weakest-link theory and on a Weibull statistic (  and  ).

The Beremin model enjoys strong recognition at the international level. The Beremin approach benefits from substantial feedback from experience, owing both to its maturity and to the number of researchers and engineers who have tested and modified it. The model makes it possible to take into account numerous effects (small defect, triaxiality, warm pre-loading) in complex loading situations. Numerous applications have been considered, both on specimens and on structures.

## 3 Description of the Beremin model

In this section, the foundations of the Beremin model are first recalled. The assumptions linked to the definition of the potential cleavage sites are set out explicitly. The mechanism considered as critical is the propagation of a cleavage crack into the adjacent grain, itself considered as initiated by the mechanisms of plasticity. The fracture probability of these sites, which leads to the fracture probability of the structure, follows from this. The classical formulation of the Beremin model is extended to more general loadings by including a variant of plastic correction often used in this model .

The Beremin model relies on knowledge of the local mechanical fields loading the structure considered. A structure subjected to a history of thermomechanical loadings from the time instant $t=0$ fixed arbitrarily is considered. The aim is to determine the cumulative fracture probability of this structure at any instant.

By assumption, this structure consists (at least in part) of a steel liable to fail by cleavage at low temperature, exhibiting an elastoviscoplastic constitutive law, one of whose internal variables corresponds to the cumulative equivalent plastic strain: an example of a list of compatible laws is provided in the following paragraph.

### 3.1 Foundations

#### 3.1.1 General assumptions

The model considers a representative elementary volume ${\Omega}$ of the material and rests on several fundamental assumptions summarised in :

**Assumption 1**

It is assumed that the initiation of microcracks on the damage sites can occur only when plasticity is active (the cumulative plastic strain rate $\dot{p}(u)$ at instant $u$ must be positive $\dot{p}(u)>0$ ) and that their number no longer increases during the history of the loadings.

- Let us emphasise that this active-plasticity condition, which will be the one considered throughout the remainder of the document, is different from the condition classically adopted ( $p>0$). These two conditions are equivalent in the case of a monotonic loading path.
- For more general loading paths, this active-plasticity condition $\dot{p}>0$ leads on the contrary to much better results  .

**Notes**

**Assumption 2**

It is noted that only the maximum principal stress ${{\sigma}}_{I}$ is involved in the propagation of the defect, the defects then being considered as oriented perpendicularly to the principal direction.

**Assumption 3**

The propagation of microcracks is controlled by a criterion of Griffith type.

**Assumption 4**

Fracture will initiate from the most important defects in terms of size; only knowledge of the distribution of the latter is necessary.

**Assumption 5**

The loaded structure can be considered as the juxtaposition of several volume elements ${V}_{0}$ which are perfectly independent from the point of view of fracture. ${V}_{0}$ must be as small as possible in order to verify statistical independence, but large enough for the probability of finding there a defect of sufficient size to be reasonable (in practice ${V}_{0}$ includes a few grains).

**Assumption 6**

The weakest-link theory is used, according to which the fracture of one of the elementary volumes ${\delta}V$ entails the fracture of the whole of the structure.

## 4 Implementation

### 4.1 Computation of the fracture probability

Advice on the use of this model is given in the documentation [U2.05.08](doc:u2.05.08).

Consider a domain ${\Omega}_{c}$ of the structure studied, which may be the whole of the mesh studied, a mesh-cell group or a mesh cell. Following an elastoplastic thermomechanical analysis, the evolution of the fields of stress, of strain and of cumulative plastic strain in this domain is known. From the stress field at the tip of the crack (or of the notch), the aim is to determine the cumulative cleavage fracture probability of the structure (Beremin model) or the surface density of Weibull stress (industrial version of the Beremin model). Use of the model is made in post-processing by means of the macro-command [POST_BEREMIN](doc:u4.81.08).

Moreover, in the case of a filter on active plasticity or of any plastic correction, the constitutive law of the material must include an internal variable corresponding to the plastic indicator or to the cumulative equivalent plastic strain $p$ . These are in particular the laws (non-exhaustive list): VMIS_ISOT_*, VMIS_ECMI_*, VMIS_*_CHAB, ROUSS_*, LEMAITRE, MONOCRISTAL.

The corresponding numerical integration in code_aster is carried out in two stages:

- ${{\sigma}}_{I}$ is computed at each Gauss point
- the filters requested by the user are applied (active plasticity, threshold stress, maximum of the stresses over the history)
- by quadrature over each mesh cell, then by simple summation over the target domain ${\Omega}_{c}$ , the Weibull stress and the associated fracture probability are deduced from it. The summation is weighted by a multiplicative coefficient which takes into account any symmetries and the type of modelling adopted (axisymmetric, 2D, 3D..). Care will be taken to define this coefficient (COEF_MULT) in accordance with the indications given in [POST_BEREMIN](doc:u4.81.08).

The first stage makes it possible to introduce a variant (keyword SIGM_ELMOY instead of SIGM_ELGA) leading to appreciably different results in the case of a cracked structure (presence of a gradient): in each mesh cell, ${{\sigma}}_{I}$ is determined from the average, weighted by the Gauss weights over this mesh cell, of the stress field.

### 4.2 Parameters of the material

The Beremin model requires knowledge of several parameters: the characteristic parameters of the material considered in the Weibull law, $m$ and ${\sigma}_{u}$ (possibly the threshold stress $\sigma_th$) as well as the elementary volume of the elementary plastic zone ${V}_{0}$ . The elementary volume ${V}_{0}$ must be sufficiently large in order to comply with assumption (5). In the Weibull law, the parameters ${V}_{0}$ and ${{\sigma}}_{u}$ are not independent: it is the product ${V}_{0}{{\sigma}}_{u}^{m}$ which enters the computation of the fracture probability.

The identification of these parameters is not unique, but depends strongly on the computed stress fields.

### 4.3 Projection for the industrial version of the Beremin model

The various stages making it possible to compute the surface density of Weibull stresses are presented below.

1. The integrand of the Weibull stress is computed over the volume specified by the user, as a function of the various filters or options requested (active plasticity, threshold stress, average per element, and maximum of the stress history). This stage is therefore identical for the Beremin model and for its industrial version.
2. The volume integrand is then projected onto the integration plane. For example, in the case of a formulation with a threshold stress, it is the quantity $\left< \sigma_I - \sigma_{th}\right>_+^m$ which is projected.
3. The surface integral is then computed in order to obtain $\sigma_W^{2D}$

Thus, for each integration plane, it is necessary to compute a projector making it possible to pass from a field expressed at the Gauss points of the 3D model to a field expressed on the 2D mesh of the integration plane. To this end, the field expressed at the Gauss points is transformed into fields by cells on the dual mesh. Each cell of the 2D mesh is located by its centre, and one identifies to which 3D cell or cells this centre belongs (to within a numerical precision). These operations are carried out in medcoupling.

Construction of this projector can be relatively slow, but it is carried out only once per plane. In the case of a sensitivity analysis with respect to the material parameters, it is therefore strongly recommended to supply all the material parameters in the same call to POST_BEREMIN.

## 5 Alternative approaches

Among the local models introduced into code_aster ([R7.02.06](doc:r7.02.06) and [U2.05.08](doc:u2.05.08)) which make it possible to describe brittle fracture by cleavage, there exists the Bordet model (see  and [R7.02.06](doc:r7.02.06)). This model represents an extension of the Beremin model in which it is assumed that the microcracks created at the moment when the plasticity threshold is reached remain potentially active throughout the loading which follows. However, in steels, global fracture is mainly linked to newly created microcracks. The level of plastic strain reached at each instant must therefore be taken into account. This option is possible in the Beremin model *via* the plastic-correction option . The effect of plasticity on the micro-defects is taken into account in the Bordet model by considering that the cleavage probability is expressed as the product of the probabilities of nucleation and of propagation at the same instant.

Among the other alternative approaches there is the Gp approach, which makes it possible to define a criterion for initiation of an already existing crack through the computation of the elastic energy-release rate. This approach is valid in linear elasticity as well as in non-linear elasticity.  [R7.02.16](doc:r7.02.16)
