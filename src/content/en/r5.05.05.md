# r5.05.05 Non-linear dynamic algorithm

**Summary:**

The operator DYNA_NON_LINE [DYNA_NON_LINE](doc:u4.53.01) is used for the non-linear dynamic analysis of structures by direct time integration. The non-linearities may come from the material behaviour, from the constraints (frictional contact), or from large geometric transformations (large displacements and large rotations).

The organisation of DYNA_NON_LINE is very similar to that of the non-linear quasi-static operator STAT_NON_LINE [r5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01). *A priori*, all the constitutive relations developed in the framework of STAT_NON_LINE work in that of DYNA_NON_LINE.

One presents here the general formulation of the non-linear dynamic problem in order to specify the links between the purely dynamic aspects and those already treated in other operators or formulations available in *code_aster*: management of the boundary conditions, of the fluid–structure couplings, of the damping, of the computation in a relative frame, then the properties of the numerical time-integration scheme, which is developed independently of any constitutive relation. One sets out how the latter is articulated with the Newton algorithm in order to treat the material and geometric non-linearities.

Three complementary and efficient implicit time schemes are available:

- the Newmark family,
- the modified average acceleration (“HHT” in DYNA_NON_LINE, with the option MODI_EQUI='NON'),
- the complete HHT scheme (“HHT” in DYNA_NON_LINE, with the option MODI_EQUI='OUI'), as well as its “without overshooting” version proposed in ('NOHHT' in DYNA_NON_LINE).

The operator also proposes two explicit schemes:

- the central differences,
- the dissipative scheme of Tchamwa–Wielgosz.

A few pieces of advice are given for proper use, which the documentation [General advice on the use of the operator DYNA_NON_LINE](doc:u2.06.13) complements.

## 2 Non-linear dynamics: spatial discretisation of the continuous problem

Solving a non-linear dynamic problem requires first describing the equations of the continuous problem, then presenting their spatial discretisation, here by finite elements, and finally describing the method of time integration, associated with the treatment of the material and geometric non-linearities.

### 2.1 Discretisation of the linear dynamic problem

One denotes by $u$ the field of absolute displacements with respect to the reference configuration, and parameterised by the time instant $t$, belonging to the affine space of the admissible fields ${V}_{\text{adm}}$.

The direct method consists in solving the problem resulting from the finite-element discretisation of the displacement formulation.

The discretisation of the virtual work of the inertia forces, in a field $\delta v\in {V}_{\text{adm}}^{0}$, the directing vector space of ${V}_{\text{adm}}$, is written:

${\int}_{\Omega}\rho \ddot{u}.\delta vd\Omega {=}^{t}\delta V.M.\ddot{U}$

The discretisation of the virtual variation of the work dissipated by viscosity (damping brought by a dependence of the stresses on the strain rates) is:

${\int}_{\Omega}c\dot{u}.\delta vd\Omega {=}^{t}\delta V.C.\dot{U}$

One specifies in r5.05.05-terme-inertie how the damping operator $\mathrm{C}$ is built in DYNA_NON_LINE.

The discretisation of the virtual work of the internal forces in linear elasticity is written:

${\int}_{\Omega}\varepsilon (u).A.\varepsilon (\delta v)d\Omega {=}^{t}\delta V.K.U$

Finally, $\mathrm{L}$ denotes the right-hand side resulting from the discretisation of the virtual work of the external forces.

In linear elasticity, this therefore leads one to consider the following hyperbolic differential-algebraic system, for the degrees of freedom $\mathrm{U}$, with the initial conditions:

$\begin{array}{}\text{Trouver}U\in {ℝ}^{n}:\\ M.\ddot{U}+C.\dot{U}+K.U=L\\ U({t}_{0})={U}_{0}\\ \dot{U}({t}_{0})={\dot{U}}_{0}\end{array}$

accompanied by boundary conditions.

The initial conditions are supplied to the algorithm by the keyword ETAT_INIT (operands DEPL and VITE).

If the initial state results from a linear or non-linear static simulation, one does not take an initial velocity into account, and the displacement, as well as the state variables (stresses, internal variables), are extracted from the result of this simulation at the starting time instant considered.

The dynamic equilibrium system becomes unstable if one can find a complex angular frequency $\omega$, whose imaginary part is negative, for which one can cancel the determinant of: $-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{M}+i\omega \mathrm{C}+\mathrm{K}$.

### 2.2 Discretisation of the non-linear dynamic problem

One now places oneself in a non-linear mechanical framework.

One denotes the virtual work $\underset{\Omega}{\int}\sigma (\mathrm{u},\dot{\mathrm{u}},t).\varepsilon (\delta \mathrm{v})d\Omega$ of deformation (also called that of the internal forces) of the non-linear mechanical problem. However one frequently adds to the internal forces coming from the constitutive law the internal forces of viscous damping built in a global manner, *cf.* [r5.05.05-amortissement]: $C\cdot \dot{U}$, where $\mathrm{C}$ is the damping matrix. The virtual work of the internal forces is therefore written after discretisation:

${}^{t}\delta \mathrm{V}.\left(\mathrm{R}(\mathrm{U},\dot{\mathrm{U}},t)+\mathrm{C}.\dot{\mathrm{U}}\right){=}^{t}\delta \mathrm{V}.\left({}^{t}\mathrm{Q}\left(\mathrm{U}\right).\sigma \left(\mathrm{U},\dot{\mathrm{U}},t\right)+\mathrm{C}.\dot{\mathrm{U}}\right)$

where one has deliberately distinguished the linear viscous forces (operator $\mathrm{C}$) from the other internal forces. In the case of small displacements, the assembled strain operator ${}^{t}\mathrm{Q}$ is constant (and defined on the initial configuration, coincident with the deformed configuration).

The mechanical energy balance is written:

$\mathrm{L}.\delta \mathrm{v}={\int}_{\Omega}\rho \ddot{\mathrm{u}}.\delta \mathrm{v}d\Omega +{\int}_{\Omega}\sigma \left(\mathrm{u},\dot{\mathrm{u}},t\right).\epsilon \left(\delta \mathrm{v}\right)d\Omega$

The stress field $\sigma$ at the time instant $t$ is written in a general way $\sigma (\mathrm{u},\dot{\mathrm{u}},\mathrm{Z},t,\mathrm{H})$, if one denotes by $Z$ the field of command variables, such as for example $T$ the temperature field, and by $\mathrm{H}$ the past history of the structure up to the time instant $t$. For incremental behaviours, the history is the set of states (displacement, stress and internal-variable fields) at the previous time instant.

In the linear case (*cf.* r5.05.05-discretisation-dynamique-lineaire), the virtual work of deformation becomes:

$\mathrm{R}\left(\mathrm{U},\dot{\mathrm{U}}\right)+\mathrm{C}.\dot{\mathrm{U}}=\mathrm{K}.\mathrm{U}+\mathrm{C}.\dot{\mathrm{U}}$

where $\mathrm{K}$ is the elastic stiffness matrix of the structure.

#### 2.2.1 Inertia term

One denotes by ${}^{t}\delta \mathrm{V}.\mathrm{M}\left(\mathrm{U},\dot{\mathrm{U}},\ddot{\mathrm{U}}\right)$, after discretisation, the virtual work ${\int}_{\Omega}\rho \ddot{\mathrm{u}}.\delta \mathrm{v}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}d\Omega$ of the inertia forces of the system.

One denotes by $M$ the mass matrix of the system in small transformations. In large structural rotations (as for example beams, *cf.* [r5.03.40](doc:r5.03.40)), this virtual work is a non-linear function of $U(t)$ and of its time derivatives (specifically of the rotation degrees of freedom); one therefore brings out the usual acceleration term with a non-linear correction: $\mathrm{M}\left(\mathrm{U},\dot{\mathrm{U}},\ddot{\mathrm{U}}\right)=\mathrm{M}\left(\mathrm{U}\right).\ddot{\mathrm{U}}+{\mathrm{L}}_{\text{GR}}^{\text{iner}}\left(\mathrm{U},\dot{\mathrm{U}},\ddot{\mathrm{U}}\right)$. In the other cases, $\mathrm{M}\left(\mathrm{U},\dot{\mathrm{U}},\ddot{\mathrm{U}}\right)=\mathrm{M}\left(\mathrm{U}\right).\ddot{\mathrm{U}}$, which may vary if one updates the geometry, or which is constant in small displacements.

**Note: case of the geometric update (** DEFORMATION='PETIT_REAC' **):**

*For a dynamic problem with geometric update, having admitted conservation of mass, one notes that the mass matrix is not modified. Indeed: denoting respectively by* $det\mathrm{J}(\xi )$ *and* $\det{J}_{\mathrm{RG}}(\xi )$ *the determinant of the Jacobian matrix of the transformation from the real element* ${\Omega}_{e}$ *to the reference element* ${\Omega}_{r}$ *and that of the transformation from the updated real element* ${\Omega}_{\mathrm{RG}}$ to the reference element –cf. [r3.01.00](doc:r3.01.00), §3.5– the virtual work of the inertia forces is written:

${\int}_{{\Omega}_{\mathit{RG}}}{\rho}_{\mathit{RG}}\ddot{\mathrm{u}}.\delta \mathrm{v}d{\Omega}_{\mathit{RG}}={\int}_{{\Omega}_{r}}{\rho}_{\mathit{RG}}\ddot{\mathrm{u}}.\delta \mathrm{v}.det{\mathrm{J}}_{\mathit{RG}}(\xi )d{\Omega}_{r}={\int}_{{\Omega}_{r}}\rho \ddot{\mathrm{u}}.\delta \mathrm{v}.det\mathrm{J}(\xi )d{\Omega}_{r}$

*because* $\rho_{\mathrm{RG}} = \rho \cdot \det J_{\mathrm{RG}}^{\mathrm{EL}} = \rho \cdot \frac{\det J}{\det J_{\mathrm{RG}}}$ as a consequence of the conservation of mass in the update (Jacobian ${J}_{\mathrm{RG}}^{\mathrm{EL}}$).

#### 2.2.2 Damping

It is permissible to use discrete elements on which one places a damping behaviour via a matrix acting on the degrees of freedom, *cf.* [u4.42.01](doc:u4.42.01), but the damping may also concern solid or structural models. The damping operator $\mathrm{C}$ of the latter may be defined in *code_aster* in two ways in DYNA_NON_LINE, *cf.* also [r5.05.04](doc:r5.05.04), [u2.06.03](doc:u2.06.03):

| **1)** | **a global way on a basis of eigenmodes** $({\Phi}_{k})$ **, called modal damping** (according to the BASILE hypothesis) established beforehand on the elastic structure, expressed on the “physical” basis of the finite-element modelling. One thus defines a coefficient per chosen mode. The keyword: AMOR_MODAL of the operator DYNA_NON_LINE makes it possible to supply it with the modal basis and the reduced damping coefficients (according to the BASILE hypothesis). Indeed, the dampings are determined experimentally by modal analysis on the resonances. The displacements $\mathrm{U}$ are therefore projected onto the modes in order to obtain their generalised coordinates: ${\eta}^{k}{=}^{t}{\Phi}_{k}.\mathrm{U}$. The modal damping matrix is: $\mathrm{C}=\left(\mathrm{K}{\Phi}_{k}\right).{C}_{\text{modal}}^{k}{.}^{t}\left(\mathrm{K}{\Phi}_{k}\right)$ with the scalar ${C}_{\text{modal}}^{k}=2\frac{{\xi}_{k}}{{\omega}_{k}{.}^{t}{\Phi}_{k}.K.{\Phi}_{k}}$ where ${\xi}_{k}$ is the modal damping factor at the angular frequency ${\omega}_{k}$ and ${k}_{k}{=}^{t}{\Phi}_{k}.K.{\Phi}_{k}$ is the generalised stiffness of mode $k$. Unfortunately this matrix may have a very full profile and make costly (the matrices $C$ and $K$ not having the same profile), as will be seen in [r5.05.05-schema-newmark], its integration into the left-hand side: one will then choose to treat these modal damping forces $-\mathrm{C}.\dot{\mathrm{U}}\left(t\right)$ on the right-hand side by an explicit scheme. The validity of this modal damping modelling is acquired if one can neglect the couplings between eigenmodes. One must therefore make sure that the following criterion is verified: $\underset{k,\ell }{\text{Min}}(\frac{{\xi}_{k}{\omega}_{k}}{\mid {\omega}_{k}-{\omega}_{\ell}\mid })\text{<<}1$ |
| --- | --- |
| **2)** | **a global/local way called proportional viscous damping** (according to the RAYLEIGH hypothesis) from the elastic stiffness matrices $K$ and mass matrices $M$. The parameters are given by the material on the finite elements of the model (keywords AMOR_ALPHA/AMOR_BETA of the command DEFI_MATERIAU). The viscous damping matrix is $C=\alpha K+\beta M$. It is diagonalisable on the basis of the real eigenmodes, which makes it possible to carry out a transient analysis on a modal basis by decoupling the modes: see [r5.06.04](doc:r5.06.04). This formulation, in the linear case, leads to a damping factor linked to the frequency $f$: $\xi =\alpha \pi f+\beta /\left(4\pi f\right)$. In the non-linear case, this evaluation no longer holds. One adjusts in practice the coefficients $\alpha ,\beta$ so that the damping $\xi$ is almost uniform in the range $[{f}_{1,}{f}_{2}]$ of frequencies of interest for the structure studied. Whence, in a reasonable way: $\alpha =\frac{\xi}{\pi ({f}_{1}+{f}_{2})}$ and $\beta =\frac{4\pi \cdot \xi \cdot {f}_{1}\cdot {f}_{2}}{{f}_{1}+{f}_{2}}$ This modelling of the damping is not very realistic if the structure responds over a wide frequency band, because the damping introduced then varies a great deal over the range studied. If the constitutive law of the material is non-linear and dissipative, the choice of the damping parameters concerns the range where the structure remains almost elastic. During a non-linear transient analysis, one will use for the solution an assembled stiffness matrix which may be the tangent matrix (updated at each step, for example) or the elastic matrix. Historically, for practical reasons, one built the Rayleigh damping using the stiffness matrix as it was specified by the user for the computation of the internal forces. Thus if the user chose the tangent matrix, one had: $\mathrm{C}=\alpha {\mathrm{K}}^{\mathrm{T}}+\beta \mathrm{M}$ This makes the interpretation of the effect of proportional damping delicate. In particular in the event of the appearance of negative eigenvalues of the matrix ${K}^{T}$ (for example in the event of damage of the material), the damping may become negative and reinforce the instabilities! Without going that far, on a simple elasto-plastic example, one observes a drop in the viscous damping because the tangent stiffness drops. This loss of damping may be partly compensated by the material dissipation, but the total dissipation level will be poorly controlled. This is why it was decided, in version 10 of the code, to give the user the choice for the definition of Rayleigh damping. Either one adopts the stiffness matrix already used for the internal forces (as in the previous versions of the code), or one specifies the use of the elastic matrix. This second choice is particularly recommended for softening laws and for global laws of GLRC type, cf. [R7.01.32]. |

#### 2.2.3 Constraints

In practice, one may have bilateral or unilateral constraint conditions, or constraints of “impedance” or “absorbing” type, *cf.* [r4.02.05](doc:r4.02.05)*.*

**Bilateral constraints**

The bilateral constraints are written in the form of the following relation: $\mathrm{B}.\mathrm{u}={\mathrm{u}}^{\mathrm{d}}\left(t\right)$.

They are perfect: they do not dissipate energy. They are “holonomic” constraints, in which the velocity $\dot{U}$ does not intervene. These constraints are in general dualised by *Code_Aster*, *cf.* [r3.03.01](doc:r3.03.01).

**Unilateral constraints**

The mechanical system which is the object of the finite-element simulation may come into contact (unilateral constraints) with an “obstacle”, which is a solid whose motion is known a priori, whence the definition of a gap ${d}_{0}(t)$. The unilateral constraints (for example unilateral contact) are written on the configuration at every time instant $t$: $\mathrm{A}\left(\mathrm{u}\right).\mathrm{u}\left(t\right)\le {\mathrm{d}}_{0}\left(t\right)$ (non-penetration, or verification that the effective gap remains positive or zero in every configuration). The operator $\mathrm{A}\left(\mathrm{u}\right)$ may depend on the configuration in the presence of large displacements by update during the time step, *cf.* [r5.03.50](doc:r5.03.50).

One will consider only “holonomic” constraints, of the type $A(U,t)=0$, which involve only the values of the degrees of freedom $U(t)$ and the time explicitly if the obstacle is mobile. One will not consider “non-holonomic” constraints, for example of the rolling-without-sliding type, which involve the velocity explicitly and are written ${\mathrm{A}}_{1}\left(\mathrm{U},t\right).\dot{\mathrm{U}}+{\mathrm{A}}_{2}\left(\mathrm{U},t\right)=0$, ${\mathrm{A}}_{2}$ and the direct dependence on time being present only if there is a mobile obstacle.

If the obstacle is immobile, the constraint as such will be explicitly independent of time (one also says “scleronomic”).

These “contact loads” are defined by the operator DEFI_CONTACT. The presence of unilateral constraints requires the velocities of the solid to be defined in a particular function space in order to ensure the existence of solutions of the system of dynamics. Indeed, at the (countable) instants of impact, the velocities $\dot{U}({t}^{-})$ and $\dot{U}({t}^{+})$ may not coincide. In order to guarantee this result it is necessary that the data (loading, constraint equations) verify a property of analyticity, which is acquired in practice with the finite-element discretisation chosen (see ).

One does not introduce *a priori* a constitutive relation of the impacts (dissipative behaviour in simple rebound, expressed by means of a normal coefficient of restitution $e\in \left[0,1\right]$), expressed on the velocity differentials of the two points in impact:

${\dot{\mathrm{U}}}_{2\text{\_norm}}^{+}\left(t\right)={\dot{\mathrm{U}}}_{1\text{\_norm}}^{+}\left(t\right)-e\left({\dot{\mathrm{U}}}_{2\text{\_norm}}^{-}\left(t\right)-{\dot{\mathrm{U}}}_{1\text{\_norm}}^{-}\left(t\right)\right)$

This type of behaviour is usually introduced in order to treat the contact-impact of rigid bodies, whereas the numerical modelling with deformable solids makes it possible to represent directly the vibratory behaviour under the shock and the material non-linearities. But it is possible to add in the modelling discrete contact-shock elements placed on the interface in contact, equipped with the law DIS_CHOC, which bring a damping dissipation (provided that one assumes small motions…).

One may adjoin to the unilateral constraints a friction behaviour (COULOMB criterion), which dissipates energy in relative sliding of the surfaces in contact.

One sometimes refers to the fact that the dynamic friction coefficient is smaller than that measured in quasi-statics (adhesion), see , . This comes from the fact that in dynamics high-frequency vibrations appear on the normal reaction and weaken the value of the COULOMB friction threshold. One would therefore not need to supply two values of coefficients to *Code_Aster*, since one models the deformable solids in frictional contact (provided that one can simulate these high-frequency vibrations, which are generally attenuated by the time-integration schemes…).

One knows that dissipation is a necessary condition for the existence of theoretical solutions to the dynamic problem with impact . The use of the HHT scheme, see [r5.05.05-variante-schema-newmark], which introduces numerical dissipation may prove necessary.

**Local dissipative constraints**

Specific relations (such as DIS_CHOC) are designed to treat certain types of dissipative point constraints, acting directly on the degrees of freedom of discrete elements of the system, see [r5.03.17](doc:r5.03.17). They constitute a constitutive law in generalised forces as a function of generalised displacements, integrated like the set of internal forces $R(U,\dot{U},t)$ of the structures studied.

**Absorbing constraints**

The constraints of “absorbing” type, *cf.* [r4.02.05](doc:r4.02.05), make it possible to simulate the “filtering” of part of the dynamic response, by preventing the exit of reflected and diffracted waves, and avoid the “incident” field being disturbed on a boundary of the model: see [r5.05.05-equations-mouvement]. They introduce damping terms of the type ${A}_{\text{abso}}(\dot{u})$ on a boundary of the solid considered.

#### 2.2.4 Discretised dynamic problem

The dualisation of the DIRICHLET boundary conditions $B.u={u}^{d}(t)$ and of the unilateral conditions leads after discretisation to defining the unknowns at every time instant $t$: $(U,\lambda ,\mu )$, where $\lambda$ represents the “LAGRANGE multipliers” of the DIRICHLET boundary conditions [r3.03.01](doc:r3.03.01), and $\mu$ represents the “Lagrange multipliers” of the unilateral conditions.

The non-linear dynamic problem is written, with the initial conditions [R3.03.01], [r5.03.50](doc:r5.03.50):

Find the trajectory $U(t)$:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{}M(U,\dot{U},\ddot{U})+R(U,\dot{U},t)+C.\dot{U}{+}^{t}B.\lambda {+}^{t}A.\mu =L(t)\\ B.U={U}_{d}(t)\\ A.U\le {d}_{0}(t)\\ \forall j,{\mu}_{j}\ge 0\\ \forall j,(A.U-{d}_{0}{)}_{j}.{\mu}_{j}=0\\ U({t}_{0})={U}_{0}\\ \dot{U}({t}_{0})={\dot{U}}_{0}\end{array} \right.
$$

$L$ represents the vector of the external forces (mechanical loadings). These forces may depend on time and on space. One assumes that, like the constraints, they depend in a regular way on the parameters, which ensures the existence of the solution of the problem (CAUCHY theorem). One may consider “follower” forces $L(U,t)$, for example the pressure, if one takes the changes of geometry into account.

The vector ${}^{t}B.\lambda$ is interpreted as the opposite of the support reactions at the corresponding nodes ($B$ is the linear operator expressing the passage to the degrees of freedom of the supports). The vector ${}^{t}A.\mu$ is interpreted as the nodal forces due to contact ($B$ is the linear operator expressing the passage to the degrees of freedom of the zones in contact).

The stability analysis of the dynamic equilibrium system is more complex than in the linear case, but a sufficient condition for loss of stability is the possibility of finding an angular frequency $\omega$ with negative imaginary part for which one can cancel the determinant of: $-{\omega}^{2}M+i\omega {C}^{T}+{K}^{T}$, defined on the tangent operators, at the time instant considered.

#### 2.2.5 Initial conditions

The initial conditions ${U}_{0},{\dot{U}}_{0}$ are supplied to the code by the keyword ETAT_INIT (operands DEPL and VITE).

If the initial state results from a linear or non-linear static simulation, the displacement, as well as the state variables (stresses, internal variables), are extracted from the result of this simulation, and the initial velocity is by default assumed to be zero.

### 2.3 Taking into account a prestressed initial state

If the dynamic problem to be solved “follows” an initial mechanical state, two main situations are open to us:

- one wishes to compute the displacements $u$ and the dynamic stresses from the initial “virgin” state, where all the fields are zero;
- one wishes to compute the displacements $u$ and the dynamic stresses in “differential” form from a pre-loaded state, in the elastic framework.

1. In the first situation, one should beforehand carry out the computation of the static state, possibly non-linear (material, large transformations), prior to the dynamics.

Thus, if the structure has a non-linear behaviour, one must proceed directly in non-linear dynamics, after having evaluated the initial state by a static simulation, possibly non-linear (with the operator STAT_NON_LINE), and the displacement field is evaluated from the beginning of the history. It may be necessary to take the variations of geometry into account. The information necessary to describe the initial state (results of a previous simulation via the result concept EVOL_NOLI or the necessary mechanical fields: DEPL, SIGM, VARI) is supplied by the keyword ETAT_INIT, for example if one is in the framework of an incremental behaviour, see [u4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03).

The initial state may also be obtained by a “very slow” dynamic simulation, taking care to put a “slow” ramp of time dependence on the applied static forces, as well as a strong damping (physical, *cf.* [r5.05.05-terme-inertie] and/or numerical *cf.* [r5.05.05-variante-schema-newmark]). This way of proceeding has the advantage of injecting both into the operator of the prediction phase of the NEWTON algorithm, and into that of the correction phase, terms $\widehat{\mathrm{K}}$ coming from the mass matrix and from the damping matrix, in order to establish the static mechanical state. This is valuable in situations of frictional contact, of damage… in order to improve the convergence.

1. The second situation concerns the case of a structure which has undergone an “ordinary” thermo-mechanical pre-loading, leading to a linear elastic equilibrium state. If one measures by $\mathrm{u}$ the displacement from this pre-loaded state, which has generated a stress state ${\sigma}_{1}$, then the elastic strain energy is completed by a geometric stiffness term:

${\int}_{\Omega}\epsilon \left(\mathrm{u}\right)\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}.\mathrm{A}.\epsilon \left(\delta \mathrm{v}\right)\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}d\Omega +{\int}_{\Omega}{\sigma}_{1}.\nabla \mathrm{u}\otimes \nabla \left(\delta \mathrm{v}\right)\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}d\Omega {=}^{t}\delta \mathrm{V}.\left(\mathrm{K}+{\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{g}}\right)\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}.\mathrm{U}$

One then simply assembles the matrices $\mathrm{K}$ and ${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{g}}$, and one carries out the solution in linear dynamics, as in [r5.05.05-discretisation-dynamique-lineaire]. This may be for example the case of a seismic study on an arch dam. With DYNA_NON_LINE, it is necessary to supply by the keyword ETAT_INIT the stress field SIGM resulting from the pre-loaded state and to specify at the level of the keyword DEFORMATION under COMPORTEMENT the taking into account of the non-linear strain terms.

It is frequent that the contribution of ${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{g}}$ is negligible: one may then content oneself with a dynamic analysis on the basis of a totally virgin initial state. One will also note that the computation of the matrix ${\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{g}}$ is not available for all the finite elements proposed by *Code_Aster*, see the docs. [u3].

### 2.4 Coupled fluid–structure vibro-acoustic problems

For more details one may refer to the documents [r4.02.02](doc:r4.02.02), [r4.02.04](doc:r4.02.04), [r4.02.05](doc:r4.02.05).

One considers the small motions in an Eulerian approach of a perfect compressible fluid, possibly bathing a wall of a solid structure. The fluid is said to be barotropic:

1. one considers small irrotational perturbations about the initial (hydrostatic) state: ${\vec{\mathrm{U}}}_{\text{fl}}={\vec{\mathrm{u}}}_{\text{fl}}^{0}+{\vec{\mathrm{u}}}_{\text{fl}}$, $P={P}_{0}+p$ and ${\rho}_{f}={\rho}_{0}+\rho$, the index 0 denoting the permanent part of the fields,
2. the constitutive law of the fluid gives the “fluctuating” stresses: $\sigma =-p\text{Id}=-\rho {c}_{0}^{2}\text{Id}\approx {\rho}_{0}{c}_{0}^{2}\left(div\vec{{\mathrm{U}}_{\text{fl}}}\right)\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}\text{Id}$, because ${\rho}_{0}={\rho}_{f}\left(1+div{\vec{\mathrm{U}}}_{\text{fl}}\right)$,
3. the fluid velocities derive from a potential ${\vec{\mathrm{v}}}_{\text{fl}}={\dot{\vec{\mathrm{u}}}}_{\text{fl}}=\overrightarrow{\nabla \dot{\phi }}$ and are modelled with the aid of the fields $\left(p,\phi \right)$: fluctuating pressure and displacement potential, which are not independent because: $\dot{p}=-{\rho}_{0}{c}_{0}^{2}\Delta \dot{\phi }$ (by combining the continuity equation and the constitutive law).

One admits that one does not consider fluctuating body forces $\vec{f}$ exerted on the fluid. The dynamic equilibrium of the fluid is written: $\vec{\nabla p}+{\rho}_{f}{\vec{\ddot{\mathrm{u}}}}_{\text{fl}}=\vec{0}$ (linearised Euler equation), which is valid for a non-heavy compressible fluid or a heavy incompressible fluid; on the other hand, for a heavy compressible fluid, the Eulerian and Lagrangian approaches do not coincide even in small motions: this case is not treated by *Code_Aster*.

The dynamic equilibrium of the fluid will be written in variational form under the action of a fluctuating pressure ${p}_{\text{imp}}$ imposed on a part of the boundary. In the harmonic regime, the dynamic equilibrium of the fluid is expressed by the variational formulation of the Helmholtz equation, .

*Code_Aster* possesses a symmetrised formulation, see [r4.02.02](doc:r4.02.02), with elements $(P,\varphi )$ where $P$ is the vector of the pressure degrees of freedom and $\varphi$ is the potential for describing the perturbations (fluctuations) in the fluid, knowing that $\overrightarrow{\nabla p}+{\rho}_{0}\overrightarrow{\nabla \ddot{\varphi}}=\overrightarrow{0}$. An equation in $P$ solves the dynamic equilibrium in the fluid domain, that in $\varphi$ expressing the derived continuity equation combined with the fluid constitutive law. Boundary conditions in $P$ and $\varphi$ complete the system of equations in order to describe the evolutions of the fluid. Thus, because of the formulation $(P,\phi )$, if on a boundary $\partial {\Omega}_{\text{f\_p}}$ of the fluid domain a fluctuating pressure is applied: $P={P}_{\text{imp}}(t)$, one must also impose there a condition on $\varphi$: $\varphi (t)={\varphi}_{\text{imp}}(t)$, verifying ${\rho}_{0}{\ddot{\varphi}}_{\text{imp}}(t)=-{P}_{\text{imp}}(t)$.

As one envisages a **common fluid–structure boundary** $\partial {\Omega}_{\text{FS}}$, where one defines the normal $\overrightarrow{n}$ outgoing from the structure domain towards the fluid, the wall loading of the fluid is coupled with the displacement of the structure. The normal displacements are continuous: $(\overrightarrow{{u}_{\mathrm{fl}}}+{\overrightarrow{u}}_{\mathrm{fl}}^{0})\cdot \overrightarrow{n}={\overrightarrow{u}}_{\mathrm{st}}\cdot \overrightarrow{n}$ on $\partial {O}_{\text{FS}}$, ${\overrightarrow{u}}_{\text{fl}}^{0}$ denoting the permanent part of the fluid displacement. Using the fluid potential, one has: $\overrightarrow{\nabla \dot{\varphi}}.\overrightarrow{n}={\overrightarrow{\dot{u}}}_{\text{st}}.\overrightarrow{n}$ on $\partial {\Omega}_{\text{FS}}$. In a dual manner, the stress vectors are continuous: $-p\overrightarrow{n}=\sigma .\overrightarrow{n}$ on $\partial {\Omega}_{\text{FS}}$. The structure thus receives the fluctuating loading of the fluid: ${\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{\text{FS}}}p\partial \overrightarrow{v}.(-\overrightarrow{n})\text{dS}$.

If one envisages a **free surface** (subjected to a constant pressure), also treated in an Eulerian description, see [r4.02.04](doc:r4.02.04), one denotes by $z$ (and $Z$ the associated degrees of freedom after discretisation) the (small) fluctuating altitude of the free surface $\partial {\Omega}_{\text{SL}}$ and the Eulerian pressure fluctuation in the fluid verifies: ${\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{\text{SL}}}(p-{\rho}_{0}\text{gz})\delta z\text{dS}$, in any virtual altitude $\delta z$. This is the only consequence of gravity that one can take into account in this formulation.

Moreover, one may need to take into account an artificial boundary with an infinite medium (which must treat the radiation condition at infinity): *Code_Aster* proposes absorbing-boundary finite elements (paraxial or anechoic elements), see [r4.02.05](doc:r4.02.05). As they a priori bring a term in the third time derivative, because of the introduction of the field $\varphi$, one prefers to treat it by means of a transformation into a non-symmetric term in $\dot{P}$ which will be carried over to the right-hand side, in an explicit manner.

Altogether one obtains the semi-discrete differential-algebraic equations of the coupled problem:

$$
\left( \begin{array}{cccc} M & 0 & M_{\text{FS}} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & M_{\text{fl}} & 0 \\ {}^{t}M_{\text{FS}} & {}^{t}M_{\text{fl}} & H_{\text{fl}} & M_{z} \\ 0 & 0 & {}^{t}M_{z} & 0 \end{array} \right) \left( \begin{array}{c} \ddot{U} \\ \ddot{P} \\ \dot{\phi} \\ \ddot{Z} \end{array} \right) + \left( \begin{array}{cccc} C & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & A_{\text{fa}} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \end{array} \right) \left( \begin{array}{c} \dot{U} \\ \dot{P} \\ \dot{\phi} \\ \dot{Z} \end{array} \right) + \left( \begin{array}{cccc} K & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & Q_{\text{fl}} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & K_{z} \end{array} \right) \left( \begin{array}{c} U \\ P \\ \phi \\ Z \end{array} \right) = \left( \begin{array}{c} L_{\text{st}} \\ 0 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{array} \right) \quad \begin{array}{l} \text{structure} \\ \text{fluide} \\ \text{fluide} \\ \text{surf. libre} \end{array}
$$

accompanied by the initial conditions:

$U({t}_{0})={U}_{0}$, $\dot{U}({t}_{0})={\dot{U}}_{0}$, $P({t}_{0})={P}_{0}$, $\varphi ({t}_{0})={\varphi}_{0}$ and $Z({t}_{0})=0$,

and the boundary conditions: $U(t)={U}_{\text{imp}}$ on the edge $\partial {\Omega}_{{S}_{u}}$ of the structure, possible unilateral conditions, and $P(t)={P}_{\text{imp}}(t)$ with $\varphi (t)={\varphi}_{\text{imp}}(t)$, verifying ${\rho}_{0}{\ddot{\varphi}}_{\text{imp}}(t)={P}_{\text{imp}}(t)$ on the edge $\partial {\Omega}_{\text{f\_p}}$ of the fluid.

**Note**:

One notes that in the non-linear case, one replaces in the term $KU$ *by the non-linear internal forces* $R(U,\dot{U},Z,t)$*.*

The various operators are:

1. the matrices $K$, $M$, $C$ defined above for the solid structure,
2. ${Q}_{\text{fl}}$ is the matrix built from ${\int}_{{\Omega}_{f}}\frac{1}{{\rho}_{0}{c}_{0}^{2}}p.\text{qd}\Omega$, which has the physical meaning of an elastic energy of the fluid,
3. ${H}_{\text{fl}}$ is built from ${\int}_{{\Omega}_{f}}-{\rho}_{0}\overrightarrow{\nabla \varphi }.\overrightarrow{\nabla \Psi }d\Omega$, and describes the transport of fluid mass,
4. ${M}_{\text{fl}}$ is built from ${\int}_{{\Omega}_{f}}\frac{1}{{c}_{0}^{2}}\varphi .\text{qd}\Omega$, and describes the inertia of the fluid,
5. ${M}_{\text{FS}}$ is built from ${\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{\text{FS}}}{\rho}_{0}\varphi .\delta \overrightarrow{v}.(-\overrightarrow{n})\text{dS}$ ($\overrightarrow{n}$ is the normal from the structure domain towards the fluid), and describes the Eulerian mass flow rate at the fluid–structure interface,
6. ${A}_{\text{fa}}$ is built from ${\int}_{{\Omega}_{f}}{\rho}_{0}p.\psi d\Omega$, and denotes the absorbing-boundary operator, acting on $\dot{P}$, modifying the equation in $\varphi$ of the absorbing wall: the absorbing elements (model 3D_FLUI_ABSO) de-symmetrise the system resulting from the formulation $(P,\varphi )$ and one will carry this term over to the right-hand side, which will be denoted ${L}^{\text{abso}}$, by explicit time discretisation at each iteration, see the paragraph r5.05.05-schema-integration-temporelle,
7. ${K}_{z}$ is the “stiffness” of the free surface, built from ${\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{\text{SL}}}{\rho}_{0}\text{gz}.\delta z\text{dS}$,
8. ${M}_{z}$ comes from the work of the fluctuating pressure on the free surface, built from ${\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{\text{SL}}}{\rho}_{0}z.\psi \text{dS}$,
9. the term ${L}_{\text{st}}$ contains, among other loadings, the effect of the hydrostatic pressure exerted by the fluid on the structure.

In summary, taking into account a fluid domain in barotropic fluctuating evolution, interacting with the structure, leads one to consider in the non-linear dynamic system enriched on particular degrees of freedom $(P,\varphi )$ and $Z$:

1. an inertia-force operator $M(U).\ddot{U}$ enriched by:

$\mathrm{M}^{\text{fs}} \left( \begin{array}{c} \ddot{\mathrm{U}} \\ \ddot{\mathrm{P}} \\ \ddot{\varphi} \\ \ddot{\mathrm{Z}} \end{array} \right) = \left( \begin{array}{cccc} 0 & 0 & \mathrm{M}_{\text{FS}} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \mathrm{M}_{\text{fl}} & 0 \\ {}^{t}\mathrm{M}_{\text{FS}} & {}^{t}\mathrm{M}_{\text{fl}} & \mathrm{H}_{\text{fl}} & \mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{z}} \\ 0 & 0 & {}^{t}\mathrm{M}_{\mathrm{z}} & 0 \end{array} \right) \left( \begin{array}{c} \ddot{\mathrm{U}} \\ \ddot{\mathrm{P}} \\ \ddot{\phi} \\ \ddot{\mathrm{Z}} \end{array} \right)$

1. an internal-force operator $R(U,\dot{U})$ enriched by:

$K^{\text{fs}} \left( \begin{array}{c} U \\ P \\ \phi \\ Z \end{array} \right) = \left( \begin{array}{cccc} 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & Q_{\text{fl}} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & K_{z} \end{array} \right) \left( \begin{array}{c} U \\ P \\ \phi \\ Z \end{array} \right)$

1. a right-hand side enriched by the carrying over to the right-hand side, by explicit time discretisation, of $-{A}_{\mathrm{fa}}\dot{P}$ on the degrees of freedom $\varphi$.

The fluid must remain in small motions (basic hypothesis of this modelling), but one may consider large motions of the structure, bathed by the fluid, via an update of the geometry $\mathrm{X}\to \mathrm{X}+\Delta \mathrm{U}$ (via the keyword COMPORTEMENT, operand DEFORMATION:'PETIT_REAC', valid if one considers small rotations) of the boundaries $\partial {\Omega}_{\text{FS}}$, which causes the term ${M}_{\text{FS}}$ to be recomputed but also all the others since the domain ${\Omega}_{f}$ has evolved; the scalar fields $(P,\phi )$ are then simply transported identically onto the updated geometry. See also the test case FDNV100 [v8.03.100](doc:v8.03.100).

### 2.5 Taking into account viscous constitutive laws and damping

A viscous constitutive law, see [r5.03.08](doc:r5.03.08), is expressed like an elastoplastic law by an evolution of the work of the internal forces $R(U,\dot{U},t)$. Thus, while bringing a “physical” damping into the dynamic equilibrium, it does not produce a direct contribution to the damping term $C.\dot{U}$ of the dynamic equilibrium equation, but nevertheless in an indirect manner if one has chosen a Rayleigh damping (*cf.* r5.05.05-terme-inertie) via the tangent stiffness matrix of the integration scheme.

Indeed with a viscous constitutive law, the strain tensor comprises an elastic part, a thermal part, an anelastic (known) part and a viscous, deviatoric part (stress deviator denoted $\tilde{\sigma}$), for example verifying:

$\begin{array}{c}{\epsilon}_{\text{tot}}={\epsilon}_{\mathrm{e}}+{\epsilon}_{\text{th}}+{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{a}}+{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{v}}\\ \sigma =\mathrm{A}\left(T\right).{\epsilon}_{\mathrm{e}}\\ {\dot{\epsilon}}_{v}=g\left({\sigma}_{\text{eq}},\lambda ,T\right)\frac{3}{2}\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}}\end{array}$

For the viscous constitutive relation LEMAITRE, the function $g$ is explicit, but this is not always the case. After implicit discretisation in time, the viscous flow is:

$\frac{\Delta {\epsilon}_{\mathrm{v}}}{\Delta t}=\frac{3}{2}g\left({\sigma}_{\text{eq}},{\lambda}^{-}+{\left(\Delta {\epsilon}_{v}\right)}_{\text{eq}},T\right)\frac{\tilde{\sigma}}{{\sigma}_{\text{eq}}}$

One may also adopt a semi-implicit scheme, which seems to give better results:

$\frac{\Delta {\varepsilon}_{v}}{\mathrm{\Delta t}}=\frac{3}{2}g({({\sigma}^{-}+\frac{\Delta \sigma }{2})}_{\text{eq}},{\lambda}^{-}+\frac{{(\Delta {\varepsilon}_{v})}_{\text{eq}}}{2},{T}^{-}+\frac{\mathrm{\Delta T}}{2})\frac{({\tilde{\sigma}}^{-}+\frac{\Delta \tilde{\sigma}}{2})}{{({\sigma}^{-}+\frac{\Delta \sigma }{2})}_{\text{eq}}}$

After solution of a local non-linear equation by elimination of $\Delta {\epsilon}_{\mathrm{v}}$ in order to compute ${\sigma}_{\text{eq}}={\left({\sigma}^{-}+\mathit{\Delta \sigma }\right)}_{\text{eq}}$, one thus obtains the stress at the end of the current time step $\sigma ={\sigma}^{-}+\Delta \sigma$.

### 2.6 Equations in “relative” motion [r4.05.01](doc:r4.05.01)

#### 2.6.1 Generalities

In numerous applications, notably in earthquake engineering, one wishes to compute directly the displacement field of the structure deduced from the “entrainment” motion coming from the imposed displacements ${\mathrm{U}}_{d}\left(t\right)$ of the supports of the structure.

One then denotes by ${u}_{a}$ the absolute displacement of the structure: ${\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{a}}={\mathrm{u}}_{\text{ent}}+\mathrm{u}$, ${\mathrm{u}}_{\text{ent}}$ being the entrainment displacement (for example, ${\mathrm{u}}_{\text{ent}}$ may be an incident field: it is then called “pseudo-static displacement”). In the MONO_APPUI case, it is a rigid-body motion. In the MULTI_APPUI case, ${\mathrm{u}}_{\text{ent}}$ is defined as being the linear static solution of the structure subjected to the displacements imposed at the supports, see [r5.05.05-decomposition-relevement-elastostatique].

And one calls $u$ the “relative” displacement (so called by abuse of language if ${\mathrm{u}}_{\text{ent}}$ is not a rigid-body motion).

Indeed the codification (RCC-M, ASME…) introduces the distinction between “primary stresses” due to the “relative” vibratory motion and “secondary stresses” due to the “entrainment” vibratory motion. The relevance of this distinction disappears *a priori* obviously as soon as one envisages a non-linear behaviour of the material.

If one treats the problem of dynamic interaction with the soil (which is an infinite half-space), in an earthquake for example, the relative displacement field $\mathrm{u}$ verifies: $\underset{\mathrm{x}\to \infty }{\lim}\mathrm{u}\left(\mathrm{x}\right)=0$: only the incident field ${\mathrm{u}}_{\text{ent}}$ is perceptible at infinity — it is the seismic loading datum. One uses in order to define this imposed-displacement loading the command AFFE_CHAR_MECA and the factor keyword ONDE_PLANE, on a given boundary of the mesh considered.

In this case of a problem of interaction with the soil, one does not know a priori the “entrainment” displacement applied directly to the structure, since it results from the total coupled response: thus the MULTI_APPUI case, where the displacement is necessarily known at the interface, is not applicable.

On the other hand, not being able to simulate in finite elements with *Code_Aster* the field in the whole infinite half-space (the soil), one is led to place “absorbing elastic” boundaries, *cf.* [r4.02.05](doc:r4.02.05), at the edge of the soil mesh. The virtual work associated with these absorbing boundaries, of outgoing normal $n$, is treated as a right-hand side (for the paraxial absorbing finite elements of *Code_Aster*, which are of order 0), because it is integrated explicitly in the time-integration scheme (*cf.* [r5.05.05-schema-integration-temporelle]) of DYNA_NON_LINE: one indeed takes the value ${\dot{u}}^{-}$ of the velocity at the previous time instant. The associated linear form is:

$$
{L}_{\text{abso}}^{\text{ordre}0}({u}_{\text{ent}},{\dot{u}}_{\text{ent}},{\dot{u}}^{-}).\delta v={\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{\text{abso}}}({A}_{\text{abso}}^{\text{ordre}0}({\dot{u}}^{-})+\sigma ({u}_{\text{ent}}).n-{A}_{\text{abso}}^{\text{ordre}0}({\dot{u}}_{\text{ent}})).\delta v\text{dS}

After spatial discretisation, one deduces the right-hand side:

One separates the constraint conditions, see , into a group of “entrainment” conditions by the supports: ${B}_{s}.{U}_{a}={U}_{d}(t)$ and a group of constraint conditions denoted by ${B}_{L}.{U}_{a}=0$ which one wishes to impose directly on the absolute displacement ${U}_{a}$ of the structure (for example internal constraints such as “3D_POU”…) with which are associated their LAGRANGE parameters ${\lambda}_{L}$. #### 2.6.2 Decomposition by elastostatic lifting

One builds an “elastostatic lifting”, that is to say a basis of ${V}_{e}$ from the linear elastic static solutions of the structure under the only imposed basis displacements $({X}_{\text{ks}})$ of the supports (no imposed-force loading). After discretisation by finite elements, this amounts to solving $k=1\to {N}_{s}$ elastostatic problems (stiffness matrix $K$):

Find $({\Psi}_{k},{\lambda}_{s{\Psi}_{k}},{\lambda}_{L{\Psi}_{K}})$ such that

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l} K \cdot \Psi_k + {}^{t}B_s \cdot \lambda_{s\Psi_k} + {}^{t}B_L \cdot \lambda_{L\Psi_k} = 0 \quad \text{dans le domaine} \\ B_s \cdot \Psi_k = X_{\text{ks}} \quad \text{sur les supports } \Gamma_s \\ B_L \cdot \Psi_k = 0 \end{array} \right.
$$

One calls “linear static modes” these ${N}_{s}$ solutions ${\Psi}_{k}$ (supplied via the operand “MODE_STAT” of DYNA_NON_LINE). They are computed beforehand by the operator MODE_STATIQUE [u4.52.14](doc:u4.52.14) with the option MODE_STAT.

One necessarily has with : ${}^{t}{\Psi}_{\ell}.K.{\Psi}_{k}{+}^{t}{X}_{\mathrm{\ell s}}.{\lambda}_{s{\Psi}_{k}}=0$, $\forall \ell ,k=1\to {N}_{s}$.

**Note:**

*It is essential that the same constraints* ${B}_{L}$ be represented in the solution of the static-mode search problem as in the complete dynamic problem.

The displacement field of the “entrained” structure ${u}_{\text{ent}}$, after discretisation by finite elements, is therefore described by the ${U}_{\text{ent}}={U}_{\text{ent}}^{k}(t){\Psi}_{k}$, verifying in particular ${B}_{s}.{U}_{\text{ent}}={U}_{d}^{k}(t){X}_{\text{ks}}$ on the supports, running over the discrete subspace ${V}_{e}$, of the “degrees of freedom entrained” by the supports.

The discrete subspace ${V}_{e}$ is therefore generated by the basis $({\Psi}_{k})$. One necessarily has by construction:

${U}_{\text{ent}}^{k}(t)={U}_{d}^{k}(t),\forall k$

The displacement degrees of freedom of the “entrained” structure are therefore given directly by the value of the ${U}^{d}(t)$.

Having characterised the discrete subspace ${V}_{e}$ from the linear static modes, after discretisation, let us study a displacement field $W$ of the structure, arbitrary and kinematically admissible, but zero on the supports: ${B}_{s}.W=0$ and verifying the “ordinary” constraints ${B}_{L}.W=0$. By virtue of , one necessarily has:

$\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l} {}^{t}W \cdot K \cdot \Psi_k + {}^{t}W \cdot {}^{t}B_s \cdot \lambda_{s\Psi_k} + {}^{t}W \cdot {}^{t}B_L \cdot \lambda_{L\Psi_k} = 0 \\ {}^{t}W \cdot B_s \cdot \Psi_k = {}^{t}W \cdot X_{\text{ks}} \\ {}^{t}W \cdot B_L \cdot \Psi_k = 0 \end{array} \right. \quad \forall W, \text{ tel que } B_s \cdot W = 0$

Whence simply the linear static problem:

$$
{}^{t}W.K.{\Psi}_{k}=0\forall W,\text{tel}\text{que}{B}_{s}.W=0\text{et}{B}_{L}.W=0
$$

The elastic stiffness operator $K$ being positive definite (having eliminated the rigid-body modes), one notes that every absolute displacement field ${W}_{a}$ kinematically admissible of the structure, after discretisation, may be written by unique decomposition:

$$
{W}_{a}=W+{W}_{e} sur la somme des sous-espaces supplémentaires :math:`{V}_{a}=V\oplus {V}_{e}
$$

with ${V}_{e}$ generated from the static modes $({\Psi}_{k})$, and $V$ containing the fields called “active degrees of freedom” such that ${B}_{s}.W=0$ (zero on the supports). One calls $V$ the discrete subspace of the “active degrees of freedom”.

#### 2.6.3 MONO_APPUI and MULTI_APPUI cases

For a loading of MONO_APPUI type, the static modes are simply the rigid-body modes of the structure: $K.{\Psi}_{k}=0$, verifying the “ordinary” constraints ${B}_{L}\cdot {\Psi}_{k}=0$.

If the loading is MULTI_APPUI, the static modes $({\Psi}_{k})$ are arbitrary.

This decomposition on two supplementary subspaces ${V}_{a}=V\oplus {V}_{e}$ of every kinematically admissible field built with the aid of the elasticity operator $K$ is applicable in any non-linear evolution of the structure, including with shocks…, provided that the bilateral constraints remain the same during the history.

Let us exploit this decomposition and now project the non-linear dynamic problem separately onto the subspace $V$ then onto the subspace ${V}_{e}$, exploiting . The result is simplified (only a little) because ${B}_{s}.W=0$ (that is to say the “active degrees of freedom” do not work in the reactions of the support supports ${B}_{s}$), ${B}_{L}.{\Psi}_{k}=0$ (that is to say the static modes do not work in the constraint reactions ${B}_{L}$), ${B}_{s}.{\Psi}_{k}={X}_{\text{ks}}$:

Find ${U}_{a}={U}_{e}+U$, ${\lambda}_{s}$, ${\lambda}_{L},\mu$ such that:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l} U_e = U_d^{\ell}(t) \Psi_{\ell} \\ {}^{t}W \cdot M(U + U_e, \dot{U} + \dot{U}_e, \ddot{U} + \ddot{U}_e) + {}^{t}W \cdot C(\dot{U} + \dot{U}_e) + {}^{t}W \cdot R(U, \dot{U} + \dot{U}_e, t) = {}^{t}W \cdot (L(t) + L^{\text{abso}}(U_e, \dot{U}_e, \dot{U))} - {}^{t}W \cdot {}^{t}B_L \cdot \lambda_L - {}^{t}W \cdot {}^{t}A \cdot \mu \quad \forall W \in V \\ {}^{t}\Psi_k \cdot M(U + U_e, \dot{U} + \dot{U}_e, \ddot{U} + \ddot{U}_e) + {}^{t}\Psi_k \cdot C(\dot{U} + \dot{U}_e) + {}^{t}\Psi_k \cdot R(U, \dot{U} + \dot{U}_e, t) = {}^{t}\Psi_k \cdot (L(t) + L^{\text{abso}}(U_e, \dot{U}_e, \dot{U})) - {}^{t}X_{\text{ks}} \cdot \lambda_s - {}^{t}\Psi_k \cdot {}^{t}A \cdot \mu \quad \forall k \\ B_s \cdot U = 0 \\ B_L \cdot U = 0 \\ A(U + U_e) \le d_0(t) \\ \mu \ge 0 \\ \forall j, \left(A(U + U_e) - d_0\right)_j \cdot \mu_j = 0 \\ (U + U_e)(t_0) = U_0 \\ (\dot{U} + \dot{U}_e)(t_0) = \dot{U}_0 \end{array} \right.

One notes that this is rather complicated.

Let us first restrict ourselves to the dynamic problem where the inertia operators $M$ and internal-force operators $R$ are linear, and in the absence of absorbing boundaries: ${L}^{\text{abso}}({U}_{e},{\dot{U}}_{e},\dot{U})=0$.

One makes the hypothesis in *Code_Aster* that: $C.{\dot{U}}_{e}=0$, including in multi-support (whereas this is exact only in single-support, where the static modes are the rigid modes $K.{\Psi}_{k}=0$ without strain). This amounts to neglecting the contribution of the entrainment displacements of the structure to the viscous damping forces.

The static modes ${\Psi}_{k}$ verifying , the system is restricted to:

This advantage is lost if unilateral constraints are present, since the condition $A.(U+{U}_{d}^{k}(t){\Psi}_{k})\le {d}_{0}(t)$ requires the value of ${U}_{d}^{k}(t)$ in order to be expressed correctly, except if one is lucky that the entrainment motion has no component on the interface where the gap of the unilateral constraint is computed, that is to say if the stops of these unilateral supports are fixed in the absolute frame!

It is the same thing if one wishes to reconstitute the complete solution for the post-processing in stresses with their so-called “secondary” contribution, linked to ${U}_{e}={U}_{d}^{k}(t){\Psi}_{k}$. This contribution is zero in single-support, since it is then a rigid mode.

Now one considers the dynamic problem of a structure interacting with a soil (“infinite” medium), source of an incident seismic wave, *cf.* [r4.05.01](doc:r4.05.01) and [r4.02.05](doc:r4.02.05). One is necessarily in a “MONO_APPUI” framework, where the static modes are the rigid modes of the structure, whence: $K.{\Psi}_{k}=0$ and one usually admits that ${t}^{}{\Psi}_{k}.\mathrm{C}.\dot{\mathrm{U}}=0$ (indeed, physically, the damping comes from the strains in the solid). One therefore uses absorbing-boundary elements, and the term ${L}_{\text{abso}}({U}_{e},{\dot{U}}_{e},\dot{U})$ is present in [éq2.6-7]. ## 3 Time-integration scheme: NEWMARK scheme and NEWTON method

The mechanical problem to be analysed being modelled by finite elements, according to the approach described in [r5.05.05-dynamique-non-lineaire], one computes the displacement, velocity and acceleration fields at the nodes at a discrete sequence of computation time instants ${t}_{1},{t}_{2}\dots {t}_{i-1},{t}_{i}\dots {t}_{N}$: ${\left\lbrace \lbrace {t}_{i}\rbrace \right\rbrace }_{1\le i\le N}$.

The user of DYNA_NON_LINE may currently choose between five one-step time schemes, of which three are implicit: that of NEWMARK (1959), its variant called “modified average acceleration”, or that of HILBER-HUGUES-TAYLOR (HHT, 1977): see the paragraph [r5.05.05-variante-schema-newmark] and two explicit ones: that of the central differences (which is a particular case of the Newmark family) and the Tchamwa–Wielgosz scheme (which introduces a high-frequency numerical dissipation) ). The state of the structure being known at the time instant ${t}_{i-1}$, one seeks to compute its state at the time instant ${t}_{i}$ by a prediction-correction method.

### 3.1 NEWMARK scheme

One presents here this scheme in its classical form ( and ) relative to a translational motion or a small rotation. For the large rotations of structural elements , the formulae are more complicated, but they make it possible in the same way to update the angular velocity and the angular acceleration as a function of the displacement increment, which in this case is the rotation vector.

One denotes hereafter by $\Phi$ the configuration, that is to say the parameterisation of the system by the degrees of freedom of the finite elements: displacements and rotations $U$, pressure $P$, potential $\varphi$…

The NEWMARK scheme rests on the following expansions of the configuration vector, a function of time, where $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are two parameters:

$$
\Phi (t+\mathrm{\Delta t})\approx \Phi (t)+\Delta t\dot{\Phi}(t)+\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{2}\left[(1-2\beta )\ddot{\Phi}(t)+2\beta \ddot{\Phi}(t+\Delta t)\right]
$$

$$
\dot{\Phi}(t+\Delta t)\approx \dot{\Phi}(t)+\Delta t\left[(1-\gamma )\ddot{\Phi}(t)+\gamma \ddot{\Phi}(t+\Delta t)\right]
$$

This is the acceleration parameterisation, called “A-form”, where one expresses the displacements and the velocities with the accelerations. Equation  is also written with , if $\gamma \ne 0$:

$\Phi (t+\Delta t)\approx \Phi (t)+\frac{\Delta t}{\gamma}((\gamma -\beta )\dot{\Phi}(t)+\beta \dot{\Phi}(t+\Delta t))+\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{2\gamma }(\gamma -2\beta )\ddot{\Phi}(t)$

These parameters $\beta$ and $\gamma$ are supplied respectively via the operands BETA and GAMMA of the keyword SCHEMA_TEMPS (SCHEMA='NEWMARK') of the command DYNA_NON_LINE.

See [r5.05.05-qualites-defauts-schema-newmark] for the characteristics of the scheme as a function of the values of these parameters.

The brackets on the right-hand sides of equations  and  are thus weighted averages of $\ddot{\Phi}(t)$ and of $\ddot{\Phi}(t+\Delta t)$.

One notes that one cannot have $\beta =0$. At the first computation step one exploits directly the initial conditions: $\Phi (0)$ and $\dot{\Phi}(0)$. One also needs $\ddot{\Phi}(0)$, except if $\gamma =1$: see the note made in [r5.05.05-phase-prediction].

In practice these expressions are not usable because one will have to express the values in displacements at the time instant $t+\Delta t$ from those at the time instant $t$. Between these two time instants, one denotes the increment and the average of the fields:

$\Delta \Phi =\Phi (t+\Delta t)-\Phi (t)$         ;    $\bar{\Phi}=\frac{1}{2}(\Phi (t)+\Phi (t+\Delta t))$

These results will be used in [r5.05.05-point-vue-energetique]. Equation  gives:

$$
\begin{array}{}\ddot{\Phi}(t+\Delta t)=\frac{1}{\beta \Delta {t}^{2}}\left[\Phi (t+\Delta t)-\Phi (t)\right]-\frac{1}{\beta \Delta t}\dot{\Phi}(t)+\frac{2\beta -1}{2\beta }\ddot{\Phi}(t)\\ \iff \Delta \ddot{\Phi}=\frac{1}{\beta \Delta {t}^{2}}\Delta \Phi -\frac{1}{\beta \Delta t}\dot{\Phi}(t)-\frac{1}{2\beta }\ddot{\Phi}(t)\end{array}
$$

And, according to :

$$
\begin{array}{}\dot{\Phi}(t+\Delta t)=\frac{\gamma}{\beta \Delta t}\left[\Phi (t+\Delta t)-\Phi (t)\right]+\frac{\beta -\gamma }{\beta}\dot{\Phi}(t)+\frac{(2\beta -\gamma )\Delta t}{2\beta }\ddot{\Phi}(t)\\ \iff \Delta \dot{\Phi}=\frac{\gamma}{\beta \Delta t}\Delta \Phi -\frac{\gamma}{\beta}\dot{\Phi}(t)+\frac{(2\beta -\gamma )\Delta t}{2\beta }\ddot{\Phi}(t)\\ \iff \Delta \dot{\Phi}=\Delta t(\ddot{\bar{\Phi}}-\frac{1-2\gamma }{2}\Delta \ddot{\Phi})\end{array}
$$

The expressions  and  constitute the displacement parameterisation, called “D-form”, where one expresses the accelerations and the velocities with the displacements, which will be advantageous in the non-linear case for expressing the constitutive laws. Finally, one may write:

$$
\Delta \Phi =\Delta t.\dot{\Phi}(t)+\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{4}(4\beta -1).\Delta \ddot{\Phi}+\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{2}\ddot{\bar{\Phi}}=\Delta t.\dot{\bar{\Phi}}+\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{2}(2\beta -\gamma ).\Delta \ddot{\Phi}
$$

In the case of the large rotations of structural elements the expressions homologous to  and  are more complex , but the relations which follow are fairly easily transposable.

During a time step (from $t$ to $t+\Delta t$), where the values at the time instant $t$ are known, equations  and  define the increments of velocity $\delta \dot{\Phi}$ and of acceleration $\delta \ddot{\Phi}$ corresponding to an arbitrary displacement increment $\delta \Phi$ from the position at the time instant $t$, which one will need during the NEWTON correction iterations (within the time step, see the paragraph r5.05.05-phase-correction-methode-newton):

$$
\delta \dot{\Phi}=\frac{\gamma}{\beta \Delta t}\delta \Phi
$$

$$
\delta \ddot{\Phi}=\frac{1}{\beta \Delta {t}^{2}}\delta \Phi
$$

Let us place ourselves at the time instant $t={t}_{i-1}$, and one writes the equilibrium [éq2.2.4-1] after spatial discretisation at the time instant ${t}_{i}={t}_{i-1}+\Delta t$, possibly with the complementary elements brought in [éq2.2-6]. One denotes ${U}_{i}=U({t}_{i})$ the degrees of freedom at the new time instant ${t}_{i}$ and exploiting the terms of the time scheme  and , one arrives at the non-linear system of dynamic equilibrium:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l} \hat{K} \cdot U_i + R(U_i, \dot{U}_i, t) + {}^{t}B \cdot \lambda_i + {}^{t}A \cdot \mu_i = \hat{L}(t_i) - L_{\text{GR}}^{\text{iner}}(U_i, \dot{U}_i, \ddot{U}_i) \\ B \cdot U_i = U^d(t_i) \\ A \cdot U_i \le d_0(t_i) \\ \forall j,\ \mu_j \ge 0 \\ \forall j,\ \left(A \cdot U_i - d_{0_i}\right)_j \cdot \mu_j = 0 \end{array} \right.
$$

with:

$$
\hat{K}={K}^{\text{fs}}+\frac{1}{\beta \Delta {t}^{2}}(M+{M}^{\text{fs}})+\frac{\gamma}{\beta \Delta t}C
$$

$$
\begin{array}{}\hat{L}({t}_{i})=L({t}_{i})+{L}^{\text{abso}}({t}_{i})+\frac{1}{\beta \Delta {t}^{2}}(M+{M}^{\text{fs}}).({U}_{i-1}+\Delta t{\dot{U}}_{i-1}+\Delta {t}^{2}(\frac{1-\mathrm{2\beta }}{2}){\ddot{U}}_{i-1})\\ +\frac{1}{\beta \Delta t}C.(\gamma {U}_{i-1}+\Delta t(\gamma -\beta ){\dot{U}}_{i-1}+\Delta {t}^{2}(\frac{\gamma -2\beta }{2}){\ddot{U}}_{i-1})\end{array}
$$

**Note**:

*One notes that the matrix* $\hat{K}$ *contributing to the generalised stiffness of the system to be solved is enriched by terms coming from the mass matrix* $M$ *of the system (provided that one has assigned a density on all the finite elements of the model) and from the damping matrix* $C$*. One will see further on that the linearisation of the internal forces* $R({U}_{i},{\dot{U}}_{i},t)$ *also contributes to* $\hat{K}$*. Thus, even if the mechanical system considered comprises rigid modes, for example in a study where a solid is in free fall, the mass matrix comes to “prevent” the “stiffness” matrix* $\hat{K}$ *from not being factorisable. In a way, it is the inertia forces of the solid body (in its rigid modes) which ensure the equilibrium with the external forces, which could not be done in quasi-statics.*

*However, one observes that the time step* $\Delta t$ *also appears. If it is too large, the mass terms will not be important enough compared with those of stiffness, and the matrix risks being almost non-factorisable (“almost-zero pivots”).*

**Note**:

*The terms with the exponent* ${}^{\text{fs}}$ appear if the system contains fluid domains (see r5.05.05-problemes-couples-fluide-structure-vibro-acoustique and the terms coming from the fluid domain in ); one recalls that no fluid loading ${L}^{\mathrm{fl}}$ *is provided for.*

**Note**:

Unlike the case of Rayleigh damping (see [r5.05.05-terme-inertie]), the damping matrix $C$ *does not appear in the matrix* $\hat{K}$ *in the presence of modal damping (keyword* *AMOR_MODAL), because in this case, the force* $C.{\dot{U}}_{i-1}$ *is built directly without storage of the matrix* $C$*, and is carried over to the right-hand side* $\hat{L}({t}_{i})$*.*

*The term* ${L}^{\mathrm{abso}}$ in the right-hand side of  appears in the presence of absorbing boundaries of a fluid or of a solid (elastic formulation), see the paragraph r5.05.05-equations-mouvement. It is treated according to an explicit scheme as a function of the solution fields obtained at ${t}_{i-1}$*.*

**Note**:

*Unlike the* $\theta$–scheme also used in transient thermal analysis, cf. [r5.02.01](doc:r5.02.01), where the derivatives are written explicitly, while the conservation equation is written at a fictitious time instant resulting from a combination with the parameter $\theta$ *of the values at the time instants* ${t}_{i-1}$ *and* ${t}_{i}$, cf. r5.05.05-schemas-explicites, the dynamic equilibria are verified at the time instants ${t}_{i-1}$ *and* ${t}_{i}$, while the derivatives are combinations determined by the Newmark scheme  and . On the other hand, the complete HHT scheme, see the paragraph r5.05.05-schema-hht-methode-acceleration-moyenne-modifiee, exploits a shift, in the manner of the $\theta$*–scheme.*

It still remains to treat the non-linear terms: the internal forces $R({U}_{i},{\dot{U}}_{i},t)$ and the non-linear contribution of the inertia forces in large structural rotations ${L}_{\mathrm{GR}}^{\mathrm{iner}}({U}_{i},{\dot{U}}_{i},{\ddot{U}}_{i})$ (*cf.* r5.05.05-amortissement).

At the first time instant ${t}_{1}$, one sees in the right-hand side  that one needs ${U}_{0},{\dot{U}}_{0}$, supplied by the initial conditions, but also ${\ddot{U}}_{0}$ because of the NEWMARK scheme (except if $\gamma =2\beta =1$): see the note made in the paragraph r5.05.05-phase-prediction.

One therefore uses a Newton method in order to solve this non-linear problem .

### 3.2 Prediction phase

The system  is non-linear and is integrated, after an EULER prediction by linearisation, with the aid of an iterative NEWTON method, as in non-linear statics [r5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01). The computation of this prediction may be slightly erroneous, so long as the correction phase by NEWTON iterations [r5.05.05-phase-correction-methode-newton] is capable of correcting to convergence…

#### 3.2.1 General time step

In the prediction phase, one exploits the solution at the previous step or the values of the initial state, and one denotes the initial tangent stiffness matrix of the step (*cf.* definition of $Q$ in [r5.05.05-discretisation-dynamique-non-lineaire]):

$$
{K}_{i-1}=\frac{\partial R}{\partial U}{\mid}_{({U}_{i-1},{\dot{U}}_{i-1},{t}_{i-1})}=\frac{d{}^{t}Q}{dU}{\mid}_{({U}_{i-1})}.{\sigma}_{({U}_{i-1},{\dot{U}}_{i-1},{t}_{i-1})}+{}^{t}{Q}_{({U}_{i-1})}.\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial U}{\mid}_{({U}_{i-1},{\dot{U}}_{i-1},{t}_{i-1})}
$$

These terms (*cf.* r5.05.05-discretisation-dynamique-non-lineaire) are evaluated on the previous step, the first term of the right-hand side of  appearing only in large displacements ($Q$ then not being constant).

If the behaviour is linear, the matrix ${K}_{i-1}$ is simply the elastic stiffness matrix $K$ of the structure.

One may also decide, in order to save computation time, not to update this matrix (only in the case where the time step does not vary), and to take the elastic stiffness matrix, see [u4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03), keyword Newton, operand PREDICTION, value ‘ELASTIQUE’, rather than ‘TANGENTE’. If one specifies nothing, the default choice made by *code_aster* is consistent with that made on the NEWTON correction iterations described hereafter in the paragraph r5.05.05-phase-correction-methode-newton.

Moreover, in the presence of large rotations (structural elements: beams…), one must also differentiate the non-linear inertia term ${L}_{\mathrm{GR}}^{\mathrm{iner}}({U}_{i},{\dot{U}}_{i},{\ddot{U}}_{i})$:

$$
{K}_{i-1}^{\mathrm{M1}}=\frac{\partial {L}_{\text{GR}}^{\text{iner}}}{\partial U}{\mid}_{({U}_{i-1},{\dot{U}}_{i-1},{\ddot{U}}_{i-1})}
$$

The matrix $\hat{K}$ having been established by , this new matrix ${K}_{i-1}^{\mathrm{M1}}$ is combined with the matrix $\hat{K}+{K}_{i-1}$, see below .

One denotes the loading increment $\Delta \hat{L}({t}_{i})$, $\hat{L}$ being defined with ref:éq 3.1-10 <éq 3.1-10>]:

$$
\Delta \hat{L}({t}_{i})=\hat{L}({t}_{i})-\hat{L}({t}_{i-1})
$$

One also defines the increments of imposed conditions $\Delta {U}_{d}({t}_{i})$, and one groups in $\Delta {L}^{\text{anél}}({t}_{i})$ the dependences of the stresses as a function of the various parameters or “command variables” $Z$ of the constitutive law of the constitutive material: such as the temperature…:

$\Delta {L}^{\text{anél}}({t}_{i})=-{}^{t}Q({U}_{i-1}).\frac{d\sigma }{dZ}{\mid}_{({U}_{i-1},{\dot{U}}_{i-1},{\ddot{U}}_{i-1},Z)}.\Delta Z$

In the presence of modal damping the damping forces are carried over to the right-hand side. One then adds to $\Delta \hat{L}({t}_{i})$ the corresponding term: $-C.{\dot{U}}_{i-1}$.

The term $\Delta {L}^{\text{abso}}({t}_{i})=-{A}_{\text{fa}}\Delta \dot{P}({t}_{i-1})+\Delta {L}_{\text{abso}}^{\text{ordre}0}({U}_{\text{ent}}({t}_{i}),{\dot{U}}_{\text{ent}}({t}_{i}),\dot{U}({t}_{i-1}))$ denotes the contribution integrated explicitly from the solutions at ${t}_{i-1}$ (in order to avoid having to treat a non-symmetric matrix, *cf.* [r4.02.05](doc:r4.02.05)) of an absorbing fluid boundary and of an absorbing elastic boundary, *cf.*  and .

One then computes predictive values for the current time step $(\Delta {U}_{i}^{0},\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0},\Delta {\mu}_{i}^{0})$:

**Prediction**

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l} (\hat{K} + K_{i-1} + K_{i-1}^{\mathrm{M1}}) \Delta U_i^0 + {}^{t}B \cdot \Delta \lambda_i^0 + {}^{t}A \cdot \Delta \mu_i^0 = \Delta \hat{L}(t_i) + \Delta L^{\text{anél}}(t_i) \\ B \cdot \Delta U_i^0 = \Delta U_d(t_i) \\ A \cdot U_i^0 \le d_0(t_i) \\ \forall j,\ \mu_{i_j}^0 \ge 0 \\ \forall j,\ \left(A \cdot U_i^0 - d_{0_i}\right)_j \cdot \mu_{i_j}^0 = 0 \end{array} \right.
$$

where one defines the prediction ${U}_{i}^{0}={U}_{i-1}+\Delta {U}_{i}^{0}$ for the new time instant ${t}_{i}={t}_{i-1}+\Delta t$, under the contact conditions established at the previous time step.

If one has chosen MATRICE= ‘ELASTIQUE’ in the keyword NEWTON, one does not re-evaluate at each time step $\hat{K}+{K}_{i-1}+{K}_{i-1}^{\mathrm{M1}}=\hat{K}+{K}_{0}+{K}_{0}^{\mathrm{M1}}$, which avoids the cost of re-assembly and of inversion, but increases the number of correction iterations.

After the establishment of a candidate solution of , on the basis of the constraints already in contact at the previous step, without ensuring a new verification of the contact criterion, one launches the active-constraint algorithm in order to satisfy the contact conditions: one thus corrects ${U}_{i}^{0},{\lambda}_{i}^{0},{\mu}_{i}^{0}$ [r5.03.50](doc:r5.03.50).

**Note: singular tangent matrix:**

One verifies on  that if the tangent matrix ${K}_{i-1}$ *is singular (case of a rigid mode, of a damaged material, or of a ductile plateau…), the dynamics is driven by the inertia forces, and that, the matrix* $\hat{K}$ being in general regular (cf. note made in [r5.05.05-schema-newmark]), one nevertheless does find a predictor ${U}_{i}^{0}$*, provided that the precision in the matrix* $\hat{K}$ *is not lost (almost-zero pivot) because of a choice of time step that is too large, which plays as* $1/\beta \Delta {t}^{2}$ in . This is for example the case in free-fall dynamics (cf. the note of equation 3.1-10).

#### 3.2.2 First time step

If one is at the first time step, of a study or of a restart (poursuite), the predictor is computed differently in order to take the initial state into account (${U}_{0}$, ${\dot{U}}_{0}$, ${\sigma}_{0}$):

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l} (\hat{K} + K_0 + K_0^{\mathrm{M1}}) \cdot U_1^0 + {}^{t}B \cdot \lambda_1^0 + {}^{t}A \cdot \mu_1^0 = \hat{L}(t_1) + \Delta L^{\text{anél}}(t_1) - {}^{t}Q \cdot \sigma_0 - C \cdot \dot{U}_0 \\ B \cdot U_1^0 = U_d(t_1) \\ A(U_1^0) \le d_0(t_1) \\ \forall j,\ \mu_{1_j}^0 \ge 0 \\ \forall j,\ \left(A \cdot U_1^0 - d_{0_1}\right)_j \cdot \mu_{1_j}^0 = 0 \end{array} \right.
$$

with:

$$
\begin{array}{}\hat{L}({t}_{1})=L({t}_{1})+\frac{1}{\beta \Delta {t}^{2}}(M+{M}^{\text{fs}})({U}_{0}+\Delta t{\dot{U}}_{0}+\Delta {t}^{2}(\frac{1-2\beta }{2}){\ddot{U}}_{0}^{0})\\ +\frac{1}{\beta \Delta t}C(\gamma {U}_{0}+\Delta t(\gamma -\beta ){\dot{U}}_{0}+\Delta {t}^{2}(\frac{\gamma -2\beta }{2}){\ddot{U}}_{0}^{0})\end{array}
$$

and the acceleration ${\ddot{\mathrm{U}}}_{0}^{0}$ evaluated by the prior solution of the system (one simplifies by assuming the contact constraints blocked, the NEWTON iterations taking charge of correcting):

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l} M \cdot \ddot{U}_0^0 + {}^{t}B \cdot \lambda_0 + {}^{t}A \cdot \mu_0 = L(t_0) + L^{\text{anél}}(t_0) - {}^{t}Q \cdot \sigma_0 - C \cdot \dot{U}_0 \\ B \cdot U_0^0 = 0 \\ A \cdot U_0^0 = 0 \end{array} \right.
$$

**Note:**

*It would be more exact to compute:* $\mathrm{M}{\ddot{\mathrm{U}}}_{0}^{0}+{}^{t}B.{\lambda}_{0}+{}^{t}\mathrm{A}.{\mu}_{0}=\mathrm{L}({t}_{0})+{\mathrm{L}}^{\text{anél}}({t}_{0})-{}^{t}\mathrm{Q}.{\sigma}_{0}-\mathrm{C}{\dot{\mathrm{U}}}_{0}$*, but in order to simplify, knowing that this will have only little influence on the subsequent solutions, one neglects* $\mathrm{C}{\dot{\mathrm{U}}}_{0}$ in the right-hand side of .

One must note that:

1. the matrix $M$ must be invertible: one will have to assign a density on all the finite elements of the model,
2. it may be necessary to establish by a static computation (possibly non-linear) the equilibrium state under the initial loading, hence the stresses ${\sigma}_{0}$, before the imposition of the initial dynamic conditions ${U}_{0},{\dot{U}}_{0}$. Indeed, otherwise, the acceleration ${\ddot{U}}_{0}^{0}$ could be “excessive” and lead onto an undesired branch of equilibrium,
3. in the presence of “kinematic loads”, *cf.* [u4.44.03](doc:u4.44.03), the latter are set to zero at this stage of computation of ${\ddot{U}}_{0}^{0}$.

However, certain finite elements do not possess a mass on all the degrees of freedom, for example the beams with warping POU_D_TG precisely on the warping degrees of freedom, or in fluid/structure coupling. For the vibro-acoustic coupling elements [r5.05.05-problemes-couples-fluide-structure-vibro-acoustique] indeed, the mass matrix of the problem  is not invertible. In these cases, the matrix $M$ is not invertible, and one contents oneself with taking a zero initial acceleration on the whole model and the subsequent iterations will have to take charge of correcting this less “good” prediction. One will then choose a small time step in order to ensure convergence at least at the beginning of the transient.

The term ${\mathrm{L}}^{\text{abso}}({t}_{0})$ is evaluated from the initial velocity ${\dot{U}}_{0}$ (*cf.* [r4.02.05](doc:r4.02.05)).

### 3.3 Correction phase by the NEWTON method

One seeks the values $(\Delta {U}_{i},\Delta {\lambda}_{i},\Delta {\mu}_{i})$ of the increments of displacements and LAGRANGE parameters from the values $({U}_{i-1},{\lambda}_{i-1},{\mu}_{i-1})$ obtained at the previous equilibrium (time instant ${t}_{i-1}$). One takes as initial values $(\Delta {U}_{i}^{0},\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{0},\Delta {\mu}_{i}^{0})$ obtained at the end of the prediction phase, before beginning the iterations of the NEWTON method.

At each NEWTON iteration $n$, one denotes by $\delta$ the evolutions leading to the estimate of the increments $\Delta$ (from time step $i$ to time step $i+1$) at convergence of the iterations: $\Delta {U}_{i}^{n+1}={U}_{i}^{n}+\delta {U}_{i}^{n+1}-{U}_{i-1}$ and $\Delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1}={\lambda}_{i}^{n}+\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1}-{\lambda}_{i-1}$ (likewise for the $\mu$).

One must then solve a system making it possible to determine $(\delta {U}_{i}^{n+1},\delta {\lambda}_{i}^{n+1},\delta {\mu}_{i}^{n+1})$, increments of the displacements and of the LAGRANGE parameters from the result $({U}_{i}^{n},{\lambda}_{i}^{n},{\mu}_{i}^{n})$ of the previous iteration:

**Correction (iteration °** $n$ **)**

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l} (\hat{K} + K_i^n + K_i^{\mathrm{Mn}}) \cdot \delta U_i^{n+1} + {}^{t}B \cdot \delta \lambda_i^{n+1} = \hat{L}(t_i) - F_i^n \\ B \cdot \delta U_i^{n+1} = U^d(t_i) - U_i^n \\ A \cdot U_i^{n+1} \le d_0(t_i) \\ \forall j,\ (\mu_i^{n+1})_j \ge 0 \\ \forall j,\ \left(A \cdot U_i^{n+1} - d_{0_i}\right)_j \cdot (\mu_i^{n+1})_j = 0 \end{array} \right.
$$

with a right-hand side called “residual”, because it tends to zero at convergence. One denotes (*cf.* equilibrium ):

$$
{F}_{i}^{n}={}^{t}Q({U}_{i}^{n}).{\sigma}_{({U}_{i}^{n},{\dot{U}}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}+C.{\dot{U}}_{i}^{n}+M({U}_{i}^{n}).{\ddot{U}}_{i}^{n}-{{L}_{\text{GR}}^{\text{iner}}}_{({U}_{i}^{n},{\dot{U}}_{i}^{n},{\ddot{U}}_{i}^{n})}+{}^{t}B.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}+{}^{t}A.{\mu}_{i}^{n}
$$

The term $\hat{L}({t}_{i})$ is defined as in ; the matrix $\hat{K}$ is given by .

In the presence of modal damping, the damping forces are carried over to the right-hand side. According to the value given to the keyword AMOR_MODAL, REAC_VITE, one adds to $\hat{L}({t}_{i})$ the updated term: $-C.{\dot{U}}_{i}^{n}$, or the non-updated term $C.{\dot{U}}_{i-1}$.

The matrix ${K}_{i}^{n}$ is the matrix of the tangent linear mapping of the “internal forces” part of the system of non-linear equations ; it is therefore:

$$
{K}_{i}^{n}=\frac{\partial F}{\partial U}{\mid}_{({U}_{i}^{n},{\dot{U}}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}=\frac{\partial R}{\partial U}{\mid}_{({U}_{i}^{n},{\dot{U}}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}-\frac{\partial {L}_{i}^{\text{méca}}}{\partial U}{\mid}_{({U}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}
$$

In the absence of follower forces, the last term is zero. The follower forces may be: the pressure exerted on the edges of solid elements, the gravity loading for the cable elements, the centrifugal force in large displacements, the gravity loading for all the THM modellings of unsaturated porous media [r7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10).

If one envisages the update of the geometry (in large displacements), one has more precisely:

$$
{\mathrm{K}}_{i}^{n}{=}^{t}\mathrm{Q}(\mathrm{U}).\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \mathrm{U}}{\mid}_{({\mathrm{U}}_{i}^{\mathrm{n}},{\dot{\mathrm{U}}}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}+\frac{\partial {\mathrm{t}}^{}\mathrm{Q}(\mathrm{U})}{\partial \mathrm{U}}{\mid}_{({\mathrm{U}}_{i}^{\mathrm{n}},{\dot{\mathrm{U}}}_{i}^{n},{t}_{i})}.\sigma -\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}_{i}^{\text{méca}}}{\partial \mathrm{U}}{\mid}_{({\mathrm{U}}_{i}^{\mathrm{n}},{t}_{i})}
$$

The first term is the contribution of the behaviour as in small transformations, with the difference that this contribution is here evaluated in the current configuration. The second term is the contribution of the geometry which is not present in small transformations. In the framework of the PETIT_REAC update, this term is not present in the computation of the tangent matrix. It is taken into account for the other options of geometric non-linearity (GREEN and SIMO_MIEHE)

The matrix ${K}_{i}^{\mathrm{Mn}}$ is the matrix of the tangent linear mapping of the “inertia forces” part of the system of non-linear equations  which is therefore:

$$
{\mathrm{K}}_{i}^{\mathrm{Mn}}=\frac{\partial {\mathrm{L}}_{\text{GR}}^{\text{iner}}}{\partial \mathrm{U}}{\mid}_{({\mathrm{U}}_{i}^{\mathrm{n}},{\dot{\mathrm{U}}}_{i}^{n},{\ddot{\mathrm{U}}}_{i}^{n})}
$$

In practice one may use the “true” tangent matrix ${K}_{i}^{n}$, but this presents a certain computation cost (computation and factorisation), or content oneself with an update from time to time: see in [u4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03) the factor keyword Newton, keyword MATRICE.

**Note: singular tangent matrix:**

One verifies on  that if the tangent matrix ${K}_{i}^{n}$ *is singular (case of a damaged material, or of a ductile plateau…), the dynamics is driven by the inertia forces, and that, the matrix* $\hat{K}$ *being regular, one nevertheless does find a corrector* $\delta {U}_{i}^{n+1}$ *(as if one were for example in a free-fall situation). However, if the time step is “too large”, the matrix* $(\hat{K}+{K}_{i}^{n}+{K}_{i}^{\mathrm{Mn}})$ *may be ill-conditioned and the solver finds for it an almost-zero pivot.*

After each Newton iteration having established a candidate solution of  without verifying the contact criterion, one launches the active-constraint algorithm in order to satisfy the contact conditions: one thus corrects $\delta {U}_{i}^{n+1}$. For the other frictional-contact algorithms of *Code_Aster*, such as that used by the discrete “shock” elements (DIS_CHOC), *cf.* , the Newton iteration  treats all the non-linearities at the same time.

### 3.4 Updating

In the case of small rotations, for the usual modellings (solid elements, beams, plates, shells, discrete elements…), the updating at the end of iteration $n+1$ relies on the formulae  and :

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l} U_i^{n+1} = U_i^n + \delta U_i^{n+1} \\ \Delta U_i^{n+1} = \Delta U_i^n + \delta U_i^{n+1} \\ \dot{U}_i^{n+1} = \dot{U}_i^n + \dfrac{\gamma}{\beta \Delta t} \delta U_i^{n+1} \\ \ddot{U}_i^{n+1} = \ddot{U}_i^n + \dfrac{1}{\beta \Delta t^2} \delta U_i^{n+1} \end{array} \right.
$$

In the case of the large rotations of structural elements (beams…) the updating, distinctly more complex, is indicated in [r5.03.40](doc:r5.03.40).

### 3.5 Stopping criterion

The global convergence criterion of the NEWTON algorithm is identical to that practised in STAT_NON_LINE, *cf.* . It expresses the verification of the dynamic equilibrium.

At the time instant ${t}_{i}$, one stops the iterations at rank $n$, as soon as the following inequality is satisfied:

$$
\begin{array}{}\frac{{\parallel {F}_{i}^{n}-\hat{L}({t}_{i})\parallel }_{\infty}}{{\parallel \hat{L}({t}_{i})+{L}^{\text{anél}}({t}_{i}){-}^{t}B.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}{-}^{t}A.{\mu}_{i}^{n}\parallel }_{\infty}}\\ =\frac{{\parallel {}^{t}Q({U}_{i}^{n}).{\sigma}_{i}^{n}+C.{\dot{U}}_{i}^{n}+M({U}_{i}^{n}).{\ddot{U}}_{i}^{n}{+}^{t}B.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}{+}^{t}A.{\mu}_{i}^{n}-{{L}_{\text{GR}}^{\text{iner}}}_{({U}_{i}^{n},{\dot{U}}_{i}^{n},{\ddot{U}}_{i}^{n})}-\hat{L}({t}_{i})\parallel }_{\infty}}{{\parallel \hat{L}({t}_{i})+{L}^{\text{anél}}({t}_{i}){-}^{t}B.{\lambda}_{i}^{n}{-}^{t}A.{\mu}_{i}^{n}\parallel }_{\infty}}\le \eta \end{array}
$$

$\eta$ is a tolerance, introduced as data by the user (keyword CONVERGENCE, operand RESI_GLOB_RELA), equal by default to 10–6, and ${\parallel .\parallel }_{\infty}$ is the maximum norm on the degrees of freedom.

The denominator of  is a norm of the loading at the time instant ${t}_{i}$, to which one refers the numerator, which is a norm of the forces not (yet) balanced.

**Note**:

*It comprises a contribution of the anelastic loads, useful for normalising the error on the equilibrium in the absence of external load. It must contain the internal forces due to the command variables. One moreover adds to it the inertia forces, so that in the rare cases where no other force acts, the criterion remains computable.*

Just as in statics, one must attach importance to a correct convergence because otherwise the estimates of internal forces and of contact reactions, verifying the constitutive relations and the frictional-contact constraint relations, move away from equilibrium, and it is not an inertia force $M({U}_{i}^{n}).{\ddot{U}}_{i}^{n}$ too far from the “exact” value which will suffice to produce at the following time step a good transient dynamic response.

One may also employ other convergence criteria as proposed in STAT_NON_LINE [u4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03).

## 4 Qualities and defects of the NEWMARK scheme

### 4.1 Properties of the NEWMARK scheme

This paragraph and the following one partly take up certain parts of . One may also consult ,  and .

#### 4.1.1 Generalities

One defines with the aid of the methods of numerical analysis several types of properties for a time-integration scheme. Here is their meaning:

1. Convergence: the solution tends towards a limit when the time step $\Delta t$ tends towards 0;
2. Accuracy: rate of convergence when the time step $\Delta t$ tends towards 0;
3. Consistency and order of the scheme: the residual is bounded by ${(\Delta t)}^{k+1}$ (example $k=\mathrm{2 }$ : for the trapezoidal rule): a polynomial of order $k+1$ is integrated exactly;
4. Stability: a finite perturbation of the initial state does not entail an exaggeratedly amplified perturbation (“numerical explosion”) of the subsequent state; the amplification (spectral radius) must be less than 1: for a scheme of general form ${U}_{i}=A{U}_{i-1}+{L}_{i}$, one must have $\rho (A)\le 1$. This is a necessary condition for not diverging! If the amplification $\rho (A)<1$ there is numerical attenuation.

The error in time $e$ induced by the NEWMARK scheme , , which approximates a Taylor expansion of the solution, is given by, *cf.* :

$$
e\approx \frac{\Delta {t}^{3}}{6}\parallel \Delta {\stackrel{\cdots }{U}}_{i}\parallel \approx \frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{6}\parallel \Delta {\ddot{U}}_{i}\parallel
$$

which one may normalise by the position vector $\parallel X\parallel$ or the amplitude $\parallel U\parallel$. One may also replace in  the ${L}_{2}$ norm ($\parallel \Delta {\ddot{U}}_{i}\parallel$) by the ${L}_{\infty}$ norm: ${\mid \Delta {\ddot{U}}_{i}\mid }_{\infty}=\underset{\mathrm{noeuds}k}{\max}(\mid \Delta {\ddot{U}}_{i}^{k}\mid )$. *Code_Aster* does not currently supply the information of this estimator.

The numerical analysis of the NEWMARK scheme is done on the treatment of the equation of the **linear** oscillator with one degree of freedom, without dissipative phenomenon:

$$
m\ddot{x}+\text{kx}=0
$$

The time schemes do **not give the exact solution** of :

$x={C}_{1}\cos\omega t+{C}_{2}\sin\omega t$

with $\omega =\sqrt{k/m}$, ${C}_{1}$ and ${C}_{2}$ depending on the initial conditions, but an **approximate solution**:

${x}_{i}=\left[\exp(-\xi \tilde{\omega}{t}_{i})\right]({C}_{1}^{'}\cos\tilde{\omega}{t}_{i}+{C}_{2}^{'}\sin\tilde{\omega}{t}_{i})$

Two errors are therefore introduced:

1. on the one hand, an artificial damping defined by the reduced damping ratio $\xi$ is introduced, which is the logarithmic decrement divided by $2\pi$;
2. on the other hand, the angular frequency $\omega$ corresponding to the exact period $T$ is replaced by the angular frequency $\tilde{\omega}$ corresponding to a period $\tilde{T}$.

These errors depend on the ratio $\frac{\Delta t}{T}$ and on the scheme itself.

By writing the equilibrium of the system with 1 degree of freedom , without imposed force, and the equations of the NEWMARK scheme ,  one obtains:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{pmatrix} x_{i+1} \\ \Delta t \dot{x}_{i+1} \\ \Delta t^2 \ddot{x}_{i+1} \end{pmatrix} = \dfrac{1}{1 + \beta \omega^2 \Delta t^2} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 & \frac{1}{2} - \beta \\ -\gamma \omega^2 \Delta t^2 & 1 + \omega^2 \Delta t^2 (\beta - \gamma) & 1 - \gamma - \frac{1}{2} \omega^2 \Delta t^2 (\gamma - 2\beta) \\ -\omega^2 \Delta t^2 & -\omega^2 \Delta t^2 & -\omega^2 \Delta t^2 \left( \frac{1}{2} - \beta \right) \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_i \\ \Delta t \dot{x}_i \\ \Delta t^2 \ddot{x}_i \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

whence in terms of increments:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\begin{pmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta t \Delta \dot{x} \\ \Delta t^2 \Delta \ddot{x} \end{pmatrix} = \dfrac{1}{1 + \beta \omega^2 \Delta t^2} \begin{pmatrix} -\beta \omega^2 \Delta t^2 & 1 & \frac{1}{2} - \beta \\ -\gamma \omega \Delta t & -\gamma \omega^2 \Delta t^2 & 1 - \gamma - \frac{1}{2} \omega^2 \Delta t^2 (\gamma - 2\beta) \\ -\omega^2 \Delta t^2 & -\omega^2 \Delta t^2 & -\frac{1}{2} \omega^2 \Delta t^2 - 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x_i \\ \Delta t \dot{x}_i \\ \Delta t^2 \ddot{x}_i \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
$$

The properties of this amplification matrix — which must preserve the harmonic character of the response, more precisely those of its characteristic polynomial — serve to characterise the properties of the scheme, in the linear regime, .

The properties of the NEWMARK scheme are summarised hereafter.

Because of the way chosen of expressing the scheme (*cf.*  and ), one cannot take $\beta =0$.

**For linear problems**, the scheme is **unconditionally stable**, even in the presence of physical damping — a perturbation is not amplified by the scheme — that is to say stable whatever the size of the time step, if the parameters satisfy the inequalities:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{l} 2\gamma \ge 1 \\ 4\beta \ge \left( \gamma + \dfrac{1}{2} \right)^2 \end{array} \right.
$$

If $2\gamma >1$ and $\beta <\frac{1}{4}{(\gamma +\frac{1}{2})}^{2}$ the scheme is **conditionally stable**: the time step must be chosen less than: $\Delta t\le \frac{{T}_{\min}}{\pi}{({(\gamma +\frac{1}{2})}^{2}-4\beta )}^{-1/2}$ — this relation being valid in the absence of physical damping $\xi =0$ but also in the presence of physical damping — as a function of the smallest vibration period ${T}_{\min}$ of the system studied, see , .

In the presence of physical damping $\xi >0$, one may allow slightly larger $\Delta t$.

In the presence of contact with impact, the stability of the scheme is ensured if $2\beta \ge \gamma \ge \mathrm{½}$, .

The NEWMARK scheme is of second order in time (at worst) if and only if $\gamma =1/2$. As soon as $\gamma >1/2$, the NEWMARK scheme is of order 1, and introduces a numerical dissipation proportional to $(\gamma -\frac{1}{2})\Delta t$. In order to have a numerical damping increasing with the frequency, it is appropriate to choose: $\beta \ge \frac{{(\gamma +1/2)}^{2}}{4}$, equality being the optimal choice, *cf.* the HHT scheme, see [r5.05.05-variante-schema-newmark].

If one chose $\gamma <1/2$, the NEWMARK scheme would bring a negative numerical damping which would lead to an instability.

$$
{e}_{\text{moy}}^{\text{norm}}\approx \frac{{\omega}^{3}\Delta {t}^{3}}{2\pi \left(1+\beta {\omega}^{2}{\mathit{\Delta t}}^{2}\right)}\sqrt{4+{\omega}^{2}\Delta {t}^{2}}
$$

This expression may serve as a reference error for the scheme applied to the dynamic response of an arbitrary structure.

**Note**:

The properties of the Newmark scheme obtained on the case of the oscillator with 1 degree of freedom are generalisable to the case of the linear dynamic system with several degrees of freedom (using a projection onto a modal basis) provided that the damping operator is proportional to the reduced mass and stiffness operators.

**Complementary note**:

*The Newmark scheme is known for introducing a notable error as soon as a non-infinitesimal rotation intervenes in the dynamic response of the solid. Moreover, the Newmark scheme may also generate an error if one intervenes too “brutally” on variations of parameters (time step,* $\beta$ or $\gamma$*) in the succession of the time discretisation over the duration of the transient.*

#### 4.1.2 Average-acceleration scheme or trapezoidal rule

The “trapezoidal rule” ($\beta =1/4$, $\gamma =1/2$), **unconditionally stable**, is the most commonly adopted, associated with a consistent mass (default option MASS_DIAG = 'NON'). One shows, for example in , that the relative frequency distortion brought by the integration scheme, for a damped system (the reduced damping is denoted $\xi =\frac{c}{2\sqrt{\text{km}}}=\frac{c}{\mathrm{2m}\omega }$) with 1 degree of freedom in free vibration, of damped natural angular frequency ${\omega}_{d}=\omega \sqrt{1-{\xi}^{2}}$:

$$
m\ddot{x}+c\dot{x}+\text{kx}=0
$$

is:

$$
\frac{{\omega}_{d}-{\omega}_{d}^{\text{Newmark}}}{{\omega}_{d}}=(\frac{1}{12}-\frac{{\xi}^{2}}{3}){\omega}^{2}\Delta {t}^{2}+o({\omega}^{4}\Delta {t}^{4})
$$

where ${\omega}_{d}^{\text{Newmark}}$ denotes the approximate value of ${\omega}_{d}$ by the scheme (via a spectral analysis of the response).

In forced vibrations, the dependence of  on ${\omega}^{2}\Delta {t}^{2}$ remains valid. In a general way, the frequencies are shifted downwards, and this shift increases if the time step increases and decreases for an increasing damping.

If one wishes to use a scheme where the frequencies are shifted upwards, it is appropriate to choose the option MASS_DIAG = 'OUI'.

One also shows in , that the relative damping distortion brought by the integration scheme of the “trapezoidal rule” is not zero for a system comprising a material damping. For small time steps:

$$
\frac{\xi -{\xi}^{\text{Newmark}}}{\xi}=\frac{{\omega}^{2}\Delta {t}^{2}}{6}+o({\omega}^{4}\Delta {t}^{4})
$$

where ${\xi}^{\mathit{Newmark}}$ denotes the value of $\xi$ resulting from the scheme.

The total damping decreases, and decreases when the time step increases. In forced vibrations, the dependence of the damping distortion  becomes in $\omega \Delta t$, *cf.* .

For larger time steps close to the *numerical cut-off angular frequency* due to the scheme, such that ${\hat{\omega}}_{d}^{\text{Newmark}}\Delta t=\pi$, *cf.* , one obtains the estimate (case of free vibrations):

$$
{\xi}^{\text{Newmark}}=\xi \frac{4\sqrt{1-{\xi}^{2}}}{\pi \omega \Delta t}+o\left({\omega}^{-2}\Delta {t}^{-2}\right)
$$

One may therefore summarise by the fact that the accuracy is good if one chooses $\Delta t=\frac{1}{10\omega }$ (*cf.* ), for the angular frequency $\omega$ of the system the highest that one seeks to describe, *cf.* [r5.05.05-choix-pas-temps].

### 4.2 Energy point of view

In the case of a linear elastic conservative system in small transformations without physical damping, one can easily evaluate the variations of the various energies during the time step $\Delta t$ between ${t}_{i}$ and ${t}_{i+1}$. Between these two time instants, one denotes the increment and the average of the fields:

$\Delta \Phi =\Phi ({t}_{i+1})-\Phi ({t}_{i})$             $\bar{\Phi}=\frac{1}{2}(\Phi ({t}_{i+1})+\Phi ({t}_{i}))$

Thus one has respectively for the kinetic energy, the strain energy ; $M$ and $K$ being symmetric, and the potential of the external forces independent of time — the system being conservative:

$\Delta {E}_{\text{cin}}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta \left({}^{t}\dot{\Phi}.\mathrm{M}.\dot{\Phi}\right)={\Delta}^{t}\dot{\Phi}\phantom{\rule{1em}{0ex}}.\mathrm{M}.\overline{\dot{\Phi}}$

$\Delta {E}_{\text{déf}}=\frac{1}{2}\Delta \left({}^{t}\Phi .\mathrm{K}.\Phi \right)={\Delta}^{t}\Phi \phantom{\rule{1em}{0ex}}.\mathrm{K}.\overline{\Phi}$

$\Delta {V}_{\mathit{ext}}=-\Delta \left({}^{t}\Phi .\mathrm{F}\right)=-{\Delta}^{t}\Phi \phantom{\rule{1em}{0ex}}.\overline{\mathrm{F}}$

Using the equilibrium at the time instants ${t}_{i}$ and ${t}_{i+1}$. under the action of the external forces, one obtains:

$$
\mathrm{M}.\Delta \ddot{\Phi}+\mathrm{K}.\Delta \Phi =0`     ;    :math:`\mathrm{M}.\overline{\ddot{\Phi}}+\mathrm{K}.\overline{\Phi}=\overline{\mathrm{F}}
$$

The variations of the various energies, including the total energy of the system — or Hamiltonian: $\Delta {E}_{\text{tot}}=\Delta {E}_{\text{cin}}+\Delta {E}_{\text{déf}}+\Delta {V}_{\text{ext}}$, are expressed, exploiting  in order to eliminate the loading $F$ and the terms of the scheme  and :

$$
\begin{array}{}\Delta {E}_{\text{cin}}=\Delta t.({}^{t}\bar{\ddot{\Phi}}-\frac{1-2\gamma }{2}\Delta {}^{t}\ddot{\Phi}).M.\bar{\dot{\Phi}}\\ \Delta {E}_{\text{déf}}=\Delta t.(\bar{\dot{\Phi}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}(2\beta -\gamma ).\Delta \ddot{\Phi}).K.\bar{\Phi}\\ \Delta {V}_{\text{ext}}=-\Delta t.(\bar{\dot{\Phi}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}(2\beta -\gamma ).\Delta \ddot{\Phi}).\bar{F}\\ \Delta {E}_{\text{tot}}{=}^{t}\bar{\dot{\Phi}}.M.\Delta \dot{\Phi}{-}^{t}\bar{\ddot{\Phi}}.M.\Delta \Phi \end{array}
$$

And one obtains that the variation of total energy of the system is expressed:

$$
\Delta {E}_{\text{tot}}=-\frac{\Delta t}{2}.\left(\left(1-2\gamma \right)\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}.\Delta {\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}}^{t}\ddot{\Phi}.\mathrm{M}.\overline{\dot{\Phi}}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}+\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}\Delta t.\left(2\beta -\gamma \right)\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}\Delta {\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}}^{t}\ddot{\Phi}.\mathrm{M}.\overline{\ddot{\Phi}}\right)
$$

One verifies that when $\gamma =1/2$ and $\beta =1/4$ (trapezoidal rule), one has no numerical energy dissipation brought by the scheme. One notes that a different choice of $\gamma$ and/or $2\beta \ne \gamma$ brings a dissipation proportional to the time step.

One also verifies that if $\gamma >\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}1/2$ and $2\beta -\gamma >0$, the numerical damping is positive, *cf.* , which is also consistent with what was stated in [r5.05.05-generalites].

### 4.3 Properties of the NEWMARK scheme for non-linear problems

**For non-linear problems** (large strains, material non-linearities), the scheme is **unconditionally stable**, if $\beta \ge \frac{1}{4}$ and with $\gamma =1/2$, *cf.* proof in .

In **the presence of unilateral contact**, one finds in the literature, *cf.* , the advice to take $\beta =\gamma$ (for example with $\beta =\gamma =1/2$) which ensures the compatibility of the velocities during the phase where the contact is maintained between two solids.

This is obtained directly from equations  and ; the jump $[\text{ . }]$ of velocity between the two points remaining in contact over the step $\Delta t$ ($\left[\Phi \left(t\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}\right)\right]=0$ and $\left[\Phi \left(t\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}+\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}\Delta t\right)\right]=0$) at the time instant $t+\Delta t$ is indeed:

$\left[\dot{\Phi}(t+\Delta t)\right]=(1-\gamma /\beta ).\left[\dot{\Phi}(t)\right]+(1-\gamma /(2\beta )).\Delta t.\left[\ddot{\Phi}(t)\right]$

If one chooses $\beta =\gamma$, then $[\dot{\Phi}(t+\Delta t)]=o(\Delta t)$

.However this choice is not compatible with that of an optimum in terms of numerical dissipation, as defined by the $\alpha$-method (*cf.* [r5.05.05-variante-schema-newmark]): one would have to take $\alpha =-1-\sqrt{2}$ which gives an enormous numerical damping! One therefore does not encourage the user to follow this recommendation.

If the unilateral contact is solved by a penalisation method, one does not escape the risk of instability even by introducing a numerical damping.

### 4.4 Choice of the time steps

The time step to be chosen must respect a certain number of conditions.

The first, obvious, is that it must be **adapted to the time sampling of the loadings** applied to the system studied. Incidentally, it may be opportune to reconsider a modelling with a time dependence of the applied loadings that is too “violent”, by smoothing slope breaks for example.

One advises, for **reasons of accuracy** (“Shannon”-type criterion on the cut-off frequency), a time step to be chosen such that:

$$
\Delta t\le 0,10{T}_{\min}
$$

where ${T}_{\min}$ denotes the smallest vibration period of the system that one wishes to study.

The space step of the finite-element mesh cells chosen also intervenes: the maximum time step $\Delta t$ to be chosen is of the order of $h/c$, where $c$ is the celerity of the elastic compression waves of the material and $h$ a characteristic size of the mesh cells, if one seeks to describe partially high-frequency phenomena, for which however this numerical formulation of elastodynamics is not truly adapted (other numerical methods exist for doing so).

Finally in the case of solids presenting rigid modes (free fall for example), in accordance with the note made in [r5.05.05-schema-newmark], it is appropriate to choose a time step $\Delta t$ sufficiently small for the mass terms to be of the same order as those of stiffness (in the matrix $\hat{K}$, *cf.* ). Thus, one may choose:

$$
\Delta t\approx \sqrt{\frac{L}{50g}}
$$

where $L$ is the diameter of the solid considered in “free fall” under the acceleration of gravity $g$. Thus the displacement increment $g\Delta {t}^{2}/2$ during this time step is small compared with the dimensions of the solid, or, in another way, similar to an elastic displacement under a field of action of the same magnitude.

It is possible to have several dynamic analyses succeed one another, over successive time intervals, communicating by restarts by choosing as initial state the result of the last step studied previously, using very distinct time steps according to the a priori idea that one has of the response of the system studied. One does not possess a general result with this type of choice in terms of convergence…

One also knows that the treatment of collisions is sensitive to the timing of the time steps, with respect to the “real” instants of shock: one will have to study the sensitivity of the response obtained.

However, a “too small” time step may exacerbate the oscillations induced by the discontinuity.

One may also turn towards the choice of an explicit scheme, of lower order (order 1), *cf.* for example  even if such a choice leads one to take very small time steps.

It is however advised to keep the time step constant during a phase of stationary linear dynamic response, in order to keep the properties stated previously.

**Notes**:

One must note that Code_Aster does not today propose a multi-domain method in time and space, which would make it possible to define a time step per zone in the solid studied, nor an error criterion in dynamics.

*In the operator* *DYNA_NON_LINE, one may choose to solve the equilibrium in displacement or in velocity for the implicit schemes (for the explicit schemes it is in acceleration). The following keyword allows this choice:* FORMULATION = 'DEPLACEMENT' or 'VITESSE'.

## 5 A variant of the Newmark scheme: the HHT schemes and the modified average acceleration

### 5.1 Motivation

In mechanical analysis, one wishes the low frequencies to be reproduced as faithfully as possible.

One wishes on the other hand that the high frequencies be attenuated by the computation because they may generate numerical instabilities and because the associated mechanical stresses are in general small.

The curves giving the damping factor $\xi$ as a function of $\frac{\Delta t}{T}$ (period $T=2\pi /\omega$) must therefore:

- start from the origin with a horizontal tangent in order to give a very weak damping to the low frequencies,
- be increasing functions in order to attenuate the high frequencies, and this all the more so as they are higher.

In order to try to reach these objectives, Hilber, Hughes and Taylor (HHT) proposed in  to define the Newmark parameters $\beta$ and $\gamma$ as a function of a third parameter $\alpha$ negative by the following relations, which are modelled on the stability conditions :

$$
\gamma =\frac{1}{2}-\alpha
$$

$$
\beta =\frac{{(1-\alpha )}^{2}}{4}
$$

This choice offers the best compromise on the accuracy and the damping at high frequencies.

This parameter $\alpha$, negative, is supplied via the operand ALPHA of the keyword SCHEMA_TEMPS (SCHEMA='HHT') of [u4.53.01](doc:u4.53.01).

### 5.3 Properties of the modified average-acceleration scheme

One must have:

$$
\alpha \le 0
$$

for the stability conditions of the scheme to be fulfilled, *cf.* [r5.05.05-proprietes-schema-newmark-problemes-non-lineaires]: the scheme is **unconditionally stable**.

The “modified average-acceleration” scheme (also called the $\alpha$-method), therefore brings $\beta ={(\gamma +\frac{1}{2})}^{2}/4$, which is the optimal choice for bringing a damping increasing on the high frequencies.

When $\alpha =0$, the “modified average-acceleration” scheme becomes again the “trapezoidal rule” and the damping is zero [r5.05.05-AmortHHHTOvershoot].

The value $\alpha =-1$, that is $\gamma =\frac{3}{2}$ and $\beta =1$ produces the strongest dissipation at high frequency, but destroys a great deal of the accuracy on the low-frequency modes.

In practice, in the original “modified average-acceleration” scheme, one limits oneself to $\alpha \in \left[-\frac{1}{3},0\right]$, which ensures the monotonicity of the increase of the damping as a function of the frequency. The choice $\alpha =-0.10$ seems to be effective, even if it is possible to take values of $\alpha$ higher in absolute value.

### 5.4 Properties of the HHT scheme

One must have:

$$
\alpha \le 0
$$

for the stability conditions of the scheme to be fulfilled, *cf.* [r5.05.05-proprietes-schema-newmark-problemes-non-lineaires]: the scheme is **unconditionally stable**.

### 5.5 HHT scheme and overshooting

As mentioned in the paragraph r5.03.01-proprietes-schema-hht, the HHT scheme initially introduced in  induces a damping in the high frequencies, while presenting a limited damping in the low frequencies. This makes it possible in particular to eliminate modes associated with high frequencies, for which the spatial and/or time discretisation is not sufficient.

However, certain components of the numerical solution may be weakly damped (notably in the case of *stiff* systems), which induces the existence of oscillations altering the solution, leading to the phenomenon known under the name of *overshooting*.
In particular, Hulbert and Hughes demonstrated the existence of overshooting in velocity in the case of the HHT scheme (see ).
Moreover, Erlicher *et al.* showed that the entirety of the family of $G-\alpha$ schemes (of which the HHT scheme is part) may present overshooting (see ). Furthermore, Bauchau *et al.* also propose a discussion relative to the effects of overshooting on the responses obtained with the HHT scheme.

Thus, in spite of its appreciable numerical damping properties, the HHT scheme may be subject
to overshooting phenomena. In this context, Kaiping proposed in  a modification concerning the set of schemes of $G-\alpha$ type, with a view to limiting the effects of overshooting. In particular, a new version of the HHT scheme, entitled NOHHT (*No Overshooting HHT*), was able to be proposed, via a modification of the coefficients of the scheme. One refers to  for further details relative to this modification.

The NOHHT scheme of  may be used in [u4.53.01](doc:u4.53.01), via the keyword SCHEMA_TEMPS.

With a view to comparing the HHT and NOHHT schemes, one considers the elementary case (introduced in )
of a system with one degree of freedom, of mass $m=1$ kg, of natural period $T=1$ s, and endowed with a reduced damping $\xi \in [0,1[$.
In order to study the effect of overshooting in velocity, one computes the absolute error between the velocities obtained with the analytical solution of the problem and the numerical solution at the first time instant following the initial time instant, and this for several values of time step $h > 0$.
The results obtained in the cases $\xi=0$ (respectively $\xi=0.5$) are given in Figure r5.05.05-sdld32a-overshooting (respectively r5.05.05-sdld32b-overshooting). In the case of the classical HHT scheme, the error in velocity increases significantly when the chosen time step becomes large. On the other hand, this effect is appreciably limited in the case of the NOHHT scheme. This latter scheme therefore does indeed make it possible to limit the effects of overshooting.

## 6 The explicit schemes

*code_aster* allows the use of two different explicit schemes: that of the central differences and that of Tchamwa–Wielgosz , which, for its part, is dissipative. These schemes, unlike the implicit schemes, are conditionally stable: there exists a limit time step that one cannot exceed on pain of divergence.

These schemes are very well adapted to fast-dynamics problems, where one seeks to analyse the phenomena of wave propagation. Their cost becomes prohibitive, at least on a physical basis, when one must carry out numerical simulations over long times, as is the case when one seeks to obtain a vibratory response or when the imposed loading is of long duration: from several seconds, as, for example, in an earthquake.

One must however point out that, compared with a solution with an implicit scheme, the explicit approaches cost much less on a time step. Indeed, whereas in implicit one must truly solve the assembled global system of large dimension at each step (or even at each iteration in the non-linear case), in explicit one dispenses with this very costly inversion by using a diagonal operator (based on the mass matrix alone, which therefore does not require an update: with the keyword MASS_DIAG = 'OUI'). With an explicit scheme, one can solve only in acceleration (keyword FORMULATION = 'ACCELERATION').

### 6.1 The central-difference scheme

This scheme (keyword SCHEMA_TEMPS, SCHEMA='DIFF_CENT'), the most common in fast-dynamics codes, is a particular case of the Newmark family. It is built by taking $\beta =0$ and $\gamma =1/2$. This method of integration in time, which does not introduce numerical dissipation, is of order 2.

Its stability time step (CFL condition: Courant Friedrichs and Levy ) is $2/\omega$, with $\omega$ which is the highest natural angular frequency of the structure considered.

**Notes**

On a physical basis, one can show that the critical time step is equivalent to the minimum time that a wave takes to cross the smallest finite element of the mesh. In practice, one approximates this value by computing the minimum, for all the finite elements, of: $\Delta t=D/C$ with d which is the diameter of the largest inscribed sphere and $c$ which is the celerity of the elastic compression waves in the element considered.

This method of computation of the CFL condition, directly applicable on the solid elements, needs to be corrected for the structural elements.

For the classical industrial applications, the CFL condition obliges one to take very small time steps (generally of the order of ${10}^{-5}s$. for metallic structures).

On the other hand, on a modal basis, because of the strong truncation generally made for industrial cases, the critical time step, given directly by $2/\omega$ with $\omega$ which is the cut-off angular frequency, will be much larger than on a physical basis.

The explicit solution is always done in acceleration (the operator to be inverted then reduces to the mass matrix alone which one recommends “lumping”, hence making it diagonal).

### 6.2 The Tchamwa–Wielgosz scheme

This recent scheme may be seen as the counterpart of the HHT scheme for the explicit case. The objective sought is the same: to introduce a high-frequency numerical dissipation, which does not disturb “too much” the low-frequency solution. This Tchamwa–Wielgosz scheme  (keyword SCHEMA_TEMPS, SCHEMA='TCHAMWA') is not deduced from the Newmark family.

Just like HHT, this scheme is parameterised by a real number, named $\phi$. If one takes $\phi$ =1, the scheme does not dissipate. In practice it is recommended to choose a value of $\phi$ between 1 and 1.1.

The critical time step is that of the central-difference scheme divided by $\phi$.

### 6.3 Performance of the explicit schemes in code_aster

*code_aster* is not a code dedicated to fast dynamics; it is a general-purpose code oriented towards implicit approaches. It is therefore not optimised for explicit solutions, in particular as regards the computation of the internal forces and the structuring of the objects generated, such as the solution data structure.

It is therefore not recommended to use an explicit scheme for large-size problems, all the more so if one wishes to compute over a very large number of steps.

### 6.4 Explicit computations on a Ritz basis with subdomains in code_aster

The comments of the previous paragraph are however to be qualified inasmuch as it is possible to proceed to explicit computations on a Ritz basis with subdomains (with “generalised” degrees of freedom) in *Code_Aster*. That is to say that one can by means of the factor keyword PROJ_MODAL carry out the computation with an explicit time-integration scheme on a modal (or Ritz) projection basis computed beforehand.

This option intervenes in the case where one wishes to condense dynamically a part of the model with linear behaviour, by computing strictly by DYNA_NON_LINE only subdomains with non-linear behaviour. This, in order to reduce the size of the computational model. In this case, it is necessary to compute a Ritz modal basis on the whole model: the non-linear subdomain modelled for the computation calling on DYNA_NON_LINE and the other subdomains assumed linear and condensed dynamically. This basis must be orthogonalised with respect to the mass and linear stiffness matrices of the whole model. It must simply be representative of the motions activating the whole model.

The modelled computational domain thus comprises a non-linear computational subdomain $I$ and a series of linear subdomains $E$ “external” to the computation which will be condensed dynamically.

The matrices $M$, $C$, $K$, the forces $F$ may be decomposed into a part associated with the domain $I$ and a part associated with the subdomain(s) $E$. The equilibrium of the structure solved by DYNA_NON_LINE may then be written in terms of global finite-element degrees of freedom $U$:

$$
({M}_{I}+{M}_{E}).\ddot{U}={F}_{I}^{\text{ext}}+{F}_{E}^{\text{ext}}–{F}_{I}^{int}(U,\dot{U})–{K}_{E}U-({C}_{I}+{C}_{E}).\dot{U}
$$

Let $\lbrace \Phi \rbrace$ be a Ritz modal basis orthogonalised by elementary substructuring on the set of subdomains $I$ and $E$. One then uses the Ritz transform: $U = \Phi \, q`$ and therefore by projecting  by ${}^{t}\Phi$ on the left one obtains:

$$
({\tilde{M}}_{I}+{\tilde{M}}_{E}).\ddot{q}={}^{t}\Phi .{F}_{I}^{\text{ext}}+{\tilde{F}}_{E}^{\text{ext}}{–}^{t}\Phi .({F}_{I}^{int}+{C}_{I}.\dot{U})-{\tilde{K}}_{E}.q-{\tilde{C}}_{E}.\dot{q}
$$

The modes $\Phi$ reduced to $I$ are denoted $\phi$ and the degrees of freedom $U$ reduced to $I$ are denoted $u$ hence  becomes for each time step:

$$
\tilde{M}.\ddot{q}={\phi }^{t}.{F}_{I}^{\text{ext}}–{\phi }^{t}.({F}_{I}^{int}(u,\dot{u})+{C}_{I}.\dot{u})+{\tilde{F}}_{E}^{\text{ext}}-{\tilde{K}}_{E}.q-{\tilde{C}}_{\mathrm{E.}}\dot{q}
$$

One therefore needs the generalised mass matrices $\tilde{M}=({\tilde{M}}_{I}+{\tilde{M}}_{E})$ diagonal as well as the matrices of the subdomains $E$: ${\tilde{K}}_{E}$ and ${\tilde{C}}_{E}$ full and the generalised vector ${\tilde{F}}_{E}^{\text{ext}}$ which participate in the complementary terms to the standard dynamic problem reduced to the subdomain $I$ which would be solved without dynamic condensation in the absence of subdomains $E$ by DYNA_NON_LINE:

$\tilde{M}.\ddot{q}={\phi }^{t}.{F}_{I}^{\text{ext}}–{\phi }^{t}.({F}_{I}^{int}(u,\dot{u})+{C}_{I}.\dot{u})$

At each time step, one evaluates and adds the complementary terms resulting from the dynamic condensation, cf. , to those of the standard dynamic problem without dynamic condensation and one solves with the explicit time-integration scheme in generalised coordinates $q$, having expressed with the explicit scheme the terms in $\mathrm{u},\dot{\mathrm{u}}$ on the subdomain $I$.

The knowledge of the orthogonalised Ritz modal basis $\lbrace \phi \rbrace$ then makes it possible to obtain both the degrees of freedom $u$ on the solution subdomain $I$ and $U$ on the whole model constituted of its subdomains.

One will supply behind the keyword MODE_MECA only the modes $\lbrace \phi \rbrace$ obtained by reduction of the Ritz basis to the non-linear computational domain $I$ treated by DYNA_NON_LINE, behind the keyword MASSE_GENE the generalised mass matrix $\tilde{M}$, as well as behind the keywords RIGI_GENE AMOR_GENE the generalised operators of the subdomains $E$: ${\tilde{K}}_{E}$, ${\tilde{C}}_{E}$ and finally with the factor keyword EXCIT_GENE ${\tilde{F}}_{E}$. An example of computation is supplied by the test case SDNV107A [v5.03.107](doc:v5.03.107).

The main nuance with respect to the restriction on the use of an explicit scheme for large-size problems consists both in the possibility of reducing the size of the computational model and in the use of a CFL condition on the time step linked to the maximum frequency of the projection basis, which makes it possible to reduce strongly the number of computation time steps.

## 7 Mass shift

It is possible to enter a coefficient $\mathrm{coef}$ behind the operand COEF_MASS_SHIFT in the keyword SCHEMA_TEMPS in order to carry out a “shift” of the mass matrix $M$ which becomes: $M'=M+\mathrm{coef.}K$.

The introduction of this coefficient makes it possible to invert in dynamics with an explicit scheme on a physical basis the mass matrix when the latter has zero terms for certain specific degrees of freedom, for example the pressure for the HM modelling elements. It should be noted that this shift is not necessary in dynamics with an explicit scheme on a modal basis (presence of the keyword PROJ_MODAL) because in this case the matrix to be inverted is the generalised mass matrix which is always invertible.

The entry of this coefficient also makes it possible to improve strongly the convergence in dynamics with an implicit or explicit scheme whatever the type of modelling by imposing a cut-off frequency inversely proportional to the value of $\mathrm{coef}$. This practice, akin to the “selective mass scaling” method proposed by Lars Olovsson in numerous publications, makes it possible to satisfy the convergence criteria with larger time steps at the price of a distortion of the set of natural frequencies of the system, slight however for the low frequencies.

A natural angular frequency of origin $\omega$ then becomes $\omega '$ such that: $\omega {'}^{2}=\frac{{\omega}^{2}}{1+\mathit{coef}.{\omega}^{2}}$

One notes from this expression that $\omega '$ tends towards a maximum value ${\mathit{coef}}^{-0.5}$.

Thus, for example, for a maximum value in practice for $\mathrm{coef}={10}^{-6}$, the corrected maximum natural angular frequency of the system $\omega '$ is $1000r{\mathrm{d.s}}^{-1}$, and a value for a natural frequency of origin of $30\mathrm{Hz}$ is corrected by this method to $29,481\mathit{Hz}$.

**Notes**:

*It is appropriate to point out that even if one activates the mass “shift” option, for the computation of the initial acceleration, it is always* *the non-shifted mass matrix which is used. The fact that it may be non-invertible does not however pose a problem, because in this case, one imposes the initial acceleration at 0 at every point.*

*The stiffness matrix used for this “shift” is in practice that which is computed during the prediction phase of the Newton algorithm.*

## 9 Conclusion

The Newmark time-integration scheme and its variants called “modified average acceleration” and HHT, accompanied by the Newton method, make it possible to treat numerous types of non-linear dynamic problems, for material or geometric non-linearities.

The treatment of highly non-linear and non-regular dynamic problems, such as the analysis of structures in large displacements or of frictional contact, is subject to numerical instability, even with time-integration methods unconditionally stable in the linear domain. One then succeeds in integrating the equations of motion with respect to time only by introducing artificial dissipation. The whole art is to dose this dissipation so that, in the range of frequencies which present a mechanical interest, it is roughly equivalent to the natural damping, without shifting too much the vibratory spectrum of the structure.

Two explicit schemes are also proposed. One recommends that the users of the operator DYNA_NON_LINE read the documentation [u2.06.13](doc:u2.06.13) whose role is to help to use these transient solution methods well.
