# r4.06.03 Cyclic dynamic substructuring

**Summary:**

This report rests on the notions of analysis by modal synthesis described in the document [R4.06.02](doc:r4.06.02).

We address the methods of cyclic dynamic substructuring. Entirely devoted to the study of structures with cyclic repetitivity, they take the best advantage of the geometric particularities of the structure. The CRAIG-BAMPTON and MAC NEAL methods, developed in this framework, are presented.

## 1 Introduction

In this document, we present a synthesis of the methods of cyclic dynamic substructuring. We give a definition of cyclic repetitivity (or cyclic symmetry) and we present the principal consequences of this property for the dynamic behaviour of the structure (nodal circles and nodal diameters, double modes). Then we present, in some detail, the two methods of cyclic dynamic substructuring implemented in *Code_Aster*. Improvements have been made to the classical methods by taking into account the presence of the nodes of the axis.

These methods assume that the mesh of the basic sector is such that its traces on the right and left interfaces coincide (compatible meshes).

## 2 Cyclic repetitivity

### 2.1 Definition

A structure is said to have cyclic repetitivity of axis $\mathrm{Oz}$ if there exists an angle $0<\alpha <\pi$ such that the structure is geometrically and mechanically invariant under rotation about $\mathrm{Oz}$ through this angle. If $\alpha$ is the smallest angle satisfying this property, then any angular portion of angle $\alpha$ of the structure is called the "basic sector" (or "irreducible sector").

The global structure is then composed of $N$ sectors:

$$
N=\frac{2\Pi }{\alpha}
$$

### 2.2 Wave propagation

One denotes by $\theta$ the rotation of axis $\mathrm{Oz}$ and of angle $\alpha$ defined in ${R}^{3}$ .

Consider a basic sector of a structure with repetitivity of axis $\mathrm{Oz}$ , and two similar points of two adjacent sectors $G$ and $D$ :

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.03/1000036A00000AF500000845CCFC495C3D936127.svg)

One has the relation between the points $G$ and $D$ :

$$
G=\theta (D)
$$

One observes that the structure is left invariant by any rotation ${\theta}^{m}$ (with $m$ an integer).

One may note that all the rotations leaving the structure invariant (geometrically and mechanically) are finite in number:

$$
{\theta}^{m}m\in \lbrace 0,1,\mathrm{....}N-1\rbrace
$$

Consider a scalar state variable of the mechanical system under study $U$ , and $Z$ the associated complex:

$$
U=\text{Re}(Z)=\text{Re}(U+\mathrm{jV})
$$

It is possible to show, by the theory of finite groups, the following relation for the points $D$ and $G$ [bib5]:

$$
\exists m\in \lbrace 0,1,\mathrm{...},\frac{N}{2}\rbrace \mathrm{tel}\mathrm{que}Z(G)={e}^{\mathrm{jm}\alpha }Z(D)
$$

**Notes:**

- *the quantities are expressed in the cylindrical frame* $(r,\theta ,z)$ *,*
- *for an axisymmetric structure (particular case of cyclic repetitivity),* $m$ *is called the FOURIER index,*
- *in the case of an undamped plane wave,* ${e}^{\mathrm{jm}\alpha }$ *is the complex phase shift between two adjacent sectors; the equation means that this phase shift can take only a finite number of known values,*
- *it is possible to limit the number of values of* $m$ *to the values lying between 0 and* $N/2$ *; indeed, one shows that the wave associated with the phase shift* $N-m$ *is identical to that associated with the phase shift* $m$ , but travels in the opposite direction [bib5].

If $N$ is even: $m=0$ and $m=N/2$ correspond to real modes:

$$
\begin{array}{}m=0\Rightarrow \forall DU(\theta (D))=U(D)\\ \\ m=N/2\Rightarrow \forall DU(\theta (D))=-U(D)\end{array}
$$

All the other values of $m$ correspond to modes appearing in orthogonal pairs at a given frequency (one then speaks of degenerate modes):

$$
U=\text{Re}(Z)\mathrm{et}V=\text{Im}(Z)
$$

If

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.03/Object_62.svg)

is odd: $m=0$ corresponds to a non-degenerate real mode:

$$
m=0\Rightarrow \forall DU(\theta (D))=U(D)
$$

All the other values of $m$ correspond to degenerate modes appearing in orthogonal pairs:

$$
U=\text{Re}(Z)\mathrm{et}V=\text{Im}(Z)
$$

### 2.3 Notion of nodal diameters and nodal circles

The property of cyclic repetitivity, expressed by equation (), makes it possible to know a priori the appearance of the eigenmodes of the structure, which comes very close to what can be observed for axisymmetric structures. If one considers an eigenmode of a structure with cyclic symmetry, all the sectors have the same deformed shape but with an amplitude depending on their angular position, which can be expressed by a phase shift between substructures. This mode can be classified from the number of nodal diameters and of nodal circles which characterise it. A nodal diameter (which coincides with a diameter only if the structure is axisymmetric) is a line of points of zero motion passing through the axis of repetitivity; a nodal circle (which has the circular shape only for axisymmetric structures) is a line of points of zero motion, itself with cyclic repetitivity. One observes that it is the deformed shape of the mode of the substructure on which the mode of the complete structure rests that determines the number of nodal circle(s). By contrast, the number of nodal diameter(s) is defined by the phase shift between two consecutive sectors.

### 2.4 Boundary conditions

In general, the implementation of the cyclic substructuring methods with a reduction technique should not require any particular treatment in the case where the sector presents nodes lying on the axis of rotation. One would simply be in a case where certain nodes belong simultaneously to the right and left interfaces. Taking the boundary conditions into account in Code_Aster nevertheless imposes treating the two cases separately:

  - on the one hand the continuity relations between the facing faces, excluding the axis
  - on the other hand the relations associated with the nodes carried by the axis of rotation, if any exist.

#### 2.4.1 Connection equations between the right and left faces – axis excluded

Consider a structure with cyclic repetitivity, and two successive basic sectors of it:

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.03/1000074000001A9000000DBF4D1FA2878F443854.svg)

The connections between sectors being considered perfect, one has the conditions between the sectors:

$$
{q}_{g}^{k}={q}_{d}^{k+1}
$$

$$
{f}_{{L}_{g}}^{k}=-{f}_{{L}_{d}}^{k+1}
$$

The superscript indicates the number of the sector considered. The preceding connection conditions are expressed in the global frame.

By relation () (wave propagation in the structure) and setting: $\beta =m\alpha$ , one has:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{({q}^{k+1})}_{k+1}={e}^{j\beta }{({q}^{k})}_{k}\\ {({f}^{k+1})}_{k+1}={e}^{j\beta }{({f}^{k})}_{k}\end{array}
$$

The index $k$ means that the quantity is expressed in the frame attached to sector $k$ : ${R}^{k}$ .

The connection equations (), written in the frame attached to sector $k$ , therefore involve the transformation matrix from sector $k$ to sector $k+1$ . This matrix is none other than the rotation matrix of the right degrees of freedom towards those on the left, that is the rotation matrix of axis $\mathrm{Oz}$ and of angle $\alpha$ , denoted $\theta$ .

We therefore obtain the following system:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{({q}_{g}^{k})}_{k}={e}^{j\beta }\theta {({q}_{d}^{k})}_{k}\\ {({f}_{{L}_{g}}^{k})}_{k}=-{e}^{j\beta }\theta {({f}_{{L}_{d}}^{k})}_{k}\end{array}
$$

The boundary conditions () make it possible to compute the eigenmodes of the whole of the structure from a single basic sector.

#### 2.4.2 Equations satisfied by the degrees of freedom carried by the axis

This formulation can be extended to the case of the nodes of the axis. We then obtain, for a given sector:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{q}_{a}={e}^{j\beta }{\theta}_{a}{q}_{a}\\ {f}_{{L}_{a}}=-{e}^{j\beta }{\theta}_{a}{f}_{{L}_{a}}\end{array}
$$

The complex exponential having modulus 1, the continuity of the displacements of the axis can also be put in the more classical form

$$
{\theta}_{a}{q}_{a}={e}^{-j\beta }{q}_{a}
$$

This is an eigenvalue problem, and the axis can admit displacements only if the pairs $({q}_{a},{e}^{-j\beta })$ correspond to the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the rotation matrix ${\theta}_{a}$ . The eigenvalues of ${\theta}_{a}$ are $(1,{e}^{j\alpha },{e}^{-j\alpha })$ , associated respectively with the eigenvectors which are the axis of rotation, and with two axes orthogonal to each other and orthogonal to the axis of rotation. The only values of $\beta$ making it possible to obtain displacements of the axis are therefore:

- $\beta =0$ , that is m=0. The displacements take place only in the direction of the axis of rotation.
- $\beta =\alpha$ , that is m=1. The displacements take place in a direction normal to the axis of rotation.

Furthermore, the equilibrium relations can also be put in the form of an eigenvalue problem

$$
{\theta}_{a}{f}_{{L}_{a}}={e}^{-j(\beta +\pi )}{f}_{{L}_{a}}={e}^{-j(\beta -\pi )}{f}_{{L}_{a}}
$$

As previously, this system admits a non-identically zero solution only if ${e}^{-j(\beta -\pi )}$ is an eigenvalue of ${\theta}_{a}$ , that is $\beta \in (\pi ,\alpha +\pi ,\pi -\alpha )$ . For the cases m=0 and m=1, this amounts to having $\alpha =\pi /2$ or $\alpha =\pi$ . If the angular opening of the sector is different from $\pi$ or $\pi /2$ , that is a problem with two or four sectors, then one will necessarily have ${f}_{{L}_{a}}=0$ for the cases with 0 and 1 diameter.

## 3 Methods of cyclic substructuring

### 3.1 Craig-Bampton method

One considers the eigenvalue problem of the global structure expressed on the basic sector. The latter is therefore subjected to the connection forces which are applied to it by the adjacent sectors. Furthermore, the basic sector satisfies the connection equations (). We therefore have:

$$
\begin{array}{c}(K-{\omega}^{2}M)q={f}_{L}\\ \\ {q}_{g}={e}^{j\beta }\theta {q}_{d}\\ \\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}=-{e}^{j\beta }\theta {f}_{{L}_{d}}\end{array}
$$

We assume that the basis is composed of the dynamic eigenmodes of the basic sector clamped at its interfaces, denoted $\varphi$ , and of the constraint modes relative to the right and left interface degrees of freedom, denoted ${\Psi}_{d}$ and ${\Psi}_{g}$ .

Given that the only contribution to the displacements of an interface degree of freedom comes from the corresponding constraint mode, the RITZ transformation can be written:

$$
q=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{q}_{i}\\ {q}_{d}\\ {q}_{g}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\varphi & {\Psi}_{d}& {\Psi}_{g}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{i}\\ {q}_{d}\\ {q}_{g}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\Phi \eta
$$

Henceforth, using the RITZ transformation, the system of equations [eq 3.1-1] becomes:

$$
\begin{array}{c}(\overline{K}-{\omega}^{2}\overline{M})\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{i}\\ {q}_{d}\\ {q}_{g}\end{array}\right\rbrace ={\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\varphi & {\Psi}_{d}& {\Psi}_{g}\end{array}\right]}^{T}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ {f}_{{L}_{d}}\\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}\end{array}\right\rbrace \\ \\ {q}_{g}={e}^{j\beta }\theta {q}_{d}\\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}=-{e}^{j\beta }\theta {f}_{{L}_{d}}\end{array}
$$

The matrices surmounted by a bar are the projections of the finite-element matrices onto the modal basis of the basic sector (generalised matrices).

One can show that the constraint modes are orthogonal to the normal modes with respect to the stiffness matrix [bib5]. Thus, the corresponding products are zero.

Let us adopt the following notations:

$m$ : index relative to the eigenmodes of the sector,

$d$ : index relative to the constraint modes of the right interface,

$g$ : index relative to the constraint modes of the left interface.

One can therefore write these matrices in the form:

$$
\overline{K}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\overline{{K}_{\mathit{mm}}}& 0& 0\\ 0& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{dd}}}& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{dg}}}\\ 0& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{gd}}}& \overline{{K}_{gg}}\end{array}\right]\overline{M}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{mm}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{md}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{mg}}}\\ \overline{{M}_{\mathit{dm}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{dd}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{dg}}}\\ \overline{{M}_{\mathit{gm}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{gd}}}& \overline{{M}_{gg}}\end{array}\right]
$$

By virtue of their definition, the constraint modes satisfy:

$$
{\Psi}_{d}=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\Psi}_{\mathit{di}}\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{dd}}\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{dg}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\Psi}_{\mathit{di}}\\ \mathit{Id}\\ 0\end{array}\right]{\Psi}_{g}=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\Psi}_{\mathit{gi}}\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{gd}}\\ {\Psi}_{gg}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\Psi}_{\mathit{gi}}\\ 0\\ \mathit{Id}\end{array}\right]
$$

The right-hand side of the matrix equation () becomes:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{\varphi}_{i}^{T}& 0& 0\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{di}}^{T}& \mathit{Id}& 0\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{gi}}^{T}& 0& \mathit{Id}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ {f}_{{L}_{d}}\\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ {f}_{{L}_{d}}\\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

Taking these notations into account, let us expand the matrix equation satisfied by the basic sector:

$$
\begin{array}{c}\overline{{K}_{\mathit{mm}}}{\eta}_{i}-{\omega}^{2}(\overline{{M}_{\mathit{mm}}}{\eta}_{i}+\overline{{M}_{\mathit{md}}}{q}_{d}+\overline{{M}_{\mathit{mg}}}{q}_{g})=0\\ \overline{{K}_{\mathit{dd}}}{q}_{d}+\overline{{K}_{\mathit{dg}}}{q}_{g}-{\omega}^{2}(\overline{{M}_{\mathit{dm}}}{\eta}_{i}+\overline{{M}_{\mathit{dd}}}{q}_{d}+\overline{{M}_{\mathit{dg}}}{q}_{g})={f}_{{L}_{d}}\\ \overline{{K}_{\mathit{gd}}}{q}_{d}+\overline{{K}_{gg}}{q}_{g}-{\omega}^{2}(\overline{{M}_{\mathit{gm}}}{\eta}_{i}+\overline{{M}_{\mathit{gd}}}{q}_{d}+\overline{{M}_{gg}}{q}_{g})={f}_{{L}_{g}}\\ \\ {q}_{g}={e}^{j\beta }\theta {q}_{d}\\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}=-{e}^{j\beta }\theta {f}_{{L}_{d}}\end{array}
$$

Let us introduce the last two equations of this system into the first three:

$$
\begin{array}{c}(\overline{{K}_{\mathit{mm}}}-{\omega}^{2}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{mm}}}){\eta}_{i}-{\omega}^{2}(\overline{{M}_{\mathit{md}}}+{e}^{j\beta }\overline{{M}_{\mathit{mg}}}\theta ){q}_{d}=0\\ (\overline{{K}_{\mathit{dd}}}+{e}^{j\beta }\overline{{K}_{\mathit{dg}}}\theta ){q}_{d}-{\omega}^{2}\left(\overline{{M}_{\mathit{dm}}}{\eta}_{i}+\text{(}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{dd}}}+{e}^{j\beta }\overline{{M}_{\mathit{dg}}}\theta \text{)}{q}_{d}\right)={f}_{{L}_{d}}\\ (\overline{{K}_{\mathit{gd}}}+{e}^{j\beta }\overline{{K}_{gg}}\theta ){q}_{d}-{\omega}^{2}\left(\overline{{M}_{\mathit{gm}}}{\eta}_{i}+\text{(}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{gd}}}+{e}^{j\beta }\overline{{M}_{gg}}\theta \text{)}{q}_{d}\right)=-{e}^{j\beta }\theta {f}_{{L}_{d}}\end{array}
$$

The combination of the last two equations makes it possible to eliminate the connection-force terms. One then arrives at a final eigenvalue problem which one can write in the form:

$$
\left(\stackrel{̃}{K}(\beta )-{\omega}^{2}\stackrel{̃}{M}(\beta )\right)\stackrel{̃}{q}=0
$$

With: $\stackrel{̃}{q}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{i}\\ {q}_{d}\end{array}\right\rbrace$

$$
\stackrel{̃}{K}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\overline{{K}_{\mathit{mm}}}& 0\\ 0& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{dd}}}+{e}^{j\beta }\overline{{K}_{\mathit{dg}}}\theta +{e}^{-j\beta }{\theta}^{T}\overline{{K}_{\mathit{gd}}}+{\theta}^{T}\overline{{K}_{gg}}\theta \end{array}\right]
$$

$$
\stackrel{̃}{M}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{mm}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{md}}}+{e}^{j\beta }\overline{{M}_{\mathit{mg}}}\theta \\ \overline{{M}_{\mathit{dm}}}+{e}^{-j\beta }{\theta}^{T}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{gm}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{dd}}}+{e}^{j\beta }\overline{{M}_{\mathit{dg}}}\theta +{e}^{-j\beta }{\theta}^{T}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{gd}}}+{\theta}^{T}\overline{{M}_{gg}}\theta \end{array}\right]
$$

The mass and stiffness matrices of the final problem are Hermitian. The eigenvalues which are the solutions are therefore real. Furthermore, the problem is of reduced size.

The solution of the complex eigenvalue problem () makes it possible to determine the complex generalised coordinates of the eigenmodes of the global structure. The complex values of the displacements of the basic sector in the global mode are given, from the generalised coordinates, by the following formula:

$$
q'=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\varphi & {\Psi}_{d}+{e}^{j\beta }\theta {\Psi}_{d}\end{array}\right]\stackrel{̃}{q}
$$

To determine the real values of the displacements, one must distinguish three cases according to the values of the inter-sector phase shift:

**Case no. 1**  : $\beta =0$ :

The displacements $q'$ given by formula () are then real-valued. All the sectors have the same deformed shape and vibrate in phase. One then has a single real eigenmode:

$$
q=\text{Re}(q')=q'
$$

**Case no. 2**  : $0<\beta  <(N+1)/2$ :

The displacements supplied by formula () are complex-valued. To each of these complex modes there correspond two orthogonal degenerate real modes:

$$
{q}_{1}=\text{Re}(q'){q}_{2}=\text{Im}(q')
$$

**Case no. 3**  : $\beta  =N/2$ ( => $N$ is even ) :

The displacements supplied by () are then complex-valued. There are $N/2$ nodal diameters; two adjacent sectors then vibrate in phase opposition. Each complex mode gives rise to a single real mode:

$$
q=\text{Re}(q')=-\text{Im}(q')
$$

### 3.2 Mac Neal method

One considers the eigenvalue problem of the global structure expressed on the basic sector. The latter is therefore subjected to the connection forces which are applied to it by the adjacent sectors. Furthermore, the basic sector satisfies the connection equations (). We therefore have:

$$
\begin{array}{c}(K-{\omega}^{2}M)q={f}_{L}\\ {q}_{g}={e}^{j\beta }\theta {q}_{d}\\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}=-{e}^{j\beta }\theta {f}_{{L}_{d}}\end{array}
$$

The modal basis used to reduce the dimensions of the problem to be solved is a free-interface modal basis comprising dynamic modes and the attachment modes relative to the degrees of freedom of the right and left interfaces. Suppose that the degrees of freedom of the basic sector are ordered in the following manner:

$$
q=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{q}_{i}\\ \\ {q}_{d}\\ \\ {q}_{g}\end{array}\right\rbrace \begin{array}{c}\mathrm{degrés}\mathrm{de}\mathrm{liberté}\mathrm{internes}\\ \\ \mathrm{degres}\mathrm{de}\mathrm{liberté}\mathrm{de}l'\mathrm{interface}\mathrm{droite}\\ \\ \mathrm{degrés}\mathrm{de}\mathrm{liberté}\mathrm{de}l'\mathrm{interface}\mathrm{gauche}\end{array}
$$

Let ${B}_{d}$ and ${B}_{g}$ be the rectangular extraction matrices such that:

$$
{q}_{d}={B}_{d}q\text{et}{q}_{g}={B}_{g}q
$$

The boundary condition on the displacements becomes, with these notations:

$$
{B}_{g}q={e}^{j\beta }\theta {B}_{d}q\Rightarrow {B}_{\mathit{dg}}q=0
\textrm{avec}
{B}_{\mathit{dg}}={e}^{j\beta }\theta {B}_{d}-{B}_{g}
$$

For the forces, the boundary condition becomes:

$$
{f}_{L}={B}_{g}^{T}{f}_{{L}_{g}}+{B}_{d}^{T}{f}_{{L}_{d}}\Rightarrow {f}_{L}=({B}_{g}^{T}-{e}^{-j\theta }{B}_{d}^{T}{\theta}^{T}){f}_{{L}_{g}}=-{B}_{\mathit{dg}}^{T}{f}_{{L}_{g}}
$$

Let us consider as the basis, for the RITZ transformation, the set of dynamic eigenmodes of the basic sector, distinguishing the identified modes and the unknown modes:

$$
q=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\varphi}_{1}& {\varphi}_{2}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}\\ {\eta}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

where the index 1 (resp. 2) refers to the known modes (resp. unknown). In what follows, we shall assume that the eigenmodes are normalised to unit modal mass.

Replacing $q$ by its expression as a function of the eigenmodes, and multiplying on the left by the transpose of the mode matrix, the matrix equations () and () become:

$$
\begin{array}{c}({\lambda}_{1}-{\omega}^{2}\mathit{Id}){\eta}_{1}={\varphi}_{1}^{T}{f}_{L}\\ ({\lambda}_{2}-{\omega}^{2}\mathit{Id}){\eta}_{2}={\varphi}_{2}^{T}{f}_{L}\\ {B}_{\mathit{dg}}{\varphi}_{1}{\eta}_{1}+{B}_{\mathit{dg}}{\varphi}_{2}{\eta}_{2}=0\end{array}
$$

where $\lambda$ is the matrix of the generalised stiffnesses (the generalised masses are unity).

One can therefore deduce from this a formulation of ${\eta}_{2}$ :

$$
{\eta}_{2}={({\lambda}_{2}-{\omega}^{2}\mathit{Id})}^{-1}{\varphi}_{2}^{T}{f}_{L}
$$

Henceforth, one can eliminate ${\eta}_{2}$ from the system of equations (). One then obtains the following eigenvalue problem:

$$
\begin{array}{c}({\lambda}_{1}-{\omega}^{2}\mathit{Id}){\eta}_{1}+{\varphi}_{1}^{T}{B}_{\mathit{dg}}^{T}{f}_{\mathit{Lg}}=0\\ {B}_{\mathit{dg}}{\varphi}_{1}{\eta}_{1}-{B}_{\mathit{dg}}{\varphi}_{2}{({\lambda}_{2}-{\omega}^{2}\mathit{Id})}^{-1}{\varphi}_{2}^{T}{B}_{\mathit{dg}}^{T}{f}_{\mathit{Lg}}=0\end{array}
$$

The final system to be solved can be written:

$$
(\stackrel{̃}{K}-{\omega}^{2}\stackrel{̃}{M})\stackrel{̃}{q}=0
$$

With:

$$
\stackrel{̃}{q}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}\\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

The expressions of the stiffness and mass matrices are:

$$
\stackrel{̃}{K}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{1}& {\varphi}_{1}{B}_{\mathit{dg}}^{T}\\ {B}_{\mathit{dg}}{\varphi}_{1}& -{B}_{\mathit{dg}}{R}_{e}(\omega ){B}_{\mathit{dg}}^{T}\end{array}\right]\stackrel{̃}{M}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathit{Id}& 0\\ 0& 0\end{array}\right]
$$

The matrix $\left[\begin{array}{c}{R}_{e}(\omega )\end{array}\right]$ is the residual dynamic flexibility matrix of the unidentified modes:

$$
{R}_{e}(\omega )={\Phi}_{2}{({\lambda}_{2}-{\omega}^{2}\mathit{Id})}^{-1}{\Phi}_{2}^{T}
$$

One approximates the residual dynamic flexibility by its static contribution, taking the attachment modes into account. Then, the recovery formula which makes it possible to compute the complex values of the displacements from the generalised coordinates of the mode solutions of () is the following:

$$
q'=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\varphi}_{1}& -{R}_{e}(0){B}_{\mathit{dg}}^{T}\end{array}\right]\stackrel{̃}{q}
$$

### 3.3 Taking the nodes of the axis into account - Craig & Bampton method

From an algorithmic point of view, the interface modes associated with the degrees of freedom carried by the nodes of the axis are taken into account only for the cases m = 0 and m = 1, which are the only cases that can present non-zero motions of the axis (cf. Section  2.4 ).

**NB: It is important to note that the analyses taking into account the motions of the axis can be carried out only with a reduction method of Craig & Bampton type. The approach with the Mac Neal method is not implemented.**

It is assumed in this paragraph that the degrees of freedom carried by the nodes of the axis, in the same way as the right and left interface nodes, have been fixed for the computation of the dynamic modes of the basic sector and have been the subject of constraint-mode computations.

The projection basis is therefore composed of the dynamic eigenmodes of the basic sector clamped at its interfaces, denoted $\varphi$ , and of the constraint modes relative to the degrees of freedom of the right, left and axis interfaces, denoted ${\Psi}_{d}$ , ${\Psi}_{g}$  and ${\Psi}_{a}$ .

As we saw in Section  2.4 , if m is greater than or equal to 2, the displacement of the nodes of the axis is zero. Taking the nodes of the axis into account therefore makes sense only if m=0 or m=1. In practice, in order to limit the memory occupation and the number of operations, the matrices are assembled taking the degrees of freedom of the axis into account only in these two cases.

The eigenvalue problem of the global structure and the connection equations, expressed on this basis, then read:

$$
\begin{array}{c}(\overline{K}-{\omega}^{2}\overline{M})\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{i}\\ {q}_{d}\\ {q}_{g}\\ {q}_{a}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left[\begin{array}{cccc}\varphi & {\Psi}_{d}& {\Psi}_{g}& {\Psi}_{a}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ {f}_{{L}_{d}}\\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}\\ {f}_{{L}_{d}}\end{array}\right\rbrace \\ {q}_{g}={e}^{j\beta }\theta {q}_{d}\text{et}{q}_{a}={e}^{j\beta }\theta {q}_{a},\\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}=-{e}^{j\beta }\theta {f}_{{L}_{g}}\text{et}{f}_{{L}_{a}}=-{e}^{j\beta }\theta {f}_{{L}_{a}}\end{array}
$$

One can therefore write the matrices in the form:

$$
\overline{K}=\left[\begin{array}{cccc}\overline{{K}_{\mathit{mm}}}& 0& 0& 0\\ 0& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{dd}}}& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{dg}}}& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{da}}}\\ 0& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{gd}}}& \overline{{K}_{gg}}& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{ga}}}\\ 0& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{ad}}}& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{ag}}}& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{aa}}}\end{array}\right]\overline{M}=\left[\begin{array}{cccc}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{mm}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{md}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{mg}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{ma}}}\\ \overline{{M}_{\mathit{dm}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{dd}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{dg}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{da}}}\\ \overline{{M}_{\mathit{gm}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{gd}}}& \overline{{M}_{gg}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{ga}}}\\ \overline{{M}_{\mathit{am}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{ad}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{ag}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{aa}}}\end{array}\right]
$$

By virtue of their definition, the constraint modes satisfy:

$$
{\Psi}_{d}=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\Psi}_{\mathit{di}}\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{dd}}\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{dg}}\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{da}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\Psi}_{\mathit{di}}\\ \mathit{Id}\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right]{\Psi}_{g}=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\Psi}_{\mathit{gi}}\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{gd}}\\ {\Psi}_{gg}\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{ga}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\Psi}_{\mathit{gi}}\\ 0\\ \mathit{Id}\\ 0\end{array}\right]{\Psi}_{a}=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\Psi}_{\mathit{ai}}\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{ad}}\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{ag}}\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{aa}}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\Psi}_{\mathit{ai}}\\ 0\\ 0\\ \mathit{Id}\end{array}\right]
$$

The right-hand side of the matrix equation () becomes:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cccc}{\varphi}_{i}& 0& 0& 0\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{di}}& \mathit{Id}& 0& 0\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{gi}}& 0& \mathit{Id}& 0\\ {\Psi}_{\mathit{ai}}& 0& 0& \mathit{Id}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ {f}_{{L}_{d}}\\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}\\ {f}_{{L}_{a}}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ {f}_{{L}_{d}}\\ {f}_{{L}_{g}}\\ {f}_{{L}_{a}}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

The connection equations are taken into account by projection. One introduces

$$
\stackrel{̃}{q}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\eta \\ {q}_{d}\\ {q}_{a}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

and one considers the projector $\stackrel{̃}{P}$ defined by

$$
\stackrel{̃}{P}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\mathit{Id}& 0& 0\\ 0& \mathit{Id}& 0\\ 0& {e}^{j\beta }\theta & 0\\ 0& 0& {e}^{j\beta }{\theta}_{a}\end{array}\right]
$$

The problem projected onto $\stackrel{̃}{P}$ becomes

$$
\stackrel{̃}{{P}^{H}}(\overline{K}-{\omega}^{2}\overline{M})\stackrel{̃}{P}\stackrel{̃}{q}=\stackrel{̃}{{P}^{H}}f
$$

This problem naturally satisfies the kinematic connection equations, since one has

$$
\stackrel{̃}{P}\stackrel{̃}{q}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\eta \\ {q}_{d}\\ {e}^{j\beta }\theta {q}_{d}\\ {e}^{j\beta }{\theta}_{a}{q}_{a}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\eta \\ {q}_{d}\\ {q}_{g}\\ {q}_{a}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

and the connection forces satisfy

$$
\stackrel{̃}{{P}^{H}}f=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ {f}_{{L}_{d}}+{e}^{-j\beta }{\theta}^{H}{f}_{{L}_{g}}\\ {e}^{-j\beta }{\theta}_{a}^{H}{f}_{{L}_{a}}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ 0\\ \stackrel{̃}{{f}_{{L}_{a}}}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

In the case where the angular opening of the sector is less than $\pi$ , and where one has strictly more than two sectors, one has furthermore shown that ${f}_{{L}_{a}}=0$ (Section  2.4.2 ).

One then arrives at a final eigenvalue problem which one can write in the form:

$$
(\stackrel{̃}{K}-{\omega}^{2}\stackrel{̃}{M})\stackrel{̃}{q}=0
$$

With:

$$
\stackrel{̃}{K}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\overline{{K}_{\mathit{mm}}}& 0& 0\\ 0& \overline{{K}_{\mathit{dd}}}+\overline{{K}_{\mathit{dg}}}\theta {e}^{j\beta }+{e}^{-j\beta }{\theta}^{T}\overline{{K}_{\mathit{gd}}}+{\theta}^{T}\overline{{K}_{gg}}\theta & \overline{{K}_{\mathit{da}}}{\theta}_{a}{e}^{j\beta }+{\theta}^{T}\overline{{K}_{\mathit{ga}}}{\theta}_{a}\\ 0& {e}^{-j\beta }{\theta}_{a}^{T}\overline{{K}_{\mathit{ad}}}+{\theta}_{a}\overline{{K}_{\mathit{ag}}}\theta & {\theta}_{a}^{T}\overline{{K}_{\mathit{aa}}}{\theta}_{a}\end{array}\right]
$$

$$
\stackrel{̃}{M}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{mm}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{md}}}+\overline{{M}_{\mathit{mg}}}\theta {e}^{j\beta }& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{ma}}}{\theta}_{a}{e}^{j\beta }\\ \overline{{M}_{\mathit{dm}}}+{e}^{-j\beta }{\theta}^{T}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{gm}}}& \overline{{M}_{\mathit{dd}}}+\overline{{M}_{\mathit{dg}}}\theta {e}^{j\beta }+{e}^{-j\beta }{\theta}^{T}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{gd}}}+{\theta}^{T}\overline{{M}_{gg}}\theta & \overline{{M}_{\mathit{da}}}{\theta}_{a}{e}^{j\beta }+{\theta}^{T}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{ga}}}{\theta}_{a}\\ {e}^{-j\beta }{\theta}_{a}^{T}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{am}}}& {e}^{-j\beta }{\theta}_{a}^{T}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{ad}}}+{\theta}_{a}^{T}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{ag}}}\theta & {\theta}_{a}^{T}\overline{{M}_{\mathit{aa}}}{\theta}_{a}\end{array}\right]
$$

One recovers the complex modal displacements by the following formula:

$$
q'=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\varphi & {\Psi}_{d}+{e}^{j\beta }\theta {\Psi}_{d}& {\Psi}_{a}\end{array}\right]\stackrel{̃}{\mathrm{q}}
$$

**NB: Particular case of the problem with two and four sectors**

The proposed formulation remains valid in the particular case of the problem with two sectors, given the orthogonality between ${q}_{a}$ and ${f}_{{L}_{a}}$ in the case of cyclic symmetry. Indeed, the connection equations () lead to

$$
{q}_{a}^{H}{f}_{{L}_{a}}={({e}^{j\beta }{\theta}_{a}{q}_{a})}^{H}(-{e}^{j\beta }{\theta}_{a}{f}_{{L}_{a}})=-{q}_{a}^{H}{f}_{{L}_{a}}=0
$$

Furthermore, the solutions of () are also a solution of the quadratic problem

$$
\stackrel{̃}{q}=\underset{\stackrel{̃}{{q}_{0}}}{\text{ArgMin}}\left(\stackrel{̃}{{q}_{0}^{H}}\stackrel{̃}{{P}^{H}}(\overline{K}-{\omega}^{2}\overline{M})\stackrel{̃}{P}\stackrel{̃}{{q}_{0}}-\stackrel{̃}{{q}_{0}^{H}}\stackrel{̃}{{P}^{H}}{f}_{0}\right)
$$

Now, for the sought solutions, satisfying the matching conditions between faces and at the level of the axis, one has, according to ()

$$
\stackrel{̃}{{q}_{0}^{H}}\stackrel{̃}{{P}^{H}}{f}_{0}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{\eta}_{0}^{H}& {q}_{\mathrm{0d}}^{H}& {q}_{\mathrm{0a}}^{H}\end{array}\right\rbrace \left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ 0\\ {f}_{{L}_{\mathrm{0a}}}\end{array}\right\rbrace =0
$$

The problem () can then be written in the form

$$
\stackrel{̃}{q}=\underset{\stackrel{̃}{{q}_{0}}}{\text{ArgMin}}\left(\stackrel{̃}{{q}_{0}^{H}}\stackrel{̃}{{P}^{H}}(\overline{K}-{\omega}^{2}\overline{M})\stackrel{̃}{P}\stackrel{̃}{{q}_{0}}\right)
$$

Given the convexity of problem (), the solutions of problem () therefore also satisfy

$$
\stackrel{̃}{{P}^{H}}(\overline{K}-{\omega}^{2}\overline{M})\stackrel{̃}{P}\stackrel{̃}{q}=0
$$

Note: relation () also indicates that the projector defined by relation () is not the only admissible one. The term ${e}^{j\beta }{\theta}_{a}$ can be replaced by $\mathit{Id}$ . This choice has not been made in order to retain a classical form for the reduced problem, similar to that obtained when it is not necessary to separate the terms carried by the axis from those carried by the right and left interfaces. Furthermore, the eigenvalues of the term ${e}^{j\beta }{\theta}_{a}$ all having modulus 1, the conditioning of the global problem is not affected by this change.

## 4 Implementation in Code_Aster

The treatment of the basic sector is identical to that of the substructures in classical substructuring. It involves the operators CALC_MODES [U4.52.02](doc:u4.52.02), DEFI_INTERF_DYNA [U4.64.01](doc:u4.64.01) and DEFI_BASE_MODALE [U4.64.02](doc:u4.64.02).

The eigenmodes of the structure with cyclic symmetry are computed by the operator MODE_ITER_CYCL [U4.52.05](doc:u4.52.05) as a function of the projection basis of the basic sector defined previously and of the number of sectors of the complete structure.

The recovery of the results onto the physical basis is identical to classical substructuring. It involves the operator REST_SOUS_STRUC [U4.63.32](doc:u4.63.32) and possibly the operator DEFI_SQUELETTE [U4.24.01](doc:u4.24.01).

## 5 Conclusion

The principles of substructuring make it possible to set out the RITZ transformation and the modal recombination in order to arrive at the modal synthesis which integrates these two techniques. The rules for connecting the substructures are made explicit.

Two methods have been developed in *Code_Aster*  : that of Craig-Bampton and that of MacNeal. We present here their particularities, both in the definition of the initial modal basis and in its use.

After having set out the definition of a structure with cyclic symmetry and the properties which follow from it, we have presented the methods of cyclic substructuring implemented in *Code_Aster* . They prove very advantageous for the computation of the eigenmodes of a structure with cyclic symmetry, such as the rotors of rotating machines, of whose geometric and mechanical characteristics they take full advantage.
