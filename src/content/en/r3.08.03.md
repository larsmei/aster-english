# r3.08.03 Computation of the characteristics of a beam of arbitrary cross-section

**Summary:**

The principle of the computation of the various characteristic quantities of beam sections is presented. These are established from the geometric characteristics of the cross-section of the beam.

These values are to be supplied to the operand SECTION:’GENERALE’ of the operator AFFE_CARA_ELEM [U4.42.01](doc:u4.42.01). In order to determine them, numerical methods are presented, and implemented in the command MACR_CARA_POUTRE.

In the case of the sections ’RECTANGLE’ and ’CERCLE’, the characteristics are computed directly in AFFE_CARA_ELEM by means of simplified formulae which are set out here.

## 1 The geometric characteristics

**Hypothesis:**

Only the cross-sections of homogeneous and isotropic beams are treated here (the same material characteristics for all the points and in all directions). The command MACR_CARA_POUTRE can also compute the geometric characteristics of a set of disjoint sections.

### 1.1 Arbitrary section

#### 1.1.1 Principle

Let a section $(S)$ of area $S$ in the plane $(0,y,z)$ whose origin $O$ is the centre of gravity $G$ of the section, [Figure ].

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/100025D6000069BB00005D1FD7DE7D300241B652.svg)

Figure 1: **section in the plane** $(0,y,z)$

The geometric moment of inertia of $(S)$ with respect to the axis $(\mathrm{Oy})$ (which passes through the centre of gravity) is expressed by:

${I}_{y}={\int}_{s}\mathrm{z²}\mathrm{dS}$ with $[I]={\int}_{s}\mathrm{OM}\otimes \mathrm{OM}\mathrm{dS}$

One defines in a similar way the geometric moment with respect to $(\mathrm{Oz})$ by:

${I}_{z}={\int}_{s}{y}^{2}\mathrm{dS}$

When the centrifugal geometric moment (often called the area product of inertia) defined by is zero, ${I}_{yz}={\int}_{s}yz\mathrm{dS}$ the axes $(\mathrm{Oy})$ and $(\mathrm{Oz})$ are principal axes of the section $(S)$ . One places oneself henceforth in this assumption; ${I}_{y}$ and ${I}_{z}$ are then called the principal geometric moments.

In a general way, one must place oneself in the principal axes of a beam section for everything that concerns its characteristics, since the beam elements of *Code_Aster*  are formulated in this frame. Starting from an origin located at the centre of gravity, it is sufficient, in order to pass from an arbitrary system of axes $(G,y',z')$ to the principal system of axes $(G,y,z)$ , to carry out a rotation of angle $\theta$ such that [Figure]:

$\theta =\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Arctg}(\frac{2{I}_{y'z'}}{{I}_{z'}-{I}_{y'}})$

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/100000000000022B000001B4CE92C5312DFCDE51.png)

Figure 2: **Principal and arbitrary axes.**

The polar geometric moment with respect to the centre of gravity is given by: ${I}_{p}={\int}_{s}\mathrm{r²}\mathrm{dS}$ where r is the distance from the element $\mathrm{dS}$ to the centre of gravity [Figure ]. One naturally deduces ${I}_{p}={I}_{y}+{I}_{z}$ .

The polar geometric moment enters into the computation of the torsional stiffness of beams of circular section (Saint-Venant torsion). For the other section shapes, a torsion constant of the same dimension will be defined.

Moreover, the geometric moments may be computed in another frame $(P,y,z)$ , of arbitrary origin $P$ different from the centre of gravity $G$  (Huygens’ formula):

$\begin{array}{}{I}_{y}^{P}={I}_{y}^{G}+{(\mathrm{GP}.Z)}^{2}.S={\int}_{s}{z}^{2}\mathrm{dS}+{(\mathrm{GP}.Z)}^{2}.S\\ {I}_{z}^{P}={I}_{z}^{G}+{(\mathrm{GP}.Y)}^{2}.S={\int}_{s}{y}^{2}\mathrm{dS}+{(\mathrm{GP}.Y)}^{2}.S\\ {I}_{yz}^{P}={I}_{yz}^{G}+(\mathrm{GP}.Y)(\mathrm{GP}.Z).S={\int}_{s}yz\mathrm{dS}+(\mathrm{GP}.Y)(\mathrm{GP}.Z).S\end{array}$

in a general way, Huygens’ formula gives:

$\begin{array}{}\left[I\right]={\int}_{s}(\text{PG}+\text{GM})\otimes (\text{PG}+\text{GM})\\ \phantom{\left[I\right]}={\int}_{s}\text{PG}\otimes \text{PG}+{\int}_{s}\text{GM}\otimes \text{GM}\\ \phantom{\left[I\right]}+2{\int}_{s}\text{PG}\otimes \text{GM}\\ \phantom{\left[I\right]}=S(\text{PG}\otimes \text{PG})+{\int}_{s}\text{GM}\otimes \text{GM}\end{array}$

#### 1.1.2 Computation of the geometric characteristics by means of MACR_CARA_POUTRE

This macro-command makes it possible to determine the characteristics of a beam cross-section from a 2D mesh of the section [U4.42.02](doc:u4.42.02). It makes it possible to build a table of values, usable in the command AFFE_CARA_ELEM(SECTION: ’GENERALE’ [U4.42.01](doc:u4.42.01)).

The geometric characteristics may be computed on the complete mesh, a half-mesh with symmetry with respect to $Y$ or to $Z$ , a quarter-mesh with two symmetries with respect to $Y$ and to $Z$ [Figure].

These characteristics are computed in the table for the whole mesh and for each mesh-cell group of the list specified by the user (case of a network of beams).

The data correspond to a half or a quarter of the section if the keywords SYME_Y or SYME_Z are present.

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/100000000000019E000001614D3811A6B950B48D.png)

**Figure** 3 **: Definition of the geometric characteristics.**

The results are gathered into four groups:

- In the frame $\mathit{OYZ}$ of the description of the 2D mesh for the mesh supplied by the user
- area: A_M
- position of the centre of gravity: CDG_Y_M,CDG_Z_M
- area moments and product of inertia, at the centre of gravity $G$ in the frame GYZ: IY_G_M, IZ_G_M, IYZ_G_M
- In the same global frame, for the mesh obtained by symmetrisation if SYME_Y or SYME_Z:
- area: A
- position of the centre of gravity: CDG_Y,CDG_Z
- area moments and product of inertia, at the centre of gravity $G$ in the frame GYZ: IY_G, IZ_G, IYZ_G
- In the principal inertia frame $\mathrm{Gyz}$ . of the cross-section, whose naming corresponds to that used in the description of the beam elements of neutral fibre $\mathrm{Gx}$ [U4.24.01](doc:u4.24.01).
- principal area moments of inertia in the frame $\mathrm{Gyz}$ , usable for the computation of the bending stiffness of the beam: IY and IZ
- angle of passage from the frame $\mathit{GYZ}$ to the principal inertia frame $\mathrm{Gyz}$ : ALPHA
- characteristic distances, with respect to the centre of gravity $G$ of the section, for the computations of maximum stresses: Y_MAX, Y_MIN, Z_MAX, Z_MIN and R_MAX.
- In the global frame, at a point $P$ supplied by the user:
- Y_P, Z_P: point of computation of the moments of inertia
- IY_P, IZ_P, IYZ_P: moments of inertia in the frame PYZ
- IY_P, IZ_P: moments of inertia in the frame Pyz.

#### 1.1.3 Computations carried out

The list of the commands called by MACR_CARA_POUTRE is given in the document [U4.42.02](doc:u4.42.02).

The previous quantities are obtained by the call to POST_ELEM, for the option ’CARA_GEOM’. In addition, one may add there the keywords SYME_Y, SYME_Z, and ORIG_INER, which defines the point $P$ .

The computations are carried out in POST_ELEM, for the whole mesh, then possibly for each mesh-cell group, in the following way:

- Loop over the 2D elements (modelling D_PLAN), with a call to the elementary option ’MASS_INER’. One obtains a CHAM_ELEM with one value per element (1 Gauss point) containing the components:

${\int}_{\mathrm{élément}}\mathrm{dS},{\int}_{\stackrel{x}{\mathrm{élément}}}\mathrm{dS},{\int}_{\mathrm{élément}}\mathrm{dS}$ ${\int}_{s}\mathrm{x²}\mathrm{dS},{\int}_{s}\mathrm{y²}\mathrm{dS},{\int}_{s}xy\mathrm{dS}$ ,

- Summation of the previous elementary quantities in order to obtain: A_M, CDG_Y_M, CDG_Z_M, IY_G_M, IZ_G_M, IYZ_G_M
- Computation of A, CDG_Y, CDG_Z, IY_G, IZ_G, IYZ_G (taking SYME_Y, SYME_Z into account)
- Computation of IY, IZ, ALPHA
- Computation of Y_MAX, Z_MAX, Y_MIN, Z_MIN, R_MAX
- If one specifies a particular point $P$ (keyword ORIG_INER), one also computes the characteristics in the global frame of origin $P$ : $\mathit{PYZ}$

## 2 The shear coefficients and the shear centre

The aim is to evaluate the coefficients ${A}_{y}=\frac{1}{{k}_{y}},{A}_{z}=\frac{1}{{k}_{z}}$ appearing in the Timoshenko beam models with the shear strains taken into account. For EULER beams, these coefficients do not appear [U4.42.01 §7.4.2] and [R3.08.01 §2.3.1]. These coefficients are obtained for a linear elastic constitutive behaviour.

In the case of arbitrary sections, the shear coefficients are to be supplied by the user in AFFE_CARA_ELEM, if the element chosen is a TIMOSHENKO beam (models POU_D_T, POU_D_TG and POU_D_TGM).

In the case of circular or rectangular sections, the shear coefficients are computed by analytical methods of [§2.1].

In every case, they may be computed by MACR_CARA_POUTRE, from the plane mesh of the section. The numerical method used is set out in [§2.3]. This method applies to arbitrary sections (of homogeneous and isotropic material). In appendix 2, an extension of this method to the case of a network of parallel beams held between two rigid floors is described.

The position of the torsion centre (or shear centre) is obtained only by numerical methods (cf. [§2.3]). For rectangular and circular sections, as for all sections with 2 planes of symmetry, the torsion centre coincides with the centre of gravity of the section.

### 2.1 Analytical methods

Three analytical methods making it possible to compute shear coefficients, applicable to arbitrary sections, are described.

The first two methods differ by the definition they propose of the shear coefficient, but rest on the same assumption, which consists in postulating the form of the distribution of the shear stresses in the section.

#### 2.1.1 Assumption on the distribution of the shears: JOURAWSKI formula

Consider for example the case of a beam of cross-section $S$ , subjected to a shear force ${V}_{y}={\int}_{s}{\sigma}_{xy}\mathrm{dS}$ . One writes the equilibrium of a prismatic part of the beam, lying between the cross-sections ${S}_{x}$ and ${S}_{x+a}$ and between the cutting plane located at the ordinate $y$ and ${y}_{\max}$ (ref.[bib]). The forces acting on this part of the beam are the stress vectors on the faces ${S}_{x}$ and ${S}_{x+a}$ , and those acting on the face located at $y$ .

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/10002CA0000069BB000032F4603FA65A72DF7FD8.svg)

Figure 6 **:**  Beam section.

Applying the resultant theorem, one obtains:

$\begin{array}{cc}{\int}_{{S}_{x+a}}{\sigma}_{xx}(x,y',z)\mathrm{dy}'\text{dz}-{\int}_{{S}_{x}}{\sigma}_{xx}(x,y',z)\mathrm{dy}'\text{dz}& =N(x+a,y)-N(x,y)\\ & ={\int}_{x}^{x+a}{\int}_{\frac{-b(y)}{2}}^{\frac{b(y)}{2}}{\sigma}_{xy}(\alpha ,y,z)d\alpha \text{dz}\end{array}$

To evaluate the right-hand term, JOURAWSKI proposed to consider only the average of the shears along z:

${\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}_{xy}(x,y)=\frac{1}{b(y)}{\int}_{-\frac{b}{2}}^{\frac{b}{2}}{\sigma}_{xy}(x,y,z)\text{dz}$

then

${\int}_{x}^{x+a}{\int}_{-\frac{b(y)}{2}}^{\frac{b(y)}{2}}{\sigma}_{xy}(\alpha ,y,z)d\alpha \text{dz}={\int}_{x}^{x+a}b(y){\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}_{xy}(\alpha ,y)d\alpha$

and letting $a$ tend towards $0$ ,

$\frac{\partial N}{\partial x}=b(y){\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}_{xy}(x,y)$

The beam equilibrium equations and the distribution of the bending stresses (in elasticity) give:

$N(x,y)={\int}_{{S}_{x}}^{}{\sigma}_{xx}(x,y,z)\text{dydz}={\int}_{{S}_{x}}^{}\frac{{M}_{z}(x).y}{{I}_{z}}\text{dydz}$

$=\frac{{M}_{z}(x)}{{I}_{z}}m(y)\text{avec}m(y)={\int}_{y}^{{y}_{\max}}t\text{}b(t)\text{}\text{dt}$

hence

${\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}_{xy}(\text{x,y})=\frac{m(y)}{{I}_{z}b(y)}\frac{\partial {M}_{z}}{\partial x}=\frac{m(y)}{{I}_{z}b(y)}{V}_{y}$

The distribution of the shears along $y$ is therefore given by the JOURAWSKI formula:

> ${\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}_{xy}(\text{x,y})=\frac{m(y)}{{I}_{z}b(y)}{V}_{y}\text{avec}m(y)={\int}_{y}^{{y}_{\max}}t\text{}b(t)\text{}\text{dt}$     [1]

in accordance with [U4.24.01](doc:u4.24.01), with the notation of the [Figure]. The quantity $m(y)$ represents the static moment of the part of the section (hatched) lying between $y$ and ${y}_{\max}$ :

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/100000000000014D000000A7CD78009AD7D5214A.png)

**Figure** 7 **: beam section**

This distribution does indeed satisfy the boundary conditions along $y$ of the three-dimensional problem: the shear is indeed zero on the lower and upper fibres ($y={y}_{\min}$ , or $y={y}_{\max}$ ). But it takes into account only the average of the shears along $z$ .

Applying this formula to a solid rectangular section, one finds a parabolic distribution along $y$ . Applying it to a beam of circular section, one finds a parabolic distribution in y and in $z$ , which varies more slowly along $z$ than along $y$ .

This remains valid for the other solid sections. For sections containing holes, care must be taken to consider only the material in the computation of $b(y)$ .

#### 2.1.2 TIMOSHENKO method

Originally, TIMOSHENKO (ref. [bib]) proposed a simple definition of the shear coefficient, as being the ratio between the mean transverse shear stress in the section, denoted ${\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}_{\text{CT}}$ , and its maximum value $({\sigma}_{{\mathrm{CT}}_{\mathrm{Max}}})$ . Since one always has the shear force by:

> $V={\int}_{s}{\sigma}_{\text{CT}}\text{dS}$     [2]

one deduces:

. ${\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}_{\text{CT}}=\frac{V}{S}$

Knowing that TIMOSHENKO proposes to write:

> $k=\frac{{\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}_{\text{CT}}}{{\sigma}_{{\text{CT}}_{\text{Max}}}}$     [3]

in order to determine $k$ , it is sufficient to express the shear force $V={\int}_{s}{\sigma}_{\text{CT}}\text{dS}$ [eq] as a function of ${\sigma}_{{\mathrm{CT}}_{\mathrm{Max}}}$ . In the general case of arbitrary sections, there will naturally be two coefficients ${k}_{y}$ and ${k}_{z}$ , for each of the two principal axes.

It remains to determine ${\sigma}_{{\mathrm{CT}}_{\mathrm{Max}}}$ . For this purpose, TIMOSHENKO makes an assumption on the distribution of the transverse shear stresses: the transverse shear stress has a parabolic distribution in the direction of the shear force which produces it, with its maximum value at the centre and zero values at the edges. This is true according to the JOURAWSKI formula for a rectangular section. By extension, the method extends this assumption of a parabolic distribution to an arbitrary section

This method is not applied in *Code_Aster* , except for hollow rectangular sections. The “energy” method is used in the other cases.

#### 2.1.3 “Energy” method

In reality, the definition proposed by TIMOSHENKO turns out to be little used in practice today; one prefers a formulation based on the internal energy due to shear in the section. The latter is written:

${U}_{\text{CT}}={\int}_{s}\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\sigma}_{{\text{CT}}^{2}}}{G}\text{dS}$

where $G$ is the shear modulus (equal to $m$ ).

The new definition of the shear coefficient is sometimes attributed to MINDLIN and is expressed by:

> ${U}_{\text{CT}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{{V}^{2}}{k\text{SG}}$     [4]

As a result, by substitution, one thus defines, for a section of homogeneous material, the shear coefficient by:

> $k={\frac{\left[{\int}_{s}{\sigma}_{\text{CT}}\text{dS}\right]}{S{\int}_{s}{\sigma}_{{\text{CT}}^{2}}\text{dS}}}^{2}=\frac{{V}^{2}}{S{\int}_{s}{\sigma}_{{\text{CT}}^{2}}\text{dS}}$     [5]

By making an assumption on the stress distribution in the section, one can thus estimate the value of $k$ . From the JOURAWSKI formula [eq], the previous expression may be written [bib]:

> $k=\frac{{I}^{2}}{S{\int}_{S}^{}\frac{{m}^{2}(y)}{{b}^{2}(y)}\text{dS}}$     [6]

#### 2.1.4 COWPER method

One may also take the three-dimensional effects into account in order to determine the coefficient $k$ ; various formulations have been proposed, in particular by COWPER [bib] and taken up by BLEVINS [bib], based on the solution of the three-dimensional Saint-Venant problem.

In this case, the coefficient $k$ is a function of POISSON’s ratio, in general a first-order approximation. COWPER uses the three-dimensional equations of elasticity in the dynamic case to propose an expression of $k$ giving good results in statics and in dynamics at low frequency. The approximation which makes it possible to arrive at the proposed formula consists in considering a stress distribution which is not parabolic, but results from the static problem (solved analytically) of the cantilever beam loaded transversely at its free end. It should be noted that the distribution obtained is strictly identical to the problem with a uniformly distributed transverse loading.

### 2.2 Particular case of the rectangular and circular sections

Solid beams and tubes are distinguished.

For the solid rectangular section, the shear coefficient is determined by the method based on the internal shear energy with a parabolic distribution of the stresses:

> ${k}_{y}=\frac{{I}_{z}^{2}}{S{\int}_{S}^{}\frac{{m}_{z}^{2}(y)}{{b}_{{}_{z}}^{2}(y)}\text{dy}}$     [7]

Applied to the rectangular section, one obtains ${k}_{y}={k}_{z}=\frac{5}{6}$ . Note that this value also corresponds to the COWPER method when POISSON’s ratio is taken equal to zero.

For the rectangular tube, *Code_Aster*  uses the TIMOSHENKO method, which leads to ${k}_{y}={k}_{z}=\frac{2}{3}$ . In the case of beams of solid circular section, the energy method is used, which leads to ${k}_{y}={k}_{z}=\frac{9}{10}$ . This value is also obtained by the COWPER method when POISSON’s ratio is equal to $\frac{1}{2}$ .

For circular tubes, thin-walled tubes and thick-walled tubes are distinguished. If one denotes by $m=\frac{{r}_{i}}{{r}_{e}}$ the ratio of the internal radius to the external radius, a tube is thin-walled when $m>0.9$ , otherwise it is thick-walled.

The shear coefficient of the thin-walled circular tube is given by the COWPER method, considering that $m=1$ and for a zero POISSON’s ratio, that is ${k}_{y}={k}_{z}=\frac{1}{2}$ .

For thick-walled circular tubes, an approximate formula of the COWPER method is used, which is written: $k=\frac{1}{1,093+0,634m+1,156{m}^{2}-0,905{m}^{3}}$

Note that this formula does not ensure continuity with the limiting cases of the solid cylinder ($m=0$ ) and of the infinitely thin-walled cylinder ($m=1$ ).

If the previous choices (made by AFFE_CARA_ELEM in the case of circular and rectangular sections) are not suitable, it is always possible to compute the shear coefficients numerically by means of MACR_CARA_POUTRE, whose method is specified in the following §.

### 2.3 Numerical method for computing the shear coefficients and the shear centre

#### 2.3.1 Computation of the shear coefficients:

This method is inspired by [bib], page 62. It makes it possible to determine simultaneously the shear constants and the torsion centre. It is implemented in MACR_CARA_POUTRE, from a plane mesh of the section. It currently works only for homogeneous and isotropic sections (for non-homogeneous sections, the method is similar [bib] but not available in *Code_Aster* ).

As for the energy method, one compares, for a shear force $V$ , the internal energy ${U}_{1}$ due to shear in the section with the energy U2 associated with the MINDLIN model:

${U}_{1}={\int}_{s}\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\sigma}_{xy}^{2}+{\sigma}_{xz}^{2}}{G}\text{dS}={U}_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{{V}_{z}^{2}}{{k}_{z}\text{SG}}$

The shear coefficient is expressed by: ${k}_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{{V}_{z}^{2}}{{\text{SGU}}_{1}}$

One must therefore compute ${U}_{1}$ and therefore the shear stresses (in elasticity) in the section in order to estimate the value of $k$ . One places oneself in the principal inertia frame $(G,y,z)$ , and one assumes that the beam is subjected only to a shear force ${V}_{z}$ . It follows that:

$\begin{array}{}{\sigma}_{xx}=z\frac{{M}_{y}(x)}{{I}_{y}}\\ \frac{\partial {\sigma}_{xx}}{\partial x}=z\frac{{V}_{z}}{{I}_{y}}\end{array}$

The equilibrium equations make it possible to write:

$\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{xx}}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{xy}}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{xz}}{\partial z}=0=\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{xy}}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{xz}}{\partial z}+z\frac{{V}_{z}}{{I}_{y}}$

On the other hand, the kinematics of the beam in bending/shear is:

$\begin{array}{}u(x,y,z)=u(x)+z{\theta}_{y}(x)+\tilde{u}(y,z)\\ v(x,y,z)=0\\ w(x,y,z)=w(x)\end{array}$

$\tilde{u}(y,z)$ representing the axial displacement due to the warping of the section. The strains are written:

$\begin{array}{}{\varepsilon}_{xx}=\frac{\partial u(x)}{\partial x}+z\frac{\partial {\theta}_{y}(x)}{\partial x}\\ 2{\varepsilon}_{xy}=\frac{\partial \tilde{u}(y,z)}{\partial y}\\ 2{\varepsilon}_{xz}={\theta}_{y}(x)+\frac{\partial \tilde{u}(y,z)}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial w(x)}{\partial x}\end{array}$

Using the constitutive relation of linear elasticity, the stresses are written:

$\begin{array}{}{\sigma}_{xy}=2\mu {\varepsilon}_{xy}=\mu \frac{\partial \tilde{u}(y,z)}{\partial y}\\ {\sigma}_{xz}=2\mu {\varepsilon}_{xz}=\mu ({\theta}_{y}(x)+\frac{\partial \tilde{u}(y,z)}{\partial z}+\frac{\partial w(x)}{\partial x})\end{array}$

The shear components therefore satisfy:

$\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{xy}}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{xz}}{\partial y}=0$

This relation makes it possible to introduce the stress function ${\psi}_{z}$ such that the shear stresses in the section are written:

$\begin{array}{}{\sigma}_{xy}=\mu \frac{\partial {\psi}_{z}}{\partial y}\\ {\sigma}_{xz}=\mu \frac{\partial {\psi}_{z}}{\partial z}\end{array}$

The equilibrium equation then makes it possible to obtain the function ${\psi}_{z}$ by solution of a quasi-harmonic problem which is written:

$\begin{array}{}G\Delta {\psi}_{z}+f=0\text{dans}S\text{avec}f=\frac{{\mathrm{zV}}_{z}}{{I}_{y}}\\ G\frac{\partial {\psi}_{z}}{\partial n}=0\text{sur}\partial S\\ {\psi}_{z}=0\text{en un point}\end{array}$

This makes it possible to compute ${\psi}_{z}$ then the shears. In practice, in MACR_CARA_POUTRE, one uses THER_LINEAIRE to solve the problem, by identifying ${\psi}_{z}$ with the temperature. One chooses $V=1$ and $G=1$ ($G$  no longer appears in the expression of the shear coefficient). The boundary conditions of this steady thermal problem are:

- source $f$ equal to $\frac{{\text{zV}}_{z}}{{I}_{y}}$
- zero flux on $\partial S$
- zero temperature at a point of $S$

One can then determine ${U}_{1}^{z}={\int}_{s}\frac{1}{2}\frac{{\sigma}_{xy}^{2}+{\sigma}_{xz}^{2}}{G}\text{dS}=\frac{1}{2}{\int}_{s}G{(\nabla {\psi}_{z})}^{2}\text{dS}$ by an elementary computation on all the elements of the section, with the option ’CARA_CISA’ (computation of the gradient), then summation over these elements. One then computes ${k}_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{{V}_{z}^{2}}{{\text{SGU}}_{1}^{z}}$

The same computation is carried out with ${V}_{y}=1$ in order to determine ${k}_{y}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{{V}_{y}^{2}}{{\text{SGU}}_{{}_{1}}^{y}}$

The result supplied is ${A}_{y}=\frac{1}{{k}_{y}},{A}_{z}=\frac{1}{{k}_{z}}$ .

#### 2.3.2 Computation of the coordinates of the shear centre

The shear centre $C$ is the point of the section where the shear stresses due to a shear force produce a zero torsion moment. This point is also called the torsion centre, because it remains fixed when the section is subjected only to a torsion moment.

The torsion moment with respect to the point $G$ is equal to ${M}_{\text{xG}}={\int}_{s}({\sigma}_{xz}.y-{\sigma}_{xy}.z)\text{dS}$ :

The torsion moment with respect to the sought point $C$ is:

${M}_{\text{xC}}={\int}_{s}({\sigma}_{xz}.(y-{y}_{c})-{\sigma}_{xy}(z-{z}_{c}))\text{dS}={M}_{\text{xG}}-{y}_{c}{V}_{z}+{z}_{c}{V}_{y}$

To determine the coordinates of the shear centre, one uses the previous computation [bib]:

From the shear stresses determined for $V=1$ and ${V}_{y}=0$ , one computes: ${M}_{{}_{\text{xG}}}^{z}={\int}_{s}({\sigma}_{xz}.y-{\sigma}_{xy}.z)\text{dS}$

One obtains: ${y}_{c}=\frac{{M}_{{}_{\text{xG}}}^{z}}{{V}_{z}}={M}_{{}_{\text{xG}}}^{z}$

For $V=1$ and ${V}_{y}=0$ , one obtains: ${z}_{c}=-\frac{{M}_{\mathrm{xG}}^{y}}{{V}_{y}}=-{M}_{{}_{\text{xG}}}^{y}$

## 3 The constants related to torsion

The torsion constant denoted $\mathit{JX}$ must make it possible to take into account the warping of the (non-circular) cross-sections during a torsional deformation. It is used in the straight-beam models treated by *Aster*  (EULER, TIMOSHENKO and warped TIMOSHENKO, or POU_D_E, POU_D_T and POU_D_TG). In the case of circular sections, the sections are not warped and the torsion constant is equal to the polar geometric moment ${I}_{p}$ . The torsion constant $C$ is defined as the moment necessary to produce a rotation of 1 radian per unit length divided by the shear modulus , that is:

> $C=\frac{{M}_{x}}{\mu \frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}}$     [8]

$\mathit{JX}$ has the same dimension as the geometric moments of inertia ${I}_{y}$ and ${I}_{z}$ , that is ${m}^{4}$ .

For a circular section, the definition [eq] is consistent since one has:

${M}_{x}=\mu {I}_{p}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}$

The determination of $\mathit{JX}$ in the general case is done numerically (MACR_CARA_POUTRE) and reduces to a 2D Laplacian computation. The method presented here is detailed in ref. [bib] [§3.6.3] for simply connected sections. An original method for the computation of the torsion constants with sections containing holes is detailed in the appendix. The results are given here.

### 3.1 Computation of C in the case of arbitrary sections

The complete solution of the problem is found in the appendix. Only the results are given here.

According to the assumptions of the Saint-Venant pure torsion theory, there is no deformation of the mean line and no elongation along the longitudinal axis. The torsion is free, that is to say that it does not generate axial stresses. In other words, the sections may warp freely. If one remains in small displacements, one accepts that the angle of rotation of the cross-sections is equal to:

> ${q}_{x}(x)=\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}x=\Theta .x$     [9]

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/100000000000011A000000F714704D64081DB652.png)

Figure 8 **:**  general section.

If the point $c$ is the torsion centre (which by definition remains motionless when the beam is subjected to torsion), the displacement field $u(M)$ is given by [bib]:

$u(M)=\left[\begin{array}{c}u\\ v\\ w\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}x\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right]\wedge \left[\begin{array}{c}x\\ (y-{y}_{c})\\ (z-{z}_{c})\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}\xi (y,z)\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right]$ $=(\begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}\xi (y,z)\\ -\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}x(z-{z}_{c})\\ \frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}x(y-{y}_{c})\end{array})$

where $\xi (y,z)$ is the warping function.

HOOKE’s law is written:

$\sigma =2\mu \varepsilon +(E-2\mu )\mathrm{Trace}(\varepsilon )I$

where $I$ is the unit matrix and the strain tensor is equal to $\varepsilon =\frac{1}{2}(\text{grad}(u)+{}^{T}\text{}\text{grad}(u))$

Neglecting the second-order terms, in $\frac{{\partial}^{2}{\theta}_{x}}{\partial {x}^{2}}$ , one arrives at:

> $\sigma =\mu \frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& \frac{\partial \xi }{\partial y}-z& \frac{\partial \xi }{\partial z}+y\\ \frac{\partial \xi }{\partial y}-z& 0& 0\\ \frac{\partial \xi }{\partial z}+y& 0& 0\end{array}\right]$ $=\mu \frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& \frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial z}& -\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial y}\\ \frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial z}& 0& 0\\ -\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial y}& 0& 0\end{array}\right]$     [10]

One has set: $\varphi (y,z)$ stress function. One notes that the equilibrium relation $divs=0$ is then satisfied. Differentiating, one obtains:

$\begin{array}{c}\frac{{\partial}^{2}\varphi }{\partial {y}^{2}}=-\frac{{\partial}^{2}\xi }{\partial z\partial y}-1\\ \frac{{\partial}^{2}\varphi }{\partial {z}^{2}}=\frac{{\partial}^{2}\xi }{\partial y\partial z}-1.\end{array}$

Adding the two equations, one arrives at:

> $\Delta \varphi =-2$     [11]

It remains to establish the boundary conditions. One denotes by $n$ the outward normal to the boundary ($\Gamma$ ), which may be multiply connected:

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/10000000000001370000015B564DDD3E8B518317.png)

With no external loading, one must have $\sigma \otimes n=0$ , which may be written:

$\mu \frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}\left[\begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial z}{n}_{y}-\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial y}{n}_{z}\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right]=0$ where ${n}_{y}$ and ${n}_{z}$ are the two components of the normal.

This writing may thus be put in the form $n\wedge \text{grad}\varphi =0$ , which implies that the vectors $n$ and $\text{grad}j$ are collinear. It therefore follows that $\varphi (y,z)$ is constant on each connected component of the boundary ($\Gamma$ ). One may impose, for example, that $\varphi (y,z)$ be zero on the outer contour:

$\varphi =0$ **on** $\partial {\Omega}_{0}=\Gamma$

$\varphi ={\varphi}_{i}$ **on** $\partial {\Omega}_{i}$

In the case where the sections contain holes, the constants ${\varphi}_{i}$ are indeterminate. In order to allow the solution of the complete problem, equations must be added. These are obtained from the circulation of the warping function on each closed contour. One obtains the following conditions:

${\oint}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial n}\text{dl}=\mathrm{2A}(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$

where $A(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$ is the area enclosed by the boundary $(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$ . These conditions reduce to classical conditions of prescribed flux (where $l(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$ represents the length of the boundary $(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$ ):

$\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial \underline{n}}=\frac{\mathrm{2A}(\partial {\Omega}_{i})}{l(\partial {\Omega}_{i})}$

Finally, the problem to be solved is written:

$\Delta \varphi =-2$ on $\Omega$

$\varphi =0$ on $\partial {\Omega}_{0}=\Gamma$

$\varphi ={\varphi}_{i}$ on $(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$

$\frac{\partial \varphi }{\partial \underline{n}}=\frac{\mathrm{2A}(\partial {\Omega}_{i})}{l(\partial {\Omega}_{i})}$

Once this problem has been solved, one obtains the torsion constant by:

$\mathrm{CT}=2{\int}_{\Omega}\varphi \text{ds}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}{\varphi}_{i}A(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$ .

### 3.2 Computation of the torsion constant in MACR_CARA_POUTRE

This computation is carried out in MACR_CARA_POUTRE by the solution of a thermal problem. For this purpose the user must specify to MACR_CARA_POUTRE the mesh-cell group which defines the outer edge and, if the section contains holes, the mesh-cell groups which define the contour of each of them.

One then solves a linear thermal problem (THER_LINEAIRE) on a plane mesh of the section in order to find the function $j$ . One first places oneself in the principal inertia frame (CREA_MAILLAGE), from the coordinates of the centre of gravity and from the orientation of the principal frame computed previously.

One then defines the boundary conditions in AFFE_CHAR_THER:

- The source term is equal to –2

- The flux is equal to twice the area of the hole divided by the length of its edge. These quantities are computed beforehand .

- The computation of $\mathit{JX}$ is carried out in POST_ELEM by the keyword CARA_TORSION of the factor keyword CARA_POUTRE. In this case, one computes on each element the integral of $j$ , (option CARA_TORSION on the plane thermal elements), then one carries out the sum over all the elements.

### 3.3 Computation of the torsion radius in an arbitrary section

The torsion radius is computed by means of the computation of the stress function on the mesh of the section. $\mathrm{Rt}$ is added in the table produced by MACR_CARA_POUTRE [U4.42.02](doc:u4.42.02).

The solution of a steady thermal problem of unknown $j$ makes it possible to determine the torsion constant and the shear stresses.

The determination of the torsion radius $\mathrm{Rt}$ is the solution of: $\mathrm{Rt}=\mathrm{grad}(\varphi ).n$ (where $n$ represents the outward normal vector to the edge under consideration of the section).

$\mathrm{Rt}$ varies along the external contour; indeed, for an arbitrary section, the shears due to torsion vary on the edge. One chooses to take the value of $\mathrm{Rt}$ leading to the maximum shears on the external edge, that is to say the maximum value of $\mathrm{Rt}$ (in absolute value) on the external contour.

Moreover, if the section is cellular, one has several "several torsion radii": $\mathrm{Rt}=2\ast A(k)/L(k)$ (where $A(k)$ represents the area of the cell $k$ and $L(k)$ its perimeter). If one is content to seek the maximum value of the shear, one must take the maximum of the values $\mathrm{Rt}$ obtained on the external edge and on the cells.

The torsion radius is determined in MACR_CARA_POUTRE only by python commands. During the running of MACR_CARA_POUTRE the command POST_ELEM is called, so a new parameter $\mathrm{Rt}$ is created for this command.

### 3.4 Torsion constant of the circular and rectangular sections

Simplified expressions for these two types of section are described here. The computation of the torsion constants is then carried out directly in AFFE_CARA_ELEM.

For the circular section the previous expressions remain valid. Taking a torsion function of the form $\varphi (y,z)=\frac{1}{2}({R}^{2}-{x}^{2}-{y}^{2})$ one does indeed recover:

$\mathrm{CT}={I}_{p}=\frac{\pi}{2}({R}_{0}^{4}-{R}_{1}^{4})$

For the rectangular section, the computation is naturally more complex but may be carried out by choosing a function which does indeed vanish at the edges, of the form:

$\varphi (y,z)=\sum_{i=0}^{+\infty }\sum_{j=0}^{+\infty }{A}_{ij}\cos\left[(\mathrm{2i}+1)\frac{\pi y}{{h}_{y}}\right]\cos\left[(\mathrm{2j}+1)\frac{\pi z}{{h}_{z}}\right]$

The solution leads to a torsion constant which is written:

> $\mathrm{CT}=\frac{{h}_{y}^{3}{h}_{z}^{3}}{{h}_{y}^{2}+{h}_{z}^{2}}c(\frac{{h}_{y}}{{h}_{z}})={h}_{y}{h}_{z}^{3}\left[\frac{c(\frac{{h}_{y}}{{h}_{z}})}{1+{(\frac{{h}_{z}}{{h}_{y}})}^{2}}\right]$     [12]

where $c(\frac{{h}_{y}}{{h}_{z}})$ is expressed in the form of a series which takes the following values:

$\begin{array}{cccccc}\frac{{h}_{y}}{{h}_{z}}& 1& 2& 4& 8& +\infty \\ c(\frac{{h}_{y}}{{h}_{z}})& 0,281& 0,286& 0,299& 0,312& 1/3\end{array}$

In fact, *Code_Aster*  uses a simplified formula (ref. [bib]) for the solid rectangular section, which is written:

> $\mathrm{CT}=\frac{{h}_{y}{h}_{z}^{3}}{16}(\frac{16}{3}-3.36\frac{{h}_{z}}{{h}_{y}}+0.280{(\frac{{h}_{z}}{{h}_{y}})}^{5})$     [13]

It is valid if ${h}_{y}>{h}_{z}$ ; in the other case it is sufficient to exchange the respective places of ${h}_{y}$ and ${h}_{z}$ . The agreement between the two expressions is very good, as the following table indicates:

| $\frac{{h}_{y}}{{h}_{z}}$ | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | ∞ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\frac{\mathit{JX}}{{h}_{y}{h}_{z}^{3}}$ according to [eq] | 0,1405 | 0,2288 | 0,2814 | 0,3072 | 1/3 |
| $\frac{\mathit{JX}}{{h}_{y}{h}_{z}^{3}}$ according to *Aster* [eq] | 0,1408 | 0,2289 | 0,2809 | 0,3071 | 1/3 |

For the hollow rectangular beam, there exists an approximate solution which is written (ref. [bib] and [bib]):

$\mathit{JX}=\frac{2{\text{ep}}_{y}{\text{ep}}_{z}{({h}_{y}-{\text{ep}}_{y})}^{2}{({h}_{z}-{\text{ep}}_{z})}^{2}}{{h}_{y}{\text{ep}}_{y}-{\text{ep}}_{y}^{2}+{h}_{z}{\text{ep}}_{z}-{\text{ep}}_{z}^{2}}$

with the notation of the [Figure]: section in the plane $(0,y,z)$ .

### 3.5 The effective torsion radius

The effective torsion radius ${R}_{T}$ makes it possible to compute the maximum torsional transverse shear stress ${\sigma}_{{T}_{M}}$ as a function of the torsion moment. One may consult on this subject the account by MASSONET of this aspect (ref. [bib]). One thus has:

${\sigma}_{{T}_{M}}={M}_{x}\frac{{R}_{T}}{\mathit{JX}}$

In the case of circular cylinders, ${R}_{T}$ is equal to the radius (external if it is a tube) of the section.

For rectangular sections, the problem is distinctly more complex. *Code_Aster*  imposes the torsion radius of the solid section by:

${R}_{T}=\frac{\mathit{JX}4(3{h}_{y}+1.8{h}_{z})}{{h}_{y}^{2}+{h}_{z}^{2}}$

This approximate expression remains valid if the beam is not too flat. DHATT and BATOZ (ref.[bib]) give an expression with a more extended domain of validity, but in reality it is strictly speaking a series, numerical values of which are given by MASSONET (ref. [bib]).

For the hollow rectangular beam, *Code_Aster*  imposes an expression which is valid only if the wall is thin and of constant thickness ${\mathrm{ep}}_{z}$ , that is:

${R}_{T}=\frac{\mathit{JX}}{{\text{ep}}_{z}({h}_{y}-2{\text{ep}}_{y})({h}_{z}-2{\text{ep}}_{z})}$

This is an “adaptation” of the formula:

${R}_{T}=\frac{\mathit{JX}}{2eA}$

where $e$ is the thickness of the wall (constant) and $A$ the area contained inside the mean line. This last expression is known under the name of BREDT’s first formula (Cf. ref. [bib] and [bib]).

## 4 Computation of the warping constant

The warping constant is used by the beam model with warping (modelling POU_D_TG and POU_D_TGM), which it is important to take into account for beams of thin open sections (cf.[R3.08.04]).

This coefficient (denoted ${I}_{w}$ in [R3.08.04](doc:r3.08.04), in ${m}^{6}$ ) appears in the expression of the virtual work of the internal forces on the torsion terms:

${W}_{int}={\int}_{0}^{2}({\theta}_{x,x}^{\text{*}}\mu .C.{\theta}_{x,x}+{\theta}_{x,xx}^{\text{*}}.E.{I}_{\omega}.{\theta}_{x,xx})\text{dx}$

Taking up again the approach of [§3.1], and placing oneself in a frame linked to the torsion centre $C$ , the kinematics of the torsion of an arbitrary section is:

$u(M)=\left[\begin{array}{c}u\\ v\\ w\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}x\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right]\wedge \left[\begin{array}{c}x\\ y\\ z\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}\xi (y,z)\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right]$ $=(\begin{array}{c}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}\text{}\xi (y,z)\\ -\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}xz\\ \frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}xy\end{array})$

where $\xi (y,z)$ is the warping function (which vanishes only in the case of a circular section).

The expression of the stress field is (in elasticity):

$\begin{array}{}{\sigma}_{xx}=E{\varepsilon}_{xx}=E\xi (y,z)\frac{{\partial}^{2}{\theta}_{x}}{\partial {x}^{2}}\\ {\sigma}_{xy}=2\mu {\varepsilon}_{xy}=\mu \frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}(\frac{\partial \xi (y,z)}{\partial y}-z)\\ {\sigma}_{xz}=2\mu {\varepsilon}_{xz}=\mu \frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}(\frac{\partial \xi (y,z)}{\partial z}-y)\end{array}$

Unlike [§4], the second-order terms in $\frac{{\partial}^{2}{\theta}_{x}}{\partial {x}^{2}}$ are no longer neglected.

The first equilibrium relation ${(div\sigma )}_{x}={\sigma}_{xx,x}+{\sigma}_{xy,y}+{\sigma}_{xz,z}=0$ then implies the following condition on the warping function: $\Delta \xi =0$

On the other hand, with no external loading on the contour of the section , one must have $\sigma \otimes n$ , which may be written: $\frac{\partial \xi }{\partial y}{n}_{y}+\frac{\partial \xi }{\partial z}{n}_{z}=z.{n}_{y}-y.{n}_{z}$ , where ${n}_{y}$ and ${n}_{z}$ are the two components of the normal, or again in vector form: $\text{grad}\xi .n=\frac{\partial \xi }{\partial n}=(n\wedge \text{CM}).x$

This determines the warping function up to a constant. To remove this indeterminacy, one writes for example the expression of the normal force (for a section where torsion produces warping):

$N=\underset{S}{\int}{\sigma}_{xx}\text{ds}=\underset{S}{\int}E\xi \frac{{\partial}^{2}{\theta}_{x}}{\partial {x}^{2}}\text{ds}=0$

hence the additional condition on the warping function is $\underset{S}{\int}\xi \text{ds}=0$ . In practice, in MACR_CARA_POUTRE, one places oneself first of all in a frame linked to the torsion centre $c$ .

One then computes $\xi$ , which must satisfy:

$\begin{array}{}\Delta x\text{=0}\\ \text{grad}x.n=\frac{\partial \xi }{\partial n}=(n\wedge \text{CM}).x\\ \underset{S}{\int}x\text{ds}=0\end{array}$

The warping inertia ${I}_{\omega}$ is then obtained by: ${I}_{\omega}=\underset{S}{\int}{\xi}^{2}\text{ds}$

Determination of the torsion constant for sections with multiply connected boundaries

Let an elastic, isotropic beam, of length $L$ and of arbitrary section $\Omega$ , which may be non-simply connected. One denotes by $\partial {\Omega}_{0}$ the outer contour of $\Omega$ and by $\partial {\Omega}_{1}$ , for $i=\mathrm{1...n}-1$ , the possible inner contours. One denotes by $\partial \Omega =\underset{i=0}{\overset{n-1}{\cup}}\partial {\Omega}_{i}$ its total boundary.

One chooses the axis ${\underline{x}}_{1}$ along the line of the centres of gravity of the cross-sections. One assumes, in order to simplify the proof, that the torsion centre coincides with the centre of gravity, which makes it possible to uncouple the effects of torsion and of bending. The axes ${\underline{x}}_{2}$ and ${\underline{x}}_{3}$ are chosen along the principal directions of inertia.

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/100000000000024D0000011896FA6FDD9AC9FC4C.png)

**Figure** 9 **: Beam of arbitrary section.**

The beam is loaded on its section ${x}_{1}=L$ by a torsion moment ${\underline{M}}_{{G}_{1}}={M}_{t}{\underline{x}}_{1}$ .

On the other hand, the lateral surface of the cylinder is not loaded and the body forces are zero.

One immediately deduces that the internal-force wrench at the point ${G}_{0}$ is ${\underline{M}}_{{G}_{0}}={M}_{t}{\underline{x}}_{1}$

The elasticity problem posed previously appears to be incompletely defined. Indeed, the boundary conditions on the cross-sections ${x}_{1}=L$ and ${x}_{1}=0$ are incomplete because one does not have a condition at each point, but on average. There is therefore, *a priori* , an infinity of solutions. The Saint-Venant assumption consists in seeking a solution such that the stress tensor is of the form:

$\sigma =\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{\sigma}_{11}& {\sigma}_{12}& {\sigma}_{13}\\ {\sigma}_{12}& 0& 0\\ {\sigma}_{13}& 0& 0\end{array}\right]$

The Saint-Venant principle is valid far from the sections of application of the forces. Indeed, except in particular cases of loading, the four terms assumed to be zero decay exponentially with ${x}_{1}$ .

To solve this elasticity problem, one chooses a formulation in stresses. The equations to be written are therefore the equilibrium equations and the compatibility equations.

The equilibrium equations $div\sigma =\underline{0}$ lead to the following three scalar equations:

> ${\partial}_{1}{\sigma}_{11}+{\partial}_{2}{\sigma}_{12}+{\partial}_{3}{\sigma}_{13}=0$     [14]

> $\begin{array}{}{\partial}_{1}{\sigma}_{12}=0\\ {\partial}_{1}{\sigma}_{13}=0\end{array}$     [15]

(with the simplified notation:) ${\partial}_{i}{\sigma}_{\text{jk}}=\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{\text{jk}}}{\partial {x}_{i}}$

The Beltrami equations, which take the compatibility equations into account, are written:

> $-\Delta {\sigma}_{11}-{\partial}_{11}{\sigma}_{11}=0$     [16]
> $-\Delta {\sigma}_{12}-\frac{1}{1+\nu }{\partial}_{12}{\sigma}_{11}=0$     [17]

> $-\Delta {\sigma}_{13}-\frac{1}{1+\nu }{\partial}_{13}{\sigma}_{11}=0$     [18]

> $-{\partial}_{22}{\sigma}_{11}+\nu \Delta {\sigma}_{11}=0$     [19]

> $-{\partial}_{23}{\sigma}_{11}=0$     [20]

> $-{\partial}_{33}{\sigma}_{11}+\nu \Delta {\sigma}_{11}=0$     [21]

Equations [eq], [eq and [eq] show that ${\partial}_{11}{\sigma}_{11}$ , ${\partial}_{22}{\sigma}_{22}=0$ and ${\partial}_{33}{\sigma}_{11}$ are solutions of a homogeneous linear system and therefore that ${\partial}_{11}{\sigma}_{11}={\partial}_{22}{\sigma}_{11}={\partial}_{33}{\sigma}_{11}=0$ . With equation [eq], one deduces that ${\sigma}_{11}={a}_{0}+{a}_{1}{x}_{1}+({b}_{1}{x}_{1}+{b}_{0}){x}_{2}+({c}_{1}{x}_{1}+{c}_{0}){x}_{3}$ . Taking into account the fact that one is treating the free-torsion problem, one will take ${\sigma}_{11}$ equal to zero from now on.

Equations [eq] and [eq] show that ${\sigma}_{12}$ and ${\sigma}_{13}$ do not depend on ${x}_{1}$ . Equation [eq] is written:

${\partial}_{2}\left[{\sigma}_{12}+f({x}_{3})\right]={\partial}_{3}\left[-{\sigma}_{13}-g({x}_{2})\right]$

where $f$ and $g$ are two arbitrary functions. According to Schwartz’s theorem, there exists $\varphi ({x}_{2,}{x}_{3})$ such that:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\partial}_{2}\varphi =-{\sigma}_{13}-f({x}_{2})\\ {\partial}_{3}\varphi ={\sigma}_{12}+g({x}_{3})\end{array}=\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{12}={\partial}_{3}\varphi -f({x}_{3})\\ {\sigma}_{13}=-{\partial}_{2}\varphi +g({x}_{2})\end{array}$

Equations [eq] and [eq] give:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\partial}_{3}\Delta \varphi =0\\ {\partial}_{2}\Delta \varphi =0\end{array}$

that is $\Delta \varphi ={\partial}_{3}f-{\partial}_{2}f+K$

where $K$ is an integration constant. Since $f$ and $g$ are arbitrary, they will be taken identically zero. The problem to be solved is therefore a Laplacian problem: $\Delta \varphi =K$ on $\Omega$ then:

$\sigma =\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& {\partial}_{3}\varphi & -{\partial}_{2}\varphi \\ {\partial}_{3}\varphi & 0& 0\\ -{\partial}_{2}\varphi & 0& 0\end{array}\right]$

It remains to write the boundary conditions, which will allow us to write conditions on $\varphi \Omega$ for $\varphi$ and on $K$

The boundary conditions are to be written on the whole boundary. On the cross-sections ${x}_{1}=L$ (and likewise at ${x}_{1}=0$ ), one has:

> ${\int}_{\Omega}{\sigma}_{12}\text{ds}=0$
> ${\int}_{\Omega}{\sigma}_{13}\text{ds}=0$

> ${\int}_{\Omega}{x}_{2}{\sigma}_{13}-{x}_{3}{\sigma}_{12}\text{ds}={M}_{t}$     [22]

Let $\underline{n}$ be the outward normal to $\partial \Omega$ . One has $\sigma \otimes n=0$ . One sets $\tau ={\sigma}_{12}{x}_{2}+{\sigma}_{13}{x}_{3}$ . One may also write $\tau ={\sigma}_{12}{x}_{2}+{\sigma}_{13}{x}_{3}$ ; $\tau$ is called the tangential part of the stress in the cross-section. Let ${N}_{i}$ be the current point of the contour $\varphi \Omega$ for $i=0,\mathrm{...},n-1$ . The condition, on the lateral surface, stated above, may be written $\tau {\text{dN}}_{i}=\underline{0}$ .

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/10000000000001370000015B564DDD3E8B518317.png)

**Figure** 10 **: Definition of the normals**

Thus, on the lateral surface of a beam, the tangential stress vector $\tau$ is tangent to the contour.

- Equation $\tau \wedge {\text{dN}}_{i}=\underline{0}$ leads to a condition which $\varphi$ must satisfy on the contour: $d\varphi =0$
- Equation [eq] leads to ${M}_{t}={\int}_{\Omega}-{x}_{2}{\partial}_{2}\varphi -{x}_{3}{\partial}_{3}\varphi \text{ds}$ , which one may also write:

${M}_{t}=2{\int}_{\Omega}\varphi \text{ds}+{\int}_{\partial \Omega }\varphi ({x}_{3}{\text{dx}}_{2}-{x}_{2}{\text{dx}}_{3})$

The problem to be solved in order to obtain $\varphi$ is therefore:

$\Delta \varphi =K$ on $\Omega$

$d\varphi =0$ on $\partial \Omega$

with the constraint ${M}_{t}=2{\int}_{\Omega}\varphi \text{ds}+{\int}_{\partial \Omega }\varphi ({x}_{3}{\text{dx}}_{2}-{x}_{2}{\text{dx}}_{3})$

It remains to identify the torsion constant C. The constitutive law of beams in torsion is: ${M}_{t}=\text{CG}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}$ (cf. [§3]).

In order to solve the previous problem more easily, one sets $\psi =\frac{\varphi}{C\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}}$ and $K=-\mathrm{2C}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}$ ; the problem to be solved then becomes:

$\Delta \psi =-2$ on $\Omega$

$d\psi =0$ on $\partial \Omega$

${M}_{t}=-\mathrm{2C}\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}\left[{\int}_{\Omega}\psi \text{ds}+{\int}_{\partial \Omega }\psi ({x}_{3}{\text{dx}}_{2}-{x}_{2}{\text{dx}}_{3})\right]$

With such a notation, one obtains $C=2{\int}_{\Omega}\psi \text{ds}+{\int}_{\partial \Omega }\psi ({x}_{3}{\text{dx}}_{2}-{x}_{2}{\text{dx}}_{3})$

The contour $\partial \Omega$ is made up of several contours: an outer contour $\partial {\Omega}_{0}$ and $n-1$ inner contours $\partial {\Omega}_{i}$ . The condition $d\psi =0$ leads to the $n$ following conditions: $\psi ={\psi}_{i}$ on $\partial {\Omega}_{i}$ for $i=0,\mathrm{...},n-1$ . The ${\psi}_{i}$ are unknown constants. Noting that $\varphi$ , and therefore $\psi$ , is defined up to a constant, one may fix one of the ${\psi}_{i}$ . One will therefore take ${\psi}_{0}=0$ . It remains to determine ${\psi}_{i}$ for $i=0,\mathrm{...},n-1$ .

For this purpose, one will study the warping of the cross-section of abscissa ${x}_{1}$ . Recall that the stress tensor is written (cf. [§4]):

$\sigma =G\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& \frac{\partial \xi }{\partial y}-z& \frac{\partial \xi }{\partial z}+y\\ \frac{\partial \xi }{\partial y}-z& 0& 0\\ \frac{\partial \xi }{\partial z}+y& 0& 0\end{array}\right]$ $=G\frac{\partial {\theta}_{x}}{\partial x}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial z}& -\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial y}\\ \frac{\partial \psi }{\partial z}& 0& 0\\ -\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial y}& 0& 0\end{array}\right]$

One sets $\underline{\tau}=\underline{\text{grad}}\psi \wedge {\underline{x}}_{1}$ ; $\underline{\tau}$ is (up to a constant) the tangential part of the stress vector in the cross-section.

One therefore has: $\underline{\text{grad}}\xi =\underline{\tau}-\underline{\text{GM}}\wedge {\underline{x}}_{1}$

One circulates this equation along $\partial {\Omega}_{i}$ ; one has:

$0={\oint}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}\tau \text{dl}-{\oint}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}{x}_{3}{\text{dx}}_{2}-{x}_{2}{\text{dx}}_{3}$

One has used here the fact that the circulation of the gradient on a closed curve is zero.

One notes that the first integral may be written in terms of the flux of $\psi$ crossing $\partial {\Omega}_{i}$ . On the other hand, the second integral is equal to $\mathrm{2A}(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$ .

Finally, the problem is written:

$\Delta \psi =-2$ on $\Omega$

$\psi =0$ on $\partial {\Omega}_{0}$

$\psi ={\psi}_{i}$ on $\partial {\Omega}_{i}$

${\oint}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}\frac{\partial \text{y}}{\partial \underline{n}}\text{dl}=\mathrm{2A}(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$

Once this problem has been solved, one has: $J=2{\int}_{\Omega}\psi \text{ds}+2\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}{\psi}_{i}A(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$

The last condition: ${\oint}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}\frac{\partial \text{y}}{\partial \underline{n}}\text{dl}=\mathrm{2A}(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$ is difficult to treat numerically. In reality, the two conditions on each boundary bordering a hole are written:

$\psi ={\psi}_{i}$ on $\partial {\Omega}_{i}$

$\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial n}=\frac{\mathrm{2A}(\partial {\Omega}_{i})}{l(\partial {\Omega}_{i})}$

Variational formulation:

$\forall v\in {H}^{1}(\Omega )\text{tel que}v{\mid}_{\partial {\Omega}_{0}}=0$

$\forall \mu \in {L}^{2}(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\int}_{\Omega}\nabla \psi \nabla v\Omega +\sum_{i}{\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}{\lambda}^{i}v\text{dl}=2{\int}_{\Omega}vd\Omega +\mathrm{2A}(\partial {\Omega}_{i}){\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}v\text{dl}\\ {\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}\mu \psi \text{dl}={\psi}_{i}{\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}m\text{dl}\\ {\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}{\lambda}^{i}\text{dl}=0\end{array}$

One considers the function $\theta$ such that $\theta \equiv 1$ on $\partial {\Omega}_{i}$ . In matrix form, one has:

$\psi ={}^{t}\text{}\left[\psi \right]\left[\Phi \right]$

$v={}^{t}\left[v\right]\left[\Phi \right]$

$\theta ={}^{t}\left[\theta \right]\left[F\right]$

Where $\left[\Phi \right]$ is the vector whose components are the shape functions.

Under these conditions, the variational approximation of the weak formulation gives:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}\left[K\right]\left[\psi \right]+\sum_{i}{}^{t}\left[{B}^{i}\right]\left[{\lambda}^{i}\right]& =& \left[f\right]+\left[T\right]\\ \left[B\right]\left[\psi \right]& =& \left[0\right]\end{array}$

Where one has set:

${}^{t}\left[f\right]\left[v\right]=2{\int}_{\Omega}vd\Omega$

${}^{t}\text{}\left[T\right]\left[v\right]=\frac{\mathrm{2A}(\partial {\Omega}_{i})}{\parallel \partial {\Omega}_{i}\parallel }{\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}v\text{dl}$

$\left[B\right]=\sum_{i}\left[{B}^{i}\right]$

One can check that the condition on the flux is satisfied:

$\begin{array}{ccc}{\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial n}\text{dl}={\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}\Delta \psi \theta \text{dl}& =& {\int}_{\Omega}\Delta \psi \theta d\Omega +{\int}_{\Omega}\nabla \psi \nabla \theta d\Omega \\ & =& \underset{=0}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{-{\int}_{\Omega}2\theta d\Omega {}^{t}+\left[f\right]\left[\theta \right]}}{}^{t}+\left[T\right]\left[\theta \right]-\underset{=0}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{}^{t}\left[\theta \right]\left[{B}^{i}\right]}}\left[\lambda \right]\\ & =& \frac{\mathrm{2A}(\partial {\Omega}_{i})}{\parallel \partial {\Omega}_{i}\parallel }{\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{i}}\theta \text{dl}\\ & =& \mathrm{2A}(\partial {\Omega}_{i})\end{array}$

One sees, with this new formulation, that the mathematical problem posed amounts to a linear thermal problem with a particular loading. This is easily programmable in *Code_Aster* .

Applying the previous method to a beam whose cross-section is the annulus lying between the radii ${R}_{1}$ and ${R}_{0}$ with ${R}_{1}<{R}_{0}$ .

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/1000000000000153000001636B622F33505BFA89.png)

One has the following problem to solve:

$\Delta \psi =-2$

$\psi (r={R}_{0})={\psi}_{0}=0$

$\psi (r={R}_{1})={\psi}_{1}$

${\int}_{\partial {\Omega}_{1}}\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial n}\text{dl}=2\pi {R}_{0}^{2}$

The general solution of the problem is written $\psi =\psi (r)\text{=-}\frac{{r}^{2}}{2}+A\ln(r)+B$

Here, one has $\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial n}{\mid}_{\partial {\Omega}_{1}}=-\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial r}{e}_{r}$ . Hence $2\pi {R}_{1}^{2}-2\pi A\ln({R}_{1})=2\pi {R}_{1}^{2}\Rightarrow A=0$ . On the other hand, one has $\psi ({R}_{0})=0\Rightarrow B=\frac{{R}_{0}^{2}}{2}$ . Finally:

$\psi (r)\text{=-}\frac{{r}^{2}}{2}+\frac{{R}_{0}^{2}}{2}$ .

One can now compute $J=2{\int}_{\Omega}\psi \text{ds}+2{\psi}_{1}A(\partial {\Omega}_{i})$ . One has ${\psi}_{1}=-\frac{{R}_{1}^{2}}{2}+\frac{{R}_{0}^{2}}{2}$ and $A(\partial {\Omega}_{1})=\psi {R}_{1}^{2}$ . All computations done, one has the classical result: $J=\frac{\pi}{2}({R}_{0}^{4}-{R}_{1}^{4})$

Determination of the shear constant of a beam equivalent to a set of parallel beams

Statement of the problem:

A method developed in the command MACR_CARA_POUTRE in order to obtain the coefficients $\mathrm{AY}$ and $\mathrm{AZ}$ of a beam equivalent to a set of disjoint beams (e.g. columns built in between two floors) is set out here. This makes it possible, for example, to build “skewer” models of buildings, that is condensed into a single beam.

For a single beam, the definition of the shear coefficients rests on the energy method [§2.1.3]: the formulation is based on the complementary energy due to shear in the section.

The shear coefficient is: $k={\frac{\left[{\int}_{s}{\sigma}_{\text{CT}}\text{dS}\right]}{SG{\int}_{s}\frac{1}{G}{\sigma}_{{\text{CT}}^{2}}\text{dS}}}^{2}$

**Note:**

*This expression is valid in the case of a heterogeneous beam (* $G$ *variable).*

The distribution of the shear stress in the section, for a single beam, is based on the Jourawski formula, [§2.1.1], which supplies the distribution of the shear stresses due to a shear force in one direction and only the average of the shears in the other direction.

The Jourawski formula is written:

${\sigma}_{\text{CT}}=\frac{m(y)}{\mathrm{Ib}(y)}V$ with $m(y)={\int}_{y}^{{y}_{\max}}t\text{}b(t)\text{}\text{dt}$

The quantity $m(y)$ represents the static moment of the part of the section $A$ lying between $y$ and ${y}_{\max}$

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/1000000000000120000000B4D31742FC63458689.png)

Then $k$ may be written in the form: $k=\frac{{I}^{2}}{S\text{G}{\int}_{y}^{{y}_{\max}}\frac{{m}^{2}(y)}{{\text{Gb}}^{2}(y)}\text{dy}}$

The underlying idea is that the section carries normal stresses coming from the theory of Euler beams, and that one evaluates the sliding force of $A$ on $B$ .

For a non-connected section, such as the sectional view of a building, the Jourawski assumption cannot be made (unless one considers that the whole section deforms axially as one and the same beam at each abscissa $x$ ). One cannot know a priori the distribution of the shears or of the normal stresses in each column. The following figure gives an idea of the sectional view of a reactor building:

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/100000000000024A000002536C9CC65012F07FA4.png)

Simplified expression of the shear coefficients

**Hypothesis:**  the columns are built into the floors: the building seen from the side may be represented as a set of parallel columns built in between two floors:

One computes this particular case: each column is a beam of rectangular section (the principal axes of inertia of the different columns are not collinear). $H=3$ to $\mathrm{4m}$ .

The beams are built in at both ends. One must then seek a relation between a force $F$ imposed on the upper floor, and the displacement of this floor in the same direction, that is compute the stiffness of this system in this direction.

**•** **For one beam:**

The method used is set out for example in [bib].

**•** **Beam built in at one end and free at the other:**

The system is statically determinate and elastic. One wishes to express the displacement $u(H)$ as a function of $F$ and $\Gamma$ . The Principle of Virtual Work is written:

$\begin{array}{}f(H).v(H)=\underset{0}{\overset{H}{\int}}{M}^{f}.\kappa (v)+{V}^{f}.\gamma (v)\mathrm{dl}\\ \kappa (v)=\frac{M(v)}{\mathrm{E.}I}\\ \gamma (v)=\frac{V(v)}{\mathrm{G.}{S}_{r}}\end{array}$

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/10000000000000F1000000EAE21FD62F7CCDCFCB.png)

for every virtual displacement $v$ , and for a point force $f$ at $y=H$ , (here the normal force is zero). One chooses $f=1$ , and one computes successively the displacements due to a force $F$ and to a couple $\Gamma$ at $y=H$ . Integrating the previous expression, one finds that, under the effect of $F$ , the real displacement $u$ and the rotation are equal to: $u(H)=\frac{F.{H}^{3}}{3E.I}+\frac{F.H}{G.{S}_{r}}=\frac{F.{H}^{3}}{12E.I}(4+\frac{12\text{EI}}{G.{H}^{2}{S}_{r}})$ with ${S}_{r}=k\cdot S$ and $\theta (H)=-\frac{F.{H}^{2}}{2E.I}$

Under the effect of the moment $\Gamma$ , one obtains:

${u}_{\Gamma}(H)=-\frac{\Gamma .{H}^{2}}{2E.I}$ ${\theta}_{\Gamma}(H)=\frac{\Gamma .H}{E.I}$

If the beam has a rectangular section of width $b$ and of thickness $h$ , one obtains: ${S}_{r}=\text{bhk}=\text{bh}\frac{5}{6}$

for an imposed F: $u(H)=\frac{F.{H}^{3}}{E.{\text{bh}}^{3}}(4+\frac{12}{5}\frac{{h}^{2}}{{H}^{2}}(1+\nu ))$ $\theta (H)\text{=-}\frac{6F.{H}^{2}}{E.{\text{bh}}^{3}}$ for an imposed $\Gamma$ : ${u}_{\Gamma}(H)=-\frac{6\Gamma .{H}^{2}}{E.{\text{bh}}^{3}}$ ${\theta}_{\Gamma}(H)=\frac{12\Gamma .H}{E.{\text{bh}}^{3}}$

**•** **Beam built in at both ends: (sliding built-in support at** $y=H$ **)**

The system is hyperstatic of degree 1. One expresses the displacement $u(H)$ as a function of the hyperstatic unknowns $F$ and $\Gamma$ by means of the previous results. Under the effect of $F$ and $\Gamma$ , the real displacement u and the rotation (zero because of the built-in support) are equal to:

> $\begin{array}{}u(H)=\frac{F.{H}^{3}}{3\mathrm{E.}I}+\frac{\mathrm{F.}H}{G{S}_{r}}-\frac{\Gamma .\mathrm{H²}}{2\mathrm{E.}I}=\frac{F.{H}^{3}}{\mathrm{E.}b{h}^{3}}(4+\frac{12}{5}\frac{\mathrm{h²}}{\mathrm{H²}}(1+\nu ))\\ 0=-\frac{F.\mathrm{H²}}{2EI}+\frac{\Gamma .H}{\mathrm{E.}I}=-\frac{6F.\mathrm{H²}}{E.bh}+\frac{12\Gamma .H}{E.\mathrm{bh²}}\end{array}$

![Figure](/figures/r3.08.03/10000000000000F1000000C84874974570DF17B5.png)

The solution of this system makes it possible to obtain u(H) as a function of F:

> $\begin{array}{}u(H)=\frac{F.{H}^{3}}{12\text{EI}}(1+\frac{12\text{EI}}{{\text{GH}}^{2}{S}_{r}})=\frac{F.{H}^{3}}{E.{\text{bh}}^{3}}(1+\frac{12}{5}\frac{{h}^{2}}{{H}^{2}}(1+\nu ))\\ F=(\frac{12\text{EI}}{{H}^{3}}\frac{1}{(1+\frac{12\text{EI}}{{\text{GH}}^{2}{S}_{r}})})u(H)=(\frac{E.{\text{bh}}^{3}}{{H}^{3}}\frac{1}{(1+\frac{12}{5}\frac{{h}^{2}}{{H}^{2}}(1+\nu ))})u(H)=K.u(H)\end{array}$

One also recovers this result by considering the stiffness matrix of an “exact” beam element with 2 nodes ([bib] or [R3.08.01]). The term above corresponds exactly to the stiffness term of the shear force alone along the direction x:

${K}_{xx}=(\frac{12\text{EI}}{{H}^{3}}\frac{1}{(1+\Phi )})\Phi =\frac{12\text{EI}}{{\text{GH}}^{2}{S}_{r}}=\frac{12\text{EI}}{{\text{GH}}^{2}\text{kS}}$

**Note:**

The built-in–free situation differs only by a coefficient (4 instead of 1),:

$u(H)=\frac{F.{H}^{3}}{E.{\text{bh}}^{3}}(4+\frac{12}{5}\frac{{h}^{2}}{{H}^{2}}(1+\nu ))$

The two possibilities are offered in MACR_CARA_POUTRE:

- both ends are built in (in reality one is built in, the other is built into a moving floor: sliding built-in support)
- the upper end is free (in fact in a pinned connection with the upper floor).

One may therefore propose to express the shear-force stiffness of each column in the form:

$F=(\frac{12\text{EI}}{{H}^{3}}\frac{1}{(\xi +\frac{12\text{EI}}{{\text{GH}}^{2}{S}_{r}})})u(H)=K.u(H)$ with $\xi =\lbrace \begin{array}{c}1\text{encastré}-\text{encastré}\\ 4\text{encastré}-\text{libre}\end{array}$

For a set of beams

The method consists in computing the stiffness of each column in the previous way, and in comparing the stiffness of the set with that of an equivalent beam built in between two floors. For this purpose one expresses the global shear force applied to the set of columns (for example in the direction $y$ ):

${T}_{y}=\sum{T}_{i}={\tilde{K}}_{y}.{u}_{y}$

Each column having an arbitrary orientation with respect to the global axes, one must first of all express the forces ${T}_{i}$ in the global frame:

| ${x}_{1}$ and ${x}_{2}$ denoting the principal axes of inertia of column i, the shear force ${T}_{i}$ in this frame is: $\begin{array}{}{T}_{y}^{i}={T}_{1}^{i}\cos({\varphi}_{i})+{T}_{2}^{i}\sin({\varphi}_{i})\\ {T}_{z}^{i}\text{=-}{T}_{1}^{i}\sin({\varphi}_{i})+{T}_{2}^{i}\cos({\varphi}_{i})\end{array}$ | ![Figure](images/100000000000010900000081CC0C1A40D37F8DD7.png) |
| --- | --- |

Moreover, one assumes that the global displacement $u$ of the set of columns is uniform (of components ${u}_{x}$ and ${u}_{y}$ ) and must be collinear with the shear force $T$ . (which is not certain: couplings are possible if there are no particular symmetries). This implies, for the direction $y$ :

$\begin{array}{}{u}_{1}^{i}={u}_{y}\cos({\varphi}_{i})\\ {u}_{2}^{i}={u}_{y}\sin({\varphi}_{i})\end{array}$ and $\begin{array}{}{T}_{1}^{i}={K}_{1}^{i}.{u}_{1}^{i}\\ {T}_{2}^{i}={K}_{2}^{i}.{u}_{2}^{i}\end{array}$

one obtains:

$\begin{array}{}{T}_{y}^{i}=({K}_{1}^{i}{\cos}^{2}({\varphi}_{i})+{K}_{2}^{i}{\sin}^{2}({\varphi}_{i})){u}_{y}^{i}\\ {T}_{y}=\sum{T}_{y}^{i}=\sum({K}_{1}^{i}{\cos}^{2}({\varphi}_{i})+{K}_{2}^{i}{\sin}^{2}({\varphi}_{i})){u}_{y}={\tilde{K}}_{y}{u}_{y}\end{array}$ with ${\tilde{K}}_{y}=\frac{12\text{EI}}{{H}^{3}}\frac{1}{(\xi +\frac{12\text{EI}}{{\text{GH}}^{2}{S}_{r}})}$

likewise in the direction $z$ :

$\begin{array}{}{u}_{1}^{i}=-{u}_{z}\sin({\varphi}_{i})\\ {u}_{2}^{i}={u}_{z}\cos({\varphi}_{i})\end{array}$ hence ${T}_{z}=\sum{T}_{z}^{i}=\sum({K}_{1}^{i}{\sin}^{2}({\varphi}_{i})+{K}_{2}^{i}{\cos}^{2}({\varphi}_{i})){u}_{z}={\tilde{K}}_{z}{u}_{z}$

On the other hand, for the equivalent beam, one makes the assumption that the shear-force stiffness is expressed in the same way as that of each beam:

${T}_{y}={K}_{{}_{y}}^{\text{eq}}{u}_{y}=\frac{12{\text{EI}}_{z}^{\text{eq}}}{{H}^{3}(1+{\Phi}_{y})}{u}_{y}\text{avec}{\Phi}_{y}=\frac{12{\text{EI}}_{z}^{\text{eq}}}{{S}^{\text{eq}}{H}^{2}{\text{Gk}}_{y}^{\text{eq}}}$

In fact, one would have to check that the energies due to bending and to the normal force are indeed negligible. The two expressions of the shear force lead to the expression of the equivalent shear coefficient: ${k}_{y}^{\text{eq}}=\frac{12{\text{EI}}_{z}^{\text{eq}}}{{\text{GS}}^{\text{eq}}{H}^{2}(\frac{12{\text{EI}}_{z}^{\text{eq}}}{{H}^{3}{\tilde{K}}_{y}}-1)}$ and in the direction $z$ : ${k}_{z}^{\text{eq}}=\frac{12{\text{EI}}_{y}^{\text{eq}}}{{\text{GS}}^{\text{eq}}{H}^{2}(\frac{12{\text{EI}}_{y}^{\text{eq}}}{{H}^{3}{\tilde{K}}_{z}}-1)}$

Method used in MACR_CARA_POUTRE

Using the assumptions described previously, namely:

- only the stiffness due to the shear force is taken into account in the computation of the shear coefficients
- the equivalent beam is built in on the two floors
- two computation assumptions are to be provided concerning each column (built-in–pinned and built-in–built-in).

One may propose a method of computation in MACR_CARA_POUTRE in order to obtain shear coefficients equivalent to a set of beams of parallel axes, built into a floor at one of their ends, and free at the other, or else built in at the other end.

Restrictions on use:

- it is reasonable to place the equivalent beam on the centre of gravity of the set of columns, and in the principal inertia frame of the set, in order to avoid parasitic couplings
- one must ensure the continuity of all the degrees of freedom of the equivalent beam (translation and rotation) with the degrees of freedom of the floors (which models the built-in connection of the beam in the floor), which requires modelling the floor with shell elements or, if it is meshed in 3D, connecting it by means of beams or plates.

The method of computation is the following:

- For each column, carry out the usual computation of the geometric characteristics and of the shear coefficients of the section, in the principal inertia frame of each section (already available).
- Still for each section, computation of the shear stiffness (the user must supply H, the distance between floors.

$\begin{array}{}{K}_{1}^{i}=\frac{12{\text{EI}}_{2}^{i}}{{H}^{3}(1+{\Phi}_{1})}\text{avec}{\Phi}_{1}=\frac{12{\text{EI}}_{2}^{i}}{{S}^{i}{H}^{2}{\text{Gk}}_{1}^{i}}\\ {K}_{2}^{i}=\frac{12{\text{EI}}_{1}^{i}}{{H}^{3}(1+{\Phi}_{2})}\text{avec}{\Phi}_{2}=\frac{12{\text{EI}}_{1}^{i}}{{S}^{i}{H}^{2}{\text{Gk}}_{2}^{i}}\end{array}$

- Computation of the stiffness equivalent to the set of beams:

${\tilde{K}}_{y}=\sum({K}_{1}^{i}{\cos}^{2}({\varphi}_{i})+{K}_{2}^{i}{\sin}^{2}({\varphi}_{i}))$

${\tilde{K}}_{z}=\sum({K}_{1}^{i}{\sin}^{2}({\varphi}_{i})+{K}_{2}^{i}{\cos}^{2}({\varphi}_{i}))$

- Computation of the equivalent shear coefficients:
