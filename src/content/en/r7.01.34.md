## 1 Introduction

We present here the SUSHI finite-volume schemes developed for the modelling of miscible two-phase (unsaturated) flows in a porous medium, miscible meaning here that the components can exist in several phases. These schemes concern the family of the THM modellings. They are intended only for pure hydraulics (neither mechanics nor thermal analysis), that is, if one takes up the classical nomenclature described in [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11), the KIT_HH.

The SUSHI finite-volume schemes (Scheme Using Stabilization and Hybrid Interfaces) were developed by Ophélie Angelini in the framework of her thesis [1] in order to treat in particular problems of steep fronts on arbitrary meshes. This scheme is inspired by the Hybrid Finite Volume (HVF) scheme introduced by R. Eymard et al. [4]. At the origin of this work there was an abundant literature indicating that these schemes, ensuring by definition a good representation of the flux, were better suited to this type of hyperbolic equations applied to strongly heterogeneous domains. Implementing and having such schemes available therefore quickly seemed reasonable, if only in order to compare oneself with the members of the community of flows in porous media [2]. These schemes do not claim to replace the finite elements but propose a robust alternative.

We shall present here only briefly the physical model of two-phase flows detailed in the documentation [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11).

This documentation therefore essentially presents the finite-volume spatial discretisation schemes implemented on these models in Code_Aster. For further details, the reader is referred to the thesis of Ophélie Angélini [1]

## 2 Presentation of the problem: Assumptions, Notations

The whole of the hydraulic physical model is presented in detail in R7.01.11. We content ourselves here with recalling the main variables and assumptions as well as the equations treated. The formalism comes essentially from the works of Coussy [5]. The routines managing the constitutive laws are not affected by the use of the finite-volume scheme.

### 2.1 Modelling framework

These developments concern only the models with 2 phases (liquid gas) and 2 miscible components (for example water and hydrogen). We therefore place ourselves in the framework of the *_HH2 modellings (cf. U2.04.05). More precisely one will speak here of the HH2SUDA modelling (cf. section 4).

It is recalled that the 2 hydraulic constitutive laws that can be used are then:

- LIQU_AD_GAZ_VAPE: 2 components per phase
- LIQU_AD_GAZ: 2 components in the liquid phase, only one in the gas phase (vapour neglected)

In what follows only the complete model with 2 components, 2 phases will be discussed. The case without vapour simply amounts to cancelling the terms concerning it.

### 2.2 Notations

We assume that the pores of the solid are occupied by two constituents denoted $w$ (for water) and $h$ (for hydrogen), each coexisting in two phases at most, one liquid denoted $l$ and the other gaseous denoted $g$ .

The quantities (grandeur) $A$ associated with the phase $p$ ($p=l,g$ ) of the component $c$ will be denoted: ${X}_{p}^{c}$ . Concretely, this gives:

- ${A}_{l}^{w}$ : quantity (grandeur) $A$ for liquid water,
- ${A}_{g}^{w}$ : quantity (grandeur) $A$ for water vapour,
- ${A}_{l}^{h}$ : quantity (grandeur) $A$ for the component h dissolved in the liquid,
- ${A}_{g}^{h}$ : quantity (grandeur) $A$ for the component h in gaseous form (for example dry hydrogen).

The general assumptions made are the following:

- anisotropic constitutive behaviour,
- the gases are perfect gases,
- ideal mixture of perfect gases (total pressure = sum of the partial pressures),
- thermodynamic equilibrium between the phases of one and the same constituent.

The various notations are made explicit below.

#### 2.2.1 State variables

The variables are:

- the pressures of each constituent ${P}_{p}^{c}$
- the temperature of the medium $T$ .

These various variables are not totally independent. Indeed, if one considers a single constituent, the thermodynamic equilibrium between its phases imposes a relation between the vapour pressure and the liquid pressure of this constituent. Finally, there is only one independent pressure per constituent, just as there is only one mass-conservation equation. The number of independent pressures is therefore equal to the number of independent constituents. The choice of these pressures is free (combinations of the pressures of the constituents) provided that the pressures chosen, associated with the temperature, form a system of independent variables.

We have chosen — and in order to be homogeneous with the finite-element formulation — as independent variables descriptive of the medium:

- the total pressure of the gas ${P}_{\text{g}}={P}_{g}^{w}+{P}_{g}^{h}$ , (Dalton's law)
- The total pressure of the liquid ${P}_{l}={P}_{l}^{w}+{P}_{l}^{h}$
- the capillary pressure ${P}_{c}={P}_{\text{g}}-{P}_{l}={P}_{g}^{w}+{P}_{g}^{h}-({P}_{l}^{w}+{P}_{l}^{h})$ .

#### 2.2.2 Characteristic quantities (grandeur) of the solid phase

One denotes:

The porosity: $\phi$ ,

The intrinsic permeability tensor: $k$

#### 2.2.3 Characteristic quantities (grandeur) of the fluids

One denotes:

- The density of the phase $p$ : ${\rho}_{\text{p}}$ , ${\rho}_{\text{p}}={\rho}_{p}^{w}+{\rho}_{p}^{h}$
- The viscosity of the phase $p$ : ${\mu}_{\text{p}}$
- The saturation of the phase $p$ : , ${S}_{l}+{S}_{\text{g}}=1$ which is a decreasing function of the capillary pressure. One therefore has ${S}_{l}=f({P}_{c})$ .
- The relative permeability of the phase $p$ : ${k}_{\text{rp}}$ function of the saturation
- The hydraulic conductivity of the phase $p$ : ${\lambda}_{\text{p}}^{H}$ such that: ${\lambda}_{\text{p}}^{H}=\frac{k{k}_{\mathrm{rp}}}{{\mu}_{p}}$
- The mobility of the component $c,c=(h,w)$ associated with the phase $p$ ${k}_{\text{p}}^{c}=\frac{{\rho}_{\text{p}}^{c}{k}_{\mathit{rp}}}{{\mu}_{p}}$
- The molar mass of the component $c$ : ${M}^{c}$
- The molar concentration ${c}_{p}^{c}=\frac{{\rho}_{p}^{c}}{{M}^{c}}$
- The mass fraction of the phase $p$ and of the component $c$ : ${\zeta}_{p}^{c}=\frac{{\rho}_{p}^{c}}{{\rho}_{p}}$
- The molar fraction: ${X}_{p}^{c}=\frac{{c}_{p}^{c}}{{c}_{p}}$ where ${c}_{p}={c}_{p}^{h}+{c}_{p}^{w}$ (in the literature, these concentrations are sometimes also denoted ${C}_{p}^{c}$ )
- The compressibility modulus of water ${K}_{w}$

### 2.3 Constitutive equations of the model

The details making it possible to arrive at the final equations of the model will not be given here. For the intermediate stages, the reader is referred to [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11). We therefore content ourselves here with a succinct reminder of the main equations.

#### 2.3.1 Equilibrium equations

The equilibrium equations are given here by the conservation of the mass of each constituent, that is:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}\dot{{m}_{l}^{w}}+{\dot{m}}_{g}^{w}+\text{Div}({F}_{l}^{w}+{F}_{g}^{w})=0\\ \dot{{m}_{l}^{h}}+{\dot{m}}_{g}^{h}+\text{Div}({F}_{l}^{h}+{F}_{g}^{h})=0\end{array}$

with ${m}_{p}^{c}$ the mass supply of the constituent $c$ in phase $p$ , such that ${m}_{p}^{c}=\phi .{S}_{p}.{\rho}_{p}^{c}$ and ${F}_{p}^{c}$ the mass flux of the phase $p$ for the constituent $c$ .

${F}_{p}^{c}$ is constituted of the Fickian mass flux ${J}_{p}^{c}$ and of the Darcean mass flux ${F}_{p}$ such that:

${F}_{p}^{c}={J}_{p}^{c}+{\rho}_{p}^{c}\frac{{F}_{p}}{{\rho}_{l}},c=(h,w);p=(l,g)$

##### 2.3.1.1 Constitutive equations

#### 2.3.2 Constitutive laws of the fluids

**Evolution of the porosity:**

In the absence of mechanics, an evolution of the porosity is nevertheless allowed via the storage coefficient ${E}_{m}$ , such that:

$d\phi ={E}_{m}d{P}_{l}$

**Behaviour of the liquid:**

It is considered that water can be compressible: $\frac{d{\rho}_{l}^{w}}{{\rho}_{l}^{w}}=\frac{{\text{dP}}_{l}^{w}}{{K}_{w}}$

**Behaviour of the gas:**

It is considered that the gas is subject to the perfect-gas law:

$\frac{{P}_{g}^{c}}{{\rho}_{g}^{c}}=\frac{RT}{{M}^{c}};c=(w,h)$ where $R$ is the perfect-gas constant.

**Water-vapour equilibrium law:**

the water-vapour equilibrium is written by equality of the free enthalpies, which, for an isothermal problem, gives (see R7.01.11):

$\frac{d{P}_{g}^{w}}{{\rho}_{g}^{w}}=\frac{{\text{dP}}_{l}^{w}}{{\rho}_{l}^{w}}$

**Equilibrium law** **dry gas / dissolved:**

Henry's law relates the component $c$ in its gaseous form to its liquid form such that:

${P}_{g}^{h}={K}_{h}\frac{{\rho}_{l}^{h}}{{M}^{h}}$ where ${K}_{h}$ is the Henry coefficient.

Note: this coefficient is often found in the literature in the form $H=\frac{1}{{K}_{h}}$

#### 2.3.3 Diffusion equations

**Darcy's law:**

Darcy's law relates the flux ${F}_{p}$ of the phase $p$ to its pressure gradient, such that:

$\frac{{F}_{\text{p}}}{{\rho}_{p}}=\frac{-k{k}_{\mathrm{rp}}}{{\mu}_{p}}(\nabla {P}_{p}-{\rho}_{p}g)$

with $g$ gravity

**Fick's law:**

The Fickian mass fluxes are written such that:

${J}_{p}^{c}=-\phi {M}^{c}{S}_{p}{D}_{p}{c}_{p}\nabla {X}_{p}^{c}$ where ${D}_{p}$ is the diffusion coefficient (or Fick coefficient) of the phase $p$ .

The Fickian flux of water in the liquid is neglected (concentration of water in the liquid assimilable to 1). Finally, one therefore obtains:

${F}_{l}^{w}=-{\rho}_{l}^{w}{\lambda}_{l}^{H}(\nabla {P}_{l}-{\rho}_{l}g)$

${F}_{l}^{h}=-{\rho}_{l}^{h}{\lambda}_{l}^{H}(\nabla {P}_{l}-{\rho}_{l}g)-\phi {M}^{h}{S}_{l}{c}_{l}{D}_{l}\nabla {X}_{l}^{h}$

${F}_{g}^{w}=-{\rho}_{g}^{w}{\lambda}_{\text{g}}^{H}(\nabla {P}_{g}-{\rho}_{g}g)-\phi {M}^{w}{S}_{g}{c}_{g}{D}_{g}\nabla {X}_{g}^{w}$

${F}_{g}^{h}=-{\rho}_{g}^{h}{\lambda}_{\text{g}}^{H}(\nabla {P}_{g}-{\rho}_{g}g)-\phi {M}^{h}{S}_{g}{c}_{g}{D}_{g}\nabla {X}_{g}^{h}$

## 3 The finite-volume schemes implemented in Code_Aster

We present in this chapter the finite-volume schemes that have been implemented in Code_Aster.

### 3.1 Generalities on finite volumes

The finite-volume method consists in integrating one or several equations over a control volume and then in discretising the fluxes on each edge of these volumes. Unlike finite elements, there is not, strictly speaking, a variational formula.

Take the example of an equation of the form $div(\Theta (X,u,\nabla u))=f(x)$ . Its approximation on a set $\Omega \subset {ℝ}^{n}$ is a piecewise-constant function on a partition of $\Omega$ into control volumes denoted in what follows by $K$ .

The principle of the finite-volume method is to integrate the equation over each of these control volumes. Using Green's formula, the equations are written:

${\int}_{\partial K}\Theta (X,u,\nabla u).{n}_{K}d\gamma ={\int}_{K}f(x)\mathrm{dx},\forall K$

with ${n}_{K}$ the unit outward normal of the control volume $K$ .

One then sees that applying a finite-volume scheme consists in approximating fluxes on boundaries.

For a transient equation of the type:

$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+div(\Theta (X,u,\nabla u))=f(x)$ , one will logically have a formulation of the type

${\int}_{K}u\mathrm{dx}+{\int}_{\partial K}\Theta (X,u,\nabla u).{n}_{K}d\gamma ={\int}_{K}f(x)\mathrm{dx},\forall K$

In summary, a finite-volume scheme is defined by:

- The choice of the control volume $K$, which may be identical to the mesh cell, or else a construction (of Voronoi type for example). The approximate solution given by the finite-volume type schemes will be piecewise constant on this partition.
- The position of the approximation nodes of the unknowns for each volume.
- The manner of approximating the flux on an edge of the control volume: they are approximated by the integral formulation of the equations and the application of Green's formula on the boundary of the control volume. During the approximation, the fluxes must be conservative and consistent. Conservativity is expressed by the continuity of the fluxes at the interface, by definition ensured by the finite volumes (the flux is written explicitly). Consistency is reached when the difference between the real flux and its approximation tends to 0. There exists a very large number of schemes according to the way of computing this flux and the consistency required.

The writing of the flux can therefore relate nodes that do not necessarily belong to the same element. They may be related for example by a common interface. One therefore sees that in this case, two nodes are related, no longer when they belong to the same element as in finite elements, but when they belong to two neighbours (related by a common face).

In order to implement most of these schemes one must therefore have recourse to the topological notion of neighbourhood, which constitutes an important difference with respect to finite elements.

### 3.2 The SUSHI finite volumes applied to miscible two-phase flows

Two-phase flows in a porous medium are governed by the system of equations:

$$
\lbrace \begin{array}{}\dot{{m}_{l}^{w}}+{\dot{m}}_{g}^{w}+\text{Div}({F}_{l}^{w}+{F}_{g}^{w})=0\\ \dot{{m}_{l}^{h}}+{\dot{m}}_{g}^{h}+\text{Div}({F}_{l}^{h}+{F}_{g}^{h})=0\\ {P}_{l}(x,t)=\tilde{{P}_{l}}\mathrm{sur}\partial {\Omega}_{d}\\ {S}_{l}(x,t)=\tilde{{S}_{l}}\mathrm{sur}\partial {\Omega}_{d}\\ [{F}_{l}^{w}+{F}_{g}^{w}].n={\phi }^{w}\mathrm{sur}\partial {\Omega}_{n}\\ [{F}_{l}^{h}+{F}_{g}^{h}].n={\phi }^{h}\mathrm{sur}\partial {\Omega}_{n}\end{array}
$$

It is moreover recalled that the expression of the mass supplies is:

$$
\begin{array}{}{m}_{w}={\rho}_{w}\varphi {S}_{\text{lq}}-{\rho}_{w}^{0}{\varphi}^{0}{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0}\\ {m}_{\text{ad}}={\rho}_{\text{ad}}\varphi {S}_{\text{lq}}-{\rho}_{\text{ad}}^{0}{\varphi}^{0}{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0}\\ {m}_{\text{as}}={\rho}_{\text{as}}\varphi (1-{S}_{\text{lq}})-{\rho}_{\text{as}}^{0}{\varphi}^{0}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0})\\ {m}_{\text{vp}}={\rho}_{\text{vp}}\varphi (1-{S}_{\text{lq}})-{\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{0}{\varphi}^{0}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0})\end{array}
$$

the fluxes are then:

$$
\begin{array}{}{F}_{l}^{w}=-{\rho}_{l}^{w}{\lambda}_{l}^{H}(\nabla {P}_{l}-{\rho}_{l}g)\\ {F}_{l}^{h}=-{\rho}_{l}^{h}{\lambda}_{l}^{H}(\nabla {P}_{l}-{\rho}_{l}g)-\phi {M}^{h}{S}_{l}{c}_{l}{D}_{l}\nabla {X}_{l}^{h}\\ {F}_{g}^{w}=-{\rho}_{g}^{w}{\lambda}_{\text{g}}^{H}(\nabla {P}_{g}-{\rho}_{g}g)-\phi {M}^{w}{S}_{g}{c}_{g}{D}_{g}\nabla {X}_{g}^{w}\\ {F}_{g}^{h}=-{\rho}_{g}^{h}{\lambda}_{\text{g}}^{H}(\nabla {P}_{g}-{\rho}_{g}g)-\phi {M}^{h}{S}_{g}{c}_{g}{D}_{g}\nabla {X}_{g}^{h}\end{array}
$$

#### 3.2.1 Choice of the unknowns

The usual choice of unknowns $({P}_{c},{P}_{g})$ is retained in the mesh cell as well as on the edges of the latter. This choice is moreover compatible with the unified treatment of miscible and immiscible flows in a saturated and unsaturated medium (see [1]).

#### 3.2.2 Choice of the control volume

The control volume corresponds here to the mesh: one element is equal to one control volume.

#### 3.2.3 Writing of the fluxes

The geometric notations used in what follows for an element $K$ (here triangular, but the principle is the same for rectangles or in 3D) are indicated.

In what follows $L$ will denote the triangle neighbouring $K$ separated by the interface $\sigma$ . It is recalled that the notation $∣K∣$ denotes its measure (or area).

We can distinguish different types of flux which we shall subsequently name "mass" fluxes or "volume" fluxes. The mass fluxes are in reality the classical "physical" fluxes, in other words they are the quantities ${\int}_{\partial K}-k{k}_{p}^{c}\nabla [{P}_{p}-{\rho}_{p}\mathrm{g.}x].{n}_{K,\sigma }d\gamma$ for the Darcean terms and ${\int}_{\partial K}-\phi {M}^{c}{S}_{p}{D}_{p}({S}_{p}){c}_{p}\nabla {X}_{p}^{c}.{n}_{K,\sigma }d\gamma$ for the Fickian terms.

On the other hand, the volume fluxes are, for the Darcean terms, the quantities ${\int}_{\partial K}-k\nabla [{P}_{p}-{\rho}_{p}\mathrm{g.}x],{n}_{K,\sigma }d\gamma$ and, for the Fickian terms, the quantities ${\int}_{\partial K}-\nabla {X}_{p}^{c}.{n}_{K,\sigma }d\gamma$ . Concretely, one extracts the terms depending on space (via the saturation) from the computation of the volume flux.

One of the choices made for the extension of the SUSHI scheme to the non-linear two-phase case is to approximate by discrete fluxes the volume fluxes rather than the mass fluxes. This choice has the advantage of being able to lead to a linear equation of continuity of the volume fluxes. However, one must then ensure the continuity of the other discontinuous quantities by other techniques (for example, by upwinding or by averaging). These different approaches are compared in [1].

The discretisation of the volume flux is then denoted such that:

- ${\int}_{\partial K}-k\nabla [{P}_{p}-{\rho}_{p}\mathrm{g.}x],{n}_{K,\sigma }d\gamma$ is discretised by ${\Sigma}_{\sigma \in {\epsilon}_{K}}{F}_{p,K,\sigma }({P}_{p}-{\rho}_{p}\mathrm{g.}x),p\in (l,g)$ (one sums the fluxes for each edge $\sigma$ belonging to the set of the edges of K: ${\epsilon}_{K}$ ).
- ${\int}_{\partial K}-\nabla {X}_{p}^{c}.{n}_{K,\sigma }d\gamma$ is discretised by ${\Sigma}_{\sigma \in {\epsilon}_{K}}{\tilde{F}}_{p,K,\sigma }({X}_{p}^{c}),c\in (h,w),p\in (l,g)$ .

The discretised volume fluxes are therefore expressed in the following manner:

$$
{F}_{p,K,\sigma }({P}_{p}-{\rho}_{g}\mathrm{g.}x)=\sum_{\sigma '\in {\epsilon}_{K}}{C}_{K}^{\sigma ,\sigma '}[{P}_{p,K}-{P}_{p,\sigma '}-{\rho}_{p,K}\mathrm{g.}[{x}_{K}-{x}_{\sigma '}]]
$$

$$
{\tilde{F}}_{p,K,\sigma }({X}_{p}^{c})=\sum_{\sigma '\in {\epsilon}_{K}}{D}_{K}^{\sigma ,\sigma '}[{X}_{p,K}^{c}-{X}_{p,\sigma '}^{c}]
$$

With

$$
{C}_{K}^{\sigma ,\sigma '}=\sum_{{\sigma}^{''}\in {\epsilon}_{K}}{Y}^{{\sigma}^{''},\sigma }.{k}_{K,{\sigma}^{''}}{Y}^{{\sigma}^{''},{\sigma}^{'}}
$$

$$
{D}_{K}^{\sigma ,\sigma '}=\sum_{{\sigma}^{''}\in {\epsilon}_{K}}{Y}^{{\sigma}^{''},\sigma }.\frac{{d}_{K,{\sigma}^{''}}∣{\sigma}^{''}∣}{d}{Y}^{{\sigma}^{''},{\sigma}^{'}}
$$

Where:

$$
{k}_{K,{\sigma}^{''}}=\underset{{C}_{K,\sigma }}{\int}k(x)dx={k}_{K}\frac{{d}_{K,{\sigma}^{''}}∣{\sigma}^{''}∣}{d}
$$

And:

$$
{Y}^{\sigma ,{\sigma}^{'}}=\frac{∣\sigma ∣}{∣K∣}{n}_{K,\sigma }+\frac{{\beta}_{K}}{{d}_{k,\sigma }}[1-\frac{∣\sigma ∣}{∣K∣}{n}_{K,\sigma }.[{x}_{\sigma}-{x}_{K}]]{n}_{k,\sigma }\mathrm{si}\sigma =\sigma '
$$

$$
{Y}^{\sigma ,{\sigma}^{'}}=\frac{∣{\sigma}^{'}∣}{∣K∣}{n}_{K,{\sigma}^{'}}-\frac{{\beta}_{K}}{{d}_{k,\sigma }∣K∣}∣{\sigma}^{'}∣{n}_{K,\sigma }.[{x}_{\sigma}-{x}_{K}]{n}_{k,\sigma }\mathrm{si}\sigma \ne \sigma '
$$

The choice of discretising by the SUSHI finite-volume method only the volume fluxes leads to having to treat in a specific manner the mobilities as well as the Fickian diffusion terms. Indeed, the relative permeabilities as well as the diffusion tensors, present respectively in the mobilities and in the Fickian diffusion terms, are quantities that depend on the saturation and that can be heterogeneous.

We shall present various possible formulations of the discretisation of the equations that govern the flows in a porous medium, resulting from different upwindings of the non-linear quantities.

In what follows one will denote:

- The discretised equation of conservation of water on the element $K$ : ${R}_{K}^{w}$
- The discretised equation of conservation of the gas component on the element $K$ : ${R}_{K}^{h}$
- The discretised equation of continuity of the water flux across the interface $\sigma$ : ${R}_{\sigma}^{w}$
- The discretised equation of continuity of the flux of the gaseous component across the interface $\sigma$ : ${R}_{\sigma}^{h}$

#### 3.2.4 Upwinding

The dominant phenomenon in two-phase flows in a porous medium is Darcean diffusion; we shall therefore focus on the study of the continuity of the mobilities ${\rho}_{p}\frac{{k}_{r,p}}{{\mu}_{p}}$ . The Fickian diffusion terms will be looked at secondarily, solely in order to ensure their continuity once that of the mobilities has been ensured.

When we are in the presence of non-linear terms that depend on space, here the mobilities, we must ensure their continuity in the case of a heterogeneous problem. For this purpose we can upwind them. The upwinding of the mobilities has the objective of ensuring the monotonicity and the stability of the schemes. However, it decreases the precision of the analyses by adding numerical diffusion. There exist numerous upwindings in the literature, the non-linear mobility term ${\rho}_{p}\frac{{k}_{r,p}}{{\mu}_{p}}$ depending on space, being able to be computed by different schemes:

- the upstream scheme, which upwinds this term according to the direction of the flow of the phase in the current mesh cell or in the neighbouring mesh cell through the edge considered,
- the causal scheme, which will upwind this term differently when it is a factor of a pressure term or a factor of a gravity term. The upwinding will nevertheless be done in the current mesh cell or in the neighbouring mesh cell,
- the scheme with variable Péclet number, which makes it possible to decompose the convective part of the flux into a linear combination between the upwinded flux of the upstream scheme and the centred flux. Thus the parameter of this combination can be adjusted locally on each edge according to the diffusion introduced by the capillary pressure.

We shall present below the formulation retained in Code_Aster.

Initially 3 formulations were implemented in Code_Aster: two of them will upwind the mobilities by the upstream scheme, either on the edge of the current mesh cell or on the neighbouring mesh cell through the edge, and the third formulation will use mobilities centred in the current mesh cell.

We have decided to retain a single formulation (for reasons of stability and of computation time): the edge-upwinded formulation.

*Formulation: Edge-Upwinded Finite-Volume scheme (VFDA)*

The interest of this formulation is both to keep the notion of upwinding, while keeping a small stencil of the Jacobian matrix. For this purpose, we continue to write the continuity of the total mass fluxes but we upwind the mobilities on the edges of the current mesh cell. We therefore consider the continuity of the following quantities:

${\int}_{\partial K}\left[-k{k}_{l}^{w}\nabla [{P}_{l}-{\rho}_{l}\mathrm{g.}x]-k{k}_{g}^{w}\nabla [{P}_{g}-{\rho}_{g}\mathrm{g.}x]-\phi {M}^{w}{S}_{g}{D}_{g}{c}_{g}\nabla {X}_{g}^{w}\right].{n}_{K,\sigma }d\gamma$

and ${\int}_{\partial K}\left[-k{k}_{l}^{h}\nabla [{P}_{l}-{\rho}_{l}\mathrm{g.}x]-k{k}_{g}^{h}\nabla [{P}_{g}-{\rho}_{g}\mathrm{g.}x]-\phi {M}^{h}{S}_{g}{D}_{g}{c}_{g}\nabla {X}_{g}^{h}-\phi {M}^{h}{S}_{l}{D}_{l}{c}_{l}\nabla {X}_{l}^{h}\right].{n}_{K,\sigma }d\gamma$

The upwinding is then written:

$$
\begin{array}{}\mathrm{Si}{F}_{p,K,\sigma }\ge 0\mathrm{alors}{k}_{p,K,\sigma }^{c}={k}_{p}^{c}({P}_{p,K})\\ \mathrm{Si}{F}_{p,K,\sigma }<0\mathrm{alors}{k}_{p,K,\sigma }^{c}={k}_{p}^{c}({P}_{p,\sigma })\end{array}
$$

As regards the Fickian diffusion terms, given that molecular diffusion is not the dominant phenomenon, we decide not to apply any particular treatment to them and we shall therefore take their values in the current mesh cell.

Thus we shall be able, in this formulation, not to make the distinction between the internal edges and the boundary edges. A reservation about this formulation could be that in the end we ensure twice the continuity of the Darcean mass fluxes, since they are continuous thanks to the flux-continuity equations but also thanks to their writing in the mass conservation.

Despite this, this formulation seems able to be efficient. Indeed, it does not use the neighbours of the edges, which allows it to have a low solution time. Moreover, it makes it possible to solve heterogeneous problems.

}{c}_{g,L}\tilde{{F}_{g,L,\sigma }}({X}_{g}^{w})+{\phi }_{L}{M}^{h}{S}_{l,L}{D}_{l,L,\sigma }{c}_{l,L}\tilde{{F}_{l,L,\sigma }}({X}_{l}^{w})=0\end{array}
$$

## 4 Implementation in Code_Aster

### 4.2 Computation of the generalised stresses and strains

The finite-volume schemes benefit largely from the structure defined for the finite elements in [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10) and [R7.01.11](doc:r7.01.11).

Thus the generalised stresses at the centre of the element are physically the same as in finite elements, namely:

${m}_{l}^{w},{\mathrm{F}}_{l}^{\mathrm{w}};{m}_{g}^{w},{\mathrm{F}}_{\mathrm{g}}^{\mathrm{w}};{m}_{g}^{h},{\mathrm{F}}_{\mathrm{g}}^{\mathrm{h}};{m}_{l}^{h},{\mathrm{F}}_{l}^{\mathrm{h}}$ as well as the generalised strains: ${p}_{c},\nabla {p}_{c};{p}_{\text{g}},\nabla {p}_{\text{g}}$ .

At the interfaces, on the other hand, the fluxes in fact contain what is necessary for the continuity equation. The latter may be different according to the scheme used (see section 3.2.4).

### 4.3 Integration

As in finite elements, the main integration loop is done by element. On the other hand, within an element one will loop over the nodes (there is no longer a notion of integration points, but only approximation points, which here are the nodes). This scheme having its nodes at the centre as well as on each edge, this makes it possible here implicitly to loop over the interfaces. Finally, the finite-element structure is not modified.

In the end one can summarise the integration of the various equations treated in the following table (written for a quadrangle but easily generalisable):
