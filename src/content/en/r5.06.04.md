# r5.06.04 Time-integration algorithms of the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’GENE’

**Summary:**

This document describes the time-integration schemes which are used to solve, in the space of the modes, linear transient dynamics problems in mechanics, with, for certain schemes, the possible taking into account of localised non-linearities of the type impacts, friction, force-displacement and force-velocity relations, and the possible use of substructuring. The NEWMARK scheme (implicit, restricted here to the linear case), the DEVOGELAERE scheme, and four explicit schemes with an adaptive time step, ADAPT_ORDRE1 and ADAPT_ORDRE2, RUNGE_KUTTA_32 and RUNGE_KUTTA_54, are presented.

## 1 Introduction

The aim of transient dynamic analysis is to determine, as a function of time, the response of a structure, given an external loading or boundary conditions which are functions of time, in cases where the inertia effects cannot be neglected.

In a certain number of physical configurations, a modal or harmonic analysis is not sufficient and a transient analysis must be carried out. This is in particular the case if:

- the history of the phenomenon is important in the study,
- if the external loading is complex (earthquake, multi-component excitations, etc ...),
- if the system is non-linear (plasticity, impacts, friction, etc ...).

The transient analysis methods which can then be used are divided into two broad categories:

- the so-called direct integration methods,
- the Ritz methods, which include among others the recombination of modal projections.

The direct integration methods are so named because no transformation is carried out on the dynamic system after the discretisation by finite elements. They are presented in the document [R5.05.02](doc:r5.05.02), direct integration algorithms of the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’PHYS’.

The Ritz methods, on the contrary, carry out a transformation of the initial dynamic system, by a projection onto a subspace of the initial discretisation space. The solution is then performed on a modified system which, if it is reduced, only gives access to an approximation of the response of the real system.

Time-integration algorithms on a system in generalised coordinates are used to solve dynamic problems in mechanics for linear structures, with the possible taking into account of localised non-linearities such as impacts, friction, and force-displacement and force-velocity relations. Certain algorithms furthermore allow substructuring.

These algorithms are programmed in the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’GENE’ [U4.53.03](doc:u4.53.03).

## 2 Time-integration methods for a dynamic problem

### 2.1 Introduction

It is assumed that the equations governing the dynamic equilibrium of solids have been discretised by finite elements. A discrete system of equations is obtained, which is to be integrated in time. To this end a discretisation $\lbrace {t}_{i},i\in \mathrm{\mathbb{N}}\rbrace$ of the time interval of the study $[0,T]$ is chosen and the equilibrium of the structure is written at the time instants ${t}_{i}$ .

In general these equations take the following form:

$\mathrm{M}.{\ddot{X}}_{t}+\mathrm{C}.{\dot{X}}_{t}+\mathrm{K}.{X}_{t}={\mathrm{R}}_{\text{ext}}(t)+{\mathrm{R}}_{\text{nl}}({X}_{t},{\dot{X}}_{t},{\ddot{X}}_{t})$ **eq**  2.1 **-1**

where

- $\mathrm{M}$ is the mass matrix of the system,
- $\mathrm{K}$ is the stiffness matrix of the system,
- $\mathrm{C}$ is the damping matrix of the system,
- ${\mathrm{R}}_{\text{ext}}(t)$ is the vector of the external forces,
- ${\mathrm{R}}_{\text{nl}}({X}_{t},{\dot{X}}_{t},{\ddot{X}}_{t})$ is the vector of the non-linear forces.

The damping matrix $\mathrm{C}$ is in general difficult to evaluate because the damping is often a function of the frequency. It is, however, common to simplify the taking into account of the damping by employing the proportional damping model, or Rayleigh model.

The Rayleigh-Ritz reduction methods are presented in the document [R5.06.01](doc:r5.06.01), Ritz methods in linear and non-linear dynamics.

In the case where the term ${\mathrm{R}}_{\text{nl}}({X}_{t},{\dot{X}}_{t},{\ddot{X}}_{t})$ is not zero, the pseudo-force technique consists in projecting onto the basis of the linear system and in keeping the non-linear forces on the right-hand side. The pseudo-force technique is always associated with an explicit integration scheme. Consequently the taking into account of the non-linearities is available only for explicit schemes. The addition of the non-linearities does not modify the form of the equations.

In the Ritz method, the displacement field ${X}_{t}$ is replaced by its projection onto the modal basis such that ${X}_{t}=\Phi {\eta}_{t}$ where ${\eta}_{t}$ is the vector of the generalised coordinates and $\Phi$ is the modal basis, most often reduced.

The projected dynamic system takes the following form, with ${\eta}_{t}\in {ℝ}^{p}$ :

${\Phi}^{t}.\mathrm{M}.\Phi .{\ddot{\eta}}_{t}+{\Phi}^{t}.\mathrm{C}.\Phi .{\dot{\eta}}_{t}+{\Phi}^{t}.\mathrm{K}.\Phi .{\eta}_{t}={\Phi}^{t}.{\mathrm{R}}_{\text{ext}}(t)+{\Phi}^{t}.{\mathrm{R}}_{\text{nl}}(\Phi .{\eta}_{t},\Phi .{\dot{\eta}}_{t},\Phi .{\ddot{\eta}}_{t})$ **eq**  2.1 **-2**

When Basile's hypothesis does not apply (non-proportional damping), the projected damping matrix is not diagonal. The integration of the coupled system is then necessarily carried out with one of the following three schemes: the implicit NEWMARK scheme, the explicit EULER scheme or the explicit schemes ADAPT_ORDRE1 and ADAPT_ORDRE2.

The equation obtained in ${\eta}_{t}$ is of the same form as the equation in ${X}_{t}$ . Consequently, in the remainder of the document, the notation ${X}_{t}$ will be used both for the displacement in generalised coordinates and for the displacement in the physical space. In the case of the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’GENE’, these are generalised coordinates.

Two classes of method can be distinguished in the step-by-step integration of the equilibrium equations, the explicit integration methods and the implicit integration methods.

Consider the following linear dynamic system to be integrated in time:

$M.{\ddot{X}}_{t}+C.{\dot{X}}_{t}+K.{X}_{t}={R}_{\text{ext}}(t)$ **eq**  2.1 **-3**

This second-order differential system can be reduced to a first-order system:

$N.{\dot{U}}_{t}=H.{U}_{t}+{F}_{t}$ **eq**  2.1 **-4**

where        ${U}_{t}=(\begin{array}{c}{X}_{t}\\ {\dot{X}}_{t}\end{array})$ , $N=(\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{Id}& 0\\ 0& M\end{array})$ , $H=(\begin{array}{cc}0& \mathrm{Id}\\ -K& -C\end{array})$ , ${F}_{t}=(\begin{array}{c}0\\ {R}_{t}\end{array})$

To integrate this system of differential equations, a discretisation $\left\lbrace {t}_{i},i\in \mathbb{N}\right\rbrace$ is used, as well as a finite-difference formula to express the derivative ${\dot{U}}_{t}$ .

The integration methods in which only the derivative ${\dot{U}}_{t}$ involves unknowns at time ${t}_{i+1}$ will be called explicit integration methods. In this way the determination of the quantities sought at the time instant ${t}_{i+1}$ does not result from an inversion of a system involving the operator $H$ . If, in addition, a “mass-lumping” is carried out so as to make the matrix $M$ diagonal, the determination of ${U}_{t}$ is particularly simple. These are the main characteristics of the explicit integration methods.

The implicit or semi-implicit methods involve the discretisation of ${U}_{t}$ at a time instant later than ${t}_{i}$ , generally ${t}_{i+1}$ . The determination of the variables therefore requires the solution of a system involving the operator $H$ .

Two notions are important: consistency, or the order of the integration scheme, and stability.

The approximations used to obtain the differential operators define the consistency, or the order of the integration scheme. One may indeed consider that the approximation with which the displacement is obtained at each time step is related to the order of approximation of the first and second derivatives with respect to time.

The stability study of a scheme consists in analysing the propagation of the numerical perturbations in the course of time. A stable scheme keeps a finite solution, in spite of the perturbations, whereas an unstable scheme leads to a numerical blow-up or to a divergence of the solution.

To carry out a stability study of an integration scheme, the latter is put in the form of a linear recursive system and the eigencharacteristics of this system are determined. If all the eigenvalues of the recursion operator are smaller than 1 in modulus, the scheme is stable. Otherwise it is unstable.

Explicit integration schemes are generally conditionally stable, which means that the time step must be sufficiently small to ensure the stability of the time integration.

Certain implicit algorithms have the property of being unconditionally stable, which is what makes them of interest and makes it possible to use an arbitrarily large time step.

The schemes retained for the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’GENE’ are one implicit scheme, NEWMARK, and four explicit schemes, EULER, DEVOGELAERE, ADAPT_ORDRE1 and ADAPT_ORDRE2 (with an adaptive time step). The choice is made by the keyword METHODE: ‘EULER’, ‘DEVOGE’, ‘NEWMARK’, ‘ADAPT_ORDRE1’ or ‘ADAPT_ORDRE2’.

### 2.2 Implicit integration methods

#### 2.2.1 Introduction

The implicit methods involve the solution of a matrix system with the previously defined operator. If the solids are assumed to be linear elastic, this results in the solution of a linear system at each time step.

The advantage of these methods is their unconditional stability, which allows them to integrate the equations of dynamics with a relatively large time step while correctly representing the behaviour of the lowest-frequency modes of the structure.

An implicit version of the NEWMARK method, which has been programmed in DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’GENE’ for linear problems.

#### 2.2.2 The Newmark method [bib1]

##### 2.2.2.1 Presentation of the scheme

Newmark [bib1] introduced two parameters $\gamma$ and $\beta$ for the computation of the displacements and velocities at the time step $t+\Delta t$ :

${\dot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}={\dot{X}}_{t}+\Delta t\left[(1-\gamma )\ddot{{X}_{t}}+\gamma {\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}\right]$

${X}_{t+\Delta t}={X}_{t}+\Delta t\dot{{X}_{t}}+\Delta {t}^{2}\left[(\frac{1}{2}-\beta )\ddot{{X}_{t}}+\beta {\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}\right]$

Consider the equilibrium equations at time $t+\Delta t$ :

$M\cdot {\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}+C{\dot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}+K{X}_{t+\Delta t}={R}_{t+\Delta t}$

Substituting the previous relations and eliminating ${\dot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}$  and ${\ddot{X}}_{t+\Delta t}$ , one obtains:

$\tilde{K}\cdot {X}_{t+\Delta t}={\tilde{R}}_{t+\Delta t}$ where $\tilde{K}=K+{a}_{0}M+{a}_{1}C$

$\tilde{R}={R}_{t+\Delta t}+C\cdot \left\lbrace {a}_{1}{X}_{t}+{a}_{4}{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{5}{\ddot{X}}_{t}\right\rbrace +M\cdot \left\lbrace {a}_{0}{X}_{t}+{a}_{2}{\dot{X}}_{t}+{a}_{3}{\ddot{X}}_{t}\right\rbrace$

with: $\begin{array}{cccc}{a}_{0}=\frac{1}{(\beta .\Delta {t}^{2})}& {a}_{1}=\frac{\gamma}{(\beta .\Delta t)}& {a}_{2}=\frac{1}{(\beta .\Delta t)}& {a}_{3}=\frac{1}{2\beta }-1\\ {a}_{4}=\frac{\gamma}{\beta}-1& {a}_{5}=\frac{\Delta t}{2}(\frac{\gamma}{\beta}-2)& {a}_{6}=\Delta t.(1-\gamma )& {a}_{7}=\gamma .\Delta t\end{array}$

##### 2.2.2.2 Complete algorithm of the Newmark method

**a) initialisation**

1. initial conditions ${\mathrm{X}}_{0}$ , $\dot{{\mathrm{X}}_{0}}$ and $\ddot{{\mathrm{X}}_{0}}$
2. choice of $\Delta t$ $\gamma$ and $beta$ and computation of the coefficients ${a}_{\mathrm{1,.}\mathrm{..}},{a}_{8}$ (cf. above)
3. assemble the stiffness matrix $\mathrm{K}$ , the mass matrix $\mathrm{M}$ and the damping matrix $\mathrm{C}$
4. form the effective stiffness matrix $\tilde{\mathrm{K}}=\mathrm{K}+{a}_{0}\mathrm{M}+{a}_{1}\mathrm{C}$
5. factorise $\tilde{\mathrm{K}}$

**b) at each time step**

1. compute the effective loading $\tilde{\mathrm{R}}$ :

$\tilde{\mathrm{R}}={\mathrm{R}}_{t+\Delta t}+\mathrm{C}\cdot \left\lbrace {a}_{1}{\mathrm{X}}_{t}+{a}_{4}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+{a}_{5}{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}\right\rbrace +\mathrm{M}\cdot \left\lbrace {a}_{0}{\mathrm{X}}_{t}+{a}_{2}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+{a}_{3}{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}\right\rbrace$

1. solve $\tilde{\mathrm{K}}\cdot {\mathrm{X}}_{t+\Delta t}={\tilde{\mathrm{R}}}_{t+\Delta t}$
2. compute the velocities and accelerations at time $t+\Delta t$

${\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\Delta t}={a}_{0}\left({\mathrm{X}}_{t+\Delta t}-{\mathrm{X}}_{t}\right)-{a}_{2}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}-{a}_{3}{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}$

${\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\Delta t}={\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+{a}_{6}{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+{a}_{7}{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\Delta t}$

1. computation of the next time step: return to b)1)

##### 2.2.2.3 Stability conditions of the Newmark scheme

The Newmark method is used quite widely in the field of mechanics, because it makes it possible to choose the order of the integration, to introduce or not numerical damping, and it possesses a very good accuracy.

It is unconditionally stable if: $\gamma >0.5$ and $\beta >\frac{{(2\gamma +1)}^{2}}{4}$

A positive numerical damping is introduced if $\gamma >\frac{1}{2}$ and a negative one if $\gamma <\frac{1}{2}$ .

When $\gamma =\frac{1}{2}$ and $\beta =0$ , the Newmark formula reduces to the central-difference scheme. It is then therefore an explicit scheme.

A combination very often employed is $\gamma =\frac{1}{2}$ and $\beta =\frac{1}{4}$ , because it leads to a scheme of order two, unconditionally stable without numerical damping. This is the choice which has been made in the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’GENE’. The Newmark scheme of this operator is therefore implicit.

##### 2.2.2.4 Use

In DYNA_VIBRA/BASE_CALCUL=’GENE’, this scheme allows the integration of non-linear problems. In the framework of dynamic substructuring, it makes it possible to employ a modal basis computed by substructuring but it does not support the direct computation on the modal bases of the substructures.

##### 2.2.2.5 Numerical damping of the implicit schemes

The numerical advantage of the direct implicit integration schemes lies in the fact that the time step can be substantially large relative to the smallest natural period of the system without risking causing an instability of the results.

For modes with a natural period of the order of the time step or smaller than the time step, the integration algorithms introduce a strong damping which contributes to erasing the contribution of these high modes (cf [R5.05.02](doc:r5.05.02)).

There is no numerical damping in the particular case of the NEWMARK algorithm with $\beta =\frac{1}{4}$ and $\gamma =\frac{1}{2}$ .

On the other hand, the implicit algorithms have a noticeable effect of lengthening the periods of the response of the structure. It is observed that, in order to guarantee a good accuracy on the amplitude and the phase of the computed displacements, a criterion close to the following must be respected:

$\Delta t<\frac{0.1}{{F}_{\max}}\text{à}\frac{0.01}{{F}_{\max}}$

where ${F}_{\max}$ is the highest frequency of the motion that one wishes to capture.

#### 2.2.3 TRBDF2 algorithm

##### 2.2.3.1 Presentation

This section deals with the TRBDF2 time-integration scheme, for Trapezoidal Rule (TR) Backward Differentiation Formula Order 2 (BDF2), and with its use in the case of linear and non-linear structural dynamics. In what follows we follow the presentation of [GARDNER2021].

TRBDF2 is a two-step time-integration scheme. It was initially proposed for the numerical simulation of microprocessors [BANK1985], considered in the literature as stiff problems. To illustrate how it works, the canonical form is considered:

$$
y'=f(y,t)
$$

Thus, in order to integrate this equation between $t={t}_{n}$ and $t+\Delta t={t}_{n+1}$ , the following two sequences are chained:

- First of all, in a prediction step, the trapezoidal rule (TR) is used to pass from ${t}_{n}$ to ${t}_{n+\gamma }=t+\gamma \Delta t$ , where $\gamma \in ]0,1[$ . This amounts to solving the following equation where ${f}_{n}=f({y}_{n},{t}_{n})$ :

$$
{y}_{n+\gamma }-\gamma \frac{\Delta t}{2}{f}_{n+\gamma }={y}_{n}+\gamma \frac{\Delta t}{2}{f}_{n}
$$

- The Backward Differentiation Formula of order two (BDF2, also called the three-point implicit Euler scheme) is then used, in a correction step, to pass from $t={t}_{n}$ and $t+\Delta t={t}_{n+1}$ using the previous solution at ${t}_{n+\gamma }$ :

$$
{y}_{n+1}-\frac{1-\gamma }{2-\gamma }\Delta t{f}_{n+1}=\frac{1}{\gamma \left(2-\gamma \right)}{y}_{n+\gamma }-\frac{{\left(1-\gamma \right)}^{2}}{\gamma \left(2-\gamma \right)}{y}_{n}
$$

These steps are represented in  Figure 2.2.3.1-1 .

Figure 2.2.3.1-1 Representation of the TRBDF2 scheme

##### 2.2.3.2 Complete algorithm

**a) initialisation**

1. initial conditions ${X}_{0}$ , $\dot{{X}_{0}}$ and $\ddot{{X}_{0}}$
2. assemble the stiffness matrix $K$ , the mass matrix $M$ and the damping matrix $C$
3. compute the parameters ${a}_{1}=\frac{4}{{\left(\gamma \Delta t\right)}^{2}}$ ${a}_{2}=\frac{2}{\gamma \Delta t}$ ${\gamma}_{1}=\frac{1}{\gamma (2-\gamma )}$ ${\gamma}_{2}=\frac{-{(1-\gamma )}^{2}}{\gamma (2-\gamma )}$ ${\gamma}_{3}=\frac{1-\gamma }{2-\gamma }$ . One chooses $\gamma =2-\sqrt{(2)}$ , which produces the same effective stiffness matrix for the two steps
4. form the effective stiffness matrix $\tilde{\mathrm{K}}=\left(\mathrm{K}+{a}_{2}\mathrm{C}+{a}_{1}\mathrm{M}\right)$
5. factorise $\tilde{K}$

**b) at each time step**

**# 1**st **step by the trapezoidal method** $t\to t+\gamma \Delta t$

1. compute a prediction of the acceleration at time $t+\gamma \Delta t$ ${\tilde{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}={\mathrm{M}}^{-1}\left({F}^{\mathit{ext}}(t+\gamma \Delta t)-\mathrm{C}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}-\mathrm{K}{\mathrm{X}}_{t}\right)$
2. compute the effective loading ${\tilde{\mathrm{R}}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}$ :

${\tilde{\mathrm{R}}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}=\gamma \Delta t\left(\left({a}_{1}\mathrm{M}+{a}_{2}\mathrm{C}\right){\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+\frac{1}{2}{a}_{2}\mathrm{M}\left({\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+{\tilde{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}\right)\right)$

1. solve $\tilde{\mathrm{K}}\cdot \delta {\mathrm{X}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}={\tilde{\mathrm{R}}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}$
2. compute the displacements and velocities at time $t+\gamma \Delta t$

${\mathrm{X}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}={\mathrm{X}}_{t}+\delta {\mathrm{X}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}$

${\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}={\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+2\left(\frac{1}{\gamma \Delta t}\delta {\mathrm{X}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}-{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}\right)$

> **# 2nd step by the BDF2 method** $t\to t+\Delta t$

1. compute a prediction of the acceleration at time $t+\Delta t$ ${\tilde{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}}_{t+\Delta t}={\mathrm{M}}^{-1}\left({F}^{\mathit{ext}}(t+\Delta t)-\mathrm{C}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}-\mathrm{K}{\mathrm{X}}_{t}\right)$
2. compute the effective loading ${\tilde{\mathrm{R}}}_{t+\Delta t}$ :

$\begin{array}{cc}{\tilde{\mathrm{R}}}_{t+\Delta t}& =-{a}_{2}\mathrm{M}\left({\mathrm{X}}_{t}-{\gamma}_{1}{\mathrm{X}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}-{\gamma}_{2}{\mathrm{X}}_{t}-{\gamma}_{3}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}\right)\\ & -\left({a}_{1}\mathrm{M}+{a}_{2}\mathrm{C}\right)\left({\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}-{\gamma}_{1}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}-{\gamma}_{2}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}-{\gamma}_{3}{\tilde{\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}}_{t}\right)\end{array}$

1. solve $\tilde{\mathrm{K}}\cdot \delta {\mathrm{X}}_{t+\Delta t}={\tilde{\mathrm{R}}}_{t+\Delta t}$
2. compute the displacements, velocities and accelerations at time $t+\Delta t$

${\mathrm{X}}_{t+\Delta t}={\mathrm{X}}_{t}+\delta {\mathrm{X}}_{t+\Delta t}$

${\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\Delta t}={\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}+\frac{1}{{\gamma}_{3}\Delta t}\left(\delta {\mathrm{X}}_{t+\Delta t}-{\mathrm{X}}_{t}+{\gamma}_{1}{\mathrm{X}}_{t+\gamma \Delta t}+{\gamma}_{2}{\mathrm{X}}_{t}+{\gamma}_{3}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t}\right)$

${\ddot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\Delta t}={\mathrm{M}}^{-1}\left({F}^{\mathit{ext}}(t+\Delta t)-\mathrm{C}{\dot{\mathrm{X}}}_{t+\Delta t}-\mathrm{K}{\mathrm{X}}_{t+\Delta t}\right)$

1. computation of the next time step: return to b)1)

The details of how these expressions are obtained are in [TARDIEU2021].

##### 2.2.3.3 Stability conditions

The TRBDF2 scheme is of order two, implicit and therefore unconditionally stable, because $\gamma \ge 1/2$ . It introduces a frequency distortion which increases with the frequency. Furthermore, it is A-stable and, $\gamma =2-\sqrt{(2)}$ having been fixed, its spectral radius is zero, and therefore this scheme is L-stable.

### 2.3 Explicit integration methods

#### 2.3.1 Introduction

Several explicit integration methods are presented: the second-order central-difference scheme, a fourth-order Devogelaere-Fu scheme and the adaptive time-step schemes ADAPT and Runge-Kutta. These three methods are available in the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’GENE’. The schemes are presented considering only linear forces. However the taking into account of the non-linear forces is readily deduced from them by the pseudo-force technique.

#### 2.3.2 Devogelaere-Fu method

##### 2.3.2.1 Presentation

To present the Devogelaere-Fu algorithm, abbreviated as DEVOGE in *Code_Aster*, the dynamic problem is put in the form:

$\mathrm{M}.{\ddot{X}}_{t}+\mathrm{C}.{\dot{X}}_{t}=\mathrm{G}\left(t,{X}_{t}\right)$ where the matrix $C$ is assumed diagonal.

The displacements and the velocities are computed as follows:

1. initialisation

${X}_{-\frac{1}{2}}={X}_{0}-\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\dot{X}}_{0}+\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{8}\left({\mathrm{M}}^{-1}.\mathrm{G}\left({t}_{0},{X}_{0}\right)-{\mathrm{M}}^{-1}.\mathrm{C}.{\dot{X}}_{0}\right)$

${\dot{X}}_{-\frac{1}{2}}={\left(4I-\Delta t.{\mathrm{M}}^{-1}.\mathrm{C}\right)}^{-1}\left({\left(4I+\Delta t.{\mathrm{M}}^{-1}.\mathrm{C}\right)}^{}.{\dot{X}}_{0}-\Delta t\left(\mathrm{G}\left({t}_{-\frac{1}{2}},{X}_{-\frac{1}{2}}\right)+\mathrm{G}\left({t}_{0},{X}_{0}\right)\right)\right)$

1. at each time step

${X}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}={X}_{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\dot{X}}_{n}+\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{24}(4{M}^{-1}.G({t}_{n},{X}_{n})-{M}^{-1}.G({t}_{n-\frac{1}{2}},{X}_{n-\frac{1}{2}})-{M}^{-1}.C(4{\dot{X}}_{n}-{\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}))$

${\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}=4{(4I+\Delta t.{M}^{-1}.C)}^{-1}({\dot{X}}_{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{4}(G({t}_{n},{X}_{n})+G({t}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},{X}_{n+\frac{1}{2}})-{M}^{-1}.C.{\dot{X}}_{n}))$

${X}_{n+1}={X}_{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\dot{X}}_{n}+\frac{\Delta {t}_{2}}{6}(4{M}^{-1}.G({t}_{n},{X}_{n})+2{M}^{-1}.G({t}_{n-\frac{1}{2}},{X}_{n-\frac{1}{2}})-{M}^{-1}.C({\dot{X}}_{n}+2{\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}))$

${\dot{X}}_{n+1}=6{\left(6I+\Delta t.{\mathrm{M}}^{-1}.\mathrm{C}\right)}^{-1}.\left({\dot{X}}_{n}+\frac{\Delta t}{6}\left(\mathrm{G}\left({t}_{n+1},{X}_{n+1}\right)+4\mathrm{G}\left({t}_{n+\frac{1}{2}},{X}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right)+\mathrm{G}\left({t}_{n},{X}_{n}\right)-{\mathrm{M}}^{-1}.\mathrm{C}.\left(4{\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}+{\dot{X}}_{n}\right)\right)\right)$

##### 2.3.2.2 Order and stability of the scheme

The scheme is of order four, the approximations in the writing of the time derivatives being in $o\left(\Delta {t}^{4}\right)$ . It therefore possesses an excellent aptitude for the integration of regular solutions. Its interest is, on the other hand, less obvious if the functions to be integrated present discontinuities (impacts, friction, etc.)

It can be shown that, for an undamped linear system, the time step guaranteeing stability is: $\Delta t<\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{{\omega}_{\max}}$ .

##### 2.3.2.3 Use

This method is costly in computation time because it requires twice the evaluation of the internal-force vector $G$ , a particularly heavy operation. Consequently it is little used in mechanics for direct integration. On the other hand it is employed by the CEA [bib2] in the case of systems projected onto a modal basis.

This scheme allows the taking into account of localised non-linearities of impact and friction type.

In the framework of dynamic substructuring, it makes it possible to employ a modal basis computed by substructuring but it does not support the direct computation on the modal bases of the substructures.

#### 2.3.3 Integration schemes with an adaptive time step

##### 2.3.3.1 Introduction: interest of an adaptive time step

Carrying out the time integration of the transient response of a structure in a non-linear phase always poses problems as regards the choice of the time step. The error estimate is rarely accessible during the integration.

The explicit integration schemes oblige one to respect a maximum time step in order not to diverge. In the case of a non-linear behaviour, this step cannot be determined *a priori* and may change at each iteration. When the stiffness varies very strongly, a constant and very fine time step, in order to preserve the stability of the scheme, leads to a very large number of iterations and to a considerable computation time.

Several adaptive time-step integration algorithms have therefore been developed: ADAPT_ORDRE1, ADAPT_ORDRE2, RUNGE_KUTTA_32 and RUNGE_KUTTA54. The first two are based on the central-difference scheme, of order two, and on the Euler scheme, of order one. The last two are schemes of the Runge-Kutta family with error control. In the remainder of this chapter the algorithm of the adaptive scheme of order one will not be presented because it is modelled on the Euler scheme. The management of the adaptation of the time step is, for its part, the same as for the adaptive scheme of order two.

It may be noted that this type of scheme has also been programmed in DYNA_VIBRA/ BASE_CALCUL=’PHYS’ (cf.[R5.05.02](doc:r5.05.02)).

##### 2.3.3.2 The adaptive-step schemes ADAPT_ORDRE1 and ADAPT_ORDRE2

###### 2.3.3.2.1 Central-difference scheme with a constant step

The central-difference scheme with a constant step, on which the ADAPT_ORDRE2 scheme is based, is first presented. It is written as follows:

${\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}={\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\Delta t.{\ddot{X}}_{n}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n})+o(\Delta {t}^{2})$

${X}_{n+1}={X}_{n}+\Delta t{\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}+o(\Delta {t}^{2})$

It is observed that the velocity is expressed at half-integer indices of the time discretisation whereas the displacements and accelerations are expressed at the integer indices. Written in this way the scheme is of order two. However the acceleration is not immediately computable because the velocity is known only at the previous half-step. To circumvent this difficulty, several approximations of the velocity at the integer time step can be used.

- method 1: assume that ${\ddot{X}}_{n}\left({X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n},{t}_{n}\right)\equiv {\ddot{X}}_{x}\left({X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}},{t}_{n}\right)$ which constitutes a valid approximation if the damping is sufficiently weak ${\dot{X}}_{n}={\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+o(1)$ . If the damping is significant, the scheme then loses its second-order accuracy.
- method 2: use a first-order approximation for the velocity: which makes it possible to ${\dot{X}}_{n}={\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\ddot{X}}_{n-1}+o(\Delta t)$ preserve the second order of the scheme.
- method 3: use a predictor/corrector type scheme:

predictor: $\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{\dot{X}}_{{n}^{p}}={\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\gamma \Delta t{\ddot{X}}_{n-1}\\ {\ddot{X}}_{{n}^{p}}=\ddot{X}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{{n}^{p}})\end{array}$

corrector: $\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}{\dot{X}}_{n}={\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}(\beta {\ddot{X}}_{{n}^{p}}+(1-\beta ){\ddot{X}}_{n-1})\\ {\ddot{X}}_{n}=\ddot{X}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n})\end{array}$

where $\alpha$ and $beta$ are two parameters to be chosen. Park and Underwood [bib6] report that carrying out additional iterations does not noticeably improve the stability of the scheme.

###### 2.3.3.2.2 Adaptation of the scheme to a variable time step

When the time step varies, the expressions of the previous paragraph are no longer valid, the acceleration ${\ddot{X}}_{n}$ no longer necessarily being expressed at the centre of the interval $\left[{\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}},{\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\right]$ , as can be seen on the diagram below.

To take this into account, the velocity is computed as follows:

${\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}={\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\Delta {t}_{n-1}+\Delta {t}_{n}}{2}{\ddot{X}}_{n}$

The complete ADAPT_ORDRE2 scheme is then written as follows:

|  | estimation of ${\dot{X}}_{x}$ according to methods 1, 2 or 3 |
| --- | --- |
|  | ${\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}={\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\Delta {t}_{n-1}+\Delta {t}_{n}}{2}{\ddot{X}}_{n}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n})+o(\Delta {t}^{2})$ |
|  | ${X}_{n+1}={X}_{n}+\Delta t{\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}+o(\Delta {t}^{2})$ |

The order of the scheme is no longer rigorously equal to two, the scheme having lost its centred character.

The more $\Delta {t}_{n}$ and $\Delta {t}_{n+1}$ differ, the more the order of the scheme tends towards one. Large variations of the time step therefore lead to a loss of accuracy.

It is possible to find more complex expressions, which use the velocity or the acceleration at the previous iteration [bib7]. However the formula presented here gives satisfactory results when the time step decreases but it lowers the stability limit when the time step increases. The remedy is to regulate the step so that it increases only slowly.

###### 2.3.3.2.3 Stability and accuracy of the scheme

To study the scheme, the analysis was restricted to a free linear system with a single degree of freedom, of natural angular frequency $\omega$ and of reduced damping $\xi$

$\ddot{\xi}+2\omega \xi \dot{\xi}+{\omega}^{2}\xi =0$

The approximate solution, using the constant-step scheme, is obtained by the following recurrence relation:

$\mathrm{A}.{Y}_{n}+\mathrm{B}.{Y}_{n-1}=0$

with ${Y}_{n}=\left[\begin{array}{c}{\ddot{x}}_{n}\\ {\dot{x}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}\\ {x}_{n+1}\end{array}\right]$

$A$ and $B$ are two matrices which depend on the method chosen to compute the contribution of the damping term. A solution of the form ${Y}_{n}=\lambda {Y}_{n-1}$ is sought.

$\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of :math:`{A}^{-1}.B$ and can be written in the following form:

${\lambda}_{c}=\exp({\omega}_{c}\Delta t(-{\xi}_{c}\pm i\sqrt{1-{\xi}_{{c}^{2}}}))$ where ${\omega}_{c}$ and ${\xi}_{c}$ are the angular frequency and the reduced damping computed by the algorithm.

They can be compared with the exact solution, ${\lambda}_{e}=\exp(\omega \Delta t(-\xi \pm i\sqrt{1-{\xi}^{2}}))$ , which makes it possible to evaluate the error on the angular frequency and the error on the damping: $\frac{\mid {\omega}_{c}-\omega \mid }{\omega}$ and . $\frac{\mid {\xi}_{c}-\xi \mid }{\xi}$ .

The properties of the scheme according to the method employed to estimate the velocity at the integer steps have been studied [bib 8] and [bib10]. It was found empirically that method 3 is both more accurate and more stable than methods 1 and 2. The method, at no extra computational cost, makes it possible to increase the order of the scheme and gives in most cases a better accuracy, except in the case of weak damping. It is however less stable than method 1. It is method 2 which was finally retained in the ADAPT scheme. These studies furthermore made it possible to estimate the number of points per period necessary to guarantee a stable integration. 20 is a value which gives a good safety margin. It is the value chosen by default.

###### 2.3.3.2.4 Criteria for the adaptation of the time step

The previous developments make it possible to quantify the errors introduced during the computation of a free linear system. These criteria do not, however, make it possible to adapt the time step. They are indeed delicate to implement in the non-linear cases and do not take into account the variations of the excitation.

Another approach consists in studying the local error introduced by the scheme by means of limited expansions.

The exact solution of a single-degree-of-freedom system satisfies:

$\begin{array}{c}\lbrace  \begin{array}{c}X(t+\frac{\Delta t}{2})=X(t)+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\dot{X}(t)+\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{8}\ddot{X}(t)+\frac{\Delta {t}^{3}}{48}\stackrel{\cdots }{X}(t)+o(\Delta {t}^{3})\\ X(t-\frac{\Delta t}{2})=X(t)-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\dot{X}(t)+\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{8}\ddot{X}(t)-\frac{\Delta {t}^{3}}{48}\stackrel{\cdots }{X}(t)+o(\Delta {t}^{3})\end{array}\\ \mathrm{\Rightarrow }X(t+\frac{\Delta t}{2})=X(t-\frac{\Delta t}{2})+\Delta t\dot{X}(t)+\frac{\Delta {t}^{3}}{24}\stackrel{\cdots }{X}(t)+o(\Delta {t}^{3})\end{array}$

The difference integration formula therefore leads to a truncation error equal to:

${E}_{n}=\frac{\Delta {t}^{3}}{24}\stackrel{\cdots }{X}({t}_{n})\equiv \frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{12}({\ddot{X}}_{n}-{\ddot{X}}_{n-1})$

This error can be normalised in order to obtain a relative error:

${e}_{n}=\frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{12}\frac{\mid {\ddot{X}}_{n}-{\ddot{X}}_{n-1}\mid }{{X}_{n}},{X}_{n}\mathrm{\ne }0$

Park and Underwood [bib7] interpreted this error by defining an “apparent angular frequency”:

${\omega}_{{A}_{n}^{2}}=\frac{{\ddot{X}}_{n}}{{X}_{n}}$

Applied to the central-difference scheme, this definition makes it possible to interpret the relative error ${e}_{n}$ as a variation of the apparent angular frequency:

${e}_{n}\equiv \frac{\Delta {t}^{2}}{12}\mid {\omega}_{{A}_{n}^{2}}-{\omega}_{{A}_{n-1}^{2}}\mid$

Many algorithms use a time-step adaptation criterion based on the truncation error ([bib9], [bib11]). However, in the case of a conditionally stable scheme, this method makes it possible neither to ensure the stability of the integration, nor to guarantee an accuracy for the computation of the transients.

Other methods use an approximation of the instantaneous natural angular frequency of the system [bib12], by means of the mass and stiffness matrices. They have the drawback of not adapting to the external forces and to their fluctuations in frequency.

It is therefore useful to find a criterion which takes both approaches into account. This is why Park and Underwood introduced the notion of “perturbed apparent frequency”:

${f}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{2p}}\sqrt{\mid \frac{{\ddot{X}}_{n}-{\ddot{X}}_{n-1}}{{X}_{n}-{X}_{n-1}}\mid }$

This quantity is interpreted as the “instantaneous” frequency of the system.

In the case of a system with several degrees of freedom, an apparent frequency must be computed for each degree of freedom and the maximum taken. The time step can then be chosen so as to respect a minimum of points per apparent period.

If the denominator of the expression for the apparent frequency tends towards zero, the latter can become very large and no longer have any meaning. This leads to an unjustified refinement when the velocity vanishes. To remedy this a criterion of the following type is added:

$\frac{\mid {X}_{n}-{X}_{n-1}\mid }{\mathrm{Dt}}<{\dot{X}}_{\min}\Rightarrow {f}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{2p}}\sqrt{\mid \frac{{\ddot{X}}_{n}-{\ddot{X}}_{n-1}}{{\dot{X}}_{\min}\mathrm{Dt}}\mid }$

It is an intermediate between the perturbed apparent frequency and the truncation error. The adequate value of ${f}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}=\frac{1}{2\pi }\sqrt{\mid \frac{{\ddot{X}}_{n}-{\ddot{X}}_{n-1}}{{X}_{n}-{X}_{n-1}}\mid }$ is difficult to choose *a priori* and an unsuitable value leads to an artificial decrease of the apparent frequency. In the case of a system with several degrees of freedom this difficulty is circumvented by employing the “neighbouring” degrees of freedom:

${f}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}=\underset{1\le i\le \text{nb}\text{dll}}{\max}(\frac{1}{2\pi }\sqrt{\mid \frac{{\ddot{X}}_{n}^{i}-{\ddot{X}}_{n-1}^{i}}{{b}_{n}^{i}}\mid })$

${b}_{n}^{i}=\max(\underset{\mid i-j\mid \le \text{lb}}{\max}\mid {X}_{n}^{j}-{X}_{\text{n-1}}^{j}\mid ,{\dot{X}}_{\min}\Delta t )$

where $\text{lb}$ denotes for example the bandwidth of the stiffness matrix and where ${\dot{X}}_{\min}$ may be chosen very small.

This method proves very effective in the case where the ${X}_{n}^{i}$ denote physical components (displacements). In the case of a projection onto a modal basis it is not relevant to employ the neighbouring components to compute the apparent frequency. In this case it is better to return to the first criterion and to use one of the following two methods, specified by the keyword VITE_MIN:

- if VITE_MIN=‘NORM’ then it is a variable parameter equal to $\frac{\Vert {\dot{X}}_{n}\Vert }{100}$ $\Vert {\dot{X}}_{n}\Vert =\sqrt{\sum_{1\le i\le \text{nbddl}}{({\dot{X}}_{n}^{i})}^{2}}$ . This method gives good results when the number of degrees of freedom is large and is inapplicable to the single-degree-of-freedom case. It is no longer indicated if the order of magnitude of the velocity is very different from one degree of freedom to another.
- if VITE_MIN=‘MAXI’ then it is a variable parameter, different for each degree of freedom, ${\dot{X}}_{\min}^{j}=\underset{1\le m\le n}{\max}\frac{\mid {\dot{X}}_{m}^{j}\mid }{1001}$ . This method has the advantage of working whatever the number of degrees of freedom of the system but it cannot be used if the order of magnitude of the velocity varies too much in the course of the analysis because, in this case, one would systematically obtain: $\frac{\mid {X}_{n}^{j}-{X}_{n-1}^{j}\mid }{\Delta t }\le {\dot{X}}_{\min}^{j}$ .

###### 2.3.3.2.5 Algorithm of the central-difference scheme with an adaptive step

The rules mentioned above make it possible to fix a desired number of time steps per period of the response as a function of the accuracy wanted, $N$ . It is adjustable by the keyword NB_POIN_PERIODE. The time step $\Delta {t}_{n}$ must then be smaller than $\frac{1}{{\mathit{Nf}}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}}$ . The keyword PAS gives the initial time step, $\Delta {t}_{\text{ini}}$ , and the keyword PAS_MAXI the maximum time step not to be exceeded, $\Delta {t}_{\max}$ . The algorithm is described schematically below:

1. **initialisation:** ${X}_{0}$ **and** ${\dot{X}}_{0}$ **given**

$\Delta {t}_{-1}=0$ , $\Delta {t}_{0}=\Delta {t}_{\text{ini}}$ and ${\ddot{X}}_{0}=\ddot{X}\left({t}_{0},{X}_{0},{\dot{X}}_{0}\right)$

1. **initialisation of** ${\dot{X}}_{\min}$

1. **At each time step**

1. initialisation of the search for the time step: ${N}_{\text{iter}}=0$
2. computation of ${\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}$ then of ${X}_{n+1}$ :

estimation of the velocity (method 2): ${\dot{X}}_{n}={\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\Delta t}{2}{\ddot{X}}_{n-1}$

velocity at the half-step: ${\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}={\dot{X}}_{n-\frac{1}{2}}+\frac{\Delta {t}_{n-1}+\Delta {t}_{n}}{2}{\ddot{X}}_{n}({t}_{n},{X}_{n},{\dot{X}}_{n})$

displacement: ${X}_{n+1}={X}_{n}+\Delta t{\dot{X}}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}$

- computation of the acceleration ${\ddot{X}}_{n+1}$
- computation of the apparent frequency:

$\frac{\mid {X}_{n}-{X}_{n-1}\mid }{\Delta t}\ge {\dot{X}}_{\min}\mathrm{\Rightarrow }{f}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}=\frac{1}{2\pi }\sqrt{\mid \frac{{\ddot{X}}_{n}-{\ddot{X}}_{n-1}}{{X}_{n}-{X}_{n-1}}\mid }$

$\frac{\mid {X}_{n}-{X}_{n-1}\mid }{\Delta t}<{\dot{X}}_{\min}\mathrm{\Rightarrow }{f}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}=\frac{1}{2\pi }\sqrt{\mid \frac{{\ddot{X}}_{n}-{\ddot{X}}_{n-1}}{{\dot{X}}_{\min}\Delta t}\mid }$

1. verification of the adequacy between the time step and the apparent frequency:

computation of the indicator $\text{err}=\Delta {t}_{n}{\mathit{Nf}}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}$

- if $\text{err}\ge 1$ and ${N}_{\text{itez}}<{{N}_{\text{iter}}}_{\max}$ then reduction of the time step and a new iteration of the search for the step: $\Delta {t}_{n}\leftarrow 0,75\Delta {t}_{n}$ , ${N}_{\text{iter}}\leftarrow {N}_{\text{iter}}+1$ , return to 2)
- if $\text{err}\le 0,75$ for more than 5 consecutive time steps, then increase of the time step ${N}_{\text{iter}}\leftarrow {N}_{\text{iter}}+1$
- archiving of the solution, possible computation of ${\dot{X}}_{\min}$ and return to 1) for the next iteration.

###### 2.3.3.2.6 Comments on the parameters of the algorithm

Fixing an upper bound ${{N}_{\text{iter}}}_{\max}$ by the keyword NMAX_ITER_PAS on the number of reductions of the time step makes it possible to ensure the convergence of the algorithm in the difficult cases (for example in the case of a discontinuity in the external forces).

When the indicator $\text{err}$ is greater than 1, the time step is multiplied by a fixed factor (0.75 by default but it can be modified by the user by means of the operand COEF_DIVI_PAS). It would have been possible to write directly: $\Delta {t}_{n}\to \frac{1}{\text{err}}\Delta {t}_{n}={\mathit{Nf}}_{{\text{AP}}_{n}}$ , which is more intuitive. But this strategy leads to an excessive refinement, the computed apparent frequency often being largely greater than the real frequency, when the error is large. However, in a single time step, $\Delta {t}_{n}$ can be considerably reduced (factor ${0,75}^{{N}_{\mathit{iter}}}$ ).

On the other hand the increase of the time step is always much slower (default multiplication coefficient of 1.1, definable by COEF_MULT_PAS) and takes place only if the indicator is lower than 1 for five consecutive time steps. These restrictions are justified by the risks of loss of stability or of accuracy of the scheme when the time step varies too quickly. A coefficient of 1.2 or 1.3 can allow a faster computation but sometimes exposes one to risks of error.

In summary the default values have been validated by numerous tests and in general give satisfaction in terms of accuracy and stability [bib8].

###### 2.3.3.2.7 Performance of the algorithm

At equal accuracy, the number of iterations carried out by an adaptive scheme is at least five times smaller than with a constant step for the phenomena which justify the use of a variable step by the irregular aspect of their evolution (impacts, discontinuous excitations, etc.).

The empirical studies have shown that the adaptive time step makes it possible, in the favourable cases, to gain a factor of two or three in computation time. This scheme furthermore makes it possible to control the accuracy of the integration by means of the control of the number of points per period of the response.

In the case of very strongly damped systems, the gains can be even larger (analyses five to ten times faster).

On the other hand, when the “ideal” time step is roughly constant, the use of the adaptive scheme proves useless.

It of course allows the taking into account of localised non-linearities of impact or friction type.

In dynamic substructuring, it is compatible both with the transient analysis on the modal basis restored onto the whole system and with the transient computation on the distinct bases of the substructures.

##### 2.3.3.3 Adaptive time-step schemes of the Runge-Kutta family

The explicit one-step integration methods of Runge-Kutta type seek to determine an approximate solution of the following Cauchy problem:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\dot{y}(t)=f(t,y(t))t\in [{t}_{0,}T]\\ y({t}_{0})={y}_{0}{y}_{0}\in ℝ\end{array}$

To this end, the interval $[{t}_{0}:T]:{t}_{0}<{t}_{1}<{t}_{2}<\cdots <{t}_{N}$ is subdivided and one sets $\Delta t={t}_{n+1}-{t}_{n}$ . At each time instant of the interval the solution of the Cauchy problem is given by the integral expression:

$y({t}_{n+1})=y({t}_{n})+{\int}_{{t}_{n}}^{{t}_{n}+\Delta t}f(t,y(t))$

The approximate solution ${y}_{n}\approx y({t}_{n})$ proposed by the recursive Runge-Kutta schemes takes the form:

${y}_{n+1}={y}_{n}+\Delta t\Psi ({t}_{n},{y}_{n},\Delta t)$

with:

$\Psi :[{t}_{0,}T]\times {ℝ}^{d}\times ℝ\to {ℝ}^{d}$

where the term $\Delta t\Psi ({t}_{n},{y}_{n},\Delta t)$ is readily identified as the approximation of the integral term ${\int}_{{t}_{n}}^{{t}_{n}+\Delta t}f(t,y(t))$ .

Thus, a Runge-Kutta method with $s$ stages making it possible to approximate the solution of the Cauchy problem is given by the following quadrature method:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{k}_{1}=f({t}_{n},{y}_{n})\\ {k}_{2}=f({t}_{n}+{c}_{2}\cdot \Delta t,{y}_{n}+{a}_{21}\cdot {k}_{1})\\ ⋮\\ {k}_{s}=f({t}_{n}+{c}_{s}\cdot \Delta t,{y}_{n}+{a}_{s1}\cdot {k}_{1}+{a}_{s2}\cdot {k}_{2}+\cdots +{a}_{s\phantom{\rule{2em}{0ex}}s-1}\cdot {k}_{s-1})\\ {y}_{n+1}={y}_{n}+\Delta t\cdot ({b}_{1}\cdot {k}_{1}+{b}_{2}\cdot {k}_{2}+\cdots +{b}_{s}\cdot {k}_{s})\end{array}$

A Runge-Kutta integration scheme is therefore entirely defined by the coefficients ${b}_{i}$ , ${c}_{i}$ and ${a}_{ij}$ with, moreover, ${c}_{i}={\sum}_{j}{a}_{ij}$ . In the case of an explicit Runge-Kutta scheme, the coefficients must also satisfy the condition ${a}_{ij}=0\forall j\ge i$ .

In practice, the set of the coefficients of a Runge-Kutta scheme is presented in the form of a Butcher tableau, as the figure below illustrates:

| $\begin{array}{c}{c}_{1}\\ {c}_{2}\\ {c}_{3}\\ ⋮\\ {c}_{s}\end{array}$ | $\begin{array}{cccc}& & & \\ {a}_{21}& & & \\ {a}_{31}& {a}_{32}& & \\ \cdots & \cdots & \ddots & \\ {a}_{\mathit{s1}}& {a}_{\mathit{s2}}& \cdots & {a}_{ss-1}\end{array}$ |
| --- | --- |
| ${y}_{n+1}$ | $\begin{array}{cccc}{b}_{1}& {b}_{2}& \cdots & {b}_{s}\end{array}$ |

Furthermore, a method will be said to be of order $p$ if $\forall n,0\le n\le N,{e}_{n}=\mathrm{{\rm O}}(\Delta {t}^{p+1}),\Delta t\mathrm{\to }0$

###### 2.3.3.3.1 Embedded Runge-Kutta schemes for the control of the adaptive time step

During the numerical solution of the Cauchy problem described above, the choice of the time step $\Delta t$ is decisive for the order of magnitude of the global error. Thus, the control of the error committed ${e}_{n}$ during the computation of the solution ${y}_{n}\approx y({t}_{n})$ makes it possible to determine a so-called “optimal” choice of time step so as to guarantee an upper bound on the error by a tolerance supplied by the user.

To this end, a classical procedure consists in employing two so-called embedded Runge-Kutta methods. The first method, of order $p$ with $s$ stages, serves to compute the approximate solution ${y}_{n+1}$ , whereas the second method, of order $\widehat{p}<p$ , serves to estimate the error ${e}_{n}=\Vert {y}_{n+1}-{\widehat{y}}_{n+1}\Vert$ for the control of the time step. In general, one has $\widehat{p}=p-1$ and the method is denoted $\mathit{RK}\phantom{\rule{0.5em}{0ex}}p(\widehat{p})$ .

The advantage of this approach is that the lower-order approximation $\widehat{p}$ uses the same evaluations of $f$ and therefore the same coefficients ${a}_{ij}$ .

Within the framework of the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’GENE’, two explicit integration schemes of the Runge-Kutta family are available:

1. The ’RUNGE_KUTTA_32’ scheme: this is the Bogacki-Shampine 3(2) scheme. This scheme is of order three with 4 stages. It also includes an approximation of order two allowing the error to be controlled. Although there are 4 stages, it actually uses 3 stages because it has the FSAL (First Same As Last) property. The associated Butcher tableau is presented below:

| $\begin{array}{c}0\\ 1/2\\ 3/4\\ 1\end{array}$ | $\begin{array}{ccc}& & \\ 1/2& & \\ 0& 3/4& \\ 2/9& 1/3& 4/9\end{array}$ |
| --- | --- |
| ${y}_{n+1}$ | $\begin{array}{cccc}2/9& 1/3& 4/9& 0\end{array}$ |
| ${\widehat{y}}_{n+1}$ | $\begin{array}{cccc}7/24& 1/4& 1/3& 1/8\end{array}$ |

1. The ’RUNGE_KUTTA_54’ scheme: this is the Dormand-Prince scheme, also known under the name DOPRI5(4). This scheme is of order 5 with 7 stages. With an approximation of order 4 allowing the error to be controlled. Like the previous scheme it has the FSAL property and it actually uses only 6 stages. The associated Butcher tableau is presented below:

| c0 | 0 |  |  |  |  |  |  |  |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| c1 | 1/5 | 1/5 |  |  |  |  |  |  |
| c2 | 3/10 | 3/40 | 9/40 |  |  |  |  |  |
| c3 | 4/5 | 44/45 | -56/15 | 32/9 |  |  |  |  |
| c4 | 8/9 | 19372/6561 | −25360/2187 | 64448/6561 | −212/729 |  |  |  |
| c5 | 1 | 9017/3168 | −355/33 | 46732/5247 | 49/176 | −5103/18656 |  |  |
| c6 | 1 | 35/384 | 0 | 500/1113 | 125/192 | −2187/6784 | 11/84 |  |
| b | ${\widehat{y}}_{n+1}$ | 5179/57600 | 0 | 7571/16695 | 393/640 | −92097/339200 | 187/2100 | 1/40 |
| d | ${y}_{n+1}$ | 35/384 | 0 | 500/1113 | 125/192 | −2187/6784 | 11/84 | 0 |

These two schemes programmed in the operator DYNA_VIBRA / BASE_CALCUL=’GENE’ consider a state vector ${y}_{n}$ as being the concatenation of the displacement vector and of the velocities, namely:

${y}_{n}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{X}_{n}\\ {\dot{X}}_{n}\end{array}\right\rbrace$

Furthermore, the norm used for the control of the relative error is given by [bib14]

$\mathit{err}=\frac{1}{d}{\sum}_{k=1}^{d}\sqrt{{(\frac{{y}_{n+1}^{k}-{\hat{y}}_{n+1}^{k}}{{\mathit{sc}}^{k}})}^{2}}$

where $d$ is the dimension of the state vector $y$ , ${y}_{n+1}^{k}$ and ${\hat{y}}_{n+1}^{k}$ the $k$-th components of the vectors ${y}_{n+1}$ and ${\hat{y}}_{n+1}$ respectively. Finally, ${\mathit{sc}}^{k}$ is given by:

${\mathit{sc}}^{k}=\mathit{MAX}(\mid {y}_{n}^{k}\mid ,\mid {y}_{n+1}^{k}\mid )+\alpha$

where $\alpha$ is a regularisation parameter (default value $0,001$ ). Thus, the algorithm controls the upper bound of the relative error by the expression $\mathit{err}\le \mathit{tol}$ where $\mathit{tol}$ is a relative tolerance given by the user.

Finally, the expression for the optimal time step as a function of the error committed is:

$\Delta {t}_{\mathit{opt}}=0,9\cdot \Delta {t}_{n}\cdot {\left(\frac{\mathit{tol}}{\mathit{err}}\right)}^{\frac{1}{p+1}}$

with $p=5$ for the ’RUNGE_KUTTA_54’ scheme and $p=3$ for the ’RUNGE_KUTTA_32’ scheme. For a better understanding, the two algorithms are presented in the table below.

1. **Initialisation:** ${X}_{0}$ **,** ${\dot{X}}_{0}$ **,** ${t}_{0}$ **and** $\Delta {t}_{0}$ **given:**

$n=0$ , $\Delta t=\Delta {t}_{0}$ and ${\ddot{X}}_{0}=\ddot{X}({t}_{0},{X}_{0},{\dot{X}}_{0})$

1. **As long as the condition** ${t}_{n}<T$ **is satisfied**

1. Computation of the next state ${y}_{n+1}$ and of the relative error $\mathit{err}$ by a Runge-Kutta method
2. If the condition $\mathit{err}\le \mathit{tol}$ is satisfied (the time step is accepted):

- Archiving of ${X}_{0}$ , ${\dot{X}}_{0}$ and ${\ddot{X}}_{0}$
- Passage to the next state:

  - ${y}_{n}:={y}_{n+1}$
  - ${t}_{n}:={t}_{n}+\Delta t$
  - $n:=n+1$

1. Computation of $\Delta {t}_{\mathit{opt}}$ under the constraint $0,2\cdot \Delta {t}_{n}\le \Delta {t}_{\mathit{opt}}\le 5\cdot \Delta {t}_{n}$ (in order to avoid abrupt changes)
2. Selection of the time step $\Delta t:=min(\Delta {t}_{\mathit{opt}},{t}_{n}-T)$

Several things may be noted, particular to the ’RUNGE_KUTTA_54’ method:

- stages 5 and 6 carry out the computation at $t+\mathit{dt}$ .
- the values of $d$ correspond to the coefficients of the 6th stage of the method.
- the error is computed by means of $(b-d)\chi$ , and the step is adapted as needed. One can therefore take either $b$ or $d$ to compute the solution at $t+\mathit{dt}$ .
- $d$ makes it possible to compute the solution to order four with an error control. The evaluation carried out at the 6th stage of the method is therefore kept for reasons of CPU performance. There is no longer any need to use either $b$ or $d$ to evaluate the solution at $t+\mathit{dt}$ .

For reasons of CPU performance and of numerical error, $b-d$ is computed in rational form in the programming, for the evaluation of the error. The coefficients $b$ and $d$ are therefore no longer used when this method is programmed.

## 3 Conclusion

By way of conclusion, the different possibilities of time integration offered by the operator are summarised here:

- Euler (‘EULER’), explicit, modified so as to ensure a conditional stability,
- Newmark scheme (‘NEWMARK’), parametrised so as to be implicit,
- Devogelaere-Fu scheme (‘DEVOGE’), of order 4,
- Four explicit adaptive schemes:
- ‘ADAPT_ORDRE1’, based on the Euler scheme,
- ‘ADAPT_ORDRE2’, based on the central-difference scheme,
- two schemes of the Runge-Kutta family: ’RUNGE_KUTTA_32’ and RUNGE_KUTTA_54’.

The default scheme is EULER but it is not systematically the most suitable. The NEWMARK scheme available in *Code_Aster* is implicit and guarantees an unconditional stability but is usable only for purely linear problems.

The DEVOGE scheme is of order 4 and is therefore more accurate but it is costly in computation time.

The adaptive schemes are more particularly indicated for problems with localised non-linearities, where the “ideal” time step is not constant in the course of the transient. It is therefore the experience of modelling which makes it possible to choose the scheme best suited to the problem as a function of the (computation time)/accuracy ratio.
