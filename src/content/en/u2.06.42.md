# u2.06.42 Implementation of a modal updating procedure

**Summary:**

In this document, modal updating techniques are presented. Modal updating consists in exploiting the eigenmodes of the structure in order to obtain a numerical model which reflects as well as possible the dynamic behaviour of the structure under study.

## 1 Introduction

Modal updating consists in adjusting the parameters of the numerical model from the knowledge of the eigenmodes identified experimentally on the real structure.

Using optimisation techniques, one tries to find the numerical model for which the associated eigenmodes are close to the eigenmodes identified experimentally.

Three modal updating techniques are presented in this document. The first two are dedicated to the conservative system and the third is dedicated to the dissipative system.

## 2 Modal updating of a conservative system

Two modal updating techniques are presented. The first exploits the sensitivity of the eigenmodes and the second exploits the diagonality of the generalised matrices.

### 2.1 Exploitation of the discrepancy between modal shapes and the discrepancy between natural frequencies

One tries to find the parameters of the numerical model such that the computed modal shape restricted to the observation points is collinear with the modal shape obtained experimentally and such that the associated natural frequency is equal to the natural frequency identified experimentally.

To this end, one computes the normalised scalar product between the measured eigenmode shape and the computed eigenmode shape restricted to the degrees of freedom associated with the measurement points. This computation corresponds to the computation of the MAC (Modal Assurance Criterion).

If one denotes respectively by $({y}_{{i}_{\mathit{num}}},{f}_{{i}_{\mathit{num}}})$ and $({y}_{{i}_{\mathit{mes}}},{f}_{{i}_{\mathit{mes}}})$ the computed eigenmodes and the identified eigenmodes, one has:

> $\mathit{MAC}({y}_{{i}_{\mathit{num}}},{y}_{{i}_{\mathit{mes}}})=\frac{{({y}_{{i}_{\mathit{num}}}^{T}{y}_{{i}_{\mathit{mes}}})}^{2}}{({y}_{{i}_{\mathit{num}}}^{T}{y}_{{i}_{\mathit{num}}})({y}_{{i}_{\mathit{mes}}}^{T}{y}_{{i}_{\mathit{mes}}})}$

One defines the vectors $V$ and ${F}_{r}$ such that:

> $V=\left(\begin{array}{c}⋮\\ \mathit{MAC}({y}_{{i}_{\mathit{num}}},{y}_{{i}_{\mathit{mes}}})-1\\ ⋮\end{array}\right)$
> ${F}_{r}=\left(\begin{array}{c}⋮\\ {f}_{{i}_{\mathit{num}}}-{f}_{{i}_{\mathit{mes}}}\\ ⋮\end{array}\right)$

The functional to be minimised is formulated as follows:

> $ϵ={V}^{T}{W}_{\mathit{MAC}}V+{F}_{r}^{T}{W}_{\mathit{freq}}{F}_{r}$

One must of course evaluate the discrepancy between two analogous modes. Thus, this technique is not suited to a structure in which the modal density is high.

This procedure is used by MACR_RECAL (option DYNAMIQUE) in the test case sdls121a [V2.03.121](doc:v2.03.121).

### 2.2 Exploitation of the orthogonality of the measured eigenmodes

From the modal shapes recorded at the observation points, one carries out an expansion onto the support numerical model. One then tries to recover the parameters of the numerical model so that the generalised mass and stiffness matrices relative to the modes identified experimentally are diagonal and so that the discrepancy between the measured natural circular frequency and the computed natural circular frequency is minimal.

One estimates the natural circular frequency (or more exactly the square of the circular frequency) by computing the ratio between the generalised stiffness and the generalised mass of the identified mode.

This technique requires neither a pairing between the experimental mode and the numerical mode, nor a modal analysis. It is therefore suited to structures in which the modal density is high. It nevertheless requires the expansion of the identified modes onto the numerical model.

The expansion of the i-th identified mode onto the numerical model can be done in the following manner:

- One chooses an expansion basis composed of modal shapes computed with the support numerical model:

> $Y=[{y}_{1}\dots {y}_{n}]$

- One then computes the coordinates ${\eta}_{i}$ of the identified modal shape ${{\Phi}_{i}}^{\mathit{mes}}$ on the basis $Y$ , restricted to the observation points. These coordinates can be obtained by a least-squares type minimisation.

> $\epsilon ={\left({{\Phi}_{i}}^{\mathit{mes}}-Y{\eta}_{i}\right)}^{T}{W}_{i}\left({{\Phi}_{i}}^{\mathit{mes}}-Y{\eta}_{i}\right)$

- One then carries out an expansion of the identified shape onto the degrees of freedom of the numerical model: ${\Phi}_{i}=Y{\eta}_{i}$

The following step consists in computing the normalised generalised matrices:

> ${\mathit{MAC}}_{W}(i,j)=\frac{{({{\Phi}_{i}}^{T}W{\Phi}_{j})}^{2}}{({{\Phi}_{i}}^{T}W{\Phi}_{i})({{\Phi}_{j}}^{T}W{\Phi}_{j})}$

If the weighting matrix $W$ is equal to the mass matrix $M$ or to the stiffness matrix $K$ , ${\mathit{MAC}}_{W}$ becomes a diagonal matrix.

It is then a matter of finding the terms of the matrices $K$ and $M$ which minimise at the same time:

> ${\mathit{MAC}}_{K}(i,j)$ for $i\ne j$
> ${\mathit{MAC}}_{M}(i,j)$ for $i\ne j$
> Discrepancy between the i-th identified natural circular frequency ${\widehat{{\omega}_{i}}}^{2}$ and ${{\omega}_{i}}^{2}=\frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}K{\Phi}_{i}}{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}M{\Phi}_{i}}$

The matrix ${\mathit{MAC}}_{W}$ is symmetric; one can store its lower triangular part in a vector named ${\mathit{MAC}}_{W(i<j)}$ . The functional to be minimised can then be formulated as follows:

$\epsilon ={\mathit{MAC}}_{K(i<j)}^{T}{W}_{K}{\mathit{MAC}}_{K(i<j)}+{\mathit{MAC}}_{M(i<j)}^{T}{W}_{M}{\mathit{MAC}}_{M(i<j)}+\sum_{i}{\left({\widehat{{\omega}_{i}}}^{2}-\frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}K{\Phi}_{i}}{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}M{\Phi}_{i}}\right)}^{T}{W}_{i}\left({\widehat{{\omega}_{i}}}^{2}-\frac{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}K{\Phi}_{i}}{{\Phi}_{i}^{T}M{\Phi}_{i}}\right)$

One can choose the following weighting matrices:

> ${W}_{K}$ = nb_modes_identifiés * ${I}_{d}$
> ${W}_{M}$ = nb_modes_identifiés * ${I}_{d}$
> ${W}_{i}$ = 0.5 * nb_modes_identifiés * (nb_modes_identifiés – 1) * ${I}_{d}$

Where ${I}_{d}$ is the identity matrix

This choice of weighting makes it possible to assign the same weight to the equations on the frequencies and to the equations on the off-diagonal terms of the generalised matrices.

The implementation of this modal updating approach is illustrated in modelling d of the test case sdls121 [V2.03.121](doc:v2.03.121).

## 3 Modal updating of a dissipative system

In the case of a dissipative system, one exploits the norm relation of the complex modal shapes.

The modal updating used here consists in finding the parameters of the model such that the eigenmodes identified experimentally satisfy the norm relations associated with the numerical model.

The dissipative structure is modelled as follows:

$M\ddot{y}+B\dot{y}+\mathit{Ky}=0$

Where:     $M$  denotes the mass matrix

> $B$  denotes the damping matrix
> $K$  denotes the stiffness matrix
> $y$  denotes the displacement

One makes the assumption that the matrices of the system are symmetric.

In the majority of cases, the modal matrix of the conservative system associated with this dissipative system does not simultaneously diagonalise the three matrices $M$ , $B$ and $K$ . One then reduces the problem to a first-order differential system in the space of dimension 2N.

One introduces the state vector: $x=\left[\begin{array}{c}y\\ \dot{y}\end{array}\right]$

One transforms the N second-order equations into 2N first-order equations in the following manner:

> $\left[\begin{array}{cc}B& M\\ M& 0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}\dot{y}\\ \ddot{y}\end{array}\right]-\left[\begin{array}{cc}-K& 0\\ 0& M\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{c}y\\ \dot{y}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{c}0\\ 0\end{array}\right]$

Which one can write: $U\dot{x}-\mathit{Ax}=0$

With this transformation, the eigensolutions are of the form: ${x}_{\nu}=\left[\begin{array}{c}{y}_{\nu}\\ {s}_{\nu}{y}_{\nu}\end{array}\right]$

Where ${s}_{\nu}$ corresponds to the eigenvalue associated with ${x}_{\nu}$ .

The eigenvalues may be real or complex. If the structure is lightly damped, all the eigenvalues are complex conjugates.

The spectral matrix is then put in the following form:    $\underset{(\mathrm{2N},2N)}{S}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{S}_{2}& 0\\ 0& \bar{{S}_{2}}\end{array}\right]$

And the associated modal matrix: $\underset{(\mathrm{2N},2N)}{X}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}Y& \bar{Y}\\ {\mathit{YS}}_{2}& \bar{{\mathit{YS}}_{2}}\end{array}\right]$

With: $\underset{(N,N)}{Y}=\left[{y}_{\nu}\right]$

The modal matrix $X$ satisfies the following orthonormality relations: $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{X}^{T}\mathit{UX}={N}_{0}\\ {X}^{T}\mathit{AX}={N}_{0}S\end{array}$

Where ${N}_{0}$ is a diagonal matrix which defines the norm of $X$ : ${N}_{0}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{N}_{2}& 0\\ 0& \bar{{N}_{2}}\end{array}\right]$

Taking account of the partitioning into sub-matrices, the expansion of the first line of the orthonormality relations is written as follows:

> $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{Y}^{T}\mathit{BY}+{S}_{2}{Y}^{T}\mathit{MY}+{Y}^{T}{\mathit{MYS}}_{2}={N}_{2}\\ {Y}^{T}B\bar{Y}+{S}_{2}{Y}^{T}M\bar{Y}+{Y}^{T}M\bar{{\mathit{YS}}_{2}}=0\end{array}$                 (1)

and:    $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{S}_{2}{Y}^{T}{\mathit{MYS}}_{2}-{Y}^{T}\mathit{KY}={N}_{2}{S}_{2}\\ {S}_{2}{Y}^{T}M\bar{{\mathit{YS}}_{2}}-{Y}^{T}K\bar{Y}=0\end{array}$                         (2)

The diagonal terms of the first line of the systems of equations (1) and (2) lead to the following relations:

> $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{y}_{\nu}^{T}B{y}_{\nu}+2{s}_{\nu}{y}_{\nu}^{T}M{y}_{\nu}={n}_{\nu}\\ {s}_{\nu}^{2}{y}_{\nu}^{T}M{y}_{\nu}-{y}_{\nu}^{T}K{y}_{\nu}={s}_{\nu}{n}_{\nu}\end{array}$

The combination of these two equations leads to:

> ${s}_{\nu}^{2}{y}_{\nu}^{T}{\mathit{My}}_{\nu}+{s}_{\nu}{y}_{\nu}^{T}{\mathit{By}}_{\nu}+{y}_{\nu}^{T}{\mathit{Ky}}_{\nu}=0$                     (3)

Likewise, the diagonal terms of the second line of the two systems of equations (1) and (2) lead to the following equations:

> ${y}_{\nu}^{T}B\bar{{y}_{\nu}}+2\Re ({s}_{\nu}){y}_{\nu}^{T}M\bar{{y}_{\nu}}=0$                         (4)
> ${s}_{\nu}\bar{{s}_{\nu}}{y}_{\nu}^{T}M\bar{{y}_{\nu}}-{y}_{\nu}^{T}K\bar{{y}_{\nu}}=0$                         (5)

These three equations (3) (4) and (5) must also be satisfied for all the eigenmodes identified on the real structure. The updating technique presented here consists in finding the parameters of the numerical model which make it possible to satisfy the three equations.

Experimentally, the modal shape is measured only in the observation directions (sensitive directions of the sensors). An expansion of this shape onto the numerical model is necessary in order to obtain ${y}_{\nu}$ .

One agrees to bring the various equations associated with each mode to the same dimension. One then arrives at the following system of equations:

> $\frac{{y}_{\nu}^{T}B\bar{{y}_{\nu}}+2\Re ({s}_{\nu}){y}_{\nu}^{T}M\bar{{y}_{\nu}}}{\mid {n}_{\nu}\mid }={z}_{1\nu }$
> $\frac{{s}_{\nu}\bar{{s}_{\nu}}{y}_{\nu}^{T}M\bar{{y}_{\nu}}-{y}_{\nu}^{T}K\bar{{y}_{\nu}}}{\mid {n}_{\nu}{s}_{\nu}\mid }={z}_{2\nu }$
> $\frac{{s}_{\nu}^{2}{y}_{\nu}^{T}{\mathit{My}}_{\nu}+{s}_{\nu}{y}_{\nu}^{T}{\mathit{By}}_{\nu}+{y}_{\nu}^{T}{\mathit{Ky}}_{\nu}}{{n}_{\nu}{s}_{\nu}}={z}_{3\nu }$

For convenience, one chooses ${n}_{\nu}$ equal to the Euclidean norm of ${y}_{\nu}$ .

One then stores these quantities in the vectors ${Z}_{1}$ , ${Z}_{2}$ and ${Z}_{3}$ in order to be able to define the general form of the functional $\epsilon$ to be minimised.

> ${Z}_{1}=\left[\begin{array}{c}⋮\\ {z}_{1\nu }\\ ⋮\end{array}\right]={Z}_{\mathrm{1r}}$
> ${Z}_{2}=\left[\begin{array}{c}⋮\\ {z}_{2\nu }\\ ⋮\end{array}\right]={Z}_{\mathrm{2r}}$
> ${Z}_{3}=\left[\begin{array}{c}⋮\\ {z}_{3\nu }\\ ⋮\end{array}\right]={Z}_{\mathrm{3r}}+j{Z}_{\mathrm{3i}}$
> $\epsilon =\left({Z}_{\mathrm{1r}}^{T}{W}_{1}{Z}_{\mathrm{1r}}\right)+\left({Z}_{\mathrm{1i}}^{T}{W}_{1}{Z}_{\mathrm{1i}}\right)+\left({Z}_{\mathrm{2r}}^{T}{W}_{2}{Z}_{\mathrm{2r}}\right)+\left({Z}_{\mathrm{3r}}^{T}{W}_{3}{Z}_{\mathrm{3r}}\right)$

Where ${W}_{1}$ , ${W}_{1}$ and ${W}_{1}$ are the weightings associated with the various blocks of equations.

One notes, however, that the modal shape must be expressed on the numerical model. It is obtained by expansion of the measurement onto the numerical model. One generally uses the modes of the numerical model as the expansion basis.

An illustration of this technique is found in sdld21e.

## 4 Practical advice

Before carrying out an updating procedure, it is essential to choose carefully the parameters to be updated. This requires thought on the part of the user in order to grasp the right parameters which have a physical meaning with respect to the study to be carried out.

One must also carry out a sensitivity study of the functional to be minimised with respect to the parameters to be adjusted. Indeed, it is useless to adjust a parameter which does not make the functional vary. If the parameter is essential for the study but the functional is insensitive to this parameter, then one must find a new functional which is much better suited.

One must also limit the number of parameters to be updated.

One can distinguish two broad categories of methods.

The first category consists in exploiting the measured quantities directly. This concerns methods of sensitivity type.

The second category consists in having the characteristic properties of the eigenmodes checked on the numerical model, by substituting the computed eigenmodes with the identified eigenmodes. One carries out an expansion of the identified modal shape in order to obtain a quantity defined on the numerical model.

The advantage of the first category lies in the fact that one exploits the measured data directly. The drawback is that one is obliged to carry out a pairing between the measured mode and the analogous computed mode at each iteration of the updating procedure. This technique is therefore not appropriate when the modal density is high.

The advantage of the second category lies in the fact that one carries out neither a modal analysis on the numerical model in the course of the analysis iterations nor a pairing between the computed mode and the measured mode. The drawback is that one is obliged to carry out an expansion of the measurement onto the numerical model. This technique is therefore not appropriate when the number of measurement points is small. A second updating iteration after updating of the support numerical model may be necessary in order to refine the results. Indeed, updating the support model makes it possible to improve the quality of the modal expansion.

Overall, one can draw the following conclusion:

- If the modal density is high, exploitation of the properties of the modal matrices generally gives better results. This nevertheless requires a good distribution of the observation points in order to be able to carry out a better expansion of the modal shape.
- If the modes of the structure are isolated modes, and if one is limited in the number of observation points, then the sensitivity-type technique is much more appropriate for the adjustment of the initial numerical model.
