# r5.03.81 IMPLEX method

**Summary:**

This document presents a method for solving the non-linear problem, due to *Oliver et al.* [1], taking the place of the Newton method for certain damage and plasticity constitutive laws (ENDO_FRAGILE [3], ENDO_ISOT_BETON [4] and VMIS_ISOT_LINE [5] at the present time). It is based on an explicit extrapolation of the internal variables in order to determine the degrees of freedom (displacement) from which the constitutive behaviour is integrated implicitly. The vanishing of the residual is not checked. It thereby introduces an approximation of the solution but makes it possible to guarantee the robustness of the analysis.

It is therefore up to the user to take a critical look at the results obtained, these not being converged in the classical sense of the term, and potentially being able to be far from the exact solution; one therefore advises carrying out several analyses with different load increments in order to make sure that the discrepancy in the results obtained is small.

In cases of a sudden expansion of the damaged zone resulting in a strong snap-back of the global force/displacement response, the method, if it makes it possible to pass the instability, cannot be reliable in terms of the result.

## 1 Introduction

One presents here a solution method, robust but approximate, of the incremental problem of non-linear quasi-static mechanics, for certain damage and plasticity constitutive behaviours. It is activated by the keyword METHODE = 'IMPLEX' of the operator STAT_NON_LINE, and it takes the place of the classical NEWTON method (cf. [2]).

## 2 Statement of the problem

One places oneself within the general framework of the solution of a typical problem of non-linear mechanics discretised in space $(K\in \Omega )$ and in time $(t\in [0,T])$ , and written in displacement. Its solution, at the time instant ${t}_{n+1}$ , consists in determining the displacements ${U}_{n+1}$ (and therefore the strains $\varepsilon ({U}_{n+1})$ ), the internal variables ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ and the stresses ${\sigma}_{n+1}$ satisfying:

1. At the global level, the equilibrium of the forces:

> ${F}_{\text{ext}}({t}_{n+1})-{F}_{int}({\sigma}_{n+1}({U}_{n+1}),{t}_{n+1})$     (1)

1. At the local level, the constitutive equations of the constitutive law considered for the material:

State law: ${\sigma}_{n+1}=\sum(\varepsilon ({U}_{n+1}),{\alpha}_{n+1})$

Evolution law: $\lbrace  \begin{array}{cc}f({\alpha}_{n+1},{\sigma}_{n+1})\le 0& \text{Convexe de réversibilité}\\ {\dot{\alpha}}_{n+1}={\dot{\lambda}}_{n+1}\ge 0& \text{Evolution des variables internes}\\ {\dot{\lambda}}_{n+1}f({\alpha}_{n+1},{\sigma}_{n+1})=0& \text{Condition de Kuhn-Tucker}\end{array}$     (2)

with ${F}_{\text{ext}}$ et ${F}_{int}$ the external and internal forces, and $\dot{{\lambda}_{n+1}}$ the multiplier (of damage or of plasticity). One considers in what follows a constitutive behaviour independent of physical time (no viscosity and no dynamic effect); the “pseudo-instant” ${t}_{n+1}$ therefore represents the applied load factor.

In the great majority of cases, each of equations (1) and (2) is relatively easy to solve.

At the global level, the Newton method leads to a succession of linear problems of the type:

${K}_{n+1}^{i}\delta {U}_{n+1}^{i+1}={F}_{\text{ext}}({t}_{n+1})-{F}_{int}({\sigma}_{n+1}^{i}({U}_{n+1}^{i}))$     (3)

where the upper index represents the Newton iteration considered, $\delta {U}_{n+1}^{i+1}$ is the displacement increment between two successive Newton iterations and ${K}_{n+1}^{i}$ is the global tangent matrix. Note that this global tangent matrix is written (cf. [2])

$\begin{array}{ccc}{K}_{n+1}& =& \frac{\partial {F}_{int}({\sigma}_{n+1}({U}_{n+1}))}{\partial {U}_{n+1}}\\ & =& {\text{A}}_{e=1}^{p}({\int}_{{\Omega}_{\varepsilon}}\nabla {N}_{e}.{C}_{{e}_{n+1}}.\nabla {N}_{e}d\Omega )\end{array}$     (4)

where $\text{A}$ is the assembly operator, $p$ the number of elements of the mesh, ${C}_{{e}_{n+1}}$ the local tangent operator (coming from the constitutive behaviour) and ${N}_{e}$ the shape functions (for the element considered, denoted $e$ ). The quantity ${F}_{\text{ext}}({t}_{n+1})-{F}_{int}({\sigma}_{n+1}^{i}({U}_{n+1}^{i}))$ represents the equilibrium residual, which one must strictly speaking cancel. Determining $\delta {U}_{n+1}^{i+1}$ therefore amounts to the inversion of the matrix ${K}_{n+1}^{i}$ . If therefore the local elements coming from the constitutive behaviour (stresses, tangent matrix) are known and fixed, and generate a matrix $K$ that is well conditioned, the solution is easy.

Moreover locally, if the displacements ${U}_{n+1}$ , and consequently the local strains $\varepsilon ({U}_{n+1})$ , are known, the solution of the majority of the constitutive laws, that is the determination of the stresses ${\sigma}_{n+1}$ , of the internal variables ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ and of the local tangent matrix ${C}_{{e}_{n+1}}$ , is relatively easy.

It is therefore in the simultaneity of these two laws that the difficulty of the implicit solution lies. The most classical solution method is the iterative Newton algorithm (cf. [2]). This iterative scheme does not always converge. Although this remains open to discussion (there exist other possible factors of loss of convergence, such as leaving the basin of attraction of Newton or the presence of bifurcations of the solution), the authors attribute this loss of robustness mainly to the singular character of the matrices, in particular for the damage laws, whose internal variables affect the stiffness matrices of the constitutive behaviour: in the cases of significant damage, close to or reaching failure, the softening leads to locally very “weak” tangent matrices, which may in the extreme no longer be positive definite. Through the assembly process (4), when the damage progresses in the structure, the global matrix $K$ is deteriorated: it becomes too “flexible”, and its smallest eigenvalue tends towards zero. It then becomes singular and the algorithm diverges. The robustness of the analysis is then no longer ensured.

In order to increase the robustness of the analysis in these situations, Oliver *et al.* [1] propose a particular solution method, named IMPLEX. The global equilibrium is then checked approximately with the help of a local tangent matrix (secant in the case of the damage laws) extrapolated explicitly and assembled, and the constitutive laws are solved implicitly from the approximate displacement field. The method is presented below.

## 3 The IMPLEX method

The general elements of the IMPLEX method are presented here. For more details, one may refer in particular to [1].

The proposed method is based on two successive stages carried out in order to determine the set of the unknowns at the “pseudo-instant” (load) ${t}_{n+1}$ .

The first stage consists of an *explicit extrapolation* of the internal variables, then of the stresses, as a function of the quantities computed previously (at the load ${t}_{n}$ ) and of the load step $\Delta {t}_{n+1}$ . Thanks to this extrapolation, the local tangent matrix is evaluated and frozen; the solution of the equilibrium equation (3) makes it possible to determine the displacements, which one considers correct and which are therefore frozen in their turn.

The second stage consists in carrying out the *implicit integration* of the constitutive behaviour, as a function of the degrees of freedom evaluated at the previous stage.

The first stage is of explicit type, whereas the second is of implicit type, whence the name IMPLEX. At the end of these two stages, the equilibrium equation is not satisfied (it is satisfied in terms of the fields extrapolated at stage (1) only); however, the smaller the load increment, the smaller the error committed must be; one therefore advises carrying out the analyses with several different load steps in order to check that the difference between the responses obtained is small (response converged in terms of the load step). In what follows, the two stages are described. A balance of the key points is drawn up.

### 3.1 A method in 2 stages

We shall here present in succession the 2 fundamental stages of the IMPLEX method, summarised in Table 1.

#### 3.1.1 Explicit extrapolation and determination of the degrees of freedom

This first stage concerns in particular the internal variable $\alpha$ , whose evolution is governed by a law of type (2).

At the beginning of the load step ${t}_{n+1}$ , one has available all the information coming from the load step ${t}_{n}$ and from the previous load steps. It is then possible to write the following Taylor expansions (the evolution of the internal variable is considered sufficiently regular to be able to do so):

$\begin{array}{}\lbrace \begin{array}{cccc}{\alpha}_{n+1}& =& {\alpha}_{n}+\Delta {t}_{n+1}{\dot{\alpha}}_{n}+O(\Delta {t}_{n+1}^{2})& \\ {\alpha}_{n}& =& {\alpha}_{n-1}+\Delta {t}_{n}{\dot{\alpha}}_{n-1}+O(\Delta {t}_{n}^{2})& \\ \Delta {t}_{n}{\dot{\alpha}}_{n}& =& \Delta {t}_{n}{\dot{\alpha}}_{n-1}+\Delta {t}_{n}(\Delta {t}_{n}{\ddot{\alpha}}_{n-1})+\Delta {t}_{n}O(\Delta {t}_{n}^{2})& \Rightarrow \Delta {t}_{n}{\dot{\alpha}}_{n}=\Delta {t}_{n}{\dot{\alpha}}_{n-1}+O(\Delta {t}_{n}^{2})\end{array}\\ \Rightarrow \lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{\dot{\alpha}}_{n}& =& \frac{\Delta {\alpha}_{n}}{\Delta {t}_{n}}-O(\Delta {t}_{n}^{2})\\ {\alpha}_{n+1}& =& {\alpha}_{n}+\frac{\Delta {t}_{n+1}}{\Delta {t}_{n}}\Delta {\alpha}_{n}-\Delta {t}_{n+1}O(\Delta {t}_{n}^{2})+O(\Delta {t}_{n+1}^{2})\end{array}\end{array}$ (5)

with $\Delta {X}_{i}={X}_{i}-{X}_{i-1}$ . By truncation, neglecting the second-order terms, one obtains the following prediction $\tilde{{\alpha}_{n+1}}$  for the variable ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ :

> ${\tilde{\alpha}}_{n+1}={\alpha}_{n}+\frac{\Delta {t}_{n+1}}{\Delta {t}_{n}}\Delta {\alpha}_{n}$     (6)

Through (6), one notes that ${\tilde{\alpha}}_{n+1}$ is indeed obtained explicitly, at the load ${t}_{n+1}$ , as a function of the quantities (grandeurs) obtained implicitly at the load ${t}_{n}$ . Figure 1 schematises this extrapolation process.

The extrapolation error may be defined as the difference between the value of the extrapolated internal variable and its real final value; subject to a sufficiently regular evolution of the internal variable in order to carry out the Taylor expansions to a sufficient order, one can evaluate this error as:

> ${e}_{{\alpha}_{n+1}}=∣{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n+1}-{\alpha}_{n+1}∣\approx ∣{\ddot{\alpha}}_{n}∣\Delta {t}_{n+1}^{2}$     (7)

This shows that in the case of a sufficiently regular evolution of the internal variable, the error decreases quadratically as a function of the load step.

Once the internal variable has been extrapolated, one can determine the stress ${\tilde{\sigma}}_{n+1}({\varepsilon}_{n+1},{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n+1})$ and the local tangent operator ${\tilde{C}}_{{e}_{n+1}}$ .

In the particular case of the isotropic damage laws, it is the local secant operator which is used; the extrapolated stresses and this operator are then written:

> $\lbrace  \begin{array}{ccc}{\tilde{\sigma}}_{n+1}({\varepsilon}_{n+1},{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n+1})& =& (1-{\alpha}_{n+1}){C}^{\text{elas}}:{\varepsilon}_{n+1}\\ {\tilde{C}}_{{e}_{n+1}}=\frac{\partial {\tilde{\sigma}}_{n+1}}{\partial {\tilde{\varepsilon}}_{n+1}}& =& (1-{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n+1}){C}^{\text{elas}}\end{array}$     (8)

These extrapolated local quantities serve for the assembly of the global tangent matrix ${\tilde{K}}_{n+1}$ *via* (4), and for the determination of the internal force.

The equilibrium equation in terms of the extrapolated field ${F}_{\text{ext}}({t}_{n+1})-{F}_{int}({U}_{n+1},{\tilde{\sigma}}_{n+1},{t}_{n+1})=0$ is then solved with this extrapolated matrix and the displacement field ${\tilde{U}}_{n+1}$ obtained. It is solved by a classical Newton-Raphson method; it is therefore linearised by ${\tilde{K}}_{n+1}\delta {\tilde{U}}_{n+1}={F}_{\text{ext}}({t}_{n+1})-{F}_{int}({\tilde{\sigma}}_{n+1},{t}_{n+1})$ . In the case of the constitutive laws treated here (isotropic damage with secant operator and linear isotropic plasticity), the constitutive operator is constant during a time step (that is, independent of the current strain state ${\varepsilon}_{n+1}$ ); the Newton-Raphson solution process becomes linear per time step and therefore converges in one iteration. This stage is however iterative in certain cases (non-linear plasticity for example), because of the non-constant character of the tangent operator. At the first iteration, one would then take ${\varepsilon}_{n+1}^{0}={\varepsilon}_{n}$ .

At the end of this first stage, the displacement field ${U}_{n+1}$ is considered equal to the field obtained by the solution of the equilibrium equation written in terms of the extrapolated fields: ${U}_{n+1}={\tilde{U}}_{n+1}$ . This displacement field will no longer be modified subsequently.

#### 3.1.2 Implicit determination of the elements of the constitutive law

Following the first phase, the displacement field is known. It is frozen for the load step and will therefore not be modified.

One then determines the strain ${\varepsilon}_{n+1}({U}_{n+1})$ , then equations (2) of the constitutive law are solved implicitly in order to obtain the stress fields ${\sigma}_{n+1}$ and of internal variable ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ .

This stage is identical to the standard solution of the equations of the constitutive laws. The only difference is the need to store $\frac{\Delta {\alpha}_{n+1}}{\Delta {t}_{n+1}}$ in order to carry out the extrapolation of the internal variable at the next step.

At the end of this stage, the displacement field ${U}_{n+1}$ and the stress fields ${\sigma}_{n+1}$ and of internal variable ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ are therefore known. A major difference with respect to a classical implicit analysis is the fact that the real equilibrium equation is not satisfied; it is satisfied only in terms of the extrapolated fields.

| **Stage 1: Explicit extrapolation** | **Stage 2: Implicit integration** |
| --- | --- |
| Extrapolation of the internal variables: ${\tilde{\alpha}}_{n+1}={\alpha}_{n}+\frac{\Delta {t}_{n+1}}{\Delta {t}_{n}}\Delta {\alpha}_{n}$ | Frozen displacements: ${\dot{U}}_{n+1}=0$ |
| Computation of the extrapolated stress: ${\tilde{\sigma}}_{n+1}({\varepsilon}_{n+1},{\tilde{\alpha}}_{n+1})$ Solution of the equilibrium equation in extrapolated fields: ${F}_{\text{ext}}({t}_{n+1})-{F}_{int}({U}_{n+1},{\tilde{\sigma}}_{n+1},{t}_{n+1})=0$ | Implicit solution of the constitutive laws: State law: ${\sigma}_{n+1}=\sum(\varepsilon ({U}_{n+1}),{\alpha}_{n+1})$  Evolution law: $\lbrace \begin{array}{c}f({\alpha}_{n+1},{\sigma}_{n+1})\le 0\\ {\dot{\alpha}}_{n+1}={\dot{\lambda}}_{n+1}\ge 0\\ {\dot{\lambda}}_{n+1}f({\alpha}_{n+1},{\sigma}_{n+1})=0\end{array}$ |
| **Output of stage 1:** ${U}_{n+1}$ | **Output of stage 2:** ${U}_{n+1}$ , ${\sigma}_{n+1}$ , ${\alpha}_{n+1}$ |

### 3.2 Automatic management of the time step

The IMPLEX method introduces, like all explicit methods, an intrinsic error which must decrease in a quasi-quadratic manner with the load step. The solution may therefore depend slightly on the load step selected by the user.

The user may, if he or she wishes, use an automatic management of the load step *via* the command DEFI_LIST_INST, by specifying METHODE= 'AUTO' and MODE_CALCUL_TPLUS='IMPLEX' (cf. [9]). This method makes it possible to control the error while optimising the analysis duration, provided that the user has chosen a well calibrated first time step.

The aim is therefore to minimise the error defined by equation (7). For this one will bound the increment of the extrapolated variable by a quantity (grandeur) denoted $\mathit{Tol}$ :

> $\Delta {\tilde{\alpha}}_{n+1}=\frac{\Delta {t}_{n+1}}{\Delta {t}_{n}}\Delta {\alpha}_{n}\le \mathrm{Tol}$     (9)

Whence:

> $\Delta {t}_{n+1}\le \frac{\mathrm{Tol}.\Delta {t}_{n}}{\Delta {\alpha}_{n}}$     (10)

As the increments of the internal variable depend on the point considered, the time step will be chosen as the minimum value of expression (10) over the whole of the structure, that is finally:

> $\Delta {t}_{n+1}=\mathrm{Tol}.\Delta {t}_{n}{\mathrm{MIN}}_{x\in \Omega }(\frac{1}{\Delta {\alpha}_{n}(x)})$     (11)

One then adds conditions limiting the acceleration and the deceleration, as well as minimum and maximum bounds for the time increment. The acceleration $(\mu )$ and deceleration conditions are not modifiable and have been calibrated on concrete cases, whereas the minimum and maximum bounds may be modified by the user (the default values are given here).

> $\begin{array}{c}\Delta {t}_{n+1}\le \mu \Delta {t}_{n}=1,2\Delta {t}_{n}\\ \Delta {t}_{n+1}\ge 0.5\Delta {t}_{n}\\ \Delta {t}_{n+1}\le 10\Delta {t}_{0}\\ \Delta {t}_{n+1}\ge 0.001\Delta {t}_{0}\end{array}$     (12)

Thus, the first time step $\Delta {t}_{0}$ , supplied by the user, defines the bounds of the time increment. In order to choose it, one advises the user to have carried out preliminary analyses with the Newton method and to know the elastic limit of the structure; it would seem that the choice of a first time step equal to half the elastic limit allows a good efficiency of the method.

Let us then show that the error committed is indeed controlled. For this one makes the assumption that equation 9 is satisfied. One therefore has $\frac{\Delta {t}_{n+1}}{\Delta {t}_{n}}\Delta {\alpha}_{n}\le \mathrm{Tol}$ , and if one considers that the limiting acceleration and deceleration factors make it possible to write $\frac{\Delta {t}_{n+1}}{\Delta {t}_{n}}\approx 1$ , one has $\Delta {\alpha}_{n}\le \mathrm{Tol}$ ; and step by step:

> $\forall n\in \mathbb{N},\Delta {\alpha}_{n}\le \mathrm{Tol}$     (13)

Which finally makes it possible to write:

> ${e}_{{\alpha}_{n+1}}\approx ∣\ddot{{\alpha}_{n}}∣\Delta {t}_{n+1}^{2}\le \mathrm{Tol}{\mu}^{2}(1+\mu )$     (14)

The error is therefore controlled.

### 3.3 Key points of the method

After this summary presentation, a few remarks are made here in order to understand the interest of the method and also its limitations.

First of all, it is based on an extrapolation of the internal variables, determined from a Taylor expansion. The Taylor expansions being valid only for sufficiently regular functions, the method will be so as well. Thus, the crossing of the elastic limit, or the passage from a loading state to unloading, are points for which the method is strictly speaking not suited: the derivative of the damage is zero on the unloading side or on the elastic-limit side, and non-zero on the loading side. However, if the load steps are sufficiently small, the approximation may be made, inasmuch as the implicit correction takes place and therefore the extrapolation errors are partly smoothed out. In unstable situations however, for example when the damaged zone grows suddenly (which is in general characterised by a significant snap-back of the global force/displacement response), the method, although robust, cannot guarantee a reliable response, whatever the load increment used: it is not suited to this kind of situation.

According to equation (8), in the case of isotropic damage laws, because of the limitation of $\alpha$ to 1 and of the symmetric and positive definite character of the local elastic matrix ${C}^{\text{elas}}$ , the local secant matrix ${\tilde{C}}_{{e}_{n+1}}$ is always symmetric positive definite. As a result, by assembly, the global tangent matrix ${\tilde{K}}_{n+1}$ remains well conditioned: the robustness problems mentioned in the introduction, linked to the increasingly singular character of $K$ , must therefore be eliminated.

Moreover, for a given load step and for the constitutive laws developed here (ENDO_FRAGILE cf. [3], ENDO_ISOT_BETON cf. [4] and VMIS_ISOT_LINE cf. [5]), the local tangent matrix is known by explicit extrapolation and remains constant throughout the load step. The linearisation of the equilibrium equation (1) by a Newton-Raphson method leads to a constant global tangent matrix ${\tilde{K}}_{n+1}$ ; in other words, the equilibrium equation becomes linear at each load step, and its solution requires only one iteration.

Next, and at the risk of being redundant, the method leads to external and internal forces that are not balanced at the end of each load step; they are balanced only at the end of the first phase, that is solely in terms of extrapolated fields:

> $\lbrace \begin{array}{ccc}{F}_{\text{ext}}({t}_{n+1})-{F}_{int}({\sigma}_{n+1}({U}_{n+1}),{t}_{n+1})& \ne & 0\\ {F}_{\text{ext}}({t}_{n+1})-{F}_{int}({U}_{n+1},{\tilde{\sigma}}_{n+1},{t}_{n+1})& =& 0\end{array}$      (15)

Consequently, during the analysis, one must not ask the algorithm to check the residual to within a tolerance, as it is usual to do (in implicit). From this point of view again, robustness is guaranteed, since the classical stopping criterion is irrelevant.

Finally, this method has no other purpose than to increase the robustness of the analysis, and not the quality of the response obtained. Thus, it introduces an intrinsic error through the extrapolation. At best, one can obtain only results almost as good as those obtained by a classical implicit method of local solution. This error must decrease in a quasi-quadratic manner as a function of the imposed load step, subject to a sufficiently regular evolution of the internal variable (which in fact excludes the unstable propagations of damaged zones).

The use of this method therefore requires a certain critical look. In order to guard against large errors, one recommends carrying out analyses with different and small load increments, in order to check that the solution does not differ too much from one increment to the next (in other words, that the solution is close to convergence in terms of load increments). Moreover, in the case of the crossing of unstable situations, the results obtained must be considered only for their qualitative aspect.

## 4 Practical aspects of use

This solution method is activated by specifying, under the simple keyword METHODE of the operator STAT_NON_LINE, METHODE= 'IMPLEX' and by specifying the constitutive law used under COMPORTEMENT.

Only certain constitutive laws are currently available with the IMPLEX method. Table 2 summarises the constitutive laws available according to the type of element considered.

For each constitutive law, the last internal variable is modified and corresponds in this case to the ratio $\frac{\Delta \alpha }{\Delta t}$ .

The method imposes an updating of the matrix at each increment (REAC_INCR = 1) and a single iteration. The equilibrium residual is computed, but no criterion is associated with it. One will be able to realise a possibly large error by seeing that the relative residual is high.

For more information on the practical aspects, one will refer to [6] and [7]. The IMPLEX method is illustrated through the test case SSNP140 [8] and SSLS132.
