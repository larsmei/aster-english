# r7.01.29 Constitutive law ENDO_HETEROGENE

**Summary:**

This documentation presents the theoretical writing and the numerical integration of the constitutive law ENDO_HETEROGENE which takes into account the effect of the heterogeneity of the rock on its failure probability. This model has been integrated as a non-local damage law (with stress gradient) based on a two-threshold model.

## 1 Introduction – Field of application

The constitutive law ENDO_HETEROGENE aims to model the damage of heterogeneous materials and has been developed specifically for geomaterials (for example argillite). This law is based on a probabilistic model of rock fracturing developed within the framework of a collaboration between the BRGM and the LMT-Cachan around the thesis of Nicolas Guy [bib1]. This model takes into account the effect of the heterogeneity of the rock on its failure probability. The initial distribution of micro-defects is indeed given as a function of this heterogeneity from a classical Poisson-Weibull model. The model then rests on the consideration of two thresholds: an initiation threshold and a propagation threshold based on the regularised stress intensity factor.

Regularisation of the stresses allows us:

- to obtain results independent of the spatial discretisation
- to evaluate the two thresholds from the same quantity (grandeur), i.e. the regularised stress
- to compute the “regularised” stress intensity factor without mesh refinement and with good accuracy.

Finally, this model supplies explicitly a cracking path and takes account of the interaction of the cracks with one another.

## 2 A few general points on probabilistic models of crack-network formation

By way of introduction, we specify here the framework within which this model is developed and we recall a few basic notions. In order to explain the formation of cracks, one assumes that they all initiate on pre-existing defects, modelled by cracks possessing a given size. In other words, the start of propagation is considered as the initiation. The Weibull model is widely used to study the degradation and the failure of materials with brittle behaviour [bib8] or quasi-brittle behaviour [bib3]. During the failure of a rock specimen, one may consider that it is the failure of the most critical defect, the local failure, which leads to the failure of the structure, i.e. the global failure. In other words, the failure of the weakest link leads to the failure of the chain (i.e. the structure). Within the framework of the weakest-link assumption, the global failure probability ${P}_{F}$ of a domain Ω is related to the failure probability ${P}_{\mathit{F0}}$ of each element ${\Omega}_{0}$ of area ${S}_{0}$ , by :

$$
{P}_{F}=1-\exp\left[\frac{1}{{S}_{0}}{\int}_{\Omega}\ln(1-{P}_{\mathrm{F0}})d\Omega \right]
$$

The cumulative probability of local failure *PF0*  may be written for example from a Weibull law [bib9] :

$$
\ln(1-{P}_{\mathrm{F0}})=-{(\frac{\langle {\sigma}_{1}-{\sigma}_{u}\rangle }{{\sigma}_{0}})}^{m}
$$

This Weibull formalism is widely developed in the documentation [R7.02.06](doc:r7.02.06).

One simply recalls that for a given crack of size $a$ and in mode $I$ , the expression of the stress intensity factor may be rewritten in the following manner:

$$
K=Y\sigma \sqrt{a}
$$

If one looks at an application of rock-mechanics type, one can show that a crack network can be formed in a geological medium (around a cell for example) following the application of an internal pressure, if a crack can initiate [bib5]. This is due to the decrease of the stress intensity factor when the size of a crack becomes large with respect to the diameter of the cell. The computation of the stress intensity factor for a crack breaking out on the inner edge of a hollow cylinder subjected to an internal pressure $p\int$ shows two different trends as a function of the crack size [bib1]. In the first part, the effect of the crack size with respect to the level of the applied stress is predominant and $K$ increases with it. On the other hand, in the second part, the effect of the decrease of the level of stress applied to the crack becomes more important than the effect of the increase of its size and $K$ decreases as a function of the crack size. A crack can initiate as soon as $K$ reaches the toughness (${K}_{\mathit{IC}}$ ). The crack propagates as the internal pressure increases. When the internal pressure reaches its maximum value, the crack continues to propagate and stops when the value of $K$ becomes lower than ${K}_{\mathit{IC}}$ , neglecting dynamic effects.

These results show that an isolated crack propagates up to a given size for which the value of the stress intensity factor becomes lower than the toughness of the rock. When a first crack stops, another can initiate on a second initiation site. The second crack will stop at the same length as the first, a third crack can initiate, and so on. This means that a crack network can form.

Let us assume that all the cracks initiate from initial defects distributed in a random manner in the rock mass. A Poisson point process is considered in order to describe the distribution of the initiation sites. Among all these potential crack-initiation sites, only a fraction will give rise to cracks. When the value of the stress intensity factor for a defect (a crack) exceeds a critical value (${K}_{\mathit{IC}}$ ), the crack begins to propagate. When a crack is initiated, the presence of this crack creates a stress-relaxation zone around it. In order to explain the initiation of a crack, one must model the interaction of the zones (i.e. the volume, the area or the length) affected by the decrease of stress and the other sites which can create cracks. If the site lies in a stress-relaxation zone, the microscopic stress is lower than the applied macroscopic stress. In this case, this site cannot give rise to a new crack.

In this approach, the obscuration zone (i.e. stress relaxation) is the key quantity (grandeur) for describing the formation of a crack network. The obscuration zone is defined as a zone in which the stress level is lower than the applied stress. The shape and the size of this zone can be defined with the help of numerical simulations. One says that the potential defects are screened by the neighbouring cracks.

## 3 Development of the law ENDO_HETEROGENE

We have chosen in *Code_Aster*  to represent the formation and the propagation of the cracks by an integrated isotropic damage model. The damage-elasticity coupling law being given classically by:

$$
\underline{\sigma}=(1-d)\underline{\underline{C}}\underline{\epsilon}
$$

With $\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}$ the stress tensor, $\underline{\varepsilon}$ the strain tensor, $\underline{\underline{C}}$ the isotropic elasticity tensor and $d$ the damage variable.

One places oneself in the framework recalled in section  2 . The presence of cracks in the structure is modelled by lines of broken elements (i.e. for which the damage d is such that $d=1$ ). For the sound elements, $d=0$ . The failure of the elements may be caused by two phenomena which are distinct here; namely the initiation of a new crack from the element considered or the propagation, through the element considered, of a crack which has initiated in another element. These two types of failure are described by two types of different threshold functions in this approach.

Moreover, for constitutive laws corresponding to brittle materials, the introduction of a heterogeneity of the material projected onto the mesh has the effect of introducing a dependence on the size of the zone (and this even with the use of classical non-local models [bib1]). Furthermore, a local damage law cannot describe a brittle behaviour of weakest-link type.

In order to remedy this problem, it is proposed here to:

1. modify the local law in order to represent a brittle failure of weakest-link type in a heterogeneous material
2. introduce a regularisation operator based on the gradient of the stresses.

It is shown that a non-local description with stress gradient is, on the one hand, very close to the non-local models with strain gradient and, on the other hand, allows us to compute correctly the stress intensity factor, which is the key parameter for describing the propagation of macro-cracks. One introduces a regularised stress such that

$$
\underline{\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}-{l}_{c}^{2}\Delta \underline{\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}=\underline{\sigma}
$$

With $\underline{\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}$ the regularised stress tensor and ${l}_{c}$ the characteristic length. One will consider the so-called natural Neumann boundary conditions, namely

$$
(\nabla \underline{\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}).n=0
$$

with $n$ the outward normal to the edge considered. One considers a perfectly brittle constitutive law of the type:

$$
d=H\left(\langle \stackrel{̄}{{\sigma}_{1}}-{\sigma}_{y}(\mathit{el})\rangle \right),\dot{d}\ge 0
$$

Where ${\sigma}_{y}$ is a stress associated with the yield stress.

The two thresholds will therefore be written as a function of a stress regularised over a characteristic length ${l}_{c}$ which will be large compared with the size of the elements. One will endeavour in the present part to justify the choice of the two thresholds as well as the manner in which they are coordinated.

### 3.1 Initiation of the cracks

As said previously, the initiation of new macro-cracks is represented by a Weibull model. With a view to describing in a relevant manner the cracking spacing, a scatter on the material parameters (Weibull modulus, scale parameter) specific to each finite element is introduced. By carrying out beforehand a random draw on a uniform distribution for each finite element in order to draw from it a value of the failure probability of each element $P(\mathrm{el})\in [:ref:`0;1 <0;1>$]` , one can describe the stress for which a crack will initiate in a finite element by the following equation:

$$
{\sigma}_{a}(\mathrm{el})=\frac{{\sigma}_{0}}{{({\lambda}_{0}{Z}_{\mathrm{el}})}^{1/m}}.{\left[-\ln(1-P(\mathrm{el}))\right]}^{\frac{1}{m}}
$$

With ${Z}_{\mathrm{el}}$ the size (the volume or the area according to the dimension) of the finite element, $m$ the Weibull modulus and $\frac{{\sigma}_{0}^{m}}{{\lambda}_{0}}$ the scale parameter.

The use of a Weibull model to describe the initiation of new cracks has a definite advantage with a view to obtaining results independent of the mesh. Let us consider two different partitions of the domain ${Z}_{\mathrm{do}}$  each made up of subdomains ${Z}_{\mathrm{di}}^{1}$ and ${Z}_{\mathrm{di}}^{2}$ . One may write:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{n1}}{Z}_{\mathrm{di}}^{1}={Z}_{\mathrm{do}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathrm{n2}}{Z}_{\mathrm{di}}^{2}
$$

where $\mathit{n1}$ and $\mathit{n2}$ denote the numbers of subdomains in the two cases. The probability of initiation of the first crack of a subdomain for a loading $\sigma$ is written:

$$
{P}_{\mathrm{asdi}}=1-\exp\left[-{Z}_{\mathrm{sd}}{\lambda}_{0}{(\frac{\sigma}{{\sigma}_{0}})}^{m}\right]
$$

where ${Z}_{\mathrm{sd}}$ is the size (volume) of a subdomain. The probability of initiating the first crack over the set of subdomains ${P}_{\mathrm{aesd1}}$  for a loading $\sigma$ can be deduced from it:

$$
{P}_{\mathrm{aesd1}}=1-\prod_{i=1}^{{n}_{\mathrm{sd}}}\left[1-{P}_{\mathrm{asdi1}}\right]=1-\exp\left[-\sum_{i=1}^{{n}_{1}}{Z}_{\mathrm{di}}^{1}{\lambda}_{0}{(\frac{\sigma}{{\sigma}_{0}})}^{m}\right]=1-\exp\left[-\sum_{i=1}^{{n}_{2}}{Z}_{\mathrm{di}}^{2}{\lambda}_{0}{(\frac{\sigma}{{\sigma}_{0}})}^{m}\right]={P}_{\mathrm{aesd2}}
$$

Because:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{{n}_{1}}{Z}_{\mathrm{di}}^{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{{n}_{2}}{Z}_{\mathrm{di}}^{2}={Z}_{\mathrm{do}}
$$

The equation allows us to recover a probability of initiation of the first crack over a domain of size ${Z}_{\mathrm{do}}$  independent of the subdivision into subdomains.

Finally one may write the evolution law of the damage d related to the initiation of a new crack in an element:

$$
d=H(\langle \stackrel{ˉ}{{\sigma}_{1}}-{\sigma}_{a}(\mathrm{el})\rangle ),\dot{d}\ge 0
$$

where $H$ is the Heaviside step function and $\stackrel{ˉ}{{\sigma}_{1}}$ the maximum principal regularised stress.

### 3.2 Propagation of the cracks

We shall show in this part that the non-local approach proposed allows us to compute correctly the stress intensity factor for cracks modelled as a series of broken elements. Indeed, it is possible to link a criterion in stress intensity factor, coming from fracture mechanics, to the regularised stress. One may consider that the asymptotic solution of the stress field at the crack tip is a good approximation of the fields that one will be able to find at the tip of a propagating crack. The stress field given by the Westergaard solution may be written in a frame at the crack tip such as presented on the Figure as a composition of elastic fields (for a plane problem):

$$
\underline{\underline{\sigma}}(r,\theta )={K}_{I}\underline{\underline{{f}_{I}}}(r,\theta )+{K}_{\mathit{II}}\underline{\underline{{f}_{\mathit{II}}}}(r,\theta )
$$

With ${K}_{I},{K}_{\mathrm{II}}$ the stress intensity factors in mode $I$ and $\mathit{II}$ , and $\underline{\underline{{f}_{I},{f}_{\mathrm{II}}}}$ the associated weight functions (equation and ) in mode $I$ and $\mathit{II}$ .

$$
\underline{\underline{{f}_{I}}}(r,\theta )=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi r}}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\left(1-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\frac{3\theta }{2}\right)& \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\frac{3\theta }{2}& 0\\ \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\frac{3\theta }{2}& \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\left(1+\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\frac{3\theta }{2}\right)& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array}\right]
$$

$$
\underline{\underline{{f}_{\mathit{II}}}}(r,\theta )=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi r}}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\left(2-\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\frac{3\theta }{2}\right)& \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\left(1-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\frac{3\theta }{2}\right)& 0\\ \cos\frac{\theta}{2}\left(1-\sin\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\frac{3\theta }{2}\right)& \sin\frac{\theta}{2}\cos\frac{\theta}{2}\sin\frac{3\theta }{2}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array}\right]
$$

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.29/10000200000002E7000002746F467E25E2D3604F.png)

**Figure** 3.2-a **: Definition of a frame at the crack tip**

The regularised stress at the crack tip can be estimated by

$$
\underline{\underline{\stackrel{̄}{{\sigma}_{p}}}}({K}_{I},{K}_{\mathit{II}})=\sum_{i=1}^{\mathit{II}}\left[\frac{{K}_{i}}{\pi {l}_{c}^{2}}{\int}_{\frac{-\pi }{2}}^{\frac{\pi}{2}}{\int}_{0}^{{\%}_{c}}\underline{\underline{{f}_{i}}}(r,\theta )r\partial r\partial \theta \right]
$$

Which can finally be written in the following form:

$$
\underline{\underline{\stackrel{̄}{{\sigma}_{p}}}}({K}_{I},{K}_{\mathit{II}})=\frac{{\Gamma}^{2}(3/4)}{5\pi \sqrt{\pi \mathit{lc}}}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}{\mathrm{4K}}_{I}& {\mathrm{4K}}_{\mathit{II}}& 0\\ {\mathrm{4K}}_{\mathit{II}}& {\mathrm{6K}}_{I}& 0\\ 0& 0& \stackrel{̄}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{33p}}}\end{array}\right]
$$

where $\stackrel{ˉ}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{33p}}}=-\nu (\stackrel{ˉ}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{11p}}}+\stackrel{ˉ}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{22p}}})$ in plane strain and $\stackrel{ˉ}{{\sigma}_{\mathrm{33p}}}=0$ in plane stress. Moreover one recalls that $\Gamma$ is the Gamma function, such that $\Gamma (a)=\gamma (a,\infty )$ and $\gamma (a,b)={\int}_{0}^{b}{t}^{a-1}\exp(-t)\mathrm{dt}$ the incomplete gamma function. Concretely $\Gamma (3/4)=1,225416$ .

Then, on the basis of the expression of the regularised stress at the crack tip, it is possible to propose an evolution law associated with the propagation of a macro-crack bearing on the maximum principal stress of the type:

$$
d=H\left(\langle \stackrel{̄}{{\sigma}_{I}}-{\sigma}_{p}(\mathit{el})\rangle \right),\dot{d}\ge 0
$$

With:

$$
{\sigma}_{p}(\mathit{el})=\frac{6{\Gamma}^{2}(3/4)}{5\pi }\frac{{K}_{\mathit{Ic}}}{\sqrt{\pi {l}_{c}}}
$$

${\sigma}_{p}(\mathrm{el})$ is the failure threshold of an element by propagation of a crack at the macroscopic scale and ${K}_{\mathrm{Ic}}$ the toughness of the material. For a loading in pure mode I, the threshold proposed corresponds to a threshold of stress-intensity-factor type coming from fracture mechanics. The threshold proposed has in particular the advantage of being independent of whether one places oneself in plane stress or plane strain for a 2D problem and it comes close to a criterion in stress intensity factor in mode I for a 3D problem. One notes that in practice the criterion presented is a mixed-mode criterion; indeed, if one observes the failure threshold by propagation of a crack at the macroscopic scale under a loading composed for example of modes I and II in plane stress one obtains at the crack tip

$$
\stackrel{̄}{{\sigma}_{\mathit{Ip}}}=\frac{6{\Gamma}^{2}(3/4)}{5\pi }\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi {l}_{c}}}\left[{\mathrm{5K}}_{I}+\sqrt{{K}_{I}^{2}+{\mathrm{16K}}_{\mathit{II}}^{2}}\right]
$$

which also depends on the level of loading in mode $\mathit{II}$ .

### 3.3 Coordination of the thresholds

The two-parameter Weibull model describes the initiation of the cracks with a very conservative character. Indeed, using a two-parameter Weibull model amounts to assuming the existence of a non-zero probability of crack initiation as soon as the maximum principal stress becomes positive. On the other hand, the use of a crack-propagation criterion in stress intensity factor on a crack at the microscopic scale amounts to assuming the existence of a non-zero initiation threshold. For example, let us consider the case of a zone which has a dimension of the order of ${l}_{c}$ and which is contained in a larger zone loaded uniformly. If one assumes the existence of a Weibull initiation threshold ${\sigma}_{a}$ , one can associate with it a crack length by a fracture-mechanics criterion:

$$
{\sigma}_{a}(\mathit{el})=\frac{{\sigma}_{0}}{{({\lambda}_{0}{Z}_{\mathit{el}})}^{1/m}}{[-\ln(1-P(\mathit{el}))]}^{1/m}=\frac{{K}_{\mathit{Ic}}}{\sqrt{\pi a(\mathit{el})}}
$$

With $a(\mathrm{el})$ the half-length of the largest crack in the zone considered. For the sake of consistency with the propagation threshold ${\sigma}_{p}$ , care must be taken that the use of a Weibull model to describe crack initiation does not imply assuming the presence, in a zone considered, of a crack larger than the size of the zone considered. Furthermore it seems reasonable to assume that a given element will be more easily broken by the propagation of a macro-crack than by initiation of a new crack. In this way, we shall also guarantee that the initiation sites which lie in the neighbourhood of the tip of a crack will be screened by it. This assumption amounts to setting the following inequality:

$$
{\sigma}_{a}(\mathit{el})\ge {\sigma}_{p}(\mathit{el})
$$

which is equivalent to:

$$
a(\mathit{el})\le {\left(\frac{5\pi }{6\Gamma (3/4)}\right)}^{2}{l}_{c}
$$

in order to define a critical value of the initial-defect size. One can interpret the previous inequality as follows: one cannot assume that an initial crack of a size comparable to the macroscopic scale is contained in a zone of a size comparable to the microscopic scale. In practice, if there exist initial cracks of half-length greater than about ${l}_{c}$ they will not be able to be taken into account by the initiation model proposed; one will on the other hand be able to represent them by a line of broken finite elements. The Weibull model will therefore be used to represent the initiation of cracks from initial defects of size lower than $2{l}_{c}$ . The characteristic length ${l}_{c}$ appears as a transition scale between the microscopic scale of crack initiation and the macroscopic scale of crack propagation. The characteristic length can then be interpreted as being of the order of magnitude of the largest cracks initially present at the microscopic scale.

Moreover one notes that if each finite element does not contain an initial defect larger than itself and if the characteristic length ${l}_{c}$ is larger than the size of the finite elements, then the inequality is automatically satisfied.

In order to carry out a coordination of the thresholds, a locating of the elements is necessary. To this end, a criterion bearing on the neighbourhood of a finite element has been developed. Within this framework, the propagation criterion will be checked only on elements which lie in the direct neighbourhood of a crack already initiated. This model therefore requires knowledge of the neighbours of an element. The details of this locating are explained in the following chapter.

## 4 Numerical integration

### 4.1 Initialisation

In order to carry out an analysis using the two damage thresholds, it is necessary to define beforehand a set of parameters. The parameters characterising the material are the following: Young's modulus $E$ , Poisson's ratio $\nu$ , the toughness ${K}_{\mathrm{Ic}}$ , the Weibull modulus $m$ , the characteristic length ${l}_{c}$ and a nominal initiation stress at the scale of the characteristic length. The nominal initiation stress at the scale of the characteristic length is introduced in place of the scale factor. One can express it by:

$$
{\sigma}_{{l}_{c}}=\frac{{\sigma}_{0}^{m}}{{({\lambda}_{0}{l}_{c}^{3})}^{1/m}}
$$

With ${\sigma}_{0}^{m}/{\lambda}_{0}$ the scale factor and m the Weibull modulus. The nominal initiation stress at the scale of the characteristic length ${\sigma}_{{l}_{c}}$  gives us an idea of the order of magnitude of the initiation stress of a domain of a size ${l}_{c}^{3}$  which is possibly made up of several finite elements during an analysis. More precisely the initiation probability corresponding to a loading of ${\sigma}_{{l}_{c}}$ is about 0.63. In addition to the parameters characterising the material, one specifies the thickness of the modelled structure ${E}_{\mathrm{ps}}$ . It is possible to use a variant of the surface Weibull model instead of the volume one, for which the unit of the scale factor is the MPamm² instead of the MPamm3. In this case, one must specify that the structure has a thickness of ${E}_{\mathrm{ps}}={l}_{c}$ During the initialisation phase one computes for each element the value of the initiation threshold (stored in as an internal variable). As shown on the , the computation of the initiation threshold ${\sigma}_{a}$ is carried out for each finite element and consists of several steps.

- For each finite element, one begins by computing the size of the elements ${Z}_{\mathrm{el}}$  as a function of the position of the nodes and of the thickness ${E}_{\mathrm{ps}}$ . Then, one computes the propagation threshold by the equation .
- The initiation threshold (itself also an internal variable) is then computed (equation ) with $P(\mathrm{el})\in [:ref:`0;1 <0;1>$]` a number drawn at random on a uniform probability density. If the consistency condition of the two thresholds (equation ) is not satisfied, one starts again after having drawn at random another value for $P(\mathit{el})$ . In order to make the two thresholds consistent, one carries out here a truncation of the Weibull distribution. Other choices could have been envisaged; one carries out here a truncation for the sake of simplicity. One notes that the choice of carrying out a truncation on the Weibull distribution obliges one to choose a realistic characteristic length. Indeed, if the characteristic length chosen is inconsistent ( *i.e.,*  small) with the Weibull parameters used, one can truncate the major part of the Weibull distribution. In this case, the description of the initiation is not faithful to the Weibull parameters considered.

One will refer to section  4.4.2 for the choice of the parameters.

### 4.2 Solution of the global problem

The global problem bears on the computation of the displacements and of the regularised stresses. The constitutive law which makes it possible to compute at each Newton iteration the stress and the damage is implemented according to an implicit scheme. The problem is discretised with the help of the new particular finite elements introduced into Code_Aster as described on the figure .

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.29/10049D02000034C7000020A94E53F4121721DD75.svg)

**Figure** 4.2-a **: Nodal unknowns of a triangle element**

The finite elements used are quadratic in displacement and linear in regularised stresses; they therefore comprise 21 degrees of freedom for a triangle element for a plane problem (28 for a quadrangle). Two sets of shape functions and derived shape functions are associated with the displacements $({N}_{u}\mathit{et}{B}_{u})$ and with the regularised stresses $({N}_{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}\mathit{et}{B}_{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}})$ .

Two equations govern the global problem, one corresponds to the classical equilibrium; the other corresponds to the computation of the regularised stresses (for more details on the development of non-local gradient problems, one will refer for example to [R5.04.01](doc:r5.04.01)). The integral formulation of the problem is:

$$
\forall {u}^{\text{*}}\in {V}_{\mathit{ad}}{\int}_{\Omega}\underline{\sigma}:\underline{ϵ}({u}^{\text{*}})d\Omega ={\int}_{G}{u}^{\text{*}}.{T}_{G}\mathit{dG}+{\int}_{\Omega}{u}^{\text{*}}.{f}_{V}d\Omega
$$

With ${V}_{\mathrm{ad}}$ the space of admissible displacements, ${T}_{G}$ the forces imposed on the edge G, ${f}_{V}$ the body forces and:

$$
\forall \underline{{s}^{\text{*}}}\in {[{H}^{1}(\Omega )]}^{6}{\int}_{\Omega}(\underline{{s}^{\text{*}}}.\underline{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}+\nabla \underline{{s}^{\text{*}}}.{l}_{c}^{2}\underline{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}})d\Omega ={\int}_{\Omega}\underline{{s}^{\text{*}}}.\underline{\sigma}d\Omega
$$

One will denote in the part which follows $\sigma ,\varepsilon \mathrm{et}\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}$ , the writing in vector form of the tensors of stresses, strains and regularised stresses; one denotes by u the displacement vector. The global solution amounts to the vanishing of a residual bearing on the displacements which can be written for each of the elements (for more details, one will refer to [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01)):

$$
{F}^{u}={F}_{int}+{D}^{T}\lambda -{F}_{\mathit{ext}}
$$

With ${F}_{i}={\int}_{\Omega}{B}_{u}^{T}\sigma d\Omega ,{F}_{e}={\int}_{\Gamma}{N}_{u}^{T}{T}_{g}\mathrm{dG}$ the external loading in forces with ${T}_{G}$ the nodal reactions. D is such that $\mathrm{Du}={u}^{d}$ with ${u}^{d}$ the imposed displacements and $\lambda$ the Lagrange multipliers for Dirichlet boundary conditions. One also minimises a residual bearing on the regularised stresses:

$$
{F}^{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}={K}^{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}\sigma }\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}-{F}^{\sigma}
$$

With ${F}^{\sigma}={\int}_{\Omega}{N}_{\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}^{T}\sigma d\Omega \mathrm{et}{K}^{\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}\sigma }={\int}_{\Omega}({N}_{\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}^{T}{N}_{\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}+{l}_{c}^{2}{B}_{\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}}^{T}{B}_{\stackrel{ˉ}{\sigma}})d\Omega$ . In order to minimise the residuals presented one uses the following tangent matrix:

$$
K=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\frac{\partial {F}^{u}}{\partial u}& \frac{\partial {F}^{u}}{\partial \stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}\\ \frac{\partial {F}^{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}}{\partial u}& \frac{\partial {F}^{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}}{\partial \stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}\end{array}\right]
$$

With:

$$
\frac{\partial {F}^{u}}{\partial u}={\int}_{\Omega}{B}_{u}^{T}\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \epsilon }{B}_{u}d\Omega
$$

$$
\frac{\partial {F}^{u}}{\partial \stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}={\int}_{\Omega}{B}_{u}^{T}\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}{N}_{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}d\Omega
$$

$$
\frac{\partial {F}^{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}}{\partial \stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}={\int}_{\Omega}({N}_{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}^{T}{N}_{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}+{l}_{c}^{2}{B}_{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}^{T}{B}_{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}})d\Omega
$$

$$
\frac{\partial {F}^{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}}{\partial u}={\int}_{\Omega}-{N}_{\stackrel{̄}{\sigma}}^{T}\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \epsilon }{B}_{u}d\Omega
$$

### 4.3 Implementation of the constitutive law

The sound finite elements ( *i.e.,*  for which $d=0$ ) can be found in different situations. Either they are at the tip of a crack, in which case they are subjected to the propagation threshold, or they are not at the tip of a crack, in which case they are subjected to the initiation threshold. Thus the position of the crack tips and their assigned elements are stored during the analysis. The Figure represents the evolution of the state of the finite elements during the initiation and the propagation of a crack. Initially, if all the finite elements are sound, they are all subjected to their failure threshold by initiation. If there is initiation of a crack in a given element, the element considered is broken ( *i.e., d=1* ) and it points if possible to two neighbouring elements as shown on the Figure (a). At the following Newton iteration, the pointed finite elements are no longer subjected to their failure threshold by initiation but to the failure threshold by propagation.

If the pointed finite elements undergo a loading such that they break, each of them will then if possible point to a neighbouring finite element which will then be subjected to the failure threshold by propagation. The figure makes it possible to observe more precisely the method of pointing to the neighbouring elements during the initiation phase (figure (a)) and during the propagation phase (figure (b)). If in a structural analysis the loading level reached is sufficient to generate the initiation of a crack in the finite element ${A}_{\mathit{el}}$ , then one makes the assumption that the crack considered initiates at the centre of the element and the damage of the finite element considered is equal to $d=1$ . One then defines two crack tips ${B}_{\mathit{pt}}$ and ${C}_{\mathit{pt}}$ which are the intersections of a straight line ${\Delta}_{{A}_{\mathit{el}}}$ and of the edges of the finite element considered. One defines the straight line ${\Delta}_{{A}_{\mathrm{el}}}$ as passing through the point ${A}_{\mathit{pt}}$ and being perpendicular to the vector $\overrightarrow{{n}_{{\mathrm{IA}}_{\mathrm{pt}}}}$ which corresponds to the direction associated with the maximum principal regularised stress at the point ${A}_{\mathit{pt}}$ at failure $\stackrel{̄}{{\sigma}_{{\mathit{IA}}_{\mathit{pt}}}}$ . Each of the crack tips is then taken in charge by a finite element neighbouring the finite element ${A}_{\mathit{el}}$ , namely the elements ${B}_{\mathit{el}}$ and ${C}_{\mathit{el}}$ . The elements ${B}_{\mathrm{el}}$  and ${C}_{\mathrm{el}}$ are then subjected to the failure threshold by propagation.

As shown on figure (b), if one reaches the propagation threshold at a crack tip ${D}_{\mathit{pt}}$ taken in charge by an element ${D}_{\mathit{el}}$ , then the damage of the element ${D}_{\mathrm{el}}$ passes to $d=1$ . One then moves the crack tip along a straight line ${\Delta}_{{D}_{\mathit{el}}}$ defined as passing through ${D}_{\mathrm{pt}}$ and being perpendicular to $\vec{{n}_{{\mathit{ID}}_{\mathit{pt}}}}$ the direction associated with the maximum principal regularised stress at the point ${D}_{\mathrm{pt}}$ at failure $\stackrel{̄}{{\sigma}_{{\mathit{ID}}_{\mathit{pt}}}}$ . The crack tip is then positioned at the point ${E}_{\mathrm{pt}}$ and taken in charge by the finite element ${E}_{\mathrm{el}}$ . The neighbourhood-management method developed makes it possible to manage several cracks simultaneously. It also allows each of the cracks defined to branch according to the structural problem as well as according to possible interactions with other cracks of the same structure.

The integration method of the neighbourhood-management system presented considers a finite element as being pointed only if it was pointed during the previous Newton iteration. Consequently each crack tip can lead to the failure by propagation of one element per Newton iteration. Moreover, the implementation of the material behaviour is analytical, which guarantees the stability of the converged states. Indeed, during a loading step, each crack tip can lead to the failure by propagation of as many elements as is necessary in order to reach equilibrium. Consequently, the model presented makes it possible to represent the propagation of macro-cracks even in the unstable phase. Let us consider the case of a structure subjected to a loading increment containing ${n}_{\mathit{pf}}$ crack tips numbered from 1 to ${n}_{\mathit{pf}}$ . If during the loading step no new crack initiates but each crack tip *i*  must break ${n}_{\mathit{elt}}$ elements in order for one to reach a stable state, then the number of global Newton iterations required to solve the problem on the loading increment is ${n}_{\mathit{it}}=max({n}_{\mathit{eli}})$ . One can deduce from this that the duration of the simulations depends only little on the number of cracks present in the structure.

### 4.4 Material parameters required for the law

#### 4.4.1 Keywords

In summary, 5 material parameters are required for the law, to be defined in the keyword ENDO_HETEROGENE of DEFI_MATERIAU:

1. WEIBULL: parameter m of the Weibull model,
2. SY: nominal initiation stress,
3. KI: stress intensity factor ${K}_{i}$ ,
4. EPAI: thickness ${E}_{\mathrm{ps}}$
5. GR: Seed of the random draw defining the initial defects

The characteristic length is entered under the keyword LONG_CARA of DEFI_MATERIAU.

#### 4.4.2 Identification advice

The classical Weibull model is based on the weakest-link assumption: the global failure of the structure corresponds to the local failure, and therefore directly to the heterogeneities. The parameters of the Weibull distribution are found experimentally with splitting tests, characterised by an unstable propagation of the first defect which suddenly takes the specimen to failure. The weakest-link assumption is therefore valid in this case. As we have seen, the ENDO_HETEROGENE model considers more complex types of cracking, with the formation of a crack network. For this reason one introduces the concept of initiation, distinguishing it from propagation; the weakest-link assumption and the application of the Weibull model indeed stop at the finite element. However, the experimental calibration procedure of the Weibull parameters by splitting tests is still valid because it is a completely experimental procedure and because it applies to a case where the weakest-link assumption is valid; on the other hand, this corresponds only to the initiation of cracking for ENDO_HETEROGENE.

A more complicated step concerns the determination of the remaining parameters: the toughness in mode $I$ and the characteristic length. The two parameters are used by the ENDO_HETEROGENE model for the computation of the propagation threshold. The fracture-mechanics law used is non-linear precisely because of the presence of ${l}_{c}$ . On the contrary, in the experimental tests the propagation is associated with the toughness alone, when one refers to a linear fracture-mechanics model. The two concepts of toughness used therefore do not seem to coincide, because they are not associated with the same theoretical model. One must therefore take with caution the toughness values coming from the classical tests.

As regards the characteristic length alone, one can see this value as a key for the passage from the small scale, that of the micro-defects, to the large scale, that of the macro-cracks. An empirical rule for choosing it consists in taking a length lying between the length of the smallest defect and that of a macro-crack. One could therefore make an a priori choice of the characteristic length, and then choose the toughness accordingly.

### 4.5 Description of the internal variables

The model has 12 internal variables which need to be stored at the previous time:

1. $\mathrm{VI}(1)$ : damage $d$ ,
2. $\mathit{VI}(2)$ : indicator (0 sound element, 1 pointed element, 2 broken by initiation, 3 broken by propagation),
3. $\mathrm{VI}(3)$ : initiation stress ${\sigma}_{a}$ ,
4. $\mathrm{VI}(4)$ : propagation stress ${\sigma}_{p}$ ,
5. $\mathrm{VI}(5)$ : number of the pointed element number 1,
6. $\mathrm{VI}(6)$ : number of the pointed element number 1 if initiation,
7. $\mathrm{VI}(7)$ : Newton iteration of failure,
8. $\mathrm{VI}(8)$ : current Newton iteration,
9. $\mathrm{VI}(9)$ : X coordinate of the crack tip (after failure by propagation),
10. $\mathrm{VI}(10)$ : Y coordinate of the crack tip (after failure by propagation),
11. $\mathrm{VI}(11)$ : coordinate $X$ of crack tip 2 at initiation,
12. $\mathrm{VI}(12)$ : coordinate $Y$ of crack tip 2 at initiation,

Note:

In a parallel analysis, when one carries out an analysis on several processors with the law ENDO_HETEROGENE, nothing guarantees the uniqueness of the solution because of the initiation thresholds generated in a random manner.
