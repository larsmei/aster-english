## 1 Introduction

We present here a family of THM constitutive laws for saturated and unsaturated media. We describe the relations making it possible to compute the hydraulic and thermal quantities, taking into account strong couplings between these phenomena and also with the mechanical strains. The relations presented here may be coupled with any mechanical constitutive law, subject to making the hypothesis known as that of the Bishop effective stresses and to the mechanical constitutive law defining elastic constants (useful for the coupled terms). For this reason, the purely mechanical part of the laws is not presented here.

The modellings adopted rely on the presentation of porous media elaborated notably by O. Coussy  . The constitutive relations are obtained from thermodynamic considerations and with homogenisation arguments which we do not present here, and which are entirely described in the document of P. Charles  . Likewise the general writing of the equilibrium and conservation equations is not detailed, and one refers the reader to the documents [R5.03.01](doc:r5.03.01)  and [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10) , which contain definitions useful for the understanding of the present document.

The mechanics of porous media gathers a very exhaustive collection of physical phenomena concerning solids and fluids. It makes the hypothesis of a coupling between the mechanical evolutions of the solids and of the fluids, seen as continuous media, with the hydraulic evolutions, which govern the problems of diffusion of fluids within walls or volumes, and the thermal evolutions.

Each of the constituents of the porous medium therefore has a mechanical, hydraulic and thermal behaviour. The theory attempts to gather all these physical phenomena. The chemical phenomena (transformations of the constituents, reactions producing constituents etc...), as well as the radiological phenomena, are not taken into account at this stage of the development of *Code_Aster*. The mechanical, hydraulic and thermal phenomena are taken into account or not according to the behaviour invoked by the user in the command [STAT_NON_LINE](doc:u4.51.03), according to the following nomenclature:

| **Modelling** | **Phenomena taken into account** |
| --- | --- |
| KIT_H | Hydraulics with one unknown pressure |
| KIT_HM | Mechanics, hydraulics with one unknown pressure |
| KIT_HHM | Mechanics, hydraulics with two unknown pressures |
| KIT_THH | Thermal, hydraulics with two unknown pressures |
| KIT_THM | Thermal, mechanics, hydraulics with one unknown pressure |
| KIT_THHM | Thermal, mechanics, hydraulics with two unknown pressures |

The present document describes the laws for the most general case, called THHM. The simpler cases are obtained from the general case by simply cancelling the absent quantities.

## 2 Presentation of the problem: Hypotheses, Notation

In the present chapter, one is concerned mainly with presenting the porous medium and its characteristics.

### 2.1 Description of the porous medium

The porous medium considered is a volume constituted of a more or less homogeneous solid matrix, more or less coherent (very coherent in the case of concrete, little in the case of sand). Between the solid elements, one finds pores. One distinguishes the closed pores which exchange nothing with their neighbours and the connected pores in which the exchanges are numerous. When one speaks of porosity, it is indeed of these connected pores that one speaks.

Inside these pores there are a certain number of fluids (one excludes the solidification of these fluids), present possibly in several phases (liquid or gaseous exclusively), and presenting an interface with the other constituents. In order to simplify the problem and to take into account the relative importance of the physical phenomena, the only interface considered is that between the liquid and the gas, the fluid/solid interfaces being neglected.

### 2.2 Notation

We assume that the pores of the solid are occupied by at most two constituents, each coexisting in two phases at most, one liquid and the other gaseous. The quantities $X$ associated with phase $j$ ($j=1,2$ ) of fluid $i$ will be denoted: ${X}_{ij}$. When there are two constituents in addition to the solid, they are a liquid (typically water) and a gas (typically dry air), knowing that the liquid may be present in gaseous form (vapour) in the gaseous mixture and that the air may be present in dissolved form in the water. When there is only one constituent in addition to the solid, it may be a liquid or a gas. In what follows one will speak of air for the gaseous component, but it may be any other component (hydrogen, ${\text{CO}}_{2}$ etc.).

The porous medium at the current time instant is denoted $\Omega$, its boundary $\partial \Omega$. It is denoted ${\Omega}_{0},\partial {\Omega}_{0}$ at the initial time instant.

The medium is defined by:

- parameters (position vector $x$, time $t$),
- variables (displacements, pressures, temperature),
- intrinsic quantities (stresses and strains, mass supplies, heat, enthalpies, hydraulic and thermal fluxes...).

The general hypotheses made are the following:

- hypothesis of small displacements,
- reversible thermodynamic evolutions (not necessarily for the mechanics),
- isotropic behaviour, transversely isotropic (3D), orthotropic (2D),
- the gases are perfect gases,
- ideal mixture of perfect gases (total pressure = sum of the partial pressures),
- thermodynamic equilibrium between the phases of a same constituent.

The various notations are made explicit hereafter.

#### 2.2.1 Descriptive variables of the medium

These are the variables whose knowledge as a function of time and of place makes it possible to know completely the state of the medium. These variables are decomposed into two categories:

- geometric variables,
- thermodynamic state variables.

##### 2.2.1.1 Geometric variables

In all that follows, one adopts a Lagrangian representation with respect to the skeleton (in the sense of   ) and the coordinates $\mathrm{x}={\mathrm{x}}_{\mathrm{s}}(t)$ are those of a material point attached to the skeleton. All the spatial derivation operators are defined with respect to these coordinates.

The displacements of the skeleton are denoted $\mathrm{u}(\mathrm{x},t)=(\begin{array}{c}{u}_{x}\\ {u}_{y}\\ {u}_{z}\end{array})$.

##### 2.2.1.2 Thermodynamic state variables

In a general way, one uses the following indices:

$w$ for liquid water,

$\text{ad}$ for dissolved air,

$\text{as}$ for dry air,

$\text{vp}$ for water vapour.

The thermodynamic variables are:

- the pressures of the constituents: ${p}_{w}(x,t)$, ${p}_{\text{ad}}(x,t)$, ${p}_{\text{vp}}(x,t)$, ${p}_{\text{as}}(x,t)$,
- the temperature of the medium: $T(x,t)$.

These various variables are not totally independent. Indeed, if one considers a single constituent, the thermodynamic equilibrium between its phases imposes a relation between the vapour pressure and the liquid pressure of this constituent. Finally, there is only one independent pressure per constituent, just as there is only one mass-conservation equation. The number of independent pressures is therefore equal to the number of independent constituents. The choice of these pressures is free (combinations of the pressures of the constituents) provided that the pressures chosen, associated with the temperature, form a system of independent variables.

For the so-called saturated case (a single constituent, air or water) we have chosen the pressure of this unique constituent.

For the so-called unsaturated case (presence of air and of water), we have chosen as independent variables:

- the total pressure of the gas: ${p}_{\text{gz}}(x,t)={p}_{\text{vp}}+{p}_{\text{as}}$,
- the capillary pressure: ${p}_{c}(x,t)={p}_{\text{gz}}-{p}_{\text{lq}}={p}_{\text{gz}}-{p}_{w}-{p}_{\text{ad}}$.

These pressures have a very strong physical interpretation, the total gas pressure for obvious reasons, and the capillary pressure, also called suction, because the capillary phenomena are very important in the modelling presented here. It would also have been possible to choose the vapour pressure or the relative humidity degree (ratio between the vapour pressure and the saturated vapour pressure at the same temperature) which is physically accessible. The modelling then becomes more complex and in any case, capillary pressure, gas pressure and relative humidity degree (ratio between the vapour pressure and the saturated vapour pressure) HR are linked by Kelvin’s law.

For the particular case of the behaviour called ‘LIQU_GAZ_ATM’ one makes the hypothesis known as that of Richards: the pores are not saturated by the liquid, but the pressure of the gas is assumed constant and the only pressure variable is the liquid pressure.

##### 2.2.1.3 Descriptive fields of the medium

The principal unknowns, which are also the nodal unknowns (denoted $U(x,t)$ in this document) are:

- 2 or 3 (according to the space dimension) displacements ${u}_{x}(x,t),{u}_{y}(x,t),{u}_{z}(x,t)$ for the modellings KIT_HM, KIT_HHM, KIT_THM, KIT_THHM,
- the temperature $T(x,t)$ for the modellings KIT_THH, KIT_THM, KIT_THHM,
- two pressures ${p}_{1}(\mathrm{x},t),{p}_{2}(\mathrm{x},t)$ (which are ${p}_{c}(x,t)$, ${p}_{\text{gz}}(x,t)$ in the case studied) for the modellings KIT_HHM, KIT_THH, KIT_THHM,
- one pressure ${p}_{1}(x,t)$ (which is ${p}_{w}(x,t)$ or ${p}_{\text{gz}}(x,t)$ according to whether the medium is saturated by a liquid or a gas) for the modellings KIT_H, KIT_HM, KIT_THM.

#### 2.2.2 Particle derivatives

This paragraph partly takes up the paragraph “particle derivatives, volumetric and mass densities” of the document [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10). The description that we make of the medium is Lagrangian with respect to the skeleton.

Let $a$ be an arbitrary field on $\Omega$, let ${x}_{s}(t)$ be the coordinate of a point attached to the skeleton which we follow in its motion and let ${x}_{\mathrm{fl}}(t)$ be the coordinate of a point attached to the fluid. One denotes $\dot{a}=\frac{{d}^{S}a}{\mathrm{dt}}$ the time derivative in the motion of the skeleton:

$\dot{a}=\frac{{d}^{S}a}{\mathrm{dt}}=\underset{\Delta t\to 0}{\lim}\frac{a(x(t+\Delta t),(t+\Delta t)-a(x(t),t))}{\Delta t}$

$\dot{a}$ is called the particle derivative and often denoted $\frac{\mathrm{da}}{\mathrm{dt}}$. We prefer to use a notation which recalls that the configuration used to locate a particle is that of the skeleton with respect to which a fluid particle has a relative velocity. For a fluid particle the locating ${x}_{s}(t)$ is arbitrary, that is to say that the fluid particle which occupies the position ${x}_{s}(t)$ at the time instant $t$ is not the same as that which occupies the position ${\Omega}^{0}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V})(1-\varphi )={\Omega}^{0}(1-{\varphi}^{0})(1+{\varepsilon}_{\text{Vs}})$ at another time instant $t'$.

#### 2.2.3 Quantities

The equilibrium equations are:

- the conservation of momentum for the mechanics,
- the conservation of the fluid masses for the hydraulics,
- the conservation of energy for the thermal analysis.

The writing of these equations is given in the document [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10) , which also defines what we call in a general way a THM constitutive law and gives the definitions of the generalised stresses and strains. The present document uses these definitions. The equilibrium equations involve directly the generalised stresses.

The generalised stresses are linked to the generalised strains by the constitutive laws. The generalised strains are computed directly from the state variables and from their spatial and temporal gradients.

The constitutive laws may use ancillary quantities, often stored in the internal variables. We group here under the term quantity both the stresses, the strains and ancillary quantities.

##### 2.2.3.1 Characteristic quantities of the heterogeneous medium

- The Eulerian porosity: $\phi$.

If one denotes ${\Omega}_{(\phi )}$ the part of the volume $\Omega$ occupied by the voids in the current configuration, one has:

$\varphi =\frac{{\Omega}_{(\varphi )}}{\Omega}$

The definition of the porosity is therefore that of the Eulerian porosity.

- The liquid saturation: ${S}_{\text{lq}}$

If one denotes ${\Omega}_{\text{lq}}$ the total volume occupied by the liquid, in the current configuration, one has by definition:

${S}_{\text{lq}}=\frac{{\Omega}_{\text{lq}}}{{\Omega}_{\phi }}$

This saturation is therefore finally a proportion varying between 0 and 1.

- The Eulerian densities of water ${\rho}_{w}$, of dissolved air ${\rho}_{\text{ad}}$, of dry air ${\rho}_{\text{as}}$, of vapour ${\rho}_{\text{vp}}$, of gas ${\rho}_{\text{gz}}$.

If one denotes ${\gamma}_{w}$ (resp. ${\gamma}_{\text{ad}}$, ${\gamma}_{\text{as}}$, ${\gamma}_{\text{vp}}$) the masses of water (resp. of dissolved air, of dry air and of vapour) contained in a volume of the $\Omega$ skeleton in the current configuration, one has by definition:

$\begin{array}{}{\gamma}_{w}={\int}_{\Omega}{\rho}_{w}{S}_{\text{lq}}\varphi d\Omega {\gamma}_{\text{ad}}={\int}_{\Omega}{\rho}_{\text{ad}}{S}_{\text{lq}}\varphi d\Omega \\ {\gamma}_{\text{as}}={\int}_{\Omega}{\rho}_{\text{as}}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}})\varphi d\Omega {\gamma}_{\text{vp}}={\int}_{\Omega}{\rho}_{\text{vp}}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}})\varphi d\Omega \end{array}$

The density of the gaseous mixture is simply the sum of the densities of the dry air and of the vapour:

${\rho}_{\mathrm{gz}}={\rho}_{\mathrm{as}}+{\rho}_{\mathrm{vp}}$

Likewise for the liquid mixture:

${\rho}_{\text{lq}}={\rho}_{w}+{\rho}_{\text{ad}}$

One denotes ${\rho}_{w}^{0},{\rho}_{\text{ad}}^{0},{\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{0},{\rho}_{\text{as}}^{0}$ the initial values of the densities.

- The homogenised Lagrangian density: $r$.

At the current time instant the mass of the volume $\Omega$, ${M}_{\Omega}$, is given by: ${M}_{\Omega}={\int}_{{\Omega}_{0}}\text{rd}{\Omega}_{0}$.

##### 2.2.3.2 Mechanical quantities

- The strain tensor $\varepsilon (u)(x,t)=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla u+{}^{T}\text{}\nabla u)$.

One will denote ${\epsilon}_{V}=\text{tr}(\varepsilon )$.

- The stress tensor which is exerted on the porous medium: $\sigma$.

This tensor is decomposed into an effective-stress tensor plus a pressure-stress tensor, $\sigma =\sigma '+{\sigma}_{p}$ and ${\sigma}_{p}$ are components of the generalised stresses. This splitting is finally rather arbitrary, but nevertheless corresponds to a hypothesis fairly commonly admitted, at least for media saturated with liquid.

##### 2.2.3.3 Hydraulic quantities

- The mass supplies of constituents ${m}_{w},{m}_{\text{ad}},{m}_{\text{vp}},{m}_{\text{as}}$ (unit: kilogram per cubic metre).

They represent the mass of fluid supplied between the initial and current time instants. They form part of the generalised stresses.

- The hydraulic fluxes ${M}_{w},{M}_{\text{ad}},{M}_{\text{vp}},{M}_{\text{as}}$ (unit: kilogram/second/square metre).

One could very well give no more precise definition of the mass supplies and of the fluxes, considering that their definition amounts to verifying the hydraulic equilibrium equations:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}\dot{{m}_{w}}+{\dot{m}}_{\text{vp}}+\text{Div}({M}_{w}+{M}_{\text{vp}})=0\\ \dot{{m}_{\text{as}}}+{\dot{m}}_{\text{ad}}+\text{Div}({M}_{\text{as}}+{M}_{\text{ad}})=0\end{array}$         eq 2.2.3.3-1

We shall nevertheless specify the physical meaning of these quantities, knowing that what we write now is already a constitutive law.

The velocities of the constituents are measured in a frame fixed in space and in time.

One denotes ${v}_{w}$ the velocity of the water, ${v}_{\mathrm{ad}}$ that of the dissolved air, ${v}_{\mathrm{vp}}$ that of the vapour, ${v}_{\mathrm{as}}$ that of the dry air, and ${v}_{S}=\frac{du}{\mathrm{dt}}$ that of the skeleton.

The mass supplies are defined by:

$\begin{array}{}{m}_{w}={\rho}_{w}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V})\phi {S}_{\text{lq}}-{\rho}_{w}^{0}{\phi }^{0}{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0}\\ {m}_{\text{ad}}={\rho}_{\text{ad}}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V})\phi {S}_{\text{lq}}-{\rho}_{\text{ad}}^{0}{\phi }^{0}{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0}\\ {m}_{\text{as}}={\rho}_{\text{as}}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V})\phi (1-{S}_{\text{lq}})-{\rho}_{\text{as}}^{0}{\phi }^{0}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0})\\ {m}_{\text{vp}}={\rho}_{\text{vp}}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V})\phi (1-{S}_{\text{lq}})-{\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{0}{\phi }^{0}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0})\end{array}$         eq 2.2.3.3-2

The mass fluxes are defined by:

$\begin{array}{}{M}_{w}={\rho}_{w}\varphi {S}_{l}({v}_{w}-{v}_{s})\\ {M}_{\text{ad}}={\rho}_{\text{ad}}\varphi {S}_{l}({v}_{\text{ad}}-{v}_{s})\\ {M}_{\text{as}}={\rho}_{\text{as}}\varphi (1-{S}_{l})({v}_{\text{as}}-{v}_{s})\\ {M}_{\text{vp}}={\rho}_{\text{vp}}\varphi (1-{S}_{l})({v}_{\text{vp}}-{v}_{s})\end{array}$             eq 2.2.3.3-3

The mass supplies make it possible to define the global density seen with respect to the reference configuration:

$r={r}_{0}+{m}_{w}+{m}_{\text{ad}}+{m}_{\text{vp}}+{m}_{\text{as}}$         eq 2.2.3.3-4

where ${r}_{0}$ denotes the homogenised density at the initial time instant.

One introduces other intermediate hydraulic quantities:

- concentration of the vapour density in the gas: ${C}_{\text{vp}}=\frac{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}{{\rho}_{\text{gz}}}$,
- the vapour density ${\rho}_{\text{vp}}$.
- the vapour pressure ${p}_{\text{vp}}$.

##### 2.2.3.4 Thermal quantities

- the non-convected heat ${Q}^{'}$ (see further on) (unit: Joule),
- the mass enthalpies of the constituents ${h}_{ij}^{m}$ (${h}_{w}^{m},{h}_{\text{ad}}^{m},{h}_{\text{vp}}^{m},{h}_{\text{as}}^{m}$ ) (unit: Joule/Kelvin),
- the heat flux: $q$ (unit: $J/s/{m}^{2}$).

All these quantities belong to the generalised stresses in the sense of the document [[R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10) .

#### 2.2.4 External data

- the mass force ${F}^{m}$ (in practice gravity),
- the heat sources $\Theta$,
- the boundary conditions bearing either on imposed variables, or on imposed fluxes.

## 3 Constitutive equations

### 3.1 Conservation equations

This is only a reminder; the way of establishing them is presented in [R7.01.10](doc:r7.01.10) .

#### 3.1.1 Mechanical equilibrium

Denoting by $\sigma$ the tensor of the total mechanical stresses and by $r$ the homogenised density of the medium, the mechanical equilibrium is written:

$\text{Div}(\sigma )+r{F}^{m}=0$                 eq 3.1.1-1

We recall that $r$ is linked to the variations of fluid mass by the relation:

$r={r}_{0}+{m}_{w}+{m}_{\text{ad}}+{m}_{\text{vp}}+{m}_{\text{as}}$             eq 3.1.1-2

#### 3.1.2 Conservation of the fluid masses

For the fluid the derivative $\dot{a}=\frac{{d}^{s}a}{\text{dt}}$ is in fact an Eulerian derivative and the equations that we write for the fluid comprise transport terms, even if they may be hidden by the choice of the unknowns. The conservation equations of the fluid masses are then written:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}\dot{{m}_{w}}+{\dot{m}}_{\text{vp}}+\text{Div}({M}_{w}+{M}_{\text{vp}})=0\\ \dot{{m}_{\text{as}}}+{\dot{m}}_{\text{ad}}+\text{Div}({M}_{\text{as}}+{M}_{\text{ad}})=0\end{array}$             eq 3.1.2-1

#### 3.1.3 Conservation of energy: thermal equation

$\begin{array}{c}{h}_{w}^{m}\dot{{m}_{w}}+{h}_{\text{ad}}^{m}{\dot{m}}_{\text{ad}}+{h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}{\dot{m}}_{\text{vp}}+{h}_{\text{as}}^{m}{\dot{m}}_{\text{as}}+\dot{Q}'+\text{Div}({h}_{w}^{m}{\mathrm{M}}_{\mathrm{w}}+{h}_{\text{ad}}^{m}{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{ad}}+{h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{vp}}+{h}_{\text{as}}^{m}{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{as}})+\text{Div}(\mathrm{q})=\\ ({\mathrm{M}}_{\mathrm{w}}+{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{ad}}+{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{vp}}+{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{as}}){F}^{m}+\Theta \end{array}$ eq 3.1.3-1

### 3.2 Constitutive equations

#### 3.2.1 Evolution of the porosity

##### 3.2.1.1 General isotropic case

$d\varphi =\left(b-\varphi \right)\left(d{\epsilon}_{V}-3{\alpha}_{0}\text{dT}+\frac{{\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}-{S}_{\text{lq}}{\text{dp}}_{c}}{{K}_{s}}\right)$         eq 3.2.1-1

In this equation, one sees appearing the coefficients $b$ and ${K}_{s}$. $b$ is the Biot coefficient and ${K}_{s}$ is the compressibility modulus of the solid grains. If ${K}_{0}$ denotes the “drained” compressibility modulus of the porous medium, one has the relation:

$b=1-\frac{{K}_{0}}{{K}_{s}}$                 eq 3.2.1-2

*Note*:

The expression 3.2.1-1 may appear unusual given the standard definition of the Biot coefficient. This is due to the fact that we use the Eulerian porosity $\phi$ whereas the usual definition of the Biot coefficient is based on the Lagrangian definition $\Phi$ of this quantity.

The two definitions are linked by the relation:

$\Phi =\left(1+{\epsilon}_{V}\right)\varphi$ **eq 3.2.1-3**

In the case of an isothermal evolution for a saturated medium, the variation of Lagrangian porosity is simply proportional to the variation of volume:

$d\Phi =b\,d{\varepsilon}_{V}$ **eq 3.2.1-4**

Carrying eq 3.2.1-4 into eq 3.2.1-3, one finds:

$\left(1+{\epsilon}_{V}\right)d\varphi =\left(b-\varphi \right)d{\epsilon}_{V}$

For ${\varepsilon}_{V}$ small (hypothesis retained in the THM modellings), one obtains $d\varphi =(b-\varphi )d{\varepsilon}_{V}$, which indeed corresponds to [eq 3.2.1-1] for the case examined.

##### 3.2.1.2 Transversely isotropic case

$d\phi =B:d\epsilon -\phi d{\epsilon}_{V}-3{\alpha}_{\phi }\text{dT}+\frac{{\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}-{S}_{\text{lq}}{\text{dp}}_{c}}{{M}_{\phi }}$         eq 3.2.1-5

In this equation, one sees appearing the Biot tensor $B$, the Biot modulus of the solid matrix $\frac{1}{{M}_{\phi }}$ and ${\alpha}_{\phi }$ the differential expansion coefficient.

The expression of the differential expansion coefficient is given by the relation:

${\alpha}_{\phi }=\frac{(B-\phi \delta ):{\alpha}_{0}}{3}$             eq 3.2.1-6

- $\delta$ the identity matrix,
- $B=({b}_{L}({e}_{L}\otimes {e}_{L}+{e}_{T}\otimes {e}_{T})+{b}_{N}({e}_{N}\otimes {e}_{N}))$ the Biot tensor, a function of the mean Biot coefficients ${b}_{L}$ and ${b}_{N}$ along the directions N and L of the local orthotropy frame (L,T,N). In the isotropic case ${b}_{L}={b}_{N}=b$,
- ${\alpha}_{0}=({\alpha}_{L}({e}_{L}\boldsymbol{\otimes}{e}_{L}+{e}_{T}\boldsymbol{\otimes}{e}_{T})+{\alpha}_{N}({e}_{N}\boldsymbol{\otimes}{e}_{N}))$ the thermal expansion tensor, a function of ${\alpha}_{N}$ and ${\alpha}_{L}$ the mean expansion coefficients of the porous medium and of the solid grains along the directions N and L of the local orthotropy frame (L,T,N). In the isotropic case ${\alpha}_{L}\boldsymbol{=}{\alpha}_{N}\boldsymbol{=}{\alpha}_{0}$.

**Note:**

*In the isotropic case the expression of the differential expansion coefficient is given by:*

${\alpha}_{\varphi}=(b-\varphi ){\alpha}_{0}$

The expression of the Biot modulus of the solid matrix is given by the following relation (   and   ):

$\frac{1}{{M}_{\varphi}}=(B-\varphi \delta )\boldsymbol{:}{S}_{0}^{S}\boldsymbol{:}\delta$             eq 3.2.1-7

**Note:**

*In the isotropic case the expression of the Biot modulus of the solid matrix is given by:*

$\frac{1}{{M}_{\varphi}}=3(b-\varphi )(\frac{1\boldsymbol{-}2{\nu}^{S}}{{E}^{S}})$

- ${S}_{0}^{S}$ the compliance matrix of the skeleton, a function of the Young modulus of the solid matrix ${E}^{S}$ and of the Poisson ratio of the solid matrix ${\nu}^{S}$. Assuming that microscopically the skeleton is isotropic and homogeneous  , the expression of the compliance matrix is given by:

${S}_{0}^{S}=(\begin{array}{cccccc}\frac{1}{{E}^{S}}& -\frac{{\nu}^{S}}{{E}^{S}}& -\frac{{\nu}^{S}}{{E}^{S}}& 0& 0& 0\\ -\frac{{\nu}^{S}}{{E}^{S}}& \frac{1}{{E}^{S}}& -\frac{{\nu}^{S}}{{E}^{S}}& 0& 0& 0\\ -\frac{{\nu}^{S}}{{E}^{S}}& -\frac{{\nu}^{S}}{{E}^{S}}& \frac{1}{{E}^{S}}& 0& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0& 2\frac{(1+{\nu}^{S})}{{E}^{S}}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& 0& 0& 2\frac{(1+{\nu}^{S})}{{E}^{S}}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0& 0& 0& 2\frac{(1+{\nu}^{S})}{{E}^{S}}\end{array})$ **eq 3.2.1-8**

In practice it is very difficult to have access to the microscopic parameters ${E}^{S}$ and ${\nu}^{S}$ characterising the solid matrix. We can however dispense with the knowledge of these two parameters by deduction of the compressibility coefficient of the solid grains from the elastic parameters of the porous medium.

Given what has been said previously, we have chosen to fix arbitrarily in the programming the value of ${\nu}^{S}\boldsymbol{=}\boldsymbol{0,3}$ (cf.   ). One then adopts the following approach in order to determine the compliance tensor of the solid matrix:

- one assumes that at the microscopic scale, the solid matrix is homogeneous. Moreover one considers that the volumetric strain of the solid matrix between the initial time instant and the final time instant (after mechanical loading) is negligible. Thus the definition of the compressibility modulus of the solid grains is given by:

${K}_{S}=\frac{{E}^{S}}{3\left(1-2{\nu}^{S}\right)}$

- the expressions of the components of the Biot tensor, along the axes $L$ and $N$ of the local orthotropy frame, are given by  :

$\boldsymbol{\{}\begin{array}{c}{b}_{L}\boldsymbol{=}1\boldsymbol{-}\frac{{M}_{11}+{M}_{12}+{M}_{13}}{3{K}_{S}}\\ {b}_{N}\boldsymbol{=}1\boldsymbol{-}\frac{2{M}_{13}+{M}_{33}}{3{K}_{S}}\end{array}$

with:

${M}_{11}\boldsymbol{=}\frac{{E}_{L}({E}_{N}\boldsymbol{-}{E}_{L}{\nu}_{\text{LN}}^{2})}{(1+{\nu}_{\text{LT}})({E}_{N}\boldsymbol{-}{E}_{N}{\nu}_{\text{LT}}\boldsymbol{-}2{E}_{L}{\nu}_{\text{LN}}^{2})}$

${M}_{12}=\frac{{E}_{L}({E}_{N}{\nu}_{\text{LT}}+{E}_{N}{\nu}_{\text{LN}}{\nu}_{\text{LT}}+{E}_{L}{\nu}_{\text{LN}}^{2})}{(1+{\nu}_{\text{LT}})({E}_{N}-{E}_{N}{\nu}_{\text{LT}}-2{E}_{L}{\nu}_{\text{LN}}^{2})}$

${M}_{13}\boldsymbol{=}\frac{{E}_{L}{E}_{N}{\nu}_{\text{LN}}}{(1+{\nu}_{\text{LT}})({E}_{N}\boldsymbol{-}{E}_{N}{\nu}_{\text{LT}}\boldsymbol{-}2{E}_{L}{\nu}_{\text{LN}}^{2})}$

${M}_{33}\boldsymbol{=}\frac{{E}_{L}^{2}(1\boldsymbol{-}{\nu}_{\text{LT}})}{{E}_{N}\boldsymbol{-}{E}_{N}{\nu}_{\text{LT}}\boldsymbol{-}2{E}_{L}{\nu}_{\text{LN}}^{2}}$

Considering one or the other of the two expressions above (given the isotropy hypothesis made on ${K}_{S}$), we are then in a position to isolate ${K}_{S}$.

- from the expression of ${K}_{S}$ determined previously, one can have access to the Young modulus ${E}^{S}$ of the solid matrix knowing that:

$\{\begin{array}{c}{\nu}_{S}=\boldsymbol{0,3}\\ {E}_{S}=3\left(1-2{\nu}^{S}\right){K}^{S}\end{array}$ **eq 3.2.1-9**

##### 3.2.1.3 Case without mechanical coupling

For the purely hydraulic or thermo-hydraulic modellings without mechanical coupling, it is however possible to vary the porosity via a storage coefficient ${E}_{m}$. The latter then links the variation of porosity to the variation of liquid pressure such that:

$d\varphi ={E}_{\boldsymbol{m.}}{\boldsymbol{dp}}_{\text{lq}}$

This coefficient is not taken into account in the case of the modellings with mechanics (* HM* )

#### 3.2.2 Evolution of the fluid mass supplies

Using the definition of the fluid mass supplies and putting forward purely geometric arguments, one finds:

$\begin{array}{}{m}_{\text{w}}={\rho}_{\text{w}}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V})\varphi {S}_{\text{lq}}-{\rho}_{\text{w}}^{0}{\varphi}^{0}{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0}\\ {m}_{\text{ad}}={\rho}_{\text{ad}}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V})\varphi {S}_{\text{lq}}-{\rho}_{\text{ad}}^{0}{\varphi}^{0}{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0}\\ {m}_{\text{as}}={\rho}_{\text{as}}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V})\varphi (1-{S}_{\text{lq}})-{\rho}_{\text{as}}^{0}{\varphi}^{0}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0})\\ {m}_{\text{vp}}={\rho}_{\text{vp}}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V})\varphi (1-{S}_{\text{lq}})-{\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{0}{\varphi}^{0}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{0})\end{array}$         eq 3.2.2-1

**Note:**

*If we used the Lagrangian porosity, the mass supplies would be written:*

#### 3.2.6 Water–vapour equilibrium

This relation is essential and it has the consequence of reducing the number of pressure unknowns.

One denotes ${h}_{\text{w}}^{m}$ the mass enthalpy of the water, ${s}_{\text{w}}^{m}$ its entropy and ${g}_{\text{w}}^{m}={h}_{\text{w}}^{m}-{\text{Ts}}_{\text{w}}^{m}$ its free enthalpy.

One denotes ${h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}$ the mass enthalpy of the vapour, ${s}_{\text{vp}}^{m}$ its entropy and ${g}_{\text{vp}}^{m}={h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{\text{Ts}}_{\text{vp}}^{m}$ its free enthalpy.

The water–vapour equilibrium is written:

${g}_{\text{vp}}^{m}={g}_{\text{w}}^{m}$                 eq 3.2.6-1

Which gives:

${h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{\text{w}}^{m}=T({s}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{s}_{\text{w}}^{m})$             eq 3.2.6-2

Moreover, the definition of the free enthalpy teaches us that: $\text{dg}=\frac{\text{dp}}{\rho}-\text{sdT}$, which, applied to the vapour and to the water, combined with the relation ${\text{dg}}_{{}_{\text{vp}}}^{m}={\text{dg}}_{{}_{\text{w}}}^{m}$ and using [eq 3.2.6-2] gives:

$\frac{{\text{dp}}_{\text{vp}}}{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}=\frac{{\text{dp}}_{\text{w}}}{{\rho}_{\text{w}}}+({h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{\text{w}}^{m})\frac{\text{dT}}{T}$         eq 3.2.6-3

This relation may be expressed as a function of the capillary pressure and of the gas pressure:

${\text{dp}}_{\text{vp}}=\frac{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}{{\rho}_{\text{w}}}({\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}-{\text{dp}}_{c}-{\text{dp}}_{\text{ad}})+{\rho}_{\text{vp}}({h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{\text{w}}^{m})\frac{\text{dT}}{T}$         eq 3.2.6-4

#### 3.2.7 Dry-air–dissolved-air equilibrium

The dissolved air is defined via the Henry constant ${K}_{H}$, which links the molar concentration of dissolved air ${C}_{\text{ad}}^{\text{ol}}$ ($\mathit{moles}\boldsymbol{/}{m}^{3}$) to the dry-air pressure:

${C}_{\text{ad}}^{\text{ol}}=\frac{{p}_{\text{as}}}{{K}_{H}}$             eq 3.2.7-1

with ${C}_{\text{ad}}^{\text{ol}}=\frac{{\rho}_{\text{ad}}}{{M}_{\text{ad}}^{\text{ol}}}$             eq 3.2.7-2

The molar mass of the dissolved air, ${M}_{\text{ad}}^{\text{ol}}$ is logically the same as that of the dry air ${M}_{\text{as}}^{\text{ol}}$. For the dissolved air, one takes the perfect-gas law:

$\frac{{p}_{\text{ad}}}{{\boldsymbol{\rho}}_{\text{ad}}}=\frac{R}{{M}_{\text{as}}^{\text{ol}}}T$             eq 3.2.7-3

The dissolved-air pressure is therefore linked to that of the dry air by:

${p}_{\text{ad}}=\frac{{p}_{\text{as}}}{{K}_{H}}\text{RT}$             eq 3.2.7-4

#### 3.2.8 The mechanical behaviour

One will write it in differential form:

$d\sigma =d\sigma '+d{\sigma}_{p}$             eq 3.2.8-1

In the general case (excluding the law ‘HYDR_TABBAL’), using a Bishop formulation extended to unsaturated media one writes:

$d{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{p}=-B({\mathit{dp}}_{\text{gz}}-{S}_{\text{lq}}{\mathit{dp}}_{c})$         eq 3.2.8-2

(which indeed corresponds in the saturated case to the classical Biot formulation $d{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{p}=-B{\mathit{dp}}_{\text{lq}}$).

One therefore sees appearing in the formula [eq 3.2.8-2], the Biot tensor. In the isotropic case $B=b\,1$ and ${\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{p}={\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{p}\,1$.

This formulation is valid for “reasonable” ranges of drying, namely relative humidities (or hygrometric degree) HR between 50% and 100%. One recalls that the relative humidity HR and the capillary pressure are linked by Kelvin’s law (cf. section  3.3 ).

For ranges of relative humidities going down below 50%, the model resulting from the thesis of Ginger El Tabbal is recommended. This model is called by the keyword HYDR_TABBAL under RELATION_KIT. In addition to the capillary effects used in the formulation above, the effect of adsorption is here taken into account. In this case, the hydraulic stress is written in the form $d{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}_{p}=-Bd\boldsymbol{\pi}$ with:

$\begin{array}{c}d\boldsymbol{\pi}={\mathit{dp}}_{\text{gz}}-{S}_{\mathit{BJH}}{\mathit{dp}}_{\text{c}}-\frac{1}{3}{p}_{\text{c}}({S}_{\mathit{BJH}}){\mathit{dS}}_{\mathit{BJH}}\\ +\frac{2}{3}({A}_{0}/{\boldsymbol{\phi }}^{0})(-({\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\mathit{BJH}}({S}_{\mathit{BJH}})\mathit{dt}(\mathit{HR})+t(\mathit{HR})d{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\mathit{BJH}}({S}_{\mathit{BJH}}))\boldsymbol{.}{\mathit{dp}}_{\text{c}}-{\left(\frac{\partial \gamma }{\partial {\epsilon}_{s}}\right)|}_{\mu}d{\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\mathit{BJH}}({S}_{\mathit{BJH}}))\end{array}$     eq 3.2.8-3

With ${S}_{\mathit{BJH}}$, ${A}_{0}$, ${\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\mathit{BJH}}$, $t(\mathit{HR})$ and ${\left(\frac{\partial \gamma }{\partial {\epsilon}_{s}}\right)|}_{\mu}$ the input data of the model computed beforehand. One gives here their definition:

${S}_{\mathit{BJH}}$ Volumetric fraction of liquid water as a function of HR (non-adsorbed water). This function is computed by the BJH method.

${A}_{0}$ Total surface of the pores (or specific surface of the material) per unit volume m²/m3. It is the product of the dry density (g/m3) of the material by the specific surface (m²/g) determined by the BET method or the BJH method.

${\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\mathit{BJH}}$ Surface fraction of the unsaturated pores as a function of the relative humidity HR. It is the ratio between the surface of the unsaturated pores (per unit volume) determined by the BJH method (at a given relative humidity) and the total surface of the pores ${A}_{0}$.

$t(\mathit{HR})$ Thickness of the adsorbed water layer. This thickness is evaluated by an empirical relation known as that of Badmann.

${\left(\frac{\partial \gamma }{\partial {\epsilon}_{s}}\right)|}_{\mu}$ Shuttleworth term, strongly dependent on the orientation and on the morphology of the solid surface. This term is fitted from drying-shrinkage experiments.

${S}_{\mathit{BJH}}$, ${A}_{0}$, ${\boldsymbol{\omega}}_{\mathit{BJH}}$, are therefore computed from experimental data by numerical methods known as BET or BJH. The methodology for obtaining these parameters as well as the thickness of the adsorbed layer and the Shuttleworth parameter is described in detail in the doc U2.04.05.

In the relation [eq 3.2.8-1] the evolution of the effective stress tensor ${\sigma}^{'}$ is assumed to depend only on the displacement of the skeleton and on the internal variables $\alpha$. The usual terms linked to the thermal strain are integrated into the computation of the effective stress:

$d\sigma '=f(d\varepsilon -{\alpha}_{0}\text{dT}I,d\alpha )$         eq 3.2.8-4

The reason for this choice is to be able to use any classical thermomechanical law for the computation of the effective stresses, laws which, in most writings, conform to [eq 3.2.8-3].

In practice, the thermal strain is evaluated by the following formula:

$$
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{\mathit{th}}\left(T\right)={\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{0}\left(T-{T}_{\mathit{ini}}\right)
$$

The reference temperature is given by THM_INIT/TEMP in DEFI_MATERIAU.

#### 3.2.9 Details on the two-phase transfer terms

##### 3.2.9.1 The sorption isotherm

In order to close the system, there still remains a relation to write, linking the saturation and the pressures. We have chosen to consider that the liquid saturation was an arbitrary function of the capillary pressure, that this function was a characteristic of the porous medium and supplied as data by the user.

Given that the user may very well supply a function ${S}_{\text{lq}}({p}_{c})$ piecewise affine, and given that the derivative of this function, $\frac{\partial {S}_{\text{lq}}}{\partial {p}_{c}}$, plays an essential physical role, we have chosen to ask the user also to supply this curve, it remaining his responsibility to ensure the consistency of the data thus specified. There exists however for the user the possibility of calling on an analytical model of saturation and its derivative coded “hard-wired” in the source: the Mualem–Van Genuchten model (see the following section).

One notes that in the present approach, one speaks of a one-to-one relation between saturation and capillary pressure. One knows that for most porous media, it is not the same relation which must be used for the drying paths and for the hydration paths. This is one of the limits of the present approach.

##### 3.2.9.2 The Mualem – Van Genuchten model

Concerning the hydraulic behaviour, the user currently has two choices: to enter manually, and in tabulated form, the relative permeabilities, the saturation laws and their derivatives while ensuring their consistency (keyword HYDR_UTIL or HYDR_TABBAL under RELATION_KIT), or to call on a known model programmed in analytical form: the Mualem – Van Genuchten model (keyword HYDR_VGM or HYDR_VGC under RELATION_KIT).

*Note:*

*There certainly exist other classical models for describing the hydraulic behaviour (Brooks–Corey for example) but they are currently not available in Aster. It is then up to the user to enter them in tabulated form in the command file. The Mualem–Van Genuchten model is a classical model for the description of clayey materials typical of underground storage problems. For the relative permeability to the gas, it is common to use either a Van Genuchten version, or a cubic version.*

The Mualem–Van Genuchten model is expressed by a saturation/capillary-pressure law (Van Genuchten to which one adds an entry pressure) such that:

${S}_{\text{we}}=\frac{1}{{\left[1+{\left(\frac{{P}_{c}-{P}_{e}}{{P}_{r}}\right)}^{n}\right]}^{m}}$

with ${S}_{\text{we}}=\frac{S-{S}_{\text{wr}}}{1-{S}_{\text{wr}}}$ and $m=1-\frac{1}{n}$

$n$, ${S}_{\text{wr}}$ (residual water saturation) and ${P}_{r}$ are parameters of the model supplied by the user in his command file. ${P}_{e}$ is also a parameter (by default taken equal to 0) corresponding to an entry pressure (the medium desaturates only if the capillary pressure is greater than this pressure.

The relative permeability to water is then expressed by integrating the prediction model proposed by Mualem (1976) into the capillary model of Van Genuchten:

${k}_{r}^{w}=\sqrt{{S}_{\text{we}}}{(1-{(1-{S}_{{\text{we}}^{1/m}})}^{m})}^{2}$

The permeability to the gas is formulated in a similar way in the case of the model HYDR_VGM:

${k}_{r}^{\text{gz}}=\sqrt{(1-{S}_{\text{we}})}{(1-{S}_{{\text{we}}^{1/m}})}^{2m}$

or else by a simple cubic law in the case of HYDR_VGC:

${k}_{r}^{\text{gz}}={(1-S)}^{3}$

In *code_aster*, we carry out a numerical treatment of this model in the “limit” zones ($S\boldsymbol{=}1$ or $S\boldsymbol{=}0$). For this we use two additional parameters corresponding to a treatment that one carries out on these curves, $\mathit{Smax}$ and $\mathit{CSAT}$:

For $S>\mathit{Smax}$, these curves are interpolated by a polynomial of degree 2 $\mathit{C1}$ at $\mathit{Smax}$, so as to avoid having to treat derivatives of infinite values. Indeed, for $S\boldsymbol{=}1$:

$\frac{\partial {k}_{r}^{w}(S)}{\partial S}=\infty$

and for the case HYDR_VGM $\frac{\partial {k}_{r}^{\text{gz}}(S)}{\partial S}=\infty$

In order to avoid then having to treat this problem (which a priori has no physical meaning) one replaces these functions from a saturation $\mathit{Smax}$ by a second-order polynomial ${C}^{1}$ at this point.

Which gives for the function ${k}_{r}^{w}(S)$:

For $S\boldsymbol{=}\mathit{Smax}$, one determines the polynomial $\mathit{PL}(S)$ such that:

$\{\begin{array}{}\text{PL}(Smax)={k}_{r}^{w}(Smax)\\ P{L}^{'}(Smax)=\frac{\partial {k}_{r}^{w}}{\partial S}(Smax)\end{array}$ and $\mathit{PL}(1)\boldsymbol{=}0$

For $S>\mathit{Smax}$, ${k}_{r}^{w}(S)$ is replaced by $\mathit{PL}(S)$.

And for ${k}_{r}^{\text{gz}}(S)$ in the case HYDR_VGM:

For $S\boldsymbol{=}\mathit{Smax}$, one determines the polynomial $\mathit{PG}(S)$ such that:

$\{\begin{array}{}\text{PG}(Smax)={k}_{r}^{\boldsymbol{gz}}(Smax)\\ P{G}^{'}(Smax)=\frac{\partial {k}_{r}^{\boldsymbol{gz}}}{\partial S}(Smax)\end{array}$ and $\mathit{PG}(0)\boldsymbol{=}1$

For $S>\mathit{Smax}$, ${k}_{r}^{\text{gz}}(S)$ is replaced by $\mathit{PG}(S)$.

For the suction $S(\mathit{Pc})$ and for $\mathit{Pc}<\mathit{Pcmin}$ (with $S(\mathit{Pcmin})\boldsymbol{=}\mathit{Smax}$) one extends the curve $S(\mathit{Pc})$ by a hyperbola such that the curve is ${C}^{1}$ at this point:

For $S>\mathit{Smax}$:

> $S(\text{Pc})=1-\frac{A}{B-\text{Pc}}$

with $A$ and $B$ such that the curve is $\mathit{C1}$ at $\mathit{Smax}$.

One therefore indeed has a decreasing curve which tends towards 1 when $\mathit{Pc}$ tends towards $\boldsymbol{-}\boldsymbol{\infty}$. This treatment allows us to manage negative capillary pressures (in these zones the term capillary pressure is abusive; it is implicitly a change of variable making it possible to treat a quasi-saturated problem).

$S(\mathit{Pc})$ is then multiplied by a “safety” coefficient $\mathit{CSAT}$ so that the saturation never reaches 1 (a problem that one does not know how to treat). One advises taking a value of $\mathit{CSAT}$ very close to 1 (0.999999 for example).

### 3.3 The reference state and the initial state

The introduction of the initial conditions is very important, notably for the enthalpies.

In practice, one may reason by considering that one has three states for the fluids:

- the current state,
- the reference state: it is that of the fluids in the free state. Very often one will take for the water and air pressures the atmospheric pressure. In this reference state, one may consider that the enthalpies are zero,
- the initial state: it is important to note that, in the initial state of the porous medium, the water is in a hygroscopic state different from that of free water. For the enthalpies of the water and of the vapour one will have to take:

$\begin{array}{c}{}^{\text{init}}{h}_{\text{w}}^{m}=\frac{{p}_{\text{w}}^{\text{init}}-{p}_{l}^{\text{ref}}}{{\rho}_{\text{w}}}=\frac{{p}_{\text{w}}^{\text{init}}-{p}_{\text{atm}}}{{\rho}_{\text{w}}}\\ {}^{\text{init}}{h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}=L({T}^{\text{init}})=\text{chaleur latente de vaporisation}\\ \begin{array}{}{}^{\text{init}}{h}_{\text{as}}^{m}=0\\ {}^{\text{init}}{h}_{\text{ad}}^{m}=0\end{array}\end{array}$

**Notes:**

*The initial vapour pressure must be taken consistently with the* *other data. Very often, one starts from the knowledge of an initial hygrometric state. The hygrometric degree is the ratio between the vapour pressure and the saturated vapour pressure at the temperature considered. One then uses Kelvin’s law which gives the pressure of the liquid as a function of the vapour pressure, of the temperature and of the saturated vapour pressure:* $\frac{{p}_{\text{w}}-{p}_{\text{w}}^{\text{ref}}}{{\rho}_{\text{w}}}=\frac{R}{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}Tln(\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}}{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{sat}}(T)})$ *. This relation is valid only for isothermal evolutions. One emphasises that* ${p}_{\text{w}}^{\text{ref}}$ *corresponds to an equilibrium state to which corresponds* ${p}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{sat}}$ *, this equilibrium state in fact corresponds to* ${p}_{\text{w}}^{0}={p}_{\text{gz}}^{0}=1$ *atm. For evolutions with variation of temperature, knowing a law giving the saturated vapour pressure at the temperature* ${T}_{0}$ *, for example:* ${p}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{sat}}({T}_{0})={10}^{(2\,7858+\frac{{T}_{0}-273\,5}{31\,559+\,1354({T}_{0}-273\,5)})}$ *, and a hygrometric degree* $\text{HR}$ *, one deduces from it the vapour pressure thanks to* ${p}_{\text{vp}}({T}_{0})=\text{HR}{p}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{sat}}({T}_{0})$ *.*

### 3.5 Effective stresses and total stresses. Stress boundary conditions

The partition of the stresses into total and effective stresses is written:

$\sigma =\sigma '+{\sigma}_{p}$

$\sigma$ is the total stress, that is to say that which verifies: $\text{Div}(\sigma )+r{F}^{m}=0$

${\sigma}^{'}$ is the effective stress. For the effective-stress laws, it verifies: $d{\sigma}^{'}=f(d\varepsilon -{\alpha}_{0}\text{dT},\alpha )$, where $\varepsilon =1/2(\nabla u+{}^{T}\nabla u)$ and $\alpha$ represents the internal variables. The tensor ${\sigma}_{p}$ is computed as a function of the hydraulic pressures. The writing adopted is incremental and, if one wishes the value of ${\sigma}_{p}$ to be consistent with the value ${p}^{\text{init}}$ defined under the keyword THM_INIT, one must initialise the components of ${\sigma}_{p}$ by the keyword SIGM of the factor keyword ETAT_INIT of the command [STAT_NON_LINE](doc:u4.51.03).

In the result files, one finds the effective stresses ${\sigma}^{'}$ under the component names SIXX ... and ${\sigma}_{p}$ under the name SIPXX … . The stress boundary conditions are written in total stresses.

#### 4.1.4 Vapour and air pressures

Starting from the relation [eq 3.2.6-4] into which one carries the perfect-gas constitutive law [eq3.2.3.2-1], one finds $\frac{{\text{dp}}_{\text{vp}}}{{p}_{\text{vp}}}=\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{\text{RT}}(\frac{1}{{\rho}_{w}}{\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}-\frac{1}{{\rho}_{w}}{\text{dp}}_{c}+({h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{w}^{m})\frac{\text{dT}}{T})$ which one integrates along a path first at constant temperature (one then considers the density of the water constant), then from ${T}^{-}$ to ${T}^{+}$ at constant pressures.

$\ln(\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{-}})=\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{{\text{RT}}^{+}}\frac{1}{{\rho}_{w}^{+}}\left[({p}_{{\text{gz}}^{+}}-{p}_{\text{gz}}^{-})-({p}_{{c}^{+}}-{p}_{c}^{-})\right]+\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{R}{\int}_{{T}^{-}}^{{T}^{+}}({h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{w}^{m})\frac{\text{dT}}{{T}^{2}}$

The first term corresponds to the path at constant temperature, the second to the path at constant pressures. Using the definitions [eq 3.2.4.1-1] and [eq 3.2.4.2-1] of the enthalpies, one sees that for an evolution at constant pressures:

$\frac{{h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{w}^{m}}{{T}^{2}}=\frac{{h}_{\text{vp}}^{{m}^{-}}-{h}_{w}^{{m}^{-}}}{{T}^{2}}+\frac{({C}_{\text{vp}}^{p}-{C}_{w}^{p})(T-{T}^{-})}{{T}^{2}}$

One therefore has for such a path:

${\int}_{{T}^{-}}^{{T}^{+}}({h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{w}^{m})\frac{\text{dT}}{{T}^{2}}=({h}_{\text{vp}}^{{m}^{-}}-{h}_{w}^{{m}^{-}})(\frac{1}{{T}^{-}}-\frac{1}{{T}^{+}})+({C}_{\text{vp}}^{p}-{C}_{w}^{p})(\ln(\frac{{T}^{+}}{{T}^{-}})+{T}^{-}(\frac{1}{{T}^{+}}-\frac{1}{{T}^{-}}))$

That is finally:

$\begin{array}{}\ln(\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{-}})=\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{{\text{RT}}^{+}}\frac{1}{{\rho}_{w}^{+}}\left[({p}_{{\text{gz}}^{+}}-{p}_{\text{gz}}^{-})-({p}_{{c}^{+}}-{p}_{c}^{-})\right]+\\ \frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{R}({h}_{\text{vp}}^{{m}^{-}}-{h}_{w}^{{m}^{-}})(\frac{1}{{T}^{-}}-\frac{1}{{T}^{+}})+\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{R}({C}_{\text{vp}}^{p}-{C}_{w}^{p})(\ln(\frac{{T}^{+}}{{T}^{-}})+\frac{{T}^{-}}{{T}^{+}}-1)\end{array}$     eq 4.1.4-1

One may then compute the densities of the vapour and of the air by the relations [eq3.2.3.2-1] and [eq3.2.3.2-2]:

${\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{+}=\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{R}\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{T}^{+}}$             eq 4.1.4-2

${\rho}_{\text{as}}^{+}=\frac{{M}_{\text{as}}^{\text{ol}}}{R}\frac{({p}_{\text{gz}}^{+}-{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+})}{{T}^{+}}$             eq 4.1.4-3

#### 4.1.5 Computation of the mass supplies

The equations [eq 3.2.2-1] give zero mass supplies at time instant 0. One writes in incremental form the equations [eq 3.2.2-1]:

$\begin{array}{}{m}_{w}^{+}={m}_{w}^{-}+{\rho}_{w}^{+}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V}^{+}){\varphi}^{+}{S}_{\text{lq}}^{+}-{\rho}_{w}^{-}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V}^{-}){\varphi}^{-}{S}_{\text{lq}}^{-}\\ {m}_{\text{as}}^{+}={m}_{\text{as}}^{-}+{\rho}_{\text{as}}^{+}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V}^{+}){\varphi}^{+}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{+})-{\rho}_{\text{as}}^{-}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V}^{-}){\varphi}^{-}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{-})\\ {m}_{\text{vp}}^{+}={m}_{\text{vp}}^{-}+{\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{+}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V}^{+}){\varphi}^{+}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{+})-{\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{-}(1+{\varepsilon}_{V}^{-}){\varphi}^{-}(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{-})\end{array}$         eq 4.1.5-1

#### 4.1.6 Computation of the heat capacity and of the heat Q’

One now has all the elements for applying at the end of the time step the formula [eq 3.2.4.3-5]:

${C}_{\sigma}^{{0}^{+}}=(1-{\phi }^{+}){\rho}_{s}{C}_{\sigma}^{s}+{S}_{\text{lq}}^{+}{\phi }^{+}{\rho}_{w}^{+}{C}_{w}^{p}+(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{+}){\phi }^{+}({\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{+}{C}_{\text{vp}}^{p}+{\rho}_{\text{as}}^{+}{C}_{\text{as}}^{p})$ eq 4.1.6-1

One of course uses [eq 3.2.4.3-4] which gives:

${C}_{\varepsilon}^{0+}={C}_{\sigma}^{0+}-{T}^{+}({C}_{0}:{\alpha}_{0}):{\alpha}_{0}$     eq 4.1.6-2

**Note:**

In the isotropic case [eq 4.1.6-2] becomes:

${C}_{\varepsilon}^{0+}={C}_{\sigma}^{0+}-9{T}^{+}{K}_{0}{\alpha}_{0}^{2}$

Although the heat variation $\delta {Q}^{'}$ is not a total differential, it is nevertheless legitimate to integrate it over the time step and one obtains by integrating [eq 3.2.4.3-1].

$Q{'}^{+}=Q{'}^{-}+({C}_{0}:{\alpha}_{0}):({\varepsilon}^{+}-{\varepsilon}^{-}){T}^{}+3{\alpha}_{\text{lq}}^{{m}^{+}}{T}^{}({p}_{c}^{+}-{p}_{{c}_{}}^{-})-(3{\alpha}_{\text{gz}}^{{m}^{+}}+3{\alpha}_{\text{lq}}^{{m}^{+}}){T}^{}({p}_{{\text{gz}}_{}}^{+}-{p}_{{\text{gz}}_{}}^{-})+{C}_{\varepsilon}^{{0}^{+}}({T}^{+}-{T}^{-})$ eq 4.1.6-3

where we have denoted: ${T}^{}=\frac{{T}^{+}+{T}^{-}}{2}$. We have chosen here a “mid-point” formula for the temperature variable.

**Note:**

In the isotropic case [eq 4.1.6-3] becomes:

$Q{'}^{+}=Q{'}^{-}+(3{K}_{0}{\alpha}_{0})({\varepsilon}_{V}^{+}-{\varepsilon}_{V}^{-}){T}^{}+3{\alpha}_{\text{lq}}^{{m}^{+}}{T}^{}({p}_{c}^{+}-{p}_{{c}_{}}^{-})-(3{\alpha}_{\text{gz}}^{{m}^{+}}+3{\alpha}_{\text{lq}}^{{m}^{+}}){T}^{}({p}_{{\text{gz}}_{}}^{+}-{p}_{{\text{gz}}_{}}^{-})+{C}_{\varepsilon}^{{0}^{+}}({T}^{+}-{T}^{-})$

#### 4.1.7 Computation of the mechanical stresses

The computation of the effective stresses is done by invoking the incremental mechanical laws chosen by the user. One integrates over the time step [eq 3.2.8-2] and one has:

${\sigma}_{p}^{+}={\sigma}_{p}^{-}-B({p}_{\mathit{gz}}^{+}-{p}_{\mathit{gz}}^{-})+B{S}_{\mathit{lq}}^{+}({p}_{c}^{+}-{p}_{c}^{-})$     eq 4.1.7-1

In the isotropic case one has $B=b.1$, ${\sigma}_{p}^{+}={\sigma}_{p}^{+}.1$ and ${\sigma}_{p}^{-}={\sigma}_{p}^{-}.1$

#### 4.1.8 Computation of the hydraulic and thermal fluxes

One must of course compute all the diffusion coefficients:

The Fick coefficient ${F}^{+}=F({T}^{+},{p}_{c}^{+},{p}_{\text{gz}}^{+})$

The thermal diffusivity tensor ${\lambda}^{T+}={\lambda}_{\phi }^{T}({\varphi}^{+}).{\lambda}_{S}^{T}({S}_{\text{lq}}^{+}).{\lambda}_{T}^{T}({T}^{+})+{\lambda}_{\text{cte}}^{T}$

The permeability and hydraulic conductivity tensors: ${\lambda}_{\text{lq}}^{{H}^{+}}=\frac{{K}^{int}({\varphi}^{+}).{k}_{w}^{\text{rel}}({S}_{\text{lq}}^{+})}{{\mu}_{w}({T}^{+})}{\lambda}_{\text{gz}}^{{H}^{+}}=\frac{{K}^{int}({\varphi}^{+}).{k}_{\text{gz}}^{\text{rel}}({S}_{\text{lq}}^{+},{p}_{\text{gz}}^{+})}{{\mu}_{\text{gz}}({T}^{+})}$

In the isotropic case, ${K}^{int}={K}^{int}.1$, ${\lambda}^{T}={\lambda}^{T}.1$, ${\lambda}_{T}^{T}(T)={\lambda}_{T}^{T}(T).1$ and ${\lambda}_{\mathit{cte}}^{T}={\lambda}_{\mathit{cte}}^{T}.1$

The vapour-density concentration: ${C}_{\text{vp}}^{+}=\frac{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{\rho}_{\text{gz}}^{+}}$

It then only remains to apply the formulae [eq 3.2.5.1-1], [eq 3.2.5.2-15], [eq 3.2.5.2-16] and [eq3.2.5.2-17] in order to find:

${\mathrm{q}}^{+}=-{\lambda}^{{T}^{+}}\nabla{T}^{+}$             eq 4.1.8-1

$\frac{{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{as}}^{+}}{{\rho}_{\text{as}}^{+}}={\lambda}_{\text{gz}}^{{H}^{+}}\left[-\nabla{p}_{\text{gz}}^{+}+({\rho}_{{\text{as}}_{}}^{+}+{\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{+}){\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{m}}\right]+{F}_{\text{vp}}^{+}{\rho}_{\text{gz}}^{+}\nabla{C}_{\text{vp}}^{+}$     eq 4.1.8-2

$\frac{{\mathrm{M}}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}={\lambda}_{\text{gz}}^{{H}^{+}}\left[-\nabla{p}_{\text{gz}}^{+}+({\rho}_{\text{as}}^{+}+{\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{+}){\mathrm{F}}^{\mathrm{m}}\right]-{F}_{\text{vp}}^{+}{\rho}_{\text{gz}}^{+}\nabla{C}_{\text{vp}}^{+}$     eq 4.1.8-3

$\frac{{\mathrm{M}}_{w}^{+}}{{\rho}_{w}^{+}}={\lambda}_{\text{lq}}^{{H}^{+}}\left[-\nabla{p}_{\text{lq}}^{+}+{\rho}_{w}^{+}{F}^{m}\right]$         eq 4.1.8-4

In the isotropic cases, ${\lambda}_{{\text{lq}}_{}}^{H}={\lambda}_{{\text{lq}}_{}}^{H}.1$ and ${\lambda}_{{\text{gz}}_{}}^{H}={\lambda}_{{\text{gz}}_{}}^{H}.1$.

##### 4.2.1.1 Computation of the porosity: isotropic case

$({\varphi}^{+}-{\varphi}^{-})=B:({\varepsilon}^{+}-{\varepsilon}^{-})-{\varphi}^{-}({\varepsilon}_{\text{v}}^{+}-{\varepsilon}_{\text{v}}^{-})-3{\alpha}_{\varphi}({T}^{+}-{T}^{-})+\frac{({p}_{\text{gz}}^{+}-{p}_{\text{gz}}^{-})-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{+}({p}_{\text{c}}^{+}-{p}_{\text{c}}^{-})}{{M}_{\varphi}}$

#### 4.2.2 Computation of the expansion coefficients

In the same way, the differential thermal expansion coefficient is recomputed from the porosity evaluated at the end of the time step. Given this one sets:

${\alpha}_{\varphi}^{+}=\frac{(B-{\varphi}^{+}\delta ):{\alpha}_{0}}{3}$ **eq 4.2.2-1**

**Note:**

In the isotropic case [eq 4.2.2-1] becomes:

${\alpha}_{\varphi}^{+}=(b-{\varphi}^{+}){\alpha}_{0}$

It is then a simple application of the formulae [eq 3.2.4.3-2] and [eq 3.2.4.3-3], which are evaluated at the end of the time step:

${\alpha}_{\text{vp}}^{{m}^{+}}={a}_{\text{as}}^{m+}={a}_{\text{gz}}^{m+}=\left(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{+}\right){\alpha}_{\phi }^{+}+\frac{{\phi }^{+}\left(1-{S}_{\text{lq}}^{+}\right)}{{\mathrm{3T}}^{+}}$     eq 4.2.2-2

${\alpha}_{\text{ad}}^{{m}^{+}}={S}_{\text{lq}}^{+}{\alpha}_{\phi }^{+}+\frac{{\phi }^{+}{S}_{\text{lq}}^{+}}{{\mathrm{3T}}^{+}}$             eq 4.2.2-4

#### 4.2.3 Computation of the vapour, dissolved-air and dry-air pressures and of the densities

Starting from the relation [eq 3.2.6-4] into which one carries the perfect-gas constitutive law [eq3.2.3.2-1], one finds:

$\frac{{\text{dp}}_{\text{vp}}}{{p}_{\text{vp}}}=\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{\text{RT}}(\frac{1}{{\rho}_{w}}{\text{dp}}_{w}+({h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{w}^{m})\frac{\text{dT}}{T})$         eq 4.2.3-1

Unlike the case without dissolved air ${p}_{w}$ is now no longer known:

${p}_{w}={p}_{\text{lq}}-{p}_{\text{ad}}={p}_{\text{gz}}-{p}_{c}-\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}{p}_{\text{as}}={p}_{\text{gz}}-{p}_{c}-\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}({p}_{\text{gz}}-{p}_{\text{vp}})$

hence:

${\text{dp}}_{w}={\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}-{\text{dp}}_{c}-\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}({\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}-{\text{dp}}_{\text{vp}})-\frac{R}{{K}_{H}}({p}_{\text{gz}}-{p}_{\text{vp}})\text{dT}$         eq 4.2.3-2

One integrates [eq 4.2.3.1] including therein [eq 4.2.3.2] along a path first at constant temperature (one then considers the density of the water constant), then from ${T}^{-}$ to ${T}^{+}$ at constant pressures. Finally one obtains:

$\begin{array}{}\ln(\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{-}})=\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{{\rho}_{w}^{-}}(\frac{1}{{\text{RT}}^{+}}-\frac{1}{{K}_{H}})({p}_{{\text{gz}}^{+}}-{p}_{\text{gz}}^{-})+\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{{\rho}_{w}^{-}{K}_{H}}({p}_{{\text{vp}}^{+}}-{p}_{\text{vp}}^{-})-\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{{\rho}_{w}^{-}{\text{RT}}^{+}}({p}_{{c}^{+}}-{p}_{c}^{-})+\\ \frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}R}{{\rho}_{w}^{-}{K}_{H}}({p}_{{\text{vp}}^{+}}-{p}_{\text{gz}}^{+})\ln(\frac{{T}^{+}}{{T}^{-}})+\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{R}{\int}_{{T}^{-}}^{{T}^{+}}({h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}\text{}-{h}_{w}^{m})\frac{\text{dT}}{{T}^{2}}\end{array}$     eq 4.2.3-3

Unlike the previous case, one has here a non-linear equation to solve. One will use for this a predictor-corrector type method. One sets ${\tilde{p}}_{\text{vp}}$ such that:

$\begin{array}{}\ln(\frac{{\tilde{p}}_{\text{vp}}}{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{-}})=\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{{\rho}_{w}^{-}}(\frac{1}{{\text{RT}}^{+}}-\frac{1}{{K}_{H}})({p}_{{\text{gz}}^{+}}-{p}_{\text{gz}}^{-})-\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{{\rho}_{w}^{-}{\text{RT}}^{+}}({p}_{{c}^{+}}-{p}_{c}^{-})\\ +\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{R}{\int}_{{T}^{-}}^{{T}^{+}}({h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{w}^{m})\frac{\text{dT}}{{T}^{2}}\end{array}$     eq 4.2.3-4

and therefore

${\tilde{p}}_{\text{vp}}={p}_{\text{vp}}^{-}.\exp(\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{{\rho}_{{}_{w}}^{-}}(\frac{1}{{\text{RT}}^{+}}-\frac{1}{{K}_{H}})({p}_{{\text{gz}}^{+}}-{p}_{\text{gz}}^{-})-\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{{\rho}_{{}_{w}}^{-}{\text{RT}}^{+}}({p}_{{c}^{+}}-{p}_{c}^{-})+{\int}_{{T}^{-}}^{{T}^{+}}({h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{w}^{m})\frac{\text{dT}}{{T}^{2}})$ eq 4.2.3-5

Moreover, as in the section [§4.1.4], one recalls that:

${\int}_{{T}^{-}}^{{T}^{+}}({h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{w}^{m})\frac{\text{dT}}{{T}^{2}}=({h}_{\text{vp}}^{{m}^{-}}-{h}_{w}^{{m}^{-}})(\frac{1}{{T}^{-}}-\frac{1}{{T}^{+}})+({C}_{\text{vp}}^{p}-{C}_{w}^{p})(\ln(\frac{{T}^{+}}{{T}^{-}})+{T}^{-}(\frac{1}{{T}^{+}}-\frac{1}{{T}^{-}}))$

As $\ln(\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{-}})=\ln(\frac{{\tilde{p}}_{\text{vp}}}{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{-}})+\ln(\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{\tilde{p}}_{\text{vp}}})$ and as by series expansion $\ln(\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{\tilde{p}}_{\text{vp}}})=\ln(1+\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}-{\tilde{p}}_{\text{vp}}}{{\tilde{p}}_{\text{vp}}})\approx \frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{\tilde{p}}_{\text{vp}}}-1$,

${p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}$ will therefore be given by the following linear expression:

$\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{\tilde{p}}_{\text{vp}}}=1+\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{{\rho}_{w}^{-}{K}_{H}}({p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}-{p}_{\text{vp}}^{-})+\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}R}{{\rho}_{w}^{-}{K}_{H}}({p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}-{p}_{\text{gz}}^{-})\ln(\frac{{T}^{+}}{{T}^{-}})$         eq 4.2.3-6

whence

${p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}=\frac{({\rho}_{w}^{-}{K}_{H}-{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}({p}_{\text{vp}}^{-}+{p}_{\text{gz}}^{-}R\ln(\frac{{T}^{+}}{{T}^{-}})))}{(\frac{{\rho}_{w}^{-}{K}_{H}}{{\tilde{p}}_{\text{vp}}}-{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}(1+R\ln(\frac{{T}^{+}}{{T}^{-}})))}$         eq 4.2.3-7

From there the other pressures are easily computed:

${p}_{\text{as}}^{+}={p}_{\text{gz}}^{+}-{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}$

${p}_{\text{ad}}^{+}=\frac{{p}_{\text{as}}^{+}}{{K}_{H}}{\text{RT}}^{+}$

${p}_{w}^{+}={p}_{\text{gz}}^{+}-{p}_{c}^{+}-{p}_{\text{ad}}^{+}$

One may then compute the densities of the vapour and of the air by the relations [eq3.2.3.2-1], [eq3.2.3.2-2] and [eq 3.2.7-3]:

${\rho}_{\text{vp}}^{+}=\frac{{M}_{\text{vp}}^{\text{ol}}}{R}\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+}}{{T}^{+}}$             eq 4.2.3-8

${\rho}_{\text{as}}^{+}=\frac{{M}_{\text{as}}^{\text{ol}}}{R}\frac{({p}_{\text{gz}}^{+}-{p}_{\text{vp}}^{+})}{{T}^{+}}$             eq 4.2.3-9

${\rho}_{\text{ad}}^{+}=\frac{{p}_{\text{ad}}^{+}{M}_{\text{as}}^{\text{ol}}}{{\text{RT}}^{+}}$             eq 4.2.3-10

The density of the water is found by integrating over the time step equation [eq 3.2.3.1-1].

Which gives:

$\ln(\frac{{\rho}_{w}^{+}}{{\rho}_{w}^{-}})=\frac{{p}_{\text{gz}}^{+}-{p}_{\text{gz}}^{-}-{p}_{c}^{+}+{p}_{c}^{-}-{p}_{\text{ad}}^{+}+{p}_{\text{ad}}^{-}}{{K}_{w}}-3{\alpha}_{w}({T}^{+}-{T}^{-})$         eq 4.2.3-11

#### 4.2.6 Computation of the heat capacity and of the heat Q’

One now has all the elements for applying at the end of the time step the formula [eq 3.2.4.3-5]:

**Note:**

where we have denoted: ${T}^{}=\frac{{T}^{+}+{T}^{-}}{2}$. We have chosen here a “mid-point” formula for the temperature variable.

**Note:**

### 7.3 Computation of the derivatives

#### 7.3.1 Derivatives of the pressures as a function of the generalised strains

One details here the computation of the pressure derivatives as a function of the generalised strains. Recall that the equation [eq 3.2.6.3] is $\frac{{\text{dp}}_{\text{vp}}}{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}=\frac{{\text{dp}}_{w}}{{\rho}_{w}}+L\frac{\text{dT}}{T}$ with $L={h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}-{h}_{w}^{m}$ . Moreover ${\text{dp}}_{\text{ad}}={\text{dp}}_{\text{lq}}-{\text{dp}}_{w}=\frac{R}{{K}_{H}}{p}_{\text{as}}\text{dT}+\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}{\text{dp}}_{\text{as}}$ and ${\text{dp}}_{\text{as}}={\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}-{\text{dp}}_{\text{vp}}$ . By combining these equations one obtains:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\text{dp}}_{\text{vp}}\left[\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-\frac{{\rho}_{w}}{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}\right]=(-L{\rho}_{w}+{p}_{\text{ad}})\frac{\text{dT}}{T}+(\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-1){\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}+{\text{dp}}_{c}\\ {\text{dp}}_{w}\left[\frac{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}{{\rho}_{w}}\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-1\right]=(-\text{LR}\frac{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}{{K}_{H}}+\frac{{p}_{\text{ad}}}{T})\text{dT}+(\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-1){\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}+{\text{dp}}_{c}\end{array}$

One can therefore write the partial derivatives of the water and of the vapour as a function of the generalised strains:

$\frac{\partial {p}_{w}}{\partial T}=\frac{-\text{LR}\frac{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}{{K}_{H}}+\frac{{p}_{\text{ad}}}{T}}{\frac{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}{{\rho}_{w}}\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-1};\frac{\partial {p}_{w}}{\partial {p}_{\text{gz}}}=\frac{\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-1}{\frac{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}{{\rho}_{w}}\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-1};\frac{\partial {p}_{w}}{\partial {p}_{c}}=\frac{1}{\frac{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}{{\rho}_{w}}\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-1}$

$\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{vp}}}{\partial T}\frac{(-L{\rho}_{w}+{p}_{\text{ad}})}{\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-\frac{{\rho}_{w}}{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}}.\frac{1}{T};\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{vp}}}{\partial {p}_{\text{gz}}}\frac{\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-1}{\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-\frac{{\rho}_{w}}{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}};\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{vp}}}{\partial {p}_{c}}\frac{1}{\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-\frac{{\rho}_{w}}{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}}$

The relations ${\text{dp}}_{\text{as}}={\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}-{\text{dp}}_{\text{vp}}$ and ${\text{dp}}_{\text{ad}}={\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}-{\text{dp}}_{c}-{\text{dp}}_{w}$ make it possible to differentiate all the pressures, since one will have:

$\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{as}}}{\partial T}=-\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{vp}}}{\partial T};\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{as}}}{\partial {p}_{\text{gz}}}=1-\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{vp}}}{\partial {p}_{\text{gz}}};\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{as}}}{\partial {p}_{c}}=-\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{vp}}}{\partial {p}_{c}}$

and

$\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{ad}}}{\partial T}=-\frac{\partial {p}_{w}}{\partial T};\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{ad}}}{\partial {p}_{\text{gz}}}=1-\frac{\partial {p}_{w}}{\partial {p}_{\text{gz}}};\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{ad}}}{\partial {p}_{c}}=-1-\frac{\partial {p}_{w}}{\partial {p}_{c}}$

#### 7.3.2 Second derivatives of the vapour and dissolved-air pressures as a function of the generalised strains

One computes here the second-order partial derivatives of the vapour pressure required for section [§5.5.2]. One will subsequently denote:

$\mathrm{A1}=\frac{\text{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-\frac{{\rho}_{w}}{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}$ and $\mathrm{A2}=\frac{{\rho}_{w}}{{\rho}_{\text{vp}}}(\frac{\mathrm{RT}}{{K}_{H}}-1)$

$\mathrm{A3}=\frac{1}{{p}_{\text{vp}}}\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{vp}}}{\partial T}-\frac{1}{T}-\frac{1}{{K}_{w}}\frac{\partial {p}_{w}}{\partial T}-3{\alpha}_{w}$

$\mathrm{A4}=-\frac{1}{{p}_{\text{vp}}}\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{vp}}}{\partial T}+\frac{1}{T}+\frac{1}{{K}_{w}}\frac{\partial {p}_{w}}{\partial T}-3{\alpha}_{w}$

#### 7.3.3 Second derivatives of the vapour pressure

$\frac{\partial}{\partial {p}_{c}}\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{vp}}}{\partial {p}_{\text{gz}}}=\frac{\mathrm{A2}}{{\mathrm{A1}}^{2}}(\frac{1}{{K}_{w}}\frac{\partial {p}_{w}}{\partial {p}_{c}}-\frac{1}{{p}_{\text{vp}}}\frac{\partial {p}_{\text{vp}}}{\partial {p}_{c}})$

### 7.4 Equivalence with the ANDRA formulations

In order to be able to fit into the ALLIANCE platform, it is necessary to be consistent with the formulations set out by ANDRA. We propose here an equivalence between the notations which would differ. These differences concern only the writing of:

- The energy equation
- Henry’s law
- Diffusion in the liquid
- Diffusion in the gas

**Note concerning the enthalpies:**

It is necessary, in order to have consistency between the two models, that the user take ${h}_{\text{lq}}^{{m}_{0}}=0$ and ${h}_{\text{vp}}^{{m}_{0}}={L}_{0}$.

#### 7.4.1 Energy equation

The table above recalls the two formulations:

| **Notations** *Code_Aster* | **ANDRA notations** |
| --- | --- |
| ${h}_{\text{lq}}^{m}$ | $=\frac{{\varphi}_{w}}{{\rho}_{w}{S}_{w}n}$ |
| ${h}_{\text{vp}}^{m}$ | $=\frac{{\varphi}_{v}}{{\rho}_{v}(1-{S}_{w})n}$ |
| ${h}_{\text{as}}^{m}$ | $=\frac{{\varphi}_{\text{as}}}{{\rho}_{\text{as}}(1-{S}_{w})n}$ |
| ${M}_{\text{lq}}$ | $={\rho}_{w}{f}_{w}$ |
| ${M}_{\text{as}}$ | $={\rho}_{\text{as}}{f}_{\text{as}}$ |
| ${M}_{\text{vp}}$ | $={\rho}_{v}{f}_{v}$ |

By rewriting the energy equation of *Code_Aster* with these notations, one finds:

$\begin{array}{}\frac{d\phi }{\text{dt}}+\text{Div}({\phi }_{w}\frac{{f}_{w}}{{S}_{w}n}+{\phi }_{\text{as}}\frac{{f}_{\text{as}}}{(1-{S}_{w})n}+{\phi }_{v}\frac{{f}_{v}}{(1-{S}_{w})n})+\text{Div}(q)-(T-{T}^{0})\frac{d}{\text{dt}}\left[(1-n){\rho}_{s}{C}_{s}\right]\\ +3{\alpha}_{0}{K}_{0}T\frac{d{\varepsilon}_{v}}{\text{dt}}+3{\alpha}_{\text{lq}}^{m}T\frac{{\text{dp}}_{c}}{\text{dt}}-3({\alpha}_{\text{lq}}^{m}+{\alpha}_{\text{gz}}^{m})T\frac{{\text{dp}}_{\text{gz}}}{\text{dt}}-9{\text{TK}}_{0}{\alpha}_{{0}^{2}}\frac{\text{dT}}{\text{dt}}\\ =({\rho}_{w}{f}_{w}+{\rho}_{v}{f}_{v}+{\rho}_{\text{as}}{f}_{\text{as}})g+\Theta \end{array}$

The first line being that of ANDRA and the others being a priori negligible.

#### 7.4.2 Henry’s law

In the ANDRA formulation, Henry’s formulation is given by ${\omega}_{l}^{a}=\frac{{P}_{\text{as}}}{H}\frac{{M}_{\text{as}}^{\text{ol}}}{{M}_{w}}{\rho}_{w}$ with the concentration of air in the water which one can reduce to a density such that ${\omega}_{l}^{a}={\rho}_{\text{ad}}$ . $H$ is expressed in $\mathit{Pa}$ .

In the *Code_Aster* formulation, recall that Henry’s law is expressed in the form: ${C}_{\text{ad}}^{\text{ol}}=\frac{{\rho}_{\text{ad}}}{{M}_{\text{ad}}^{\text{ol}}}$ with ${C}_{\text{ad}}^{\text{ol}}=\frac{{p}_{\text{as}}}{{K}_{H}}$ . ${K}_{H}$ is expressed in $\mathit{Pa.}{m}^{3}.{\mathit{mol}}^{-1}$ .

One therefore has the equivalence:

${K}_{H}=H\frac{{M}_{w}}{{\rho}_{w}}$

#### 7.4.3 Diffusion of the vapour in the air

In the ANDRA formulation the flux of water vapour in the air as a function of the concentration of water vapour in the air or of the relative humidity is denoted:

${f}_{{\text{Diff}}_{v}}=-{D}_{v}.\nabla {\omega}_{g}^{e}$

with the concentration defined as the molar ratio in the gas: ${\omega}_{g}^{e}=\frac{{n}_{\nu}}{{n}_{g}}$ .

In *Code_Aster*, this same flux is written: ${f}_{{\text{Diff}}_{v}}={F}_{\text{vp}}\nabla {C}_{\text{vp}}$  with the vapour Fick coefficient ${F}_{\text{vp}}=\frac{{D}_{\text{vp}}}{{C}_{\text{vp}}(1-{C}_{\text{vp}})}$ and ${D}_{\mathit{vp}}$ the Fick diffusion coefficient of the gaseous mixture. ${C}_{\mathit{vp}}$ is defined as the pressure ratio such that: ${C}_{\text{vp}}=\frac{{p}_{\text{vp}}}{{p}_{\text{gz}}}$ .

The perfect-gas law makes it possible to write that ${C}_{\text{vp}}={\omega}_{g}^{e}$ hence $\nabla {\omega}_{g}^{e}=\nabla {C}_{\text{vp}}$ and ${f}_{{\text{Diff}}_{v}}={D}_{v}.\nabla {C}_{\text{vp}}$ .

Hence the equivalence is written simply:

${F}_{\text{vp}}={D}_{v}$ .

#### 7.4.4 Diffusion of the dissolved air in the water

In the ANDRA formulation the flux of dissolved air in the water is expressed

${f}_{{a}_{{\text{ds}}_{e}}}={D}_{a}.\nabla {\omega}_{l}^{a}$

with ${\omega}_{l}^{a}=\frac{{\rho}_{\text{ad}}}{{M}_{\text{ad}}^{\text{ol}}}$ .

In Code_Aster, this same flux is written: ${f}_{{a}_{{\text{ds}}_{v}}}={F}_{\text{ad}}\nabla {C}_{\text{ad}}$ with the dissolved-air Fick coefficient ${F}_{\text{ad}}=\frac{{D}_{\text{ad}}}{{C}_{\text{ad}}(1-{C}_{\text{ad}})}$ and ${D}_{\mathit{ad}}$ the Fick diffusion coefficient of the liquid mixture. ${C}_{\mathit{ad}}$ is defined such that: ${C}_{\text{ad}}={w}_{l}^{a}$ . Hence:

${F}_{\text{ad}}={D}_{a}$ .
