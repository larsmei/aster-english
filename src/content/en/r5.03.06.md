# r5.03.06 Rousselier model in large strains

**Summary**

One presents here a variant of the Rousselier model which makes it possible to describe the plastic growth of cavities in a steel. The constitutive relation is elastoplastic with isotropic hardening, allows plastic changes of volume and is written in large strains. The latter rely on the theory proposed by Simo and Miehe, modified in order to facilitate the numerical integration of the constitutive law and to place the model back within the framework of generalised standard materials.

This model is available in the command STAT_NON_LINE through the keyword RELATION:’ROUSSELIER’ under the factor keyword COMPORTEMENT and with the keyword DEFORMATION:’SIMO_MIEHE’.

This model is implemented for the three-dimensional (3D), axisymmetric (AXIS) and plane-strain (D_PLAN) modellings.

One presents the writing and the numerical treatment of this model.

> **Contents**

## 1 Introduction

The mechanisms at the origin of the ductile fracture of steels are associated with the development of cavities within the material. One generally distinguishes three phases:

- nucleation: this is the initiation of the cavities, at sites which correspond preferentially to the defects of the material,
- growth: this is the phase which corresponds to the development proper of the cavities, driven essentially by the plastic flow of the metallic matrix which surrounds these cavities,
- coalescence: this is the phase which corresponds to the interaction of the cavities with one another in order to create macroscopic cracks.

In what follows, we treat only the phases of growth and of coalescence. Rousselier [bib1] proposed a constitutive law capable of accounting for these two phases. With respect to this original formulation, Lorentz et al. [bib2] introduced several modifications bearing essentially on the treatment of large strains (multiplicative decomposition), the evolution of the porosity (a function of the total strain) and the expression of the flow rule at the singular point of the threshold surface.

More precisely, the model is based on assumptions which introduce a microstructure made up of a cavity and of a rigid-plastic, hence isochoric, matrix. In this case, the porosity $f$ , defined as the ratio between the volume of the cavity ${V}^{C}$ and the total volume $V$ of the representative elementary volume, is directly related to the macroscopic strain by:

$J=\detF=\frac{1-{f}_{0}}{1-f}\text{avec}f=\frac{{V}^{c}}{V}\iff \dot{f}=(1-f)\text{tr}D$             eq 1-1

where ${f}_{0}$ denotes the initial porosity, $F$ the transformation-gradient tensor, $J$ the variation of volume and $\mathrm{nitalicD}$ the strain rate.

In order to construct the cavity-growth law, Rousselier drew inspiration from a phenomenological analysis which leads him to the following ingredients:

- large plastic strains,
- irreversible changes of volume,
- isotropic hardening.

These considerations lead him to write the plasticity criterion $F$ in the following form:

$F(\tau ,R)={\tau}_{\text{eq}}+{\sigma}_{1}Df\exp(\frac{{\tau}_{H}}{{\sigma}_{1}})-R(p)-{\sigma}_{y}$                 eq 1-2

where $\tau$ is the Kirchhoff stress, $R$ the isotropic hardening, a function of the cumulative plastic strain $p$ , and ${\sigma}_{1}$ , $D$ and ${\sigma}_{y}$ material parameters. The presence in the plasticity criterion of the hydrostatic stress ${\tau}_{H}$ authorises plastic changes of volume. One also notes that this model does not include a specific damage variable because the only microstructural information retained is the porosity, directly linked to the macroscopic strain by equation [eq 1-1].

As for the treatment of large strains, one adopts the theory of Simo and Miehe but in a slightly modified formulation. The approximations introduced make it possible to make easier the numerical integration of the constitutive law but also to place the theory of Simo and Miehe back within the framework of generalised standard materials.

Subsequently, one briefly gives a few notions of mechanics in large strains, then one recalls the theory of Simo and Miehe as well as the modifications introduced. One finally presents the constitutive relations of the Rousselier model and its numerical integration.

## 3 Theory of Simo and Miehe

### 3.1 Introduction

We recall here the specific features of the formulation proposed by SIMO J.C. and MIEHE C. [bib3] in order to treat large strains. This formulation has already been used for thermo-elasto-(visco)-plastic constitutive models with isotropic hardening and von Mises criterion, [R5.03.21](doc:r5.03.21) for a model without the effect of metallurgical transformations and [R4.04.03](doc:r4.04.03) for a model with the effect of metallurgical transformations.

The kinematic choices make it possible to treat large displacements and large strains but also large rotations in an exact manner.

The specific features of these models are the following:

- just as in small strains, one assumes the existence of a relaxed configuration, that is locally stress-free, which makes it possible to decompose the total strain into a thermoelastic part and a plastic part,
- the decomposition of this strain into thermoelastic and plastic parts is no longer additive as in small strains (or for the large-strain models written in strain rates with for example a Jaumann derivative) but multiplicative,
- the elastic strains are measured in the current (deformed) configuration whereas the plastic strains are measured in the initial configuration,
- as in small strains, the stresses depend only on the thermoelastic strains,
- if the plasticity criterion depends only on the deviatoric stress, then the plastic strains take place at constant volume. The variation of volume is then due only to the thermoelastic strains,
- this model leads, during its numerical integration, to an incrementally objective model (cf. [§3.2.3]) which makes it possible to obtain the exact solution in the presence of large rotations.

Subsequently, one briefly recalls a few notions of mechanics in large strains.

### 3.2 Generalities on large strains

#### 3.2.1 Kinematics

Consider a solid subjected to large strains. Let ${\Omega}_{0}$ be the domain occupied by the solid before deformation and $\Omega (t)$ the domain occupied at the instant *t*  by the deformed solid.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.06/10000790000020C3000007DB47E7BB97C64D7DAF.svg)

**Figure 3.2.1-a: Representation of the initial and deformed configurations**

In the initial configuration ${\Omega}_{0}$ , the position of any particle of the solid is designated by $X$ (Lagrangian description). After deformation, the position at the instant $t$ of the particle which occupied the position $X$ before deformation is given by the variable $x$ (Eulerian description).

The global motion of the solid is defined, with $u$ the displacement, by:

$x=\stackrel{ˆ}{x}(X,t)=X+u$                                     eq 3.2.1-1

In order to define the change of metric in the neighbourhood of a point, one introduces the transformation-gradient tensor $F$ :

$F=\frac{\partial \stackrel{ˆ}{x}}{\partial X}=\text{Id}+{\nabla}_{X}u$                                     eq 3.2.1-2

The transformations of the volume element and of the density are:

$d\Omega =\text{Jd}{\Omega}_{o}$ with $J=\detF=\frac{{\rho}_{o}}{\rho}$                     eq 3.2.1-3

where ${\rho}_{o}$ and $\rho$ are respectively the density in the initial and current configurations.

Various strain tensors can be obtained by eliminating the rotation in the local transformation. For example, by computing directly the variations of length and of angle (variation of the scalar product), one obtains:

$E=\frac{1}{2}(C-\text{Id})$ with $C={F}^{T}F$                         eq 3.2.1-4

$A=\frac{1}{2}(\text{Id}-{b}^{-1})$ with $b={\text{FF}}^{T}$                         eq 3.2.1-5

$E$ and $A$ are respectively the Green-Lagrange and Euler-Almansi strain tensors and $C$ and $b$ the right and left Cauchy-Green tensors respectively.

In the Lagrangian description, one will describe the strain by the tensors $C$ or $E$ because these are quantities defined on ${\Omega}_{0}$ , and in the Eulerian description by the tensors $b$ or $A$ (defined on $\Omega$ ).

#### 3.2.2 Stresses

The stress tensor used in the theory of Simo and Miehe is the Kirchhoff tensor $\tau$ defined by:

$J\sigma =\tau$                                             eq 3.2.2-1

where $\sigma$ is the Eulerian Cauchy tensor. The tensor $\tau$ therefore results from a “scaling” by the variation of volume of the Cauchy tensor $\sigma$ .

#### 3.2.3 Objectivity

When one writes a constitutive law in large strains, one must check that this law is objective, that is invariant under any change of spatial frame of the form:

${x}^{\text{*}}=c(t)+Q(t)x$                                 eq 3.2.3-1

where $Q$ is an orthogonal tensor which expresses the rotation of the frame and $c$ a vector which expresses the translation.

More concretely, if one carries out a tensile test in the direction ${e}_{1}$ , for example, followed by a rotation of 90° about ${e}_{3}$ , which amounts to carrying out a tensile test along ${e}_{2}$ , then the danger with a non-objective constitutive law is not to recover a uniaxial stress tensor in the direction ${e}_{2}$ (which is notably the case with the PETIT_REAC kinematics).

### 3.3 Formulation of Simo and Miehe

Subsequently, one will denote by $F$ the gradient tensor which takes one from the initial configuration ${\Omega}_{0}$ to the current configuration $\Omega (t)$ , by ${F}^{p}$ the gradient tensor which takes one from the configuration ${\Omega}_{0}$ to the relaxed configuration ${\Omega}^{r}$ , and by ${F}^{e}$ from the configuration ${\Omega}^{r}$ to $\Omega (t)$ . The index $p$ refers to the plastic part, the index $e$ to the elastic part.

![Figure](/figures/r5.03.06/10000FCA00001CD6000013A398ECE64086423371.svg)

**Figure 3.3-a: Decomposition of the gradient tensor** $F$ **into an elastic part** ${F}^{e}$ **and a plastic part** ${F}^{p}$

By composition of the motions, one obtains the following multiplicative decomposition:

$F={F}^{e}{F}^{p}$                                         eq 3.3-1

The elastic strains are measured in the current configuration with the left Eulerian Cauchy-Green tensor ${b}^{e}$ and the plastic strains in the initial configuration by the tensor ${G}^{p}$ (Lagrangian description). These two tensors are defined by:

${b}^{e}={F}^{e}{F}^{\text{eT}}$ , ${G}^{p}=({F}^{\text{pT}}{F}^{p}{)}^{-1}$ whence ${b}^{e}={\text{FG}}^{p}{F}^{T}$             eq 3.3-2

However, one will alternatively use another measure of the elastic strains $e$ which coincides with the opposite of the linearised strains when the elastic strains are small:

$e=\frac{1}{2}(\text{Id}-{b}^{e})$                                     eq 3.3-3

In the case of an isotropic material, one can show that the free-energy potential depends only on the left Cauchy-Green tensor ${b}^{e}$ (or in our case on the tensor $e$ ) and, in plasticity, on the variable $p$ linked to the isotropic hardening. Moreover, one assumes that the volumetric free energy decomposes, just as in small strains, into a hyperelastic part which depends only on the elastic strain and another linked to the hardening mechanism:

$\Phi (e,p)={\Phi}^{\text{el}}(e)+{\Phi}^{\text{bl}}(p)$                             eq 3.3-4

If instead of using the Cauchy stress $\sigma$ , one uses the Kirchhoff stress $\tau$ , the Clausius–Duhem inequality is written (one omits the thermal part):

$\tau :D-\dot{\Phi}\ge 0$                                         eq 3.3-5

an expression in which $D$ represents the Eulerian strain rate.

Under the preceding assumptions, the dissipation is also written:

$(\tau +\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial e}{b}^{e}):D+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial e}:(F{\dot{G}}^{p}{F}^{T})-\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial p}\dot{p}\ge 0$             eq 3.3-6

The second principle of thermodynamics then requires the following expression for the stress–strain relation:

$\tau =-\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial e}{b}^{e}$                                         eq 3.3-7

One finally defines the thermodynamic forces associated with the elastic strain and with the cumulative plastic strain in accordance with the framework of generalised standard materials:

$s=-\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial e}\text{}\text{soit}\text{}\tau =s{b}^{e}$                                 eq 3.3-8

$A=-\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial p}$                                             eq 3.3-9

where the thermodynamic force A is the opposite of the isotropic-hardening variable $R$ .

There then remains for the dissipation:

$\tau :(-\frac{1}{2}F{\dot{G}}^{p}{F}^{T}{b}^{e-1})+A\dot{p}=s:(-\frac{1}{2}F{\dot{G}}^{p}{F}^{T})+A\dot{p}\ge 0$             eq 3.3-10

#### 3.3.1 Original formulation

The principle of maximum dissipation applied from the elasticity threshold $F$ , a function of the Kirchhoff stress $\tau$ and of the thermodynamic force $A$ , makes it possible to deduce from it the evolution laws of the plastic strain and of the cumulative plastic strain, namely:

$-\frac{1}{2}F{\dot{G}}^{p}{F}^{T}{b}^{e\text{-1}}=\dot{\lambda}\frac{\partial F}{\partial \tau }$                                 eq 3.3.1-1

$\dot{p}=\dot{\lambda}\frac{\partial F}{\partial A}$                                             eq 3.3.1-2

$\dot{\lambda}\ge 0\text{}F\le 0\text{}F\dot{\lambda}=0$                                 eq 3.3.1-3

#### 3.3.2 Modified formulation

The approximation introduced here on the original formulation of Simo and Miehe concerns the expression of the flow rule, an approximation which is all the smaller as the elastic strains are small, since $\tau =s{b}^{e}$ . Indeed, one henceforth expresses the elasticity threshold as a function of the thermodynamic forces and no longer of the stresses $F(s,A)\le 0$ , and it is with respect to these variables that one applies the principle of maximum dissipation, which leads to the following flow rules:

$-\frac{1}{2}F{\dot{G}}^{p}{F}^{T}=\dot{\lambda}\frac{\partial F}{\partial s}$                         eq 3.3.2-1

$\dot{p}=\dot{\lambda}\frac{\partial F}{\partial A}$                                     eq 3.3.2-2

$\dot{\lambda}\ge 0\text{}F\le 0\text{}F\dot{\lambda}=0$                         eq 3.3.2-3

#### 3.3.3 Consequences of the approximation

By replacing the stress $\tau$ by the thermodynamic force $s$ associated with the elastic strain in the expression of the plasticity criterion, one in fact introduces a perturbation of the boundary of the domain of reversibility of the order of magnitude of $2\parallel e\parallel$ . With respect to the initial formulation, there obviously results an influence on the observed elastic limit but also on the direction of flow: in particular, the time derivative of the plastic variation of volume is then written:

${\dot{J}}^{p}=\dot{\lambda}{J}^{p}{b}^{e\text{-1}}:\frac{\partial F}{\partial s}$                         eq 3.3.3-1

so that in the case where the criterion $F$ depends only on the deviator of the stress tensor $s$ , one does not recover ${J}^{p}=1$ : the isochoric character of the plastic strain is no longer perfectly preserved. We shall then be led to introduce a volume correction a posteriori.

Insofar as the elastic strains remain small, the results obtained with this modified model do not depart significantly from those obtained with the former formulation (cf. [bib4]), while the numerical integration of it will be simplified. Indeed, one will see subsequently that this model follows the same integration scheme as that of the models written in small strains.

**Note:**

*This new formulation of large strains makes it possible to place the theory of Simo and Miehe back within the framework of generalised standard materials. From a numerical point of view, this has the consequence of expressing the solution of the constitutive law as a minimisation problem with respect to the increments of internal variables.*

*Indeed, one recalls that within the framework of generalised standard materials, the data of the two potentials, the free energy* $\Phi (\varepsilon ,a)$ *and the dissipation potential* $D(\dot{a})$ *, a function of the strain tensor* $\varepsilon$ *and of a certain number of internal variables* $a$ *, make it possible to define completely the constitutive law (one places oneself in the case of time-independent materials).*

$\sigma =\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial \varepsilon }$ *,* $A=-\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial a}\in \partial D(\dot{a})$ *eq 3.3.3-2*

*where* $\partial D(\dot{a})$ *is the subdifferential of the dissipation potential* $D$ *.*

*The constitutive laws of generalised-standard type which do not depend on time are characterised by a dissipation potential positively homogeneous of degree 1, which is expressed by the following property:*

$\forall \dot{a}\text{}\forall \lambda >0\text{}D(\lambda \dot{a})=\lambda D(\dot{a})\text{}\Rightarrow \text{}\partial D(\lambda \dot{a})=\partial D(\dot{a})$ *eq 3.3.3-3*

Now if one writes the problem [eq 3.3.3-2] in a form discretised in time and if one uses the property of the subdifferentials [eq 3.3.3-3], one obtains the following discretised problem:

$\sigma =\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial \varepsilon }$ *,* $A=-\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial a}\in \partial D(\Delta a)$ *eq 3.3.3-4*

One can show that equation [eq 3.3.3-4] is equivalent (cf. [bib5]) to solving the minimisation problem with respect to the increments of internal variables $\Delta a$ *following:*

$-\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial a}\in \partial D(\Delta a)\iff \Delta a=\text{Arg}\underset{\Delta {a}^{\text{*}}}{\text{Min}}\left[\Phi ({a}^{-}+{\mathrm{\Delta a}}^{\text{*}})+D(\Delta {a}^{\text{*}})\right]$ *eq 3.3.3-5*

The application of equation [eq 3.3.3-5] to the Rousselier model in modified large strains is written:

$\underset{\text{énergie continue}}{\Phi (e,p)\text{et}D({D}^{p},\dot{p})}\text{}\underset{\text{discrétisation}}{\text{=>}}\text{}\underset{\text{énergie discrétisée}}{\Phi ({e}^{\text{Tr}}+\Delta e,{p}^{-}+\Delta p)\text{et}D(\Delta e,\Delta p)}$ *eq 3.3.3-6*

$\begin{array}{}A=-\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial a}=\lbrace \begin{array}{}s=-\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial e}\\ -R=-\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial p}\end{array}\text{}\in \partial D(\Delta e,\Delta p)\\ \iff \underset{\Delta e,\Delta p}{\text{Min}}\left[\Phi ({e}^{\text{Tr}}+\Delta e,{p}^{-}+\Delta p)+D(\Delta e,\Delta p)\right]\text{}\end{array}$ *eq. 3.3.3-7*

One will find in paragraph [§4] the relation which links the plastic strain rate ${D}^{p}$ *once discretised and the elastic-strain increment* $\Delta e$ *, as well as the definition of* ${e}^{\text{Tr}}$ *.*

One sees well here that if one takes the initial formulation of Simo and Miehe, one can no longer write the minimisation problem [eq 3.3.3-7] with the Kirchhoff stress $\tau$ *because of the term in* ${b}^{e}$ *in the expression:*

$\tau =-\frac{\partial \Phi }{\partial e}{b}^{e}$ *eq 3.3.3-8*

## 4 Rousselier model

We now describe the application of large strains to the Rousselier model presented in the introduction.

### 4.1 Equations of the model

In order to describe a thermoelastoplastic model with isotropic hardening (the equivalent in small strains of the model with isotropic hardening and von Mises criterion), Simo and Miehe propose a polyconvex elastic potential. For the sake of simplicity, one chooses here the Saint-Venant potential which is written:

$\Phi (e,p)=\frac{1}{2}\left[K{(\text{tr}e)}^{2}+2\mu \tilde{e}:\tilde{e}+\mathrm{6K}\alpha \Delta T\text{tr}e\right]+\underset{0}{\overset{p}{\int}}R(u)\text{du}$             eq 4.1-1

In accordance with equations [eq 3.3-8] and [eq 3.3-9], the state laws which derive from the elastic potential above are then written:

$s=-\left[K\text{tr}e\text{Id}+2\mu \tilde{e}+\mathrm{3K}\alpha \Delta T\text{Id}\right]$                     eq 4.1-2

$A=-R(p)$                                     eq 4.1-3

The elasticity threshold is given by:

$F(s,R)={s}_{\text{eq}}+{\sigma}_{1}\text{Df}\exp(\frac{{s}_{H}}{{\sigma}_{1}})-R-{\sigma}_{y}$                     eq 4.1-4

According to equations [eq 3.3.2-1] and [eq 3.3.2-2], the flow rules are defined by:

$-\frac{1}{2}F{\dot{G}}^{p}{F}^{T}=\dot{\lambda}\left[\frac{3\tilde{s}}{2{s}_{\text{eq}}}+\frac{\text{Df}}{3}\exp(\frac{{s}_{H}}{{\sigma}_{1}})\text{Id}\right]$             eq 4.1-5

$\dot{p}=\dot{\lambda}$                                                 eq 4.1-6

$\dot{\lambda}\ge 0\text{}F\le 0\text{}F\dot{\lambda}=0$                                 eq 4.1-7

### 4.2 Treatment of the singular points

In fact, the flow equation [eq 4.1-5] expresses the membership of the flow direction in the cone normal to the surface of the elasticity domain. It is valid only at the regular points, characterised by:

${s}_{\text{eq}}\ne 0$                                             eq 4.2-1

It therefore remains to characterise the normal cone at the singular points, that is to say those satisfying:

$\tilde{s}=0\text{et}{\sigma}_{1}Df\exp(\frac{{s}_{H}}{{\sigma}_{1}})-R={\sigma}_{y}$                     eq 4.2-2

The cone normal to the elasticity convex set at such a point is the set of the flow directions which realise the following maximisation problem:

${\Delta}^{\text{*}}(s\text{,R})=\underset{{D}^{p},\dot{p}}{\sup}\left[s:{D}^{p}-R\dot{p}-\Delta ({D}^{p},\dot{p})\right]$                 eq 4.2-3

where ${\Delta}^{\text{*}}$ is the indicator function of the convex set $F$ and $\Delta ({D}^{p},\dot{p})$ the dissipation potential obtained by the Legendre–Fenchel transform of the indicator function of $F$ :

$\Delta ({D}^{p},\dot{p})=\underset{\begin{array}{}s,R\\ F(s,R)\le 0\end{array}}{\text{Sup}}\left[s:{D}^{p}-R\dot{p}\right]$                     eq 4.2-4

After a few calculations, one obtains:

$\Delta ({D}^{p},\dot{p})={\sigma}_{y}\dot{p}+{\sigma}_{1}\text{tr}{D}^{p}(\ln\frac{\text{tr}{D}^{p}}{Df\dot{p}}-1)+{I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}(\text{tr}{D}^{p})+{I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}(\dot{p}-\frac{2}{3}{D}_{\text{eq}}^{p})$             eq 4.2-5

with

${I}_{{ℝ}^{+}}(x)=\lbrace \begin{array}{}0\text{}\text{si}\text{}x\ge 0\\ +\infty \text{sinon}\end{array}$                         eq 4.2-6

For $\tilde{s}=0$ , ${\Delta}^{\text{*}}$ is equal to:

${\Delta}^{\text{*}}(s,R)=\underset{\begin{array}{}{D}^{p},\dot{p}\\ \text{tr}{D}^{p}\ge 0\\ \dot{p}-\frac{2}{3}{D}_{\mathrm{eq}}^{p}\ge 0\end{array}}{\text{Sup}}\left[\underset{G(\text{tr}{D}^{p})}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{s}_{H}\text{tr}{D}^{p}-{\sigma}_{1}\text{tr}{D}^{p}(\ln\frac{\text{tr}{D}^{p}}{Df\dot{p}}-1)}}-R\dot{p}-{\sigma}_{y}\dot{p}\right]$             eq 4.2-7

By noting that for $\text{tr}{D}^{p}\ge 0$ , the function $G(\text{tr}{D}^{p})$ is concave, the supremum with respect to the trace of the plastic strain rate ${D}^{p}$ is obtained for:

${G}^{'}(\text{tr}{D}^{p})=0\text{}\text{d'où}\text{}\text{tr}{D}^{p}=\mathrm{Df}\dot{p}\exp(\frac{{s}_{H}}{{\sigma}_{1}})$             eq 4.2-8

**Note:**

*One indeed then recovers the indicator function of the elasticity threshold* $F$ *.*

${D}^{\text{*}}(s\text{,R})=\underset{\begin{array}{}\dot{p}\\ \dot{p}\ge \frac{2}{3}{D}_{\text{eq}}^{p}\ge 0\end{array}}{\text{Sup}}\left[F\dot{p}\right]=\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}0& \text{si}F\le 0\\ +\infty & \text{sinon}\end{array}$                 eq 4.2-9

At a singular point, the normal cone, the set of the admissible flow directions, is therefore characterised by:

$\text{tr}{D}^{p}=Df\dot{p}\exp(\frac{{s}_{H}}{{\sigma}_{1}})$                                 eq 4.2-10

$\dot{p}\ge \frac{2}{3}{D}_{\text{eq}}^{p}\ge 0$                                     eq 4.2-11

### 4.3 Expression of the porosity

One saw in the introduction that the microscopic inspiration of the Rousselier model is based on a microstructure made up of a cavity and of a rigid-plastic, hence isochoric, matrix. In this case, the porosity is directly related to the macroscopic strain by the relation eq. 1-1.

In this expression, the change of volume of elastic origin is neglected. Without particular care, this approximation can prove penalising in the presence of elastic compression, even a reasonable one, because it leads to a porosity which may be negative.

One therefore prefers the following equivalent expression, together with an explicit lower bound by the initial porosity:

> $f=\max({f}_{0,}1-\frac{1-{f}_{0}}{J})$                             eq 4.3-1

Rousselier for his part proposes to express the porosity on the basis of the plastic strain rate ${D}^{p}$ . The relation is written in incremental form:

$\dot{f}=(1-f)\text{tr}{D}^{p}$                                 eq 4.3-2

This means that the porosity variable used to parameterise the plasticity criterion $F$ depends only on the plastic strain. In fact, the plastic strain rate is a quantity evaluated in the relaxed configuration. Its transport into the current configuration (like $D$ ) is still expressed:

${F}^{e}{D}^{p}{F}^{{e}^{T}}=-\frac{1}{2}F{\dot{G}}^{p}{F}^{T}$                         eq 4.3-3

In this case, the evolution law of the porosity is expressed:

$\dot{f}=(1-f)\text{tr}(-\frac{1}{2}F{\dot{G}}^{p}{F}^{T})$                         eq 4.3-4

In order to avoid the integration of the porosity interfering with that of the plasticity (since the two variables are coupled), it is necessary to separate the integration of the constitutive law into two stages: integration of the plasticity with the porosity fixed at its value at the beginning of the time step, then integration of the porosity by means of equation 4.3-4 where the plastic evolution is that computed at the previous stage.

**Important note:**

One thus has two possible versions of the model (PORO_TYPE = 1 or 2, cf. U4.43.01), according as one chooses respectively the total strain or the plastic strain in the evolution of the porosity. It has been noted that for a small initial porosity f0, the behaviour at the beginning of the evolution changes strongly as a function of this parameter. Indeed, the choice seems to have a decisive impact on the response at the scale of the structure, since it favours or not the bifurcations of the zone where the strains localise. Thus, this strong sensitivity must lead the user to great caution in the use of this model. Research is under way in order to understand this sensitivity and to discriminate between the two variants for the evolution of the porosity.

### 4.5 Stresses and internal variables

The stresses are the Cauchy stresses , $\sigma$  therefore computed on the current configuration (six components in 3D, four in 2D).

The internal variables produced in *Code_Aster*  are:

- V1, the cumulative plastic strain $p$ ,
- V2, the porosity $f$ ,
- V3, the plasticity indicator (0 if the last computed increment is elastic, 1 if a regular plastic solution, 2 if a singular plastic solution),
- V4 to V9, the elastic strain tensor $e$ **.**

**Note**:

## 5 Numerical formulation

For the variational formulation, it is the same as that given in the note [R5.03.21](doc:r5.03.21) and which refers to the constitutive law with isotropic hardening and von Mises criterion in large strains. We recall only that it is an Eulerian formulation, with updating of the geometry at each increment and at each iteration, and that one takes into account the constitutive stiffness and the geometric stiffness.

We now present the numerical integration of the constitutive law and give the expression of the tangent matrix (options FULL_MECA and RIGI_MECA_TANG).

### 5.1 Expression of the discretised model

Knowing the stress ${\sigma}^{-}$ , the cumulative plastic strain ${p}^{-}$ , the elastic strains ${e}^{-}$ , the displacements ${u}^{-}$ and $\Delta u$ , one seeks to determine $(\sigma ,p,e)$ .

The displacements being known, the gradients of the transformation from ${\Omega}_{0}$ to ${\Omega}^{-}$ , denoted ${F}^{-}$ , and from ${\Omega}^{-}$ to $\Omega$ , denoted $\Delta F$ , are known.

In order to integrate this constitutive model, one uses an implicit Euler scheme, the porosity being an explicit function of the strain via equation 4.3-1, hence known during the integration of the behaviour.

Once discretised, one then obtains the following system:

$F=\Delta F{F}^{-}$                                                                             eq 5.1-1

$J=\detF$                                                                             eq 5.1-2

$J\sigma =\tau$                                                                             eq 5.1-3

$\tau =sb{}^{e}\text{}$                                                                             eq 5.1-4

${b}^{e}=\text{Id}-2e$                                                                         eq 5.1-5

- State equations:

$s=-\left[2\mu \tilde{e}+K\text{tr}e\text{Id}+\mathrm{3K}\alpha \Delta T\text{Id}\right]$                                             eq. 5.1-6

$A=-R(p)$                                                                     eq 5.1-7

Subsequently, one expresses the flow rules and the plasticity criterion directly as a function of the elastic strain tensor $e$ .

- Flow rules

$\begin{array}{}{D}^{p}\simeq -\frac{1}{2}F{\dot{G}}^{p}{F}^{T}=-\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\left[\underset{{b}^{e}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{\text{FG}}^{p}{F}^{T}}}-\Delta F\underset{{b}^{e-}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{{F}^{-}{G}^{\text{p-}}{F}^{-T}}}\Delta {F}^{T}\right]\\ \text{}=-\frac{1}{2\Delta t}\left[\text{Id}-2e-\Delta F\left\lbrace \text{Id}-2{e}^{-}\right\rbrace \Delta {F}^{T}\right]\\ \text{}=(e-\underset{{e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{1}{2}\left[\text{Id}-\Delta F\left\lbrace \text{Id}-2{e}^{-}\right\rbrace \Delta {F}^{T}\right]}})/\Delta t=(e-{e}^{\text{Tr}})/\Delta t\end{array}$                         eq 5.1-8

By taking the trace and deviatoric parts of equation [eq 4.1-5], one obtains:

> $\text{tr}e-\text{tr}{e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}=\Delta pDf\exp(\frac{-3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(\frac{-K\text{tr}e}{{\sigma}_{1}})$                             eq 5.1-9

$\tilde{e}=\lbrace \begin{array}{}\tilde{{e}^{\text{Tr}}}-\frac{3}{2}\Delta p\frac{\tilde{e}}{{e}_{\text{eq}}}\text{}\text{si solution régulière}\\ 0\text{et}\mathrm{\Delta p}\ge \frac{2}{3}(\tilde{e}-{\tilde{e}}^{\text{Tr}}{)}_{\text{eq}}\text{}\text{si solution singulière}\end{array}$                             eq 5.1-10

- Consistency conditions

$\begin{array}{}F=\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}2\mu {e}_{\mathrm{eq}}+{\sigma}_{1}Df\exp(\frac{-3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(\frac{-K\mathrm{tr}e}{{\sigma}_{1}})-R-{\sigma}_{y}& \text{si solution régulière}\\ {\sigma}_{1}Df\exp(\frac{-3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(\frac{-K\mathrm{tr}e}{{\sigma}_{1}})-R-{\sigma}_{y}& \text{si solution singulière}\end{array}\\ \text{avec}F\le 0\Delta p\ge 0F\Delta p=0\end{array}$     eq 5.1-11

### 5.2 Solution of the non-linear system

The integration of the constitutive law therefore amounts to solving the system [eq 5.1-9], [eq 5.1-10] and [eq 5.1-11]. We shall see that this solution reduces to that of a single scalar equation, whose unknown $x$ is the increment of the trace of the elastic strains:

> $x=\text{tr}e-\text{tr}{e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}$                             eq 5.2-1

Thanks to this choice, whether the solution is elastic or plastic, reached at a singular point or not, equation [eq 5.1-9] bearing on the trace of the elastic increment is always valid and makes it possible to express the increment of cumulative plastic strain:

$\begin{array}{}\text{tr}e-\text{tr}{e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}=\Delta p\underset{G}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{Df\exp(\frac{-K\text{tr}{e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(\frac{-3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})}}\exp(\frac{-K(\text{tr}e-\text{tr}{e}^{\mathrm{Tr}})}{{\sigma}_{1}})\\ \Rightarrow \Delta p(x)=\frac{1}{G}x\exp\frac{Kx}{{\sigma}_{1}}\end{array}$ eq 5.2-2

This equation shows us that one can seek $x\ge 0$ in order to guarantee a positive cumulative plastic strain and that the elastic solution is obtained for $x=0$ . One also notes that the increment of cumulative plastic strain is a continuous and strictly increasing function of $x$ . Subject to these remarks, if one denotes by S the term [eq 5.2-3] in the plasticity criterion, it is then, there too, a continuous and strictly increasing function of $x$ :

$F=2\mu {e}_{\text{eq}}-S(x)\text{}\text{avec}\text{}S(x)=-{\sigma}_{1}\text{G exp}(-\frac{\text{Kx}}{{\sigma}_{1}})+R(p(x))+{\sigma}_{y}$             eq 5.2-3

At this stage, the solution procedure decomposes into two stages.

#### 5.2.1 Examination of the singular points

Such a singular point is characterised by [eq 5.1-10] (bottom) and [eq 5.1-11] (bottom), hence in particular by $S(x)=0$ . By virtue of the properties of $S$ , this equation admits at most one positive solution, say ${x}^{S}$ which exists if and only if $S(0)\le 0$ . The knowledge of ${x}^{S}$ makes it possible to deduce from it the elastic strain tensor $e$ , the cumulative plastic strain $p$ as well as the thermodynamic forces $s$ and $R$ .

Finally, this singular point will be the solution if the inequality in [eq 5.1-11] (bottom) is satisfied, that is if:

${\mathrm{\Delta p}}^{s}\ge \frac{2}{3}({\tilde{e}}^{s}-{\tilde{e}}^{\text{Tr}}{)}_{\text{eq}}$                                 eq 5.2.1-1

#### 5.2.2 Regular solution

The flow equation [eq 5.1-10] (top) which determines the deviatoric part of the elastic strain tensor makes it possible to deduce from it a scalar equation as a function of the increment of cumulative plastic strain:

$\tilde{e}-{\tilde{e}}^{\text{Tr}}=-\frac{3}{2}\mathrm{\Delta p}\frac{\tilde{e}}{{e}_{\text{eq}}}\text{}\Rightarrow \text{}\lbrace \begin{array}{}{e}_{\text{eq}}={e}_{\text{eq}}^{\text{Tr}}-\frac{3}{2}\Delta p\\ \tilde{e}=\frac{{e}_{\text{eq}}}{{e}_{\text{eq}}^{\text{Tr}}}{\tilde{e}}^{\text{Tr}}\end{array}$                     eq 5.2.2-1

One notes that, by reason of the positivity of ${e}_{\text{eq}}$ , the admissible value of $\Delta p$ is bounded:

$\Delta p\le \frac{2}{3}{e}_{\text{eq}}^{\text{Tr}}$                                             eq 5.2.2-2

The consistency condition now determines $x$ :

$F=2\mu {e}_{\text{eq}}^{\text{Tr}}-S(x)-3\mu \Delta p\le 0$                             eq 5.2.2-3

Given this expression, the upper bound on the admissible value of $\Delta p$ reduces to the sole condition $S(x)\ge 0$ or, equivalently, to $x\ge {x}^{S}$ .

The elastic solution is obtained for $x$ = 0. It is the solution of the problem if and only if:

$F(0)=2\mu {e}_{\text{eq}}^{\text{Tr}}-S(0)<0$                             eq 5.2.2-4

In the opposite case, one must then solve:

$F(x)=2\mu {e}_{\text{eq}}^{\text{Tr}}-S(x)-\frac{3\mu }{G}x\exp(\frac{\text{Kx}}{{\sigma}_{1}})=0\text{}\text{avec}\text{}\lbrace \begin{array}{}x>{x}^{s}\text{}\text{si}{x}^{s}\text{existe}\\ x>0\text{}\text{sinon}\end{array}$             eq 5.2.2-5

This function is continuous and strictly decreasing and tends towards $-\infty$ with $x$ . It therefore admits at most one solution. The proof of the existence of this solution is immediate. Indeed, it suffices to prove that $F$ is positive on the lower bound of the search interval.

When ${x}^{S}$ does not exist, $F(0)>0$ since the solution is not elastic.

When ${x}^{S}$ exists, the function is equal to:

$F({x}^{s})=2\mu {e}_{\text{eq}}^{\text{Tr}}-3\mu \Delta {p}^{s}>0\iff \Delta {p}^{s}<\frac{2}{3}{e}_{\text{eq}}^{\text{Tr}}$             eq 5.2.2-6

This condition is satisfied since one has rejected the singular solution.

### 5.3 Course of the computation

The procedure for solving the set of the equations of the model is the following:

1. One seeks whether the solution is elastic

- computation of $F(0)$
- if $F(0)<0$ , the solution of the problem is the elastic solution ${x}^{\text{Sol}}=0$
- otherwise one goes to 2)

              1. If $S(0)>0$ , the solution is plastic and regular
- one goes to 4)

1. If $S(0)<0$ , one seeks whether the solution is singular

- one solves $S({x}^{s})=0$
- if ${x}^{s}$ satisfies the inequality ${\mathrm{\Delta p}}^{s}\ge \frac{2}{3}({\tilde{e}}^{s}-{\tilde{e}}^{\text{Tr}}{)}_{\text{eq}}$ , then the solution is singular ${x}^{\text{Sol}}={x}^{s}$
- otherwise, ${x}^{s}$ is a lower bound for solving $F(x)=0$ ; one goes to 4)

1. The solution is plastic and regular

- one solves $F(x)=0$

### 5.4 Solution

In order to solve the two equations $S(x)=0$ and $F(x)=0$ , one uses a Newton method with controlled bounds coupled with dichotomy when Newton gives a solution outside the interval of the two bounds. One now presents the determination of the bounds for each of the preceding cases (points 2) 3) and 4) of the preceding paragraph).

#### 5.4.1 Upper and lower bounds in the case where the function S is strictly positive at the origin

One solves:

$\lbrace \begin{array}{}F(x)=0\\ F(0)>0\end{array}\iff \lbrace \begin{array}{}\underset{{f}_{1}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{2\mu {e}_{\text{eq}}^{\text{Tr}}-S(x)}}=\underset{3\mu \Delta p}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{3\mu }{G}x\exp(\frac{\text{Kx}}{{\sigma}_{1}})}}\\ {f}_{1}(0)>0\end{array}$                 eq 5.4.1-1

where the function $\mathrm{\Delta p}(x)$ is continuous, strictly increasing and zero at the origin and the function ${f}_{1}(x)$ is continuous, strictly decreasing and strictly positive at the origin (see [Figure 5.4.1-a]).

One sets:

${f}_{1}=\underset{{f}_{2}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{2\mu {e}_{\text{eq}}^{\text{Tr}}-R(x)-{\sigma}_{y}}}+{\sigma}_{1}\text{Gexp}(-\frac{\text{Kx}}{{\sigma}_{1}})\text{}\text{alors}\text{}{f}_{2}(x)<{f}_{1}(x)\text{}\forall x\ge 0$         eq 5.4.1-2

where the function ${f}_{2}(x)$ is continuous and strictly decreasing. In this case, the solution of the equations:

${f}_{2}(\Delta {p}^{\inf})=3\mu \Delta {p}^{\inf}$ and ${x}^{\inf}\exp(\frac{{\text{Kx}}^{\inf}}{{\sigma}_{1}})=G\Delta {p}^{\inf}$             eq 5.4.1-3

in order to deduce successively $\mathrm{\Delta p}$ then $x$ gives a lower bound ${x}^{\text{Inf}}$ which corresponds to the solution of the model with isotropic hardening and von Mises criterion. If ${f}_{2}(0)<0$ , the lower bound is taken equal to zero: ${x}^{\inf}=0$ .

The upper bound ${x}^{\text{Sup}}$ is such that:

${x}^{\text{Sup}}\exp(\frac{{\text{Kx}}^{\text{Sup}}}{{\sigma}_{1}})=\frac{G}{3\mu }{f}_{1}({x}^{\text{Inf}})$                         eq 5.4.1-4

The equation of the type $x\exp(\frac{\text{Kx}}{{\sigma}_{1}})=\text{constante}$ is solved by a Newton method.

### 5.5 A posteriori volume correction

In the case of the Rousselier model, the change of volume plays a crucial role, so that the error committed by the approximation $s=\tau$ in the flow equations can lead to a rather imprecise evolution of the porosity, cf. [Bib2]. Following the proposal of this reference, one will correct a posteriori (i.e. after having computed all the quantities) the trace of the elastic strain.

Indeed, in the absence of approximation, the hydrostatic part of the flow equation leads to:

> $\frac{d}{\mathrm{dt}}(\ln{J}^{p})=\dot{p}Df\exp(\frac{\text{tr}\tau }{3{\sigma}_{1}})$                         (eq. 5.5-1)

After discretisation in time, one then obtains a corrected proposal for the plastic and elastic changes of volume:

> ${J}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{p}={J}^{p-}\exp(\text{tr}e-\text{tr}{e}^{\mathrm{Tr}});{J}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{e}=\frac{J}{{J}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{p}}$                 (eq. 5.5-2)

One will then seek a new trace of elastic strain such that the elastic change of volume corresponds to the corrected value above:

> ${e}_{\mathrm{corr}}=\tilde{e}+t\mathrm{Id}\text{où}t\text{tel que :}\det(\mathrm{Id}-2{e}_{\mathrm{corr}})={{J}_{\mathrm{corr}}^{e}}^{2}$                 (eq. 5.5-3)

This leads to a polynomial of degree 3 in $t$ , of which one will choose the solution closest to $e$ .

### 5.6 Expression of the tangent matrix of the behaviour

One gives here the expression of the tangent matrix (option FULL_MECA during the Newton iterations, option RIGI_MECA_TANG for the first iteration).

For the option FULL_MECA, it is obtained by linearising the system of equations which governs the constitutive law. We give below the main lines of this linearisation.

For the option RIGI_MECA_TANG, these are the same expressions as those given for FULL_MECA but with $\Delta p=0$ . In particular, one will have $\Delta F=\text{Id}$ .

The constitutive law can be put in the following general form:

$\sigma =\sigma (\tau ,\Delta F)$                                     eq 5.6-1

$\tau =\tau (e)$                                         eq 5.6-2

$e=e({e}^{\mathrm{Tr}},f)$                                     eq 5.6-3

${e}^{\text{Tr}}={e}^{\text{Tr}}(\Delta F)$                                     eq 5.6-4

The linearisation of this system gives:

> $\delta \sigma =(\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \tau }:\frac{\partial \tau }{\partial e}:(\frac{\partial e}{\partial {e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}}:\frac{\partial {e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}}{\partial \Delta F}+\frac{\partial e}{\partial f}\frac{\partial f}{\partial J}\otimes \frac{\partial J}{\partial \Delta F})+\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \Delta F}):\delta \Delta F=H:\delta \Delta F$         eq 5.6-5

where $H$ is the tangent matrix. Subsequently, one determines separately the five terms of the preceding equation.

In the linearisation of the system, one will often use the tensor $C$ defined below and the two following equations:

${\mathrm{\delta a}}_{ij}=\frac{1}{2}({\delta}_{\text{ik}}{\delta}_{\text{jl}}+{\delta}_{\text{jk}}{\delta}_{\text{il}}){\mathrm{\delta a}}_{kl}$                                 eq 5.6-6

$\delta {a}_{\text{pp}}=\delta {}_{kl}\delta {a}_{kl}$                                         eq 5.5-7

${C}_{ijkl}=\frac{1}{2}({\delta}_{\text{ik}}{\delta}_{\text{jl}}+{\delta}_{\text{jk}}{\delta}_{\text{il}})$                                 eq 5.6-8

- Computation of $\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \tau }$ and of $\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \Delta F}$

The linearisation of the relation which links the Cauchy stress $\sigma$ and the Kirchhoff stress $\tau$ gives:

$J\sigma =\tau \text{}\iff \text{}\delta \sigma =\frac{1}{J}\delta \tau -(\frac{\sigma}{J}\otimes \frac{\partial J}{\partial \Delta F}):\delta \Delta F$             eq 5.6-9

By using the relation [eq 5.6-6], one obtains for $\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \tau }$ :

$\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \tau }=C$                                             eq 5.6-10

and for $\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \Delta F}$ :

$\frac{\partial \sigma }{\partial \Delta F}=-\frac{\sigma}{J}\otimes \frac{\partial J}{\partial \Delta F}$                                 eq 5.6-11

with

$\begin{array}{}\frac{\partial J}{\partial {F}_{11}}={\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{22}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{33}-{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{23}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{32}\\ \frac{\partial J}{\partial {F}_{22}}={\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{11}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{33}-{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{13}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{31}\\ \frac{\partial J}{\partial {F}_{33}}={\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{11}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{22}-{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{12}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{21}\\ \frac{\partial J}{\partial {F}_{12}}={\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{31}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{23}-{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{33}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{21}\text{}\frac{\partial J}{\partial {F}_{21}}={\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{13}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{32}-{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{33}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{12}\\ \frac{\partial J}{\partial {F}_{13}}={\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{21}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{32}-{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{22}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{31}\text{}\frac{\partial J}{\partial {F}_{31}}={\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{12}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{23}-{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{22}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{13}\\ \frac{\partial J}{\partial {F}_{23}}={\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{31}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{12}-{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{11}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{32}\text{}\frac{\partial J}{\partial {F}_{32}}={\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{13}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{21}-{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{11}{\mathrm{\Delta F}}_{23}\end{array}$             eq 5.6-12

- Computation of $\frac{\partial \tau }{\partial e}$

The relation which links the Kirchhoff stress $\tau$ and the elastic strain tensor $e$ is given by:

$\begin{array}{}\tau =s{b}^{e}=-2\mu e-\lambda \text{Tr}e\text{Id}+4\mu ee+2\lambda (\text{tr}e)e\\ -\mathrm{3K}\alpha \Delta T\text{Id}+\mathrm{6K}\alpha \Delta Te\end{array}$             eq 5.6-13

One obtains after linearisation:

$\delta \tau =2(\lambda \text{tr}e-\mu +\mathrm{3K}\alpha \Delta T)\delta e+\lambda (2e-\text{Id})\text{Tr}\delta e+4\mu (\delta ee+e\delta e)$             eq 5.6-14

whence

$\frac{\partial {\tau}_{ij}}{\partial {e}_{kl}}=2(\lambda \text{tr}e-\mu +\mathrm{3K}\alpha \Delta T){C}_{ijkl}+\lambda (2{e}_{ij}-{\delta}_{ij}){\delta}_{kl}+2\mu ({\delta}_{\text{ik}}{e}_{\text{lj}}+{\delta}_{\text{il}}{e}_{\text{kj}}+{e}_{\text{il}}{\delta}_{\text{kj}}+{e}_{\text{ik}}{\delta}_{\text{jl}})$ eq 5.6-15

- Computation of $\frac{\partial {e}^{\text{Tr}}}{\partial \Delta F}$

The relation between the elastic strain tensor ${e}^{\text{Tr}}$ and the increment of the transformation gradient $\Delta F$ is written:

${e}^{\text{Tr}}=\frac{1}{2}(\text{Id}-\Delta F{b}^{e-}\Delta {F}^{T})$                         eq 5.6-16

Its linearisation gives:

$\frac{\partial {e}_{ij}^{\text{Tr}}}{\partial \Delta {F}_{kl}}=-\frac{1}{2}({\delta}_{\text{ik}}\Delta {F}_{\text{jp}}{b}_{\text{pl}}^{\text{e-}}+\Delta {F}_{\text{ip}}{b}_{\text{pl}}^{\text{e-}}{\delta}_{\text{jk}})$             eq 5.6-17

- Computation of $\frac{\partial e}{\partial {e}^{\text{Tr}}}$

**Elastic case**

In the elastic case, the computation of $\frac{\partial e}{\partial {e}^{\text{Tr}}}$ is immediate since $\delta e=\delta {e}^{\text{Tr}}$ whence

$\frac{\partial e}{\partial {e}^{\text{Tr}}}=C$                                         eq 5.6-18

**Plastic case – Regular solution**

In order to determine $\frac{\partial e}{\partial {e}^{\text{Tr}}}$ , one operates in two stages. By the discretised flow rule, one first computes $\delta e$ as a function of $\delta {e}^{\text{Tr}}$ and $\delta \Delta p$ . Then the consistency condition makes it possible to deduce from it $\delta \Delta p$ as a function of $\delta {e}^{\text{Tr}}$ . These two stages are detailed subsequently.

The deviatoric part of the discretised flow rule is written:

$\tilde{e}-{\tilde{e}}^{\text{Tr}}=-\frac{3}{2}\Delta p\frac{\tilde{e}}{{e}_{\mathrm{eq}}}$                             eq 5.6-19

One obtains after linearisation:

$\underset{1/\alpha }{\underset{\underbrace{}}{(1+\frac{3}{2}\frac{\Delta p}{{e}_{\text{eq}}})}}\delta \tilde{e}=\delta {\tilde{e}}^{\text{Tr}}-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\tilde{e}}{{e}_{\text{eq}}}\delta \Delta p+\frac{9}{4}\Delta p\frac{\tilde{e}}{{e}_{\text{eq}}^{3}}(\tilde{e}:\delta \tilde{e})$             eq 5.6-20

In order to determine $\tilde{e}:\delta \tilde{e}$ , one contracts equation [eq 5.6-20] with $\tilde{e}$ which gives:

$\tilde{e}:\delta \tilde{e}=\tilde{e}:\delta {\tilde{e}}^{\text{Tr}}-{e}_{\text{eq}}\delta \Delta p$                         eq 5.6-21

whence

$\delta \tilde{e}=\underset{{A}_{1}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\alpha \left[\frac{9\Delta p}{4{e}_{\text{eq}}^{3}}\tilde{e}\otimes \tilde{e}+C\right]}}:\delta {\tilde{e}}^{\text{Tr}}\underset{{A}_{2}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\tilde{e}}{{e}_{\text{eq}}}}}\mathrm{\delta \Delta p}$             eq 5.6-22

For the trace part of the discretised flow rule, one has:

$\text{Tr}e-\text{Tr}{e}^{\text{Tr}}=Df\Delta p\exp(\frac{3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(-\frac{K}{{\sigma}_{1}}\text{Tr}e)$             eq 5.6-23

which gives, setting ${k}_{1}=1+\frac{DfK\Delta p}{{\sigma}_{1}}\exp(\frac{3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(-\frac{K}{{\sigma}_{1}}\text{Tr}e)$ :

> $\text{tr}\delta e=\begin{array}{}\underset{{\alpha}_{1}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{1}{{k}_{1}}}}\text{tr}\delta {e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}\\ +\underset{{\alpha}_{2}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{Df\exp(\frac{-K}{{\sigma}_{1}}\text{tr}e)\exp(\frac{3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})}{{k}_{1}}}}\delta \Delta p\\ +\underset{{\beta}_{1}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{\Delta p\exp(\frac{-K}{{\sigma}_{1}}\text{tr}e)\exp(\frac{3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})}{{k}_{1}}}}D\delta f\end{array}$                 eq 5.6-24

In the plastic case, the consistency condition is:

$2\mu {e}_{\text{eq}}+Df{\sigma}_{1}\exp(-\frac{3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(-\frac{K}{{\sigma}_{1}}\text{Tr}e)-R-{\sigma}_{y}=0$             eq 5.6-25

whence

> $\begin{array}{}\frac{3\mu }{{e}_{\mathrm{eq}}}(\tilde{e}:\delta \tilde{e})+D{\sigma}_{1}\exp(\frac{-3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(\frac{-K\text{tr}e}{{\sigma}_{1}})\delta f\\ -DfK\exp(\frac{-3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(\frac{-K\text{tr}e}{{\sigma}_{1}})\text{tr}\delta e-h\delta \Delta p=0\end{array}$             eq 5.6-26

By injecting the relation [eq 5.6-21] into the equation above, one then obtains, setting

${k}_{2}=3\mu +h+{\alpha}_{2}DfK\exp(\frac{-3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(-\frac{K}{{\sigma}_{1}}\text{Tr}e)$ :

$\delta \Delta p=\begin{array}{}\frac{3\mu }{{e}_{\mathrm{eq}}}\underset{{\alpha}_{3}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{1}{{k}_{2}}}}\tilde{e}:\delta {\tilde{e}}^{\mathrm{Tr}}-\underset{{\alpha}_{4}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{{\alpha}_{1}DfK\exp(\frac{-3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(-\frac{K}{{\sigma}_{1}}\text{Tr}e)}{{k}_{2}}}}\text{tr}\delta {e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}\\ \underset{{\beta}_{2}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{+\frac{\exp(\frac{-3K\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(\frac{-K}{{\sigma}_{1}}\text{Tr}e)({\sigma}_{1}-{\beta}_{1}DfK)}{{k}_{2}}}}D\delta f\end{array}$         eq 5.6-27

By replacing $\delta \Delta p$ by its value obtained above in equations [eq 5.6–22] and [eq 5.6-24], one obtains:

> $\begin{array}{}\delta e=\underset{\mathrm{ddvetr}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[{A}_{1}+({A}_{2}+\frac{1}{3}{\alpha}_{2}\mathrm{Id})\otimes (\frac{3\mu {\alpha}_{3}}{{e}_{\mathrm{eq}}}\tilde{e})\right]}}:\delta \tilde{{e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}}+\underset{\mathrm{dtretr}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\frac{1}{3}{\alpha}_{1}\mathrm{Id}+{\alpha}_{4}({A}_{2}+\frac{1}{3}{\alpha}_{2}\mathrm{Id})\right]}}\text{tr}\delta {e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}\\ +\underset{\mathrm{dedf}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\left[\frac{1}{3}{\beta}_{1}\mathrm{Id}+{\beta}_{2}({A}_{2}+\frac{1}{3}{\alpha}_{2}\mathrm{Id})\right]D}}\delta f\end{array}$         eq 5.6-28

whence

$\frac{\partial e}{\partial {e}^{\text{Tr}}}=\text{ddvetr}+(\text{dtretr}-\frac{1}{3}\text{ddvetr}:\text{Id})\otimes \text{Id}$                 eq 5.6-29

**Plastic case – Singular solution**

The procedure is identical to that used previously.

One obtains for the discretised flow rule:

$\tilde{e}=0\text{}\iff \text{}\delta \tilde{e}=0$                                 eq 5.6-30

for the deviatoric part and for the trace part, the relation is identical to that found for the regular solution.

$\text{tr}\delta e={\alpha}_{1}\text{tr}\delta {e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}+{\alpha}_{2}\delta \Delta p+{\beta}_{1}D\delta f$                         eq 5.6-31

where ${\alpha}_{1}$ , ${\alpha}_{2}$ and ${\beta}_{1}$ have the same definitions as in the preceding paragraph.

The consistency condition then makes it possible to find $\delta \Delta p$ as a function of $\partial {e}^{\text{Tr}}$ .

$Df{\sigma}_{1}\exp(\frac{\mathrm{3K}\alpha \Delta T}{{\sigma}_{1}})\exp(-\frac{K}{{\sigma}_{1}}\text{Tr}e)-R-{\sigma}_{y}=0$             eq 5.6-32

that is, after linearisation:

> $\delta \Delta p={\alpha}_{4}\text{tr}\delta {e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}+{\beta}_{2}D\delta f$                     eq 5.6-33

that is, finally:

> $\delta e=\underset{\mathrm{dtretr}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{1}{3}\left[{\alpha}_{1}+{\alpha}_{4}{\alpha}_{2}\right]\mathrm{Id}\text{tr}\delta {e}^{\mathrm{Tr}}}}+\underset{\mathrm{dedf}}{\underset{\underbrace{}}{\frac{1}{3}\left[{\beta}_{1}+{\beta}_{2}{\alpha}_{2}\right]\mathrm{Id}D\delta f}}$             eq 5.6-34

whence

$\frac{\partial e}{\partial {e}^{\text{Tr}}}=\text{dtretr}\otimes \text{Id}$                                     eq 5.6-35

- Computation of $\frac{\partial f}{\partial J}$

Taking into account relation 4.3-1, the derivative is written simply:

> $\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}\frac{\partial f}{\partial J}=D\frac{1-{f}_{0}}{{J}^{2}}& \text{si}f>{f}_{0}\\ \frac{\partial f}{\partial J}=0& \text{si}f={f}_{0}\end{array}$                         eq 5.6-36

Note:

The tangent matrix is not altered by the volume correction because the latter is carried out a posteriori, that is after the computation of the stresses.
