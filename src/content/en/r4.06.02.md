## 1 Introduction

Faced with the complexity of mechanical structures, often made up of an assembly of several components, the classical numerical or experimental methods of vibration mechanics prove costly, sometimes even unusable. In perfect consistency with the modular organisation of large projects, the substructuring methods appear as the most efficient means of carrying out the vibration study of the whole from the dynamic behaviour of the components [bib4].

In this report, we present, first of all, the theoretical bases of the methods of modal synthesis. They combine techniques of substructuring and of modal recombination. Each substructure is represented by a projection basis composed of dynamic eigenmodes and of static deflected shapes at the interfaces.

Then, we present the two techniques of analysis by classical substructuring, implemented in *Code_Aster* [bib5]: the methods of Craig-Bampton, of Mac Neal and of interface modes. They are distinguished essentially by the use of different bases for the substructures.

The specific case of the modal analysis of structures with cyclic symmetries by dynamic substructuring is addressed in the document [R4.06.03](doc:r4.06.03).

## 2 Modal synthesis

Dynamic substructuring consists in determining the behaviour of a structure from the vibration characteristics of each of its components ([bib3] and [bib4]). The methods implemented in *Code_Aster* use simultaneously the classical techniques of modal recombination and of dynamic substructuring.

These methods, although different from that of the finite elements, adopt a procedure that is appreciably comparable but nevertheless introduce an additional approximation. They bring out three essential steps:

**Step 1:** numerical study of each component by the determination of their vibration characteristics. The work consists in identifying eigenmodes and static deflected shapes by classical techniques of vibration mechanics. If one assimilates each substructure to a super-element, this step is similar to the elementary computation.

**Step 2:** connection of the substructures. One uses the vibration characteristics determined previously for each component, and one takes into account the way they are connected. This work constitutes the substructuring step proper. It is akin to an assembly.

**Step 3:** the solution and a recovery phase make it possible to obtain the sought solution in the physical frame of the global structure.

### 2.1 RITZ transformation

The RITZ transformation is the subject of the reference documentation [R5.06.01](doc:r5.06.01). We recall here its principle. For the problem of the numerical determination of the real eigenmodes of the undamped system associated with the structure, which we shall designate by **eigenmodes**, one reduces the matter to the solution of the following minimisation problem:

Let $\delta$ be a virtual displacement, one seeks:     ${\mathit{Min}}_{\delta}\frac{1}{2}{\delta}^{T}.(\mathrm{K}-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{M}).\delta$

whose solution $q$ satisfies:

$$
(K-\omega \mathrm{²}M).q=0
$$

The RITZ method consists in seeking the solution of the preceding minimisation equation on a subspace of the space of the solutions. Consider the matrix

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/Object_30.svg)

containing the vectors of the basis of the subspace in question, arranged in columns. Restricted to this space of reduced dimension, the minimisation equation takes the following form:

${\mathrm{Min}}_{p=\Phi \delta }\frac{1}{2}{p}^{T}.(K-{\omega}^{2}M).p$

That is, the sought solution:

$$
q=\Phi \eta
$$

which satisfies:

$$
(\bar{K}-\omega \mathrm{²}\bar{M})\eta =0
$$

where $\eta$ is the vector of the generalised displacements, $\bar{K}={\Phi}^{T}K\Phi$ and $\bar{M}={\Phi}^{T}M\Phi$ are called respectively generalised stiffness and mass matrices.

After having solved the system, the obtaining of the eigenmodes in the physical basis is done by means of the relation. The RITZ transformation therefore makes it possible to replace the initial eigenvalue problem by a problem of the same nature, but of reduced dimension. The new generalised stiffness and mass matrices remain symmetric.

However, this transformation must be used with caution. Indeed, the new basis being incomplete, an approximation is made at the level of the projection: one speaks of modal truncation. The accuracy of the final result then depends on the choice of the basis vectors and the relative error due to this reduction in the number of unknowns must be estimated.

### 2.2 Modal recombination

A classical use of the RITZ transformation is the dynamic analysis by modal recombination. It is commonly used for the computation of the response of a structure to a low-frequency excitation. We shall limit ourselves here to the computation of the response to an excitation of a conservative structure. In this case, the finite-element method allows us to reduce the matter to the following matrix differential equation:

$$
\mathrm{M}\ddot{\mathrm{q}}+\mathrm{K}\mathrm{q}={\mathrm{f}}_{\mathit{ext}}
$$

If one applies the RITZ transformation, with as incomplete projection basis the first eigenmodes of the structure, the relation becomes:

$$
\bar{M}\ddot{\eta}+\bar{K}\eta ={\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}
$$

where ${\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}={\Phi}^{T}{f}_{\mathrm{ext}}$ is the vector of the generalised forces.

The eigenmodes are orthogonal with respect to the mass and stiffness matrices. The differential equation therefore brings out diagonal matrices: the system is then constituted of uncoupled equations. Each of them is the equation of a single-degree-of-freedom oscillator of mass-spring type which brings out the generalised mass, stiffness and force relative to the mode $j$ (respectively: ${m}_{j}$ , ${k}_{j}$ , ${f}_{j}$ ), see Figure-a.

If one considers the RITZ transformation [eq ], at the level of one degree of freedom, one has:

${q}_{i}=\underset{j}{\Sigma}{\Phi}_{ij}{\eta}_{j}$

where:

${q}_{i}$ is the $i$ th coordinate of the vector $q$ ,

${\eta}_{j}$ is the $j$ th coordinate of the vector $\eta$ ,

${\Phi}_{ij}$ is the component of the $i$ th row and of the $j$ th column of the matrix $\Phi$ .

It therefore appears that the response of the structure is expressed as the weighted recombination of responses of uncoupled single-degree-of-freedom oscillators. The RITZ transformation makes it possible, in this case, to define an equivalent scheme of the structure, which brings out the single-degree-of-freedom oscillators associated with the identified eigenmodes. Their stiffness and their mass are the generalised stiffnesses (${k}_{j}$ ) and the generalised masses (${m}_{j}$ ) of the corresponding modes.

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/100002000000033F00000118B6D1DCB3ED98CF14.png)

**Figure** **-a: Principle of the dynamic analysis by modal recombination.**

Reserved essentially for studies that one improperly qualifies as low-frequency 1
, modal recombination consists in using the orthogonality properties of the eigenmodes of a structure in order to simplify the study of its vibration response. Besides the advantage of reducing the order of the numerical problem to be solved, the RITZ transformation on a modal basis, in this case, also makes it possible to uncouple the differential equations and to bring out a physical interpretation of the result obtained. Depending on the excitation frequency, one will use a modal basis that is more or less truncated. One must however estimate the truncation error in order to ensure the validity of the result.

### 2.3 Modal synthesis

In a general way, the methods of modal synthesis consist in using simultaneously the dynamic substructuring (splitting into substructures) and the modal recombination at the level of each substructure. Often confused, by an abuse of language, with dynamic substructuring, modal synthesis is only a particular case of the latter.

Dynamic substructuring consists in considering the displacement of a substructure in the overall motion, as its response to the connection forces which link it to the other components and to the external forces which are applied to it.

Modal synthesis means that one computes this motion, at the level of each substructure, by modal recombination. One therefore uses a projection basis which characterises each substructure. Indeed, if the global structure is too large to be subjected to a modal analysis, the dimensions of the substructures make it possible to carry out this work. Modal synthesis requires that one first study each component separately, in order to determine their projection basis.

In the remainder of this chapter, we present the types of modes and static deflected shapes used in the methods of modal synthesis with the aid of the following simple example:

The vector of the degrees of freedom of the substructure is characterised by an exponent which defines the number of the substructure, and an index which makes it possible to distinguish the internal degrees of freedom (index $i$ ), from the boundary degrees of freedom (index $j$ ).

${q}^{k}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{q}_{i}^{k}\\ {q}_{j}^{k}\end{array}\right\rbrace$

One is led, in order to study the substructure $k$ , to define an impedance at the level of the connection degrees of freedom. Within the framework of the developments carried out in *Code_Aster*, it is either zero or infinite.

The basis vectors used in the methods implemented in *Code_Aster* are:

- the normal modes,
- the constraint modes,
- the attachment modes,
- the interface modes,

which one defines below.

#### 2.3.1 The normal modes

The eigenmodes or normal modes are advantageously used as a projection basis of the substructures for several reasons:

- they can be computed and/or obtained experimentally,
- they offer interesting orthogonality properties with respect to the mass and stiffness matrices of the substructure,
- they are associated with natural frequencies of the structure.

They can be of two types according to the condition given at the connection interfaces, cf. Figures-b and -c:

- eigenmodes with fixed interfaces,
- eigenmodes with free interfaces.

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/100002000000035B000000700D2B20CA708104AB.png)

**Figure** **-b: Eigenmodes with fixed interfaces.**

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/1000020000000368000000794B28D8467D127BE8.png)

**Figure** **-c: Eigenmodes with free interfaces.**

Let us note that in the case of a free substructure, the existing rigid-body modes (or overall modes) form part of the transformation basis.

#### 2.3.2 The static deflected shapes

One defines an interface mode for each connection degree of freedom of each substructure. Depending on the cases, these may be constraint modes or attachment modes.

The constraint modes are static deflected shapes which one joins to the normal modes with fixed interfaces in order to correct the effects due to their boundary conditions. A constraint mode is defined by the static deflected shape obtained by imposing a unit displacement on one connection degree of freedom, the other connection degrees of freedom being fixed.

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/10000200000003A00000006AD6F4693BDF8F4F81.png)

**Figure** **-d: Constraint modes.**

The attachment modes are static deflected shapes which one joins to the normal modes with free interfaces in order to reduce the effect of the modal truncation. An attachment mode is defined by the static deflected shape obtained by imposing a unit force on one connection degree of freedom, the other connection degrees of freedom being free.

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/10000200000002190000009C609AD6DB0E289EA9.png)

**Figure** **-e: Attachment modes.**

In the case of a substructure possessing rigid-body modes (here, substructure 2), its stiffness matrix is no longer invertible and it is not possible to compute its attachment modes. It is then necessary to fix certain degrees of freedom in order to make the structure isostatic, or even hyperstatic.

#### 2.3.3 The harmonic deflected shapes

A third projection basis has been introduced within the framework of the developments concerning the computation of the harmonic response by classical dynamic substructuring. It is the harmonic Craig-Bampton basis.

The harmonic Craig-Bampton basis is constituted of eigenmodes with fixed interfaces and of harmonic constraint modes [bib8]. The latter are joined to the normal modes with fixed interfaces in order to correct the effects due to their boundary conditions. A harmonic constraint mode is defined by the response of the undamped substructure to a harmonic displacement, of unit amplitude and of given frequency, imposed on one connection degree of freedom, the other connection degrees of freedom being fixed.

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/10000200000003FB0000008AD88E10A42A744017.png)

**Figure** **-f: Harmonic constraint modes.**

This modal basis is more particularly suited to fluid-structure interaction problems, for which the static loadings are not applicable in *Code_Aster* (the added-mass effect is not taken into account). It can be used for any type of analysis (modal, harmonic and transient).

#### 2.3.4 The reduced interface deflected shapes or “coupling modes”

One defines an interface mode for the whole set of the connection degrees of freedom of each substructure.

The interface modes are dynamic deflected shapes which one joins to the normal modes with fixed or free interfaces in order to correct the effects due to their boundary conditions.

Concretely, the computation of the constraint modes consists in carrying out a condensation of the behaviour of the structure onto the interface degrees of freedom and thus in building an interface operator (Schur complement) which expresses the behaviour of the structure reduced to the interface. By a spectral analysis, one can then represent the deformations of the interface by linear combination of the eigenmodes of this interface operator.

### 2.4 Connection conditions between substructures

#### 2.4.1 Introduction

Consider the problem of two substructures ${S}^{1}$ and ${S}^{2}$ connected in a rigid manner and not subjected to external forces. For the motion of the complete structure to be continuous, one must impose the equality of the displacements of the two components at the interface and the action-reaction law:

$$
\forall M\in {S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}{{\mathrm{q}}_{i}^{k}u}^{1}(M)={{\mathrm{q}}_{i}^{k}u}^{2}(M)\text{et}{\mathrm{f}}_{L}^{1}(M)=-{\mathrm{f}}_{L}^{2}(M)
$$

where:

${u}^{1}(M)$ represents the displacement field of the substructure ${S}^{1}$ ,

${u}^{2}(M)$ represents the displacement field of the substructure ${S}^{2}$ ,

${f}_{L}^{1}(M)$ represents the field of the connection forces applied to the substructure ${S}^{1}$ ,

${f}_{L}^{2}(M)$ represents the field of the connection forces applied to the substructure ${S}^{2}$ .

Depending on the nature of the meshes at the interface, the preceding problem can be discretised differently.

#### 2.4.2 Case of the compatible interface mesh

In this part, we limit ourselves to the case of compatible meshes. This means that they satisfy the following properties:

- the restriction of the mesh of each of the substructures ${S}^{1}$ and ${S}^{2}$ to the interface rests strictly on the same nodes at their intersection,
- the finite elements associated with these connection mesh cells are of the same nature (linear, quadratic) on either side of the boundary.

From then on, the condition [eq ] is strictly equivalent to the formulation below:

$$
{q}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{1}={q}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{1}\text{et}{f}_{{L}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}}^{1}=-{f}_{{L}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}}^{2}
$$

where:

| ${q}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{k}$ | is the vector of the degrees of freedom at the nodes of the interface ${S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}$ of the substructure $k$ , |
| --- | --- |
| ${f}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{k}$ | is the vector of the connection forces at the nodes of the interface ${S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}$ of the substructure $k$ . |

Indeed, the meshes of the two substructures

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/Object_67.svg)

and

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/Object_68.svg)

coinciding on the interface, the shape functions associated with the finite elements are the same at the interface. It is therefore sufficient to impose the equality at the nodes of the connection interfaces of each substructure in order to impose the equality over the whole connection domain.

Let us introduce the extraction matrices of the interface degrees of freedom ${B}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{k}$ :

$$
{q}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{k}={B}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{k}{q}^{k}
$$

By using the projection equation, the displacement continuity condition [eq ] and the formulation above applied to the two substructures, one obtains:

${B}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{1}{\Phi}^{1}{\eta}^{1}={B}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{k}{\Phi}^{1}{\eta}^{1}$

That is:

$$
{L}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{1}{\eta}^{1}={L}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{2}{\eta}^{2}\text{avec}{L}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{k}={B}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{k}{\phi }^{k}
$$

where:

${L}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{1}$ is the connection matrix of ${S}^{1}$ associated with the interface ${S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}$ , it expresses the continuity of displacement between the two substructures from the generalised displacements at the interface,

${L}_{{S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}}^{2}$ is the connection matrix of ${S}^{2}$ associated with the interface ${S}^{1}\cap {S}^{2}$ .

In the case of the eigenvalue problem of the global structure, equipped with its boundary conditions, one can write:

$$
\begin{array}{c}(\bar{K}-\omega \mathrm{²}\bar{M})\eta +{L}^{T}\lambda =0\\ L\eta =0\end{array}
$$

The generalised mass and stiffness matrices, the vector of the generalised degrees of freedom and the connection matrix which appear here, are defined on the global structure. They take a particular form for each method (Craig-Bampton, MacNeal, …) which will be made explicit later. The vector of the Lagrange multipliers $\lambda$ is interpreted as the connection actions to which the interfaces are subjected.

It is therefore a classical eigenvalue-search problem, with which a linear constraint equation is associated. In *Code_Aster*, this type of problem is solved by double dualisation of the boundary conditions [bib6].

Thus, one can show that this system is also the solution of the minimisation problem of the following functional, called the *action integral*:

$f={\int}_{a}^{b}F(\eta ,\dot{\eta},{\lambda}_{1}{\lambda}_{2})\cdot \mathrm{dt}$

where    $\mathrm{F}(\eta ,\dot{\eta},{\lambda}_{1}{\lambda}_{2})=\frac{1}{2}{\eta}^{T}\bar{\mathrm{K}}\eta +\frac{1}{2}\dot{\eta}\bar{\mathrm{M}}\dot{\eta}+{({\lambda}_{1}+{\lambda}_{2})}^{T}\mathrm{L}\eta -\frac{1}{2}{({\lambda}_{1}-{\lambda}_{2})}^{2}$  is the Hamiltonian.

The variables of this functional are the generalised coordinates $\eta$ , the associated velocities and the Lagrange multipliers ${\lambda}_{1}$ and ${\lambda}_{2}$ (in number equal to twice the number of connection equations). The last term of the functional imposes the equality of the Lagrange coefficients.

The extremum is reached for the values of the variables which cancel the derivatives of $f$ , whatever the real numbers $a$ and $b$ :

$-{\lambda}_{1}+L\eta +{\lambda}_{2}=0$

${L}^{T}({\lambda}_{1}+{\lambda}_{2})+(\bar{K}-\omega \mathrm{²}\bar{M})\eta =0$

${\lambda}_{1}+L\eta -{\lambda}_{2}=0$

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}-\text{Id}& L& \text{Id}\\ {L}^{T}& \bar{K}& {L}^{T}\\ \text{Id}& L& -\text{Id}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\lambda}_{1}\\ \eta \\ {\lambda}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace -{\omega}^{2}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& 0& 0\\ 0& \bar{M}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\lambda}_{1}\\ \eta \\ {\lambda}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

The double dualisation therefore leads to a real symmetric matrix problem. One shows [R3.03.01](doc:r3.03.01) that it makes it possible to render the matrix triangulation algorithms unconditionally stable.

#### 2.4.3 Case of the incompatible interface mesh

In this part, we consider the case of incompatible meshes. This means that they do not a priori satisfy the following properties:

- the restriction of the mesh of each of the substructures ${S}^{1}$ and ${S}^{2}$ to the interface rests strictly on the same nodes at their intersection,
- the finite elements associated with these connection mesh cells are of the same nature (linear, quadratic) on either side of the boundary.

The method chosen to handle the mesh incompatibility is a method derived from that of the PPCM (“Plus Petit Maillage Commun”, least common mesh). This method is used within the framework of several types of problems in *Code_Aster*: handling of an incompatible mesh, projection of a field onto meshes of different natures… One therefore defines a “master” interface onto which one projects the nodes of the “slave” interface.

The interpolation of the fields at the nodes of the slave interface (elements surrounded by ${S}_{2}$ on the figure below) is done on the element of the master interface onto which each node is projected, with the aid of the shape functions of this element.

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/10000200000002420000017451CA0437CB0A5305.png)

**Figure** **-a:**

One defines ${\Sigma}_{e}$ as the interface restricted to one element and ${n}_{e}$ the number of degrees of freedom of the element.

By choosing the interface of the substructure ${S}_{1}$ as master interface, the relation is rewritten:

${u}^{1}(M)-\sum_{j=1}^{{n}_{e}}({\int}_{{\Sigma}_{e}}{E}_{j}^{2}(M)\xi (M)\text{dM}){q}_{j}^{2}=0$

where $\xi (M)$ are the shape functions of the element onto which the node is projected and ${E}_{j}^{2}$ the restriction of the shape functions of the element $j$ of the interface of ${S}_{2}$ .

This method makes it possible to couple substructures possessing meshes of different natures (linear with quadratic, …) and of different densities. One will however retain that the rule of good use consists in choosing as master the mesh of lowest density.

Taking into account the conditions defined in the preceding paragraphs, the expression linking the degrees of freedom must be written again. Taking the interface of the substructure ${S}_{1}$ as master interface, one has:

${q}_{j}^{1}=\sum_{l}^{{n}_{e}}{\alpha}_{l}{q}_{l}^{2}$

Or again:

${q}^{1}=A{q}^{2}$

where $A$ is an observation matrix between the degrees of freedom of the two interfaces.

The displacement continuity relations are expressed, with the generalised coordinates, by the following matrix relations:

${L}_{j}^{1}{\eta}^{1}={\tilde{L}}_{j}^{2}{\eta}^{2}$

${\tilde{L}}_{j}^{k}=A{B}^{k}{\Phi}^{k}$

One can write this relation in matrix form:

$\left[{L}_{j}^{1}-{\tilde{L}}_{j}^{2}\right]\cdot \left[\begin{array}{c}{\eta}^{1}\\ {\eta}^{2}\end{array}\right]=0$

The terms of the connection matrices are no longer equal to zero or one. They arise from the interpolations of the displacements projected onto the elements.

Finally, by using the double dualisation, the matrix system obtained to be solved, in the case of the modal problem on the global structure, is:

$\Rightarrow \left[\begin{array}{ccc}-\text{Id}& \tilde{A}L& \text{Id}\\ {\tilde{A}}^{T}{L}^{T}& \bar{K}& {\tilde{A}}^{T}{L}^{T}\\ \text{Id}& \tilde{A}L& -\text{Id}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\lambda}_{1}\\ \eta \\ {\lambda}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace -{\omega}^{2}\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& 0& 0\\ 0& \bar{M}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\lambda}_{1}\\ \eta \\ {\lambda}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace$

Where $\tilde{A}$ designates the identity matrix $\text{Id}$ if the substructure is the one whose interface is master and $A$ , the observation matrix, if it is the one whose interface is slave.

The double dualisation therefore leads to a real symmetric matrix problem even for the case of incompatible meshes.

#### 2.4.4 Conclusion

This method therefore makes it possible to treat the connection of interfaces corresponding to different types of modal basis without the management cost of an elimination which is always delicate. On the other hand, it is relatively simple. The major drawback of this formulation is that it leads to final assembled systems of dimensions larger than in the case of the elimination. Indeed, the coupling of the matrix equations has been done by introducing a number of additional degrees of freedom equal to twice the number of connection equations. This increase in the dimensions of the matrices can therefore be very large. Let us note that the Lagrange degrees of freedom introduced are, in this case, the forces applied to the interfaces in order to ensure the connection between the two substructures.

### 2.5 Elimination of the linear constraints

The technique of double dualisation makes it possible to obtain uniformity in the taking into account of the constraints. This approach is indeed usable both for linear and for non-linear constraints, on the displacements or the stresses. It therefore allows, with great flexibility, the carrying out of a great variety of analyses.

Nevertheless, within the framework of modal analysis in general, and of substructuring techniques in particular, the presence of the double Lagrange multipliers can constitute a drawback, linked both to the increase in the size of the problem with the number of constraints, and also to the numerical conditioning of the system thus obtained. The increase in the size of the problem is particularly clear in the case of the substructuring approaches, where, for the final problem, the great majority of the equations to be solved concerns the constraints. In order to circumvent this difficulty, a technique for the elimination of the kinematic constraints has been implemented on the basis of a QR decomposition of the connection matrices.

#### 2.5.1 Case of linearly independent constraints

The linear constraints associated with a dynamics problem can be written in the form of the linear system:

$$
\left[L\right]\cdot q=0
$$

$q$ designating the whole set of the degrees of freedom of the complete model (that is to say including all the substructures, in our case). The idea of the elimination consists in building a basis of the kernel of $L$ , and in solving the problem projected into this basis. The construction of this basis is done simply from the QR decomposition of ${L}^{T}$ .

For a matrix $L$ of size $n\times N$ , where $n$ is the number of constraints, and $N$ the number of degrees of freedom, this decomposition is written:

$$
{\left[{L}^{T}\right]}_{N\times n}={\left[Q\right]}_{N\times N}\cdot {\left[R\right]}_{N\times n}=\left[{[{Q}_{1}]}_{N\times n}{[{Q}_{2}]}_{N\times (N-n)}\right]\cdot \left[\begin{array}{c}{[{R}_{1}]}_{n\times n}\\ {[0]}_{(N-n)\times n}\end{array}\right]
$$

${Q}_{2}$ is then directly the sought basis, as is shown in what follows.

#### 2.5.2 Case of redundant constraints

This decomposition also makes it possible to analyse what happens in the presence of redundant constraints (nodes constrained several times, “star” substructure, etc.). When the constraints are not all independent, one can write $L$ in the form of a linear combination of ${n}_{I}<n$ linearly independent constraints ${L}_{I}$ , that is:

${\left[L\right]}_{n\times N}={\left[A\right]}_{n\times {n}_{I}}\cdot {\left[{L}_{I}\right]}_{{n}_{I}\times N}$

The decomposition of ${L}_{I}$ is written directly:

$$
{\left[{L}_{I}^{T}\right]}_{N\times {n}_{I}}=\left[{[{Q}_{I}]}_{N\times {n}_{I}}{[{Q}_{K}]}_{N\times (N-{n}_{I})}\right]\cdot \left[\begin{array}{c}{[{R}_{I}]}_{{n}_{I}\times {n}_{I}}\\ {[0]}_{(N-{n}_{I})\times {n}_{i}}\end{array}\right]
$$

and the decomposition of $L$ becomes:

$$
\left[{L}^{T}\right]=\left[{Q}_{I}{Q}_{K}\right]\cdot \left[\begin{array}{c}{R}_{I}\cdot {A}^{T}\\ 0\end{array}\right]
$$

${Q}_{K}$ a basis of $\mathrm{Ker}(L)$ . Indeed, for any element $q$ of the kernel of $L$ , there exists a vector $y$ such that $q=[{Q}_{K}]\cdot y$ . Under these conditions, one has:

$\begin{array}{ccc}[L]\cdot q& =& \left[\begin{array}{c}A\cdot {R}_{I}^{T}\\ 0\end{array}\right]\cdot \left[\begin{array}{c}{Q}_{I}^{T}\\ {Q}_{K}^{T}\end{array}\right]\cdot q\\ & =& \left[\begin{array}{c}A\cdot {R}_{I}^{T}\\ 0\end{array}\right]\cdot \left[\begin{array}{c}{Q}_{I}^{T}\\ {Q}_{K}^{T}\end{array}\right]\cdot \left[{Q}_{K}\right]\cdot y\\ & =& [A\cdot {R}_{I}^{T}]\cdot [{Q}_{I}^{T}]\cdot [{Q}_{K}]\cdot y\\ & =& 0\end{array}$

since $I(L)$ and $\mathrm{Ker}(L)$ are orthogonal. When the constraints of $L$ are linearly independent, one has directly ${Q}_{K}={Q}_{2}$ . Otherwise, one must build ${Q}_{K}$ from ${Q}_{2}$ and from the last $n-{n}_{I}$ columns of ${Q}_{1}$ .

In order to identify the rank ${n}_{I}$ of the matrix $L$ , one uses the upper triangular matrix ${R}_{1}$ , coming from the relation. According to the relation, it must indeed be put in the form:

${\left[{R}_{1}\right]}_{n\times n}=\left[\begin{array}{c}{[{R}_{I}]}_{{n}_{I}\times {n}_{I}}\cdot {[A]}_{n\times {n}_{I}}^{T}\\ {[0]}_{(n-{n}_{I})\times n}\end{array}\right]$

The rank ${n}_{I}$ is the number of non-zero diagonal terms of ${Q}_{1}$ . Consequently, the vectors of ${Q}_{1}$ belonging to the kernel of $L$ are those corresponding to the zero diagonal terms of $R$ . In practice, the search for ${n}_{I}$ depends on the value fixed as the threshold for numerical zero.

#### 2.5.3 Case of a large number of constraints

The principles set out in the preceding paragraphs can be implemented numerically as they stand and retain correct performances if the matrices associated with the constraints are of limited sizes, that is at most of the order of a thousand constraints, involving of the order of ten thousand degrees of freedom. Otherwise, one of the methods consists in building this subspace in an iterative manner.

The overall procedure consists in successively modifying the rows of the matrix $Q$ , for all the constraints of $L$ . The starting matrix $Q$ is the identity matrix. In order to illustrate the procedure, consider a particular step by choosing a set of constraints coming from $L$ . One denotes by ${Q}_{k}$ the matrix taking into account the elimination of the first $k$ constraints. The construction of ${Q}_{k+1}$ is done in the following way:

- Extract the row ${L}_{k+1}$ of $L$ ,
- Determine the number of non-zero elements of ${L}_{k+1}$ . These non-zero elements are associated with the degrees of freedom involved in the linear relation of the type of .

  - If only one element of ${L}_{k+1}$ is non-zero, one cancels the diagonal term of ${Q}_{k+1}$ associated with this degree of freedom.
  - If several non-zero elements are detected, one seeks, from the preceding ${L}_{k}$ rows, which are the degrees of freedom already involved in a linear relation. One builds the sub-matrix of ${\mathrm{Q}}_{k+1}$ making it possible to eliminate the new constraints while taking into account the presence of the preceding constraints.

For this last point, one denotes by ${q}_{i}$ the degrees of freedom involved in the preceding ${L}_{k}$ linear relations, and by ${q}_{l}$ the other degrees of freedom. The subset of linear relation associated with ${L}_{k+1}$ is therefore written $[{L}_{i}{L}_{l}]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{q}_{i}\\ {q}_{l}\end{array}\right\rbrace =0$ , and one seeks the block of ${Q}_{k+1}$ which satisfies $[{L}_{i}{L}_{l}]\left[\begin{array}{cc}{Q}_{ii}& {Q}_{il}\\ {Q}_{li}& {Q}_{ll}\end{array}\right]=0$ . The block ${Q}_{ii}$ has been built beforehand, and must not be modified so as not to affect the preceding ${L}_{k}$ relations. One therefore builds ${Q}_{il}$ , ${Q}_{li}$ and ${Q}_{ll}$ such that:

    - ${Q}_{il}=0$ ,
    - ${Q}_{li}=-{({L}_{l})}^{+}{L}_{i}{Q}_{ii}$ , where ${({L}_{l})}^{+}$ is the pseudo-inverse of ${Q}_{l}$ , making it possible to solve ${L}_{i}{Q}_{ii}+{L}_{l}{Q}_{li}=0$ ,
    - ${Q}_{ll}$ is built by the $QR$ decomposition of ${L}_{l}={Q}_{ll}{R}_{ll}$ . After decomposition, the columns of ${Q}_{ll}$ associated with the non-zero diagonal terms of ${R}_{lŀ}$ are set to zero.

Several choices exist for the successive construction of the ${L}_{k}$ , and consist either in extracting a fixed number of rows of $\mathrm{L}$ for each step, or in carrying out a search for the blocks of disjoint constraints in $L$ .

## 3 Methods of classical dynamic substructuring

### 3.1 Introduction

After having studied separately the various steps of the substructuring, and the techniques which they bring into play, it seems of interest to present the principal methods of dynamic substructuring: the Craig-Bampton method and that of Mac Neal and the method of the reduced interface modes.

Craig-Bampton uses, as projection basis of the substructures, constraint modes and normal modes with fixed interfaces [bib1].

Furthermore, MacNeal uses, as projection basis of the substructures, attachment modes and normal modes with free interfaces [bib2].

One will address a method derived from these methods named the method of the reduced interface modes which uses, as projection basis of the substructures, interface modes and normal modes with fixed interfaces.

### 3.2 Craig-Bampton method

The following presentation involves only two substructures ${S}^{1}$ and ${S}^{2}$ , but it is generalisable to an arbitrary number of components. After having studied each substructure separately, their projection bases (normal modes with fixed interfaces and constraint modes) are known. For each of them (identified by the exponent $k$ ), one establishes a partition of the degrees of freedom, distinguishing the vector of the internal degrees of freedom ${q}_{i}^{k}$ and the vector of the connection degrees of freedom ${q}_{j}^{k}$ :

${q}^{k}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{q}_{i}^{k}\\ {q}_{j}^{k}\end{array}\right\rbrace$

That is:

${\varphi}^{k}$ the matrix of the eigenvectors of the substructure ${S}^{k}$ ,

${\psi}^{k}$ the matrix of the constraint modes of the substructure ${S}^{k}$ .

The projection basis of ${S}^{k}$ is characterised by the matrix:

${\Phi}^{k}=[{\varphi}^{k}{\psi}^{k}]$

The RITZ transformation (equation ) allows us to write:

$$
{q}^{k}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{q}_{i}^{k}\\ {q}_{j}^{k}\end{array}\right\rbrace =[{\varphi}^{k}{\psi}^{k}]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{i}^{k}\\ {\eta}_{j}^{k}\end{array}\right\rbrace ={\Phi}^{k}{\eta}^{k}
$$

${\eta}_{i}^{k}$ is the vector of the generalised degrees of freedom associated with the eigenmodes of ${S}^{k}$ ,

${\eta}_{j}^{k}$ is the vector of the generalised degrees of freedom associated with the constraint modes of ${S}^{k}$ .

Now the normal modes are determined with fixed interfaces, and each constraint mode is obtained by imposing a unit displacement on one connection degree of freedom, the others being fixed.

The generalised coordinates relative to the static deflected shapes are then the values of the connection degrees of freedom:

${q}_{j}^{k}={\eta}_{j}^{k}$

Let us take an interest in the contribution of the substructure ${S}^{k}$ from an energy point of view. The kinetic and strain energies are:

${T}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{q}^{{k}^{T}}.{M}^{k}.{\dot{q}}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{({\Phi}^{k}{\dot{\eta}}^{k})}^{T}.{M}^{k}.{\Phi}^{k}{\dot{\eta}}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{\dot{\eta}}^{k}.{\bar{M}}^{k}.{\dot{\eta}}^{k}$

${U}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{q}^{{k}^{T}}.{K}^{k}.{\dot{q}}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{({\Phi}^{k}{\dot{\eta}}^{k})}^{T}.{K}^{k}.{\Phi}^{k}{\dot{\eta}}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{\dot{\eta}}^{k}.{\bar{K}}^{k}.{\dot{\eta}}^{k}$

These expressions bring out the projections of the mass and stiffness matrices onto the basis of the substructure. These matrices, called generalised, satisfy a certain number of properties:

- by virtue of the orthogonality of the normal modes with respect to the stiffness and mass matrices, the upper left block of these matrices is diagonal. In what follows, we shall consider that these modes are normalised with respect to the mass matrix,
- one can also show that the constraint modes are orthogonal to the normal modes with respect to the stiffness matrix [bib4].

The generalised stiffness and mass matrices therefore have the following form:

$$
\bar{{K}^{k}}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}^{k}& 0\\ 0& {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{K}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\end{array}\right]\text{}\bar{{M}^{k}}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{Id}& {\varphi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\\ {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\varphi}^{k}& {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\end{array}\right]
$$

where ${\lambda}^{k}$ is the matrix of the generalised stiffnesses associated with the eigenmodes of ${S}^{k}$ .

The choice of the projection basis with fixed interfaces therefore leads to a coupling of the normal modes and of the static deflected shapes by the mass matrix.

This method is of interest if one envisages a numerical study of the substructures. Indeed, the normal modes with fixed interfaces and the constraint modes lend themselves well to computation. By contrast, their experimental determination is delicate, because it is difficult to realise an experimental clamping of good quality.

One shows moreover in [bib 1] that this method is of order 2 in $\omega /{\omega}_{m}$ where ${\omega}_{m}$ is the largest identified natural angular frequency.

### 3.3 Mac Neal method

It would be possible to present this method in a manner similar to that of Craig-Bampton, the only difference residing in the use of the normal modes with free interfaces and of the attachment modes. However, it seems of interest to adopt a slightly different procedure, which makes it possible to arrive at a truncation criterion, and to bring out the attachment modes as the static contribution of the unidentified eigenmodes.

As previously, one considers the problem of a structure with 2 components. The method is generalisable to an arbitrary number of substructures. In what follows we shall identify any quantity associated with the substructure ${S}^{k}$ by the exponent $k$ .

We limit ourselves, here, to the modal analysis of the global structure, so the external forces are zero, in order to lighten the theoretical developments. Also, the displacement of ${S}^{k}$ satisfies the following dynamic equilibrium equation:

$$
({K}^{k}-\omega \mathrm{²}{M}^{k})q={f}_{L}^{k}
$$

We shall consider that the projection basis of ${S}^{k}$ is constituted of **all** its eigenmodes with free interfaces. Thus, the dimension of the projected problem is equal to the dimension of the problem coming from the finite-element modelling. We assume, moreover, that a certain number of these modes has been determined, the others being unknown:

${q}^{k}=[{\varphi}_{1}^{k}{\varphi}_{2}^{k}]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}^{k}\\ {\eta}_{2}^{k}\end{array}\right\rbrace ={\Phi}^{k}{\eta}^{k}$

where:

| ${\varphi}_{1}^{k}$ | is the matrix of the identified modal vectors of the substructure ${S}^{k}$ , |
| --- | --- |
| ${\varphi}_{2}^{k}$ | is the matrix of the unidentified modal vectors of the substructure ${S}^{k}$ , |
| ${\eta}_{1}^{k}$ | is the vector of the generalised degrees of freedom associated with the identified eigenmodes of ${S}^{k}$ , |
| ${\eta}_{2}^{k}$ | is the vector of the generalised degrees of freedom associated with the unidentified eigenmodes of ${S}^{k}$ . |

The equation becomes, with the generalised coordinates defined previously:

$({\Phi}^{{k}^{T}}.{K}^{k}.{\Phi}^{k}-{\omega}^{2}{\Phi}^{{k}^{T}}.{M}^{k}.{\Phi}^{k}){\eta}^{k}={\Phi}^{{k}^{T}}{f}_{L}^{k}$

that is:

$$
({K}^{k}-\omega \mathrm{²}{M}^{k}){\eta}^{k}={\Phi}^{{k}^{T}}{f}_{L}^{k}
$$

The eigenmodes are orthogonal with respect to the mass and stiffness matrices and we choose to normalise them with respect to the mass matrix. One therefore has:

$$
\bar{{K}^{k}}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{1}^{k}& 0\\ 0& {\lambda}_{2}^{k}\end{array}\right]\text{}\bar{{M}^{k}}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{Id}& 0\\ 0& \mathrm{Id}\end{array}\right]
$$

Let us now consider the whole set of the two substructures. Each of them satisfies the equations and . The whole set of these dynamic equations constitutes the following system:

$$
(\left[\begin{array}{cccc}{\lambda}_{1}^{1}& 0& 0& 0\\ 0& {\lambda}_{1}^{2}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& {\lambda}_{2}^{1}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0& {\lambda}_{2}^{2}\end{array}\right]-{\omega}^{2}\left[\begin{array}{cccc}\mathrm{Id}& 0& 0& 0\\ 0& \mathrm{Id}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& \mathrm{Id}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0& \mathrm{Id}\end{array}\right])\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}^{1}\\ {\eta}_{1}^{2}\\ {\eta}_{2}^{1}\\ {\eta}_{2}^{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace ={\left[\begin{array}{c}{\Phi}_{1}^{1}\\ {\Phi}_{1}^{2}\\ {\Phi}_{2}^{1}\\ {\Phi}_{2}^{2}\end{array}\right]}^{T}\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{f}_{{L}_{1}}^{1}\\ {f}_{{L}_{1}}^{2}\\ {f}_{{L}_{2}}^{1}\\ {f}_{{L}_{2}}^{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

By grouping the identified modes and the unidentified modes:

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}\\ {\eta}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}^{1}\\ {\eta}_{1}^{2}\\ {\eta}_{2}^{1}\\ {\eta}_{2}^{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace
\begin{array}{c}\text{coordonnées des modes identifiés}\\ \\ \\ \text{coordonnées des modes non identifiés}\end{array}
$$

The equation expressing the RITZ transformation becomes:

$$
q=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{q}_{1}\\ {q}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\Phi}_{1}& {\Phi}_{2}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}\\ {\eta}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

With these notations, the system of dynamic equations becomes:

$$
(\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{1}& 0\\ 0& {\lambda}_{2}\end{array}\right]-{\omega}^{2}\left[\begin{array}{cc}\text{Id}& 0\\ 0& \text{Id}\end{array}\right])\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}\\ {\eta}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\Phi}_{1}^{T}& 0\\ 0& {\Phi}_{2}^{T}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{f}_{{L}_{1}}\\ {f}_{{L}_{2}}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

This system of equations expresses the dynamic behaviour of the substructures separately. It does not represent the overall motions of the structure. For that, one must adjoin to it the connection conditions between the two components.

The equations between substructures which ensure their connection derive from the equations [eq  ] and [eq ], as well as from the organisation of the basis which we have chosen:

$$
L\cdot \eta =\left[\begin{array}{cc}{L}_{1}& {L}_{2}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}\\ {\eta}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =0
$$

$$
{f}_{{L}_{1}}=-{f}_{{L}_{2}}
$$

The equations and allow us to express the generalised coordinates relative to the unidentified modes:

$$
{\eta}_{2}=-{({\lambda}_{2}-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{Id})}^{-1}{\Phi}_{2}^{T}{f}_{{L}_{1}}
$$

From the equations and , one therefore obtains:

$$
{L}_{1}{\eta}_{1}-{L}_{2}{({\lambda}_{2}-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{Id})}^{-1}{\Phi}_{2}^{T}{f}_{{L}_{1}}=0
$$

Now, according to the formula [eq ], one knows that one can write the connection matrices, in the form:

$$
{L}_{k}={B}_{k}{\Phi}_{k}
$$

One therefore has, according to and :

$$
-{B}_{2}{\Phi}_{2}{\eta}_{1}+{B}_{2}{\Phi}_{2}{({\lambda}_{2}-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{Id})}^{-1}{\Phi}_{2}^{T}{f}_{{L}_{1}}=0
$$

One sees appearing the residual dynamic flexibility matrix associated with the unidentified modes:

$$
{R}_{e}(\omega )={\Phi}_{2}{({\lambda}_{2}-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{Id})}^{-1}{\Phi}_{2}^{T}
$$

This term shows that the connection forces at the interface degrees of freedom are non-zero if one takes into account in the modelling only the known modes.

From then on, the matrix problem can be reduced to the following equivalent system:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}_{1}-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{Id}& -{\Phi}_{1}^{T}{B}_{1}^{T}\\ -{B}_{1}{\Phi}_{1}& {B}_{2}{R}_{e}(\omega )\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{1}\\ {f}_{{L}_{1}}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

This problem has as unknowns the generalised coordinates associated with the identified eigenmodes and the connection forces applied to the first substructure. The residual flexibility term, which also appears, is not known. One proposes below a modelling which brings in the attachment modes of MacNeal.

There are two cases according to whether or not one takes into account the residual flexibility matrix.

#### 3.3.1 First case

One neglects the residual dynamic flexibility matrix associated with the unidentified modes:

${R}_{e}(\omega )=0$

The resulting dynamic substructuring method is therefore very simple. It has the disadvantage of relying on a modal recombination method very sensitive to truncation effects.

#### 3.3.2 Second case

One uses a series expansion: ${R}_{e}(\omega )={R}_{e}(0)+O({\omega}^{2}/{\omega}_{m}^{2})$ .

Let us adopt the following notations:

that is:

| $n$ | the number of modes of the complete basis, |
| --- | --- |
| $m$ | the number of unidentified modes, |
| $\Phi$ | the matrix of the $n$ normal modes with free interfaces, |
| $\Psi$ | the matrix of the $n$ attachment modes (defined for all the degrees of freedom of the system, not only for the interface degrees of freedom between the 2 substructures), |
| ${\lambda}_{i}={\omega}_{i}^{2}$ | the diagonal matrix of the $n$ eigenvalues. |

The complete matrix of the attachment modes satisfies: $K\Psi =\mathrm{Id}\text{}\iff \text{}\Psi ={K}^{-1}$ .

The complete modal basis of the normal modes with free interfaces constitutes an orthonormal basis. The stiffness matrix, expressed in this basis, is written:

$\bar{K}={\Phi}^{T}.K.\Phi =\lambda$

In the same way:

${\bar{K}}^{-1}={\Phi}^{T}.{K}^{-1}.\Phi ={\lambda}^{-1}$

One therefore deduces a new expression of the complete matrix of the attachment modes:

$\Psi ={K}^{-1}=\Phi .{\lambda}^{-1}.{\Phi}^{T}$

As the eigenmodes are orthogonal two by two and the matrix of the eigenvalues is diagonal, the complete matrix of the attachment modes takes the final form:

$\Psi =\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\varphi}_{i}{\lambda}_{i}^{-1}{\varphi}_{i}^{T}$

Let us now consider the residual dynamic flexibility matrix, coming from the MacNeal method:

${\mathrm{R}}_{e}(\omega )={\Phi}_{2}{({\lambda}_{2}-{\omega}^{2}\mathrm{Id})}^{-1}{\Phi}_{2}^{T}=\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}{\varphi}_{i}{({\lambda}_{i}-{\omega}^{2})}^{-1}{\varphi}_{i}^{T}$

When the number of identified modes is sufficiently large, the dynamic contribution becomes negligible compared with the static contribution:

$\frac{{\omega}^{2}}{{\omega}_{i}^{2}}\ll 1\Rightarrow {R}_{e}(\omega )\approx {R}_{e}(0)=\sum_{i=m+1}^{n}{\varphi}_{i}{\lambda}_{i}^{-1}{\varphi}_{i}^{T}$

where ${R}_{e}(0)$ is the residual static flexibility matrix.

In order to bring out the effect of the modal truncation, we can approximate this matrix by its expansion to order 1:

${R}_{e}(\omega )\approx \sum_{i=m+1}^{n}{\varphi}_{i}{\lambda}_{i}^{-1}(1+\frac{{\omega}^{2}}{{\omega}_{i}^{2}}){\varphi}_{i}^{T}$

This matrix can be computed as a function of the matrix of the attachment modes:

${R}_{e}(0)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}{\varphi}_{i}{\lambda}_{i}^{-1}{\varphi}_{i}^{T}-\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\varphi}_{i}{\lambda}_{i}^{-1}{\varphi}_{i}^{T}\Rightarrow {R}_{e}(0)=\Psi -\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\varphi}_{i}{\lambda}_{i}^{-1}{\varphi}_{i}^{T}$

The second term of this formulation is computable in an exact way, since it involves only the identified (free-interface) modes.

Finally, let us note that in the MacNeal method, only the contribution of the attachment modes at the interface nodes is necessary.

The solution of the system allows us to determine the natural frequencies of the global structure and the generalised coordinates of the eigenmodes. The recovery to the expression of the eigenmodes in the physical bases of the substructures is done by the following relation:

${q}_{1}^{k}={\Phi}_{1}^{k}{\eta}_{1}^{k}+{R}_{e}^{k}(0){B}_{1}^{{k}^{T}}{f}_{{L}_{1}}^{T}$

The MacNeal method therefore leads, in the case of the computation of the modes of the global structure, to an eigenvalue problem of reduced size. The mass and stiffness matrices coming from the substructuring are symmetric. Two methods are in reality proposed, according to whether or not one takes into account the residual flexibility. The literature on the subject tends to show that the use of the residual flexibility is indispensable in order to obtain reliable results [bib2].

### 3.4 Method of the interface modes (so-called reduced method)

#### 3.4.1 Introduction

The method of the interface modes is based on the works of various authors; one will find a complete theoretical justification in [bib12]. We shall not develop all these aspects in this document because the use which is made of it in *Code_Aster* is relatively simplified: it is simply a matter of reducing the size of the reduced system by the use of the spectral properties of the dynamic operator condensed at the interface.

#### 3.4.2 Definition of the interface modes

After having studied each substructure separately in order to build the bases of normal modes, it is a matter of considering the coupled problem in order to build the bases of interface modes. The procedure currently present in *Code_Aster* requires that one consider the coupled problem by dynamic substructuring. One therefore uses the Craig-Bampton method without the normal modes with fixed interfaces, which is therefore equivalent to a method of static substructuring by Guyan condensation. One obtains the following coupled system:

${\bar{\mathrm{K}}}^{k}={\psi}^{{k}^{T}}.{\mathrm{K}}^{k}.{\psi}^{k}\text{}{\bar{\mathrm{M}}}^{k}={\psi}^{{k}^{T}}.{\mathrm{M}}^{k}.{\psi}^{k}$

$\psi$ designates here the constraint modes. The computation of the eigenmodes of the global structure condensed at its interfaces by the methods of dynamic substructuring consists in solving a matrix eigenvalue problem as follows:

$(\bar{K}-{\omega}^{2}\bar{M}){\eta}_{R}+{L}^{T}\lambda =0$

$L{\eta}_{R}=0$

It goes without saying that during this computation of the interface modes, it does not make sense to compute as many modes as there are interface degrees of freedom because that would amount to returning to the system of constraint modes. An error of truncation of the projection basis is therefore introduced at this level, but one which remains physical and controllable.

The eigenmodes obtained are defined only on the interface degrees of freedom, it is therefore necessary to extend these modes onto each of the coupled substructures. One thus obtains:

${\psi}_{R}^{k}={\psi}^{k}{\eta}_{R}$

#### 3.4.3 Computation of the interface modes

In practice, the computation of the interface modes with the method presented in paragraph 3.4.2 is not realistic. Indeed, the operation of computing the constraint modes $\psi$ is very costly in memory and in computation time, all the more so as one then considers only a sub-family of $N$ vectors, $N$ being very small compared with the number of degrees of freedom of the interface. The approach adopted for the command MODE_STATIQUE [U4.52.14](doc:u4.52.14) consists in building the interface modes with the aid of a preconditioner. If one denotes by $i$ the interface degrees of freedom, and by $c$ the complementary degrees of freedom, by partitioning the stiffness matrix $\mathrm{K}$ , the constraint modes $\psi$ satisfy

$\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\psi}_{i}\\ {\psi}_{\mathrm{c}}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\mathit{Id}\\ -{\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{cc}}^{-1}{\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{ci}}\end{array}\right\rbrace$

Rather than building the constraint modes directly on this principle, one assembles and builds mass matrices $\tilde{{\mathrm{M}}_{ii}}$ and stiffness matrices $\tilde{{\mathrm{K}}_{ii}}$ defined only at the interface. These matrices are built on the basis of a model of Euler–Bernoulli beams by taking up the connectivity of the nodes of the interface. Each pair of connected nodes of the interface is then linked by a cylindrical beam whose geometric properties are adjusted with respect to the shortest distance between two connected nodes of the interface. The diameter of the beam is then fixed at 20% of this minimum distance. The material used for the beams is a steel. One then computes a family of modes $\tilde{{\phi }_{i}}$ of this interface model. It is appropriate here to compute a number of modes $M$ appreciably greater than $N$ , where $N$ is the number of interface modes which the user wishes to retain.

In order to compute these modes, one carries out a first operation which makes it possible to determine the number of disjoint sub-parts of a given interface. For each disjoint part of the interface, one computes the 6 rigid-body modes. If one denotes by $d$ the number of disjoint parts, then the total number $M$ of modes computed is worth:

- $M=3(E(\frac{N}{d})+2E(\frac{6}{d})+1)$ if $E(\frac{N}{d})>7$
- $M=3(6+E(\frac{N}{d})+2)$

where $E(.)$ corresponds to the integer part of the quantity considered.

These $M$ modes $\tilde{{\phi }_{i}}$ are then lifted statically onto the model of the substructure, and then form the basis on which one seeks the interface modes:

$\tilde{\psi}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\tilde{{\psi}_{i}}\\ \tilde{{\psi}_{\mathrm{c}}}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\tilde{{\phi }_{i}}\\ -{\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{cc}}^{-1}{\mathrm{K}}_{\mathrm{ci}}\tilde{{\phi }_{i}}\end{array}\right\rbrace$

One then projects the mass and stiffness matrices of the macro-element onto these vectors,

$\bar{\mathrm{K}}={\tilde{\psi}}^{T}.\mathrm{K}.\tilde{\psi}\text{}\bar{\mathrm{M}}={\tilde{\psi}}^{T}.\mathrm{M}.\tilde{\psi}$

and one seeks their eigenmodes:

$(\bar{\mathrm{K}}-{\omega}^{2}\bar{\mathrm{M}}){\eta}_{R}=0$

The interface modes retained for the construction of the macro-elements are finally the first $N$ vectors defined by:

$\psi =\tilde{\psi}{\eta}_{\mathrm{R}}$

The computation of the matrices $\tilde{{\mathrm{M}}_{ii}}$ and $\tilde{{\mathrm{K}}_{ii}}$ , and of the associated modes $\tilde{{\phi }_{i}}$ is rapid and inexpensive in memory, and makes it possible to limit the lifting operation to the only vectors of interest in order to build the interface modes. Naturally, if one computes as many modes $\tilde{{\phi }_{i}}$ as there are interface degrees of freedom, then the subspace described is identical to that described by the constraint modes.

#### 3.4.4 Creation of the reduced macro-elements

For each of the substructures, one has the bases of normal modes and the bases of the interface modes. The creation of the macro-elements is then identical to the case of the Craig-Bampton method. One establishes a partition of the degrees of freedom, distinguishing the vector of the internal degrees of freedom ${q}_{i}^{k}$ and the vector of the connection degrees of freedom ${q}_{j}^{k}$ :

${q}^{k}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{q}_{i}^{k}\\ {q}_{j}^{k}\end{array}\right\rbrace$

That is:

${\phi }^{k}$ the matrix of the eigenvectors of the substructure ${S}^{k}$ ,

${\psi}_{R}^{k}$ the matrix of the interface modes of the substructure ${S}^{k}$ .

The projection basis of ${S}^{k}$ is characterised by the matrix:

${\Phi}^{k}=[{\phi }^{k}{\psi}_{R}^{k}]$

The RITZ transformation (equation ) allows us to write:

$$
{q}^{k}=\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{q}_{i}^{k}\\ {q}_{j}^{k}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\phi }^{k}& {\psi}_{R}^{k}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{i}^{k}\\ {\eta}_{j}^{k}\end{array}\right\rbrace ={\Phi}^{k}{\eta}^{k}
$$

${\eta}_{i}^{k}$ is the vector of the generalised degrees of freedom associated with the eigenmodes of ${S}^{k}$ ,

${\eta}_{j}^{k}$ is the vector of the generalised degrees of freedom associated with the interface modes of ${S}^{k}$ .

Let us take an interest in the contribution of the component ${S}^{k}$ from an energy point of view. The kinetic and strain energies are:

${T}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{q}^{{k}^{T}}.{M}^{k}.{\dot{q}}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{({\Phi}^{k}{\dot{\eta}}^{k})}^{T}.{M}^{k}.{\Phi}^{k}{\dot{\eta}}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{\dot{\eta}}^{k}.{\bar{M}}^{k}.{\dot{\eta}}^{k}$

${U}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{q}^{{k}^{T}}.{K}^{k}.{\dot{q}}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{({\Phi}^{k}{\dot{\eta}}^{k})}^{T}.{K}^{k}.{\Phi}^{k}{\dot{\eta}}^{k}=\frac{1}{2}{\dot{\eta}}^{k}.{\bar{K}}^{k}.{\dot{\eta}}^{k}$

These expressions bring out the projections of the mass and stiffness matrices onto the basis of the substructure. These matrices, called generalised, satisfy a certain number of properties by virtue of the orthogonality of the normal modes with respect to the stiffness and mass matrices; the upper left block of these matrices is diagonal. Moreover, we shall consider that these modes are normalised with respect to the mass matrix.

The generalised stiffness and mass matrices therefore have the following form:

$$
{\bar{K}}^{k}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\varphi}^{k}& {\varphi}^{{k}^{T}}{K}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\\ {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{K}^{k}{\varphi}^{k}& {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{K}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\end{array}\right]{\bar{M}}^{k}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\text{Id}& {\varphi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\\ {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\varphi}^{k}& {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\end{array}\right]
$$

where ${\lambda}^{k}$ is the matrix of the generalised stiffnesses associated with the eigenmodes of ${S}^{k}$ .

### 3.5 Implementation in Code_Aster

#### 3.5.1 Study of the substructures separately

The projection basis of each substructure is composed of dynamic eigenmodes and of static deflected shapes.

The dynamic eigenmodes of the substructure are computed with the classical operator of *Code_Aster:* CALC_MODES [U4.52.02](doc:u4.52.02). In the case of the Craig-Bampton substructuring, the connection interfaces must be fixed. This is carried out with the operator AFFE_CHAR_MECA [U4.44.01](doc:u4.44.01).

The operator DEFI_INTERF_DYNA [U4.64.01](doc:u4.64.01) makes it possible to define the connection interfaces of the substructure. In particular, one specifies the type of the interface, which can be either "CRAIGB" (Craig-Bampton), or "MNEAL" (MacNeal), or finally "AUCUN" (no static modes computed).

The operator DEFI_BASE_MODALE [U4.64.02](doc:u4.64.02) makes it possible to compute the complete projection basis of the substructure. Thus, the dynamic modes computed previously are copied. Furthermore, the static deflected shapes are computed as a function of the type defined in the operator DEFI_INTERF_DYNA [U4.64.01](doc:u4.64.01). If the type is "CRAIGB", one computes the constraint modes of the interfaces of the substructure. If the type is "MNEAL", one computes the attachment modes of the interfaces of the substructure. If the type is "AUCUN", one does not compute a static deflected shape, which corresponds to a basis of MacNeal type without static correction.

The operator MACR_ELEM_DYNA [U4.65.01](doc:u4.65.01) computes the generalised stiffness and mass matrices of the substructure, as well as the connection matrices.

#### 3.5.2 Assembly

The model of the complete structure is determined by the operator DEFI_MODELE_GENE [U4.65.02](doc:u4.65.02). In particular, each substructure is defined by the macro-element which corresponds to it (coming from MACR_ELEM_DYNA) and the rotation angles which make it possible to orient it. The connections between substructures are defined by the datum of the names of the two substructures involved and of those of the two interfaces facing each other. In the case of the method of the interface modes, it is necessary to specify the option 'REDUIT' in order to allow the coupling between the macro-elements.

The numbering of the complete generalised problem is carried out by the operator NUME_DDL_GENE [U4.65.03](doc:u4.65.03). The generalised mass and stiffness matrices of the complete structure are assembled as a function of this numbering with the operator ASSE_MATR_GENE [U4.65.04](doc:u4.65.04).

## 4 Modal analysis by classical dynamic substructuring

### 4.1 Introduction

For the modal problems, the system studied is subjected to no external force. One does not take into account the dissipation in the solid. The method of modal analysis by substructuring, programmed in *Code_Aster*, which makes it possible to replace the global problem by a simplified problem, proceeds in four stages. First of all, eigenmodes and static deflected shapes are computed on each of the substructures composing the system. Then, the global problem is projected onto these fields, and one takes into account the couplings between the substructures, at the level of their interfaces. One can then solve in the classical way the reduced problem obtained. Finally, it only remains to deduce from it the overall solution by reconstitution.

### 4.2 Dynamic equations satisfied by the substructures separately

We shall consider a structure $S$ composed of ${N}_{S}$ substructures denoted ${S}^{k}$ . We assume that each substructure is modelled by finite elements. We have seen that in an analysis by dynamic substructuring, the vibration behaviour of the substructures results from the external forces which are applied to them, and from the connection forces which the other substructures exert on them. Thus, at the level of the substructure ${S}^{k}$ , and in the case of the modal analysis we can write:

$$
{M}^{k}{\ddot{q}}^{k}+{K}^{k}{q}^{k}={f}_{L}^{k}
$$

The field of the connection forces is written in complex notation:

${f}_{L}^{k}(t)=\left\lbrace {f}_{L}^{k}\right\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}$

The displacement field is written:

${q}^{k}(t)=\left\lbrace {q}^{k}\right\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}$

The velocity and acceleration fields are written:

${\dot{q}}^{k}(t)=j\omega \left\lbrace {q}^{k}\right\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}$

${\ddot{q}}^{k}(t)=-{\omega}^{2}\left\lbrace {q}^{k}\right\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}$

Finally the substructure ${S}^{k}$ satisfies the following equation:

$$
({K}^{k}-{\omega}^{2}{M}^{k})\lbrace {q}^{k}\rbrace =\lbrace {f}_{L}^{k}\rbrace
$$

The method of modal synthesis consists in seeking the unknown displacement field, coming from the finite-element modelling, on an appropriate space, of reduced dimension (Ritz transformation). We have seen that for each substructure, this space is composed of dynamic eigenmodes and of static deflected shapes:

$$
{q}^{k}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\varphi}_{i}^{k}& {\psi}_{j}^{k}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{i}^{k}\\ {\eta}_{j}^{k}\end{array}\right\rbrace ={\Phi}^{k}{\eta}^{k}
$$

${\phi }^{k}$ are the modal vectors associated with the dynamic eigenmodes of ${S}^{k}$ ,

${\psi}^{k}$ are the modal vectors associated with the static deflected shapes of ${S}^{k}$ ,
${\eta}_{i}^{k}$ is the vector of the generalised coordinates associated with the eigenmodes of ${S}^{k}$ ,

${\eta}_{j}^{k}$ is the vector of the generalised coordinates associated with the static deflected shapes of ${S}^{k}$ ,

${\eta}^{k}$ is the vector of the generalised coordinates of ${S}^{k}$ .

The equation is projected onto the basis of ${S}^{k}$ taking into account . This allows us to write:

$$
({\bar{K}}^{k}-{\omega}^{2}{\bar{M}}^{k})\lbrace {\eta}^{k}\rbrace =\lbrace {\bar{f}}_{L}^{k}\rbrace
$$

Assuming that the dynamic eigenmodes and the static deflected shapes are organised as the formula shows and considering that the eigenvectors associated with the dynamic modes are normalised with respect to the unit modal mass, the generalised mass and stiffness matrices take the following form:

${\bar{M}}^{k}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{Id}& {\varphi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\\ {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\varphi}^{k}& {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\end{array}\right]{\bar{K}}^{k}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}^{k}& {\varphi}^{{k}^{T}}{K}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\\ {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{K}^{k}{\varphi}^{k}& {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{K}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\end{array}\right]$

where:

$\mathrm{Id}$ is the Identity matrix,

${\lambda}^{k}$ is the diagonal matrix of the squares of the natural angular frequencies of the basis.

One shows, in the case of the Craig-Bampton method, that the normal modes and the constraint modes are orthogonal with respect to the stiffness matrix, whose off-diagonal terms are, from then on, zero. However, this property is not used in the algorithm programmed in *Code_Aster*.

### 4.3 Dynamic equations satisfied by the global structure

The dynamic equations which the global structure satisfies are:

$(\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{\bar{\bar{K}}}^{1}& & & & \\ & {}^{...}& & & \\ & & {\bar{\bar{K}}}^{k}& & \\ & & & {}^{...}& \\ & & & & {\bar{\bar{K}}}^{{N}_{s}}\end{array}\right]-{\omega}^{2}\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{\bar{\bar{M}}}^{1}& & & & \\ & {}^{...}& & & \\ & & {\bar{\bar{M}}}^{k}& & \\ & & & {}^{...}& \\ & & & & {\bar{\bar{M}}}^{{N}_{s}}\end{array}\right])\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}^{1}\\ {}^{...}\\ {\eta}^{k}\\ {}^{...}\\ {\eta}^{{N}_{s}}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\bar{f}}_{L}^{1}\\ {}^{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{L}^{k}\\ {}^{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{L}^{{N}_{s}}\end{array}\right\rbrace$

To which one must add the connection equations (according to [eq  ]):

$\forall k,l\text{}{L}_{{S}^{k}\cap {S}^{l}}^{k}\left\lbrace {\eta}^{k}\right\rbrace ={L}_{{S}^{k}\cap {S}^{l}}^{l}\left\lbrace {\eta}^{l}\right\rbrace$

This system is solved by double dualisation of the boundary conditions [R3.03.01](doc:r3.03.01). Its final formulation therefore brings in the vector of the Lagrange multipliers $\lambda$ and can be written in the condensed form:

$$
\begin{array}{c}(\bar{\mathrm{K}}-{\omega}^{2}\bar{\mathrm{M}})\lbrace  \eta \rbrace  +{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{T}}\lambda =0\\ \mathrm{L}\lbrace  \lambda \rbrace  =0\end{array}
$$

The problem defined by the equation is symmetric. On the other hand, its dimension is determined by the number of modes taken into consideration (dynamic modes and static deflected shapes). One is therefore led to solve a classical modal problem, of reduced size, with which a linear constraint equation is associated. Its solution therefore poses no problem.

### 4.4 Implementation in Code_Aster

#### 4.4.1 Study of the substructures separately

The dynamic eigenmodes are computed with the operator CALC_MODES [U4.52.02](doc:u4.52.02). The conditions at the connection interfaces are applied with the operator AFFE_CHAR_MECA [U4.44.01](doc:u4.44.01). The operator DEFI_INTERF_DYNA [U4.64.01](doc:u4.64.01) makes it possible to define the connection interfaces of the substructure. The operator DEFI_BASE_MODALE [U4.64.02](doc:u4.64.02) makes it possible to compute the complete projection basis of the substructure.

The operator MACR_ELEM_DYNA [U4.65.01](doc:u4.65.01) computes the generalised stiffness, mass and possibly damping matrices of the substructure, as well as the connection matrices.

#### 4.4.2 Assembly and solution

The model of the complete structure is defined by the operator DEFI_MODELE_GENE [U4.65.02](doc:u4.65.02). Its numbering is carried out by the operator NUME_DDL_GENE [U4.65.03](doc:u4.65.03). The generalised mass, stiffness and possibly damping matrices of the complete structure are assembled as a function of this numbering with the operator ASSE_MATR_GENE [U4.65.04](doc:u4.65.04).

The computation of the eigenmodes of the complete structure is carried out by the operator CALC_MODES [U4.52.02](doc:u4.52.02).

#### 4.4.3 Recovery onto the physical basis

The recovery of the results onto the physical basis is identical to the case of the modal analysis. It involves the operator REST_GENE_PHYS [U4.63.31](doc:u4.63.31).

In order to reduce the duration of the graphical processing during the visualisations, it is possible to create a coarse mesh by the operator DEFI_SQUELETTE [U4.24.01](doc:u4.24.01). This mesh, ignored during the analysis, serves as a support for the visualisations.

## 5 Harmonic response by classical dynamic substructuring

### 5.1 Introduction

For the harmonic problems, the system studied is subjected to a force that is arbitrary in space, but sinusoidal in time. The form of the loading, the excitation frequency and the modal properties each play an essential role. One must also take into account the dissipation in the solid, which one can express by the introduction of a damping matrix. The method of harmonic analysis by substructuring, programmed in *Code_Aster*, which makes it possible to replace the global problem by a simplified problem, proceeds in four stages.

First of all, eigenmodes and static deflected shapes are computed on each of the substructures composing the system. Then, the global problem is projected onto these fields, and one takes into account the couplings between the substructures, at the level of their interfaces. One can then solve in the classical way the reduced problem obtained. Finally, it only remains to deduce from it the overall solution by reconstitution.

### 5.2 Dynamic equations satisfied by the substructures separately

We shall consider a structure $S$ composed of ${N}_{S}$ substructures denoted ${S}^{k}$ . We assume that each substructure is modelled by finite elements. We have seen that in an analysis by dynamic substructuring, the vibration behaviour of the substructures results from the external forces which are applied to it, and from the connection forces which the other substructures exert on it. Thus, at the level of the substructure ${S}^{k}$ , we can write:

$$
{M}^{k}{\ddot{q}}^{k}+{C}^{k}{\dot{q}}^{k}+{K}^{k}{q}^{k}={f}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k}+{f}_{L}^{k}
$$

In a harmonic problem, one imposes a dynamic loading, arbitrary in space, but sinusoidal of angular frequency $\omega$ in time. One is then interested in the stabilised response of the system, without taking into account the transient part.

The field of the external forces is written:

${f}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k}(t)=\left\lbrace {f}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k}\right\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}$

The field of the connection forces is written:

${f}_{L}^{k}(t)=\left\lbrace {f}_{L}^{k}\right\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t}$

The displacement field is written:

${q}^{k}(t)=\left\lbrace {q}^{k}\right\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t }$

The velocity and acceleration fields are written:

${\dot{q}}^{k}(t)=j\omega \left\lbrace {q}^{k}\right\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t }$

${\ddot{q}}^{k}(t)=-{\omega}^{2}\left\lbrace {q}^{k}\right\rbrace {e}^{j\omega t }$

Finally the substructure ${S}^{k}$ satisfies the following equation of motion:

$$
({K}^{k}+j\omega {C}^{k}-{\omega}^{2}{M}^{k})\left\lbrace {q}^{k}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {f}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {f}_{L}^{k}\right\rbrace
$$

The method of modal synthesis consists in seeking the unknown displacement field, coming from the finite-element modelling, on an appropriate space, of reduced dimension (Ritz transformation). We have seen that for each substructure, this space is composed of dynamic eigenmodes and of static deflected shapes:

$$
{q}^{k}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\varphi}^{k}& {\psi}^{k}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{i}^{k}\\ {\eta}_{j}^{k}\end{array}\right\rbrace ={\Phi}^{k}{\eta}^{k}
$$

${\varphi}^{k}$ are the modal vectors associated with the dynamic eigenmodes of ${S}^{k}$ ,

${\psi}^{k}$ are the modal vectors associated with the static deflected shapes of ${S}^{k}$ ,

${\eta}_{i}^{k}$ is the vector of the generalised coordinates associated with the eigenmodes of ${S}^{k}$ ,

${\eta}_{j}^{k}$ is the vector of the generalised coordinates associated with the static deflected shapes of ${S}^{k}$ ,

${\eta}^{k}$ is the vector of the generalised coordinates of ${S}^{k}$ .

The equation is projected onto the basis of ${S}^{k}$ taking into account . This allows us to write:

$$
({\bar{K}}^{k}+j\omega {\bar{C}}^{k}-{\omega}^{2}{\bar{M}}^{k})\left\lbrace {\eta}^{k}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace {\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace {\bar{f}}_{L}^{k}\right\rbrace
$$

Assuming that the dynamic eigenmodes and the static deflected shapes are organised as the formula shows and considering that the eigenvectors associated with the dynamic modes are normalised with respect to the unit modal mass, the generalised mass and stiffness matrices take the following form:

${\bar{M}}^{k}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}\mathrm{Id}& {\varphi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\\ {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\varphi}^{k}& {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{M}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\end{array}\right]{\bar{K}}^{k}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\lambda}^{k}& {\varphi}^{{k}^{T}}{K}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\\ {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{K}^{k}{\varphi}^{k}& {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{K}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\end{array}\right]$

One shows, in the case of the Craig-Bampton method, that the normal modes and the constraint modes are orthogonal with respect to the stiffness matrix, whose off-diagonal terms are, from then on, zero. However, this property is not used in the algorithm programmed in *Code_Aster*.

We consider, as type of dissipation, only the viscous damping (it is the only one which is supported by the substructuring tools in *Code_Aster*). Two methods are usable in order to take this damping into account:

- the Rayleigh damping applied at the elementary level which consists in assuming that the elementary damping matrix ${C}_{e}$ associated with each finite element of the model is a linear combination of the elementary mass and stiffness matrices ${K}_{e}$ and ${M}_{e}$ :

${C}_{e}={\alpha}_{e}{K}_{e}+{\beta  }_{e}{M}_{e}$

The damping matrix is then assembled ${C}^{k}$ then projected onto the basis:

${\bar{C}}^{k}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\varphi}^{{k}^{T}}{C}^{k}{\varphi}^{k}& {\varphi}^{{k}^{T}}{C}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\\ {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{C}^{k}{\varphi}^{k}& {\psi}^{{k}^{T}}{C}^{k}{\psi}^{k}\end{array}\right]$

- the proportional damping applied to the dynamic eigenmodes of each substructure. The resulting matrix is therefore an incomplete diagonal (one does not know how to associate a proportional damping with the static deflected shapes):

${\bar{C}}^{k}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\xi}^{k}& 0\\ 0& 0\end{array}\right]$

### 5.3 Dynamic equations satisfied by the global structure

The dynamic equations which the global structure satisfies are:

$\begin{array}{}\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{\bar{M}}^{1}& & & & \\ & \mathrm{...}& & & \\ & & {\bar{M}}^{k}& & \\ & & & \mathrm{...}& \\ & & & & {\bar{M}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\ddot{\eta}}^{1}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\ddot{\eta}}^{k}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\ddot{\eta}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{\bar{C}}^{1}& & & & \\ & \mathrm{...}& & & \\ & & {\bar{C}}^{k}& & \\ & & & \mathrm{...}& \\ & & & & {\bar{C}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\dot{\eta}}^{1}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\dot{\eta}}^{k}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\dot{\eta}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{\bar{K}}^{1}& & & & \\ & \mathrm{...}& & & \\ & & {\bar{K}}^{k}& & \\ & & & \mathrm{...}& \\ & & & & {\bar{K}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}^{1}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\eta}^{k}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\eta}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right\rbrace \\ =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{1}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\bar{f}}_{L}^{1}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{L}^{k}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{L}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right\rbrace \end{array}$

that is, in harmonic form:

$\begin{array}{}(\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{\bar{\bar{K}}}^{1}& & & & \\ & {}^{...}& & & \\ & & {\bar{\bar{K}}}^{k}& & \\ & & & {}^{...}& \\ & & & & {\bar{\bar{K}}}^{{N}_{s}}\end{array}\right]+j\omega \left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{\bar{\bar{C}}}^{1}& & & & \\ & {}^{...}& & & \\ & & {\bar{\bar{C}}}^{k}& & \\ & & & {}^{...}& \\ & & & & {\bar{\bar{C}}}^{{N}_{s}}\end{array}\right]-{\omega}^{2}\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{\bar{\bar{M}}}^{1}& & & & \\ & {}^{...}& & & \\ & & {\bar{\bar{M}}}^{k}& & \\ & & & {}^{...}& \\ & & & & {\bar{\bar{M}}}^{{N}_{s}}\end{array}\right])\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}^{1}\\ {}^{...}\\ {\eta}^{k}\\ {}^{...}\\ {\eta}^{{N}_{s}}\end{array}\right\rbrace \\ =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{1}\\ {}^{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k}\\ {}^{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{{N}_{s}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\bar{f}}_{L}^{1}\\ {}^{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{L}^{k}\\ {}^{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{L}^{{N}_{s}}\end{array}\right\rbrace \end{array}$

To which one must add the connection equations (according to [eq ]):

$\forall k,l\text{}{L}_{{S}^{k}\cap {S}^{l}}^{k}\left\lbrace {\eta}^{k}\right\rbrace ={L}_{{S}^{k}\cap {S}^{l}}^{l}\left\lbrace {\eta}^{l}\right\rbrace$

This system is solved by double dualisation of the boundary conditions [R3.03.01](doc:r3.03.01). Its final formulation therefore brings in the vector of the Lagrange multipliers $\lambda$ and can be written in the condensed form:

$$
\begin{array}{}(\bar{K}+j{\omega}_{o}\bar{C}-{\omega}_{o}^{2}\bar{M}){\lbrace \eta \rbrace }_{o}+{L}^{T}\lambda ={\lbrace {\bar{f}}_{\text{ext}}\rbrace }_{o}\\ L{\lbrace \lambda \rbrace }_{o}=0\end{array}
$$

The problem defined by the equation is symmetric. On the other hand, its dimension is determined by the number of modes taken into consideration (dynamic modes and static deflected shapes). One is therefore led to solve a classical harmonic problem, of reduced size, with which a linear constraint equation is associated. Its solution therefore poses no problem.

#### 5.4.1 Study of the substructures separately

### 5.5 Conclusion

The method of computation of the harmonic response by modal synthesis available in *Code_Aster* relies on that of modal substructuring, also programmed. It consists in expressing the whole set of the equations in a space of reduced dimension, constituted of modes of the various substructures, by a Rayleigh-Ritz method. The definition of these fields is that used for the modal substructuring and comprises normal modes as well as other static or harmonic ones. The procedure employed results in a projection of the matrices and of the right-hand side onto the restricted space.

## 6 Transient response by classical dynamic substructuring

### 6.1 Introduction

The object of this reference documentation is to present the theoretical bases of the two methods of computation of the transient response by dynamic substructuring available in *Code_Aster*. The first consists in carrying out a transient analysis by substructuring for which the equations of the problem are projected onto the bases associated with each substructure. The difficulty resides in the double dualisation of the boundary conditions which leads to a singular mass matrix. In order to use the explicit integration schemes (which require the inversion of the mass matrix), one must therefore modify the treatment of the interfaces in the operator for the computation of the transient response. The second method consists in determining the eigenmodes of the complete structure by substructuring and in projecting onto this basis the equations of the transient problem. The step of recovery onto the physical basis of the final generalised results must therefore take this double projection into account.

The operator for the computation of the transient response which receives the substructuring is the operator DYNA_VIBRA [U4.53.03](doc:u4.53.03). Relying on methods of modal recombination, it has been designed to solve transient problems in generalised coordinates and it is very efficient for large-size problems whose number of degrees of freedom it makes it possible to reduce. On the other hand, it supports the taking into account of localised non-linearities (at the nodes) which one wishes to generalise to the case of substructuring.

In this report, we present the two methods of transient analysis by substructuring available in *Code_Aster*, as well as their implementation.

### 6.2 Transient analysis by projection onto the bases of the substructures

#### 6.2.1 Dynamic equations satisfied by the substructures separately

Let there be a structure $S$ composed of ${N}_{S}$ substructures denoted ${S}^{k}$ . We assume that each substructure is modelled by finite elements. The vibration behaviour of the substructures results from the external forces which are applied to it and from the connection forces which the other substructures exert on it. Thus, for ${S}^{k}$ , we have:

$$
{M}^{k}{\ddot{q}}^{k}+{C}^{k}{\dot{q}}^{k}+{K}^{k}{q}^{k}={f}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k}+{f}_{L}^{k}
$$

The unknown displacement field, coming from the finite-element modelling, is sought on an appropriate space, of reduced dimension (Ritz transformation) according to the expression:

$$
{q}^{k}={\Phi}^{k}{\eta}^{k}
$$

The Ritz transformation, applied to the real and virtual displacement, leads to the transient dynamic equation of the substructure, and makes it possible to write:

$$
{\bar{M}}^{k}{\ddot{\eta}}^{k}+{\bar{C}}^{k}{\dot{\eta}}^{k}+{\bar{K}}^{k}{\eta}^{k}={\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k}+{\bar{f}}_{L}^{k}
$$

The problem defined by the equation is symmetric. On the other hand, its dimension is determined by the number of modes taken into consideration (dynamic modes and static deflected shapes). One is therefore led to solve a classical transient problem but of reduced size.

#### 6.2.2 Dynamic equations satisfied by the global structure

The matrix writing of the dynamic equation satisfied by the global structure is written simply from the dynamic equations satisfied by each substructure:

$\begin{array}{}\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{\bar{M}}^{1}& & & & \\ & \mathrm{...}& & & \\ & & {\bar{M}}^{k}& & \\ & & & \mathrm{...}& \\ & & & & {\bar{M}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\ddot{\eta}}^{1}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\ddot{\eta}}^{k}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\ddot{\eta}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{\bar{C}}^{1}& & & & \\ & \mathrm{...}& & & \\ & & {\bar{C}}^{k}& & \\ & & & \mathrm{...}& \\ & & & & {\bar{C}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\dot{\eta}}^{1}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\dot{\eta}}^{k}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\dot{\eta}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccccc}{\bar{K}}^{1}& & & & \\ & \mathrm{...}& & & \\ & & {\bar{K}}^{k}& & \\ & & & \mathrm{...}& \\ & & & & {\bar{K}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}^{1}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\eta}^{k}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\eta}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right\rbrace \\ =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{1}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\bar{f}}_{L}^{1}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{L}^{k}\\ \mathrm{...}\\ {\bar{f}}_{L}^{{N}_{S}}\end{array}\right\rbrace \end{array}$

To which one must add the connection equations:

$\forall k,l\text{}{\mathrm{L}}_{{S}^{k}\cap {S}^{l}}^{k}\left\lbrace {\eta}^{k}\right\rbrace ={\mathrm{L}}_{{S}^{k}\cap {S}^{l}}^{l}\left\lbrace {\eta}^{l}\right\rbrace \text{}{\mathrm{f}}_{{L}_{{S}^{k}\cap {S}^{L}}}^{k}=-{\mathrm{f}}_{{L}_{{S}^{k}\cap {S}^{L}}}^{l}$

This system can be written in the condensed form:

$$
{\bar{M}}^{k}{\ddot{\eta}}^{k}+{\bar{C}}^{k}{\dot{\eta}}^{k}+{\bar{K}}^{k}{\eta}^{k}={\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}^{k}+{\bar{f}}_{L}^{k}
$$

$$
L\eta =0
$$

$$
\forall k,l{f}_{{L}_{{S}^{k}\cap {S}^{L}}}^{k}=-{f}_{{L}_{{S}^{k}\cap {S}^{L}}}^{l}
$$

#### 6.2.3 Double dualisation of the boundary conditions

The condensed problem, given above, presents itself in the form of a transient system with which a linear constraint equation is associated (in force and displacement). In *Code_Aster*, this type of problem is classical and it is solved by double dualisation of the boundary conditions [R3.03.01](doc:r3.03.01), that is to say by the introduction of auxiliary variables also called Lagrange multipliers in order to dualise the boundary conditions. After introduction of the Lagrange multipliers, the matrix system is put in the form:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& 0& 0\\ 0& \bar{M}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\ddot{{\lambda}_{1}}\\ \ddot{\eta}\\ \ddot{{\lambda}_{2}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& 0& 0\\ 0& \bar{C}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\dot{{\lambda}_{1}}\\ \dot{\eta}\\ \dot{{\lambda}_{2}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccc}-\text{Id}& L& \text{Id}\\ {L}^{T}& \bar{K}& {L}^{T}\\ \text{Id}& L& -\text{Id}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\lambda}_{1}\\ \eta \\ {\lambda}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ {\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}\\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

where ${\lambda}_{1}$ and ${\lambda}_{2}$ are the Lagrange multipliers.

One observes that the introduction of the Lagrange multipliers renders the mass matrix singular. From then on, the use of the explicit integration schemes developed in the operator DYNA_VIBRA [U4.53.03](doc:u4.53.03) is impossible because they require the inversion of the mass matrix.

In order to render the matrix non-singular, it is sufficient to dualise, with the same Lagrange multipliers, the condition on the second derivative of the connection equations.

Thus the displacement continuity condition is modified by the equivalent system:

$$
\forall t,L\eta =0\iff \mid \begin{array}{c}L(\eta +\ddot{\eta})=0\\ L\eta °=0\text{et}L\dot{\eta}°=0\end{array}
$$

where $\eta °$ and $\dot{\eta}°$ are the generalised displacements and velocities at the initial time instant.

The matrix system which follows from it is of the form [bib10]:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{ccc}-\text{Id}& L& \text{Id}\\ {L}^{T}& \bar{M}& {L}^{T}\\ \text{Id}& L& -\text{Id}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\ddot{{\lambda}_{1}}\\ \ddot{\eta}\\ \ddot{{\lambda}_{2}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& 0& 0\\ 0& \bar{C}& 0\\ 0& 0& 0\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\dot{{\lambda}_{1}}\\ \dot{\eta}\\ \dot{{\lambda}_{2}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccc}-\text{Id}& L& \text{Id}\\ {L}^{T}& \bar{K}& {L}^{T}\\ \text{Id}& L& -\text{Id}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\lambda}_{1}\\ \eta \\ {\lambda}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ {\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}\\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

One observes that the mass matrix has the same form as the stiffness matrix. They are therefore invertible. This system is therefore perfectly equivalent to the equation (it satisfies at every time instant the connection conditions) and it can be treated, in this form, by the operator DYNA_VIBRA.

#### 6.2.4 Treatment of the damping matrix

One observes that the displacement continuity condition, formulated in the equation, results in an undamped second-order equation. During the solution by time step of a transient problem, any numerical error risks sustaining itself, thus reducing the stability of the algorithm. In order to optimise the damping of the numerical error, it is sufficient to dualise the condition on the first derivative of the connection equations with the same Lagrange multipliers multiplied by 2 (so as to render this damping critical):

$$
\forall t,\mathrm{L}\eta =0\mathrm{\iff }\mid \begin{array}{c}\mathrm{L}(\eta +2\dot{\eta}+\ddot{\eta})=0\\ \mathrm{L}\eta °=0\text{et}\mathrm{L}\dot{\eta}°=0\end{array}
$$

The matrix system which follows from it is of the form [bib10]:

$\left[\begin{array}{ccc}-\text{Id}& \mathrm{L}& \text{Id}\\ {\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{T}}& \bar{\mathrm{M}}& {\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{T}}\\ \text{Id}& \mathrm{L}& -\text{Id}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\ddot{{\lambda}_{1}}\\ \ddot{\eta}\\ \ddot{{\lambda}_{2}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccc}-2\text{Id}& 2\mathrm{L}& 2\text{Id}\\ 2{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{T}}& \bar{\mathrm{C}}& 2{\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{T}}\\ 2\text{Id}& 2\mathrm{L}& -2\text{Id}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}\dot{{\lambda}_{1}}\\ \dot{\eta}\\ \dot{{\lambda}_{2}}\end{array}\right\rbrace +\left[\begin{array}{ccc}-\text{Id}& \mathrm{L}& \text{Id}\\ {\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{T}}& \bar{\mathrm{K}}& {\mathrm{L}}^{\mathrm{T}}\\ \text{Id}& \mathrm{L}& -\text{Id}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\lambda}_{1}\\ \eta \\ {\lambda}_{2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ {\bar{\mathrm{f}}}_{\mathit{ext}}\\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace$

One therefore observes that the treatment of the numerical error on the connection equations leads to modifying the damping matrix of the transient problem. This modification is entirely comparable to that which is carried out on the mass matrix.

By contrast, we have not wished to generalise this treatment to the case of the solution of undamped transient problems. That would have led us to create a temporary damping matrix. One might have feared increasing the computation times, without real benefit. Moreover, it is entirely possible for the user to define a damping matrix whose coefficients are zero. The modification of the latter being automatic, the undamped transient system will effectively be solved, while optimising the treatment of any numerical error intervening on the connection equations.

#### 6.2.5 Treatment of the initial conditions

Consider a substructure ${S}^{k}$ characterised by its projection basis ${\Phi}^{k}$ composed of normal modes and of static deflected shapes. One assumes that initially the substructure ${S}^{k}$ is subjected to a displacement or velocity field (this in no way modifies the demonstration) denoted :math:`` . The Ritz transformation allows us to write:

${\mathrm{q}}_{0}^{k}={\Phi}^{k}{\eta}_{0}^{k}$

where ${\eta}_{0}^{k}$ is the vector of the generalised displacements (or velocities) of ${S}^{k}$ to be determined.

The vector of the initial generalised displacements (or velocities) is determined as follows:

${q}_{0}^{k}={\Phi}^{k}{\eta}_{0}^{k}\Rightarrow {\Phi}^{{k}^{T}}{q}_{0}^{k}=({\Phi}^{{k}^{T}}{\Phi}^{k})\cdot {\eta}_{0}^{k}$

$\mathrm{\Rightarrow }{\eta}_{0}^{k}={({\Phi}^{{k}^{T}}{\Phi}^{k})}^{-1}{\Phi}^{{k}^{T}}{\mathrm{q}}_{0}^{k}$

### 6.3 Transient analysis on a global modal basis computed by substructuring

The second method developed consists in solving the transient problem on the modal basis of the complete structure computed by substructuring.

#### 6.3.1 Computation of the eigenmodes of the complete structure by substructuring

Each substructure ${S}^{k}$ is represented by a projection basis, composed of dynamic eigenmodes and of static deflected shapes, which we have denoted ${\Phi}^{k}$ . The projection basis of the complete structure which results from it is denoted $\Phi$ .

The modal basis of the complete structure is computed by substructuring. Each mode obtained is therefore a linear combination of the vectors of the projection bases of the substructures:

$$
{\Phi}_{p}=\sum_{k=1}^{{N}_{S}}{\Phi}^{k}{\alpha}^{k}=\Phi \alpha
$$

where:

| ${\Phi}_{p}$ | is the matrix of the eigenmodes of the complete structure, |
| --- | --- |
| $\alpha$ | is the matrix of the generalised modal coordinates of the structure. |

The projection of the matrices and of the vectors constitutive of the transient problem, onto the basis of the eigenmodes of the complete structure computed by modal synthesis, makes it possible to determine:

| the generalised mass matrix: | $\bar{\bar{M}}={\alpha}^{T}\bar{M}\alpha$ |
| --- | --- |
| the generalised stiffness matrix: | $\bar{\bar{K}}={\alpha}^{T}\bar{C}\alpha$ |
| possibly the generalised damping matrix: | $\bar{\bar{C}}={\alpha}^{T}\bar{C}\alpha$ |
| the vector of the generalised external forces: | ${\bar{\bar{f}}}_{\mathrm{ext}}={\alpha}^{T}{\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}$ |

By virtue of the orthogonality of the eigenmodes of the structure computed by modal synthesis, with respect to the matrices $M$ and $K$ , the generalised mass and stiffness matrices obtained above are diagonal:

$$
\bar{\bar{M}}={\alpha}^{T}\bar{M}\alpha ={(\Phi \alpha )}^{T}M\Phi \alpha ={\Phi}_{p}^{T}M{\Phi}_{p}
\bar{\bar{K}}={\alpha}^{T}\bar{K}\alpha ={(\Phi \alpha )}^{T}K\Phi \alpha ={\Phi}_{p}^{T}K{\Phi}_{p}
$$

#### 6.3.2 Dynamic equation satisfied by the global structure

The complete structure is subjected to the external forces which are applied to it. Thus, we can write:

$$
M\ddot{q}+C\dot{q}+{K}^{\bar{q}}={\bar{f}}_{\mathrm{ext}}
$$

The unknown displacement field, coming from the finite-element modelling, is replaced by its projection onto the basis of the eigenmodes of the structure, according to the formula:

$$
q={\Phi}_{p}\eta
$$

where $\eta$ is the vector of the generalised coordinates of the structure.

The Ritz transformation, applied to the transient dynamic equation of the structure, makes it possible to write:

$$
\bar{\bar{M}}\ddot{\eta}+\bar{\bar{C}}\dot{\eta}+{\bar{\bar{K}}}^{\bar{\eta}}={\bar{\bar{f}}}_{\mathrm{ext}}
$$

The step of recovery onto the physical basis requires that one take into account the double projection (onto the modal basis of the complete structure, then onto the projection bases of the substructures - cf. ).

$$
q={\Phi}_{p}\eta =\Phi \alpha \eta
$$

The problem defined by the equation is of an entirely classical form. One is led to solve a symmetric transient problem whose dimension is determined by the number of modes computed by substructuring and whose mass and stiffness matrices are diagonal.

Let us note finally that the treatment of the initial conditions is identical to the case of the transient analysis by projection onto the bases of the substructures (cf. § 6.2 ).

### 6.4 Comparative study of the two methods developed

The theoretical bases, associated with the two methodologies implemented in *Code_Aster* in order to carry out a computation of the transient response using the substructuring techniques, have been presented in the preceding chapters. We specify, here, their essential particularities.

The first methodology consists in carrying out a computation of the transient response by substructuring. The equation satisfied by the complete structure is then projected onto the bases of the substructures. The accuracy of this method is therefore directly determined by the extent of these bases. The latter can be enriched without leading to prohibitive computation times because the substructures are, in principle, of relatively reduced sizes. Be that as it may, it is difficult to estimate the effect of the modal truncation from the sole knowledge of the modes of the bases of the substructures. On the other hand, the projection bases of the substructures are composed of modes which are not all orthogonal to one another (eigenmodes and static deflected shapes). The generalised mass and stiffness matrices constitutive of the final transient problem are therefore non-diagonal. Overall, their bandwidth can be determined from the number of static deflected shapes of the projection bases of the substructures. The duration of the integration in the transient computation operator DYNA_VIBRA will therefore be all the longer as there are interface degrees of freedom. On the other hand, the maximum integration time step admissible by the explicit integration schemes is determined from the maximum frequency of the projection basis. In the case of a transient analysis by substructuring, this frequency results, in principle, from the static modes whose diagonal terms are high in the generalised stiffness matrix and low in the generalised mass matrix. From then on, the integration time step cannot be determined a priori. Experience shows that it is very small, with regard to the natural frequencies of the bases of the substructures and that the use of the integration scheme with adaptive time step of DYNA_VIBRA is very profitable.

The second methodology consists in carrying out a transient analysis on the modal basis of the complete structure obtained by substructuring. One knows that the step consisting in computing the eigenmodes of the structure can be costly in terms of computation time. This is all the more true when one considers non-linear forces because the projection basis must then be sufficiently extended in order to represent well the dynamics of the system. On the other hand, the modal basis on which the transient response is computed is generally of dimension smaller than that determined by the eigenvectors of the substructures (eigenmodes and static deflected shapes). The double projection therefore amounts to introducing a cut-off frequency. One must therefore expect this method to be less accurate than the preceding one. However, the computation of the eigenmodes makes it possible to estimate the effect of the modal truncation. On the other hand, it can make it possible to validate the models of the substructures if one has experimental results. Finally, the essential advantage of this method is that the mass and stiffness matrices used in the transient analysis are diagonal. The numerical integration is therefore very rapid.

### 6.6 Conclusion

We have presented, in this report, the work carried out in order to introduce, into *Code_Aster*, the computation of the linear transient response by dynamic substructuring. The methods which have been chosen consist, for the first of them, in projecting the transient equations onto the "bases" of each substructure, composed of dynamic eigenmodes and of static deflected shapes and, for the second, in computing the eigenmodes of the complete structure by substructuring and in projecting the transient equations onto them.

We began with an account of the theoretical bases on which the first method of transient substructuring relies in order to arrive at the matrix formulation of the final problem. In particular, an original treatment of the displacement continuity equation in order to render the mass matrix invertible and in order to ensure an optimal stability of the integration algorithm, has led us to modify the form of the mass and damping matrices of the transient problem.

For the second method, the essential difficulty consists in recovering the results obtained in generalised coordinates onto the physical basis. Indeed, one must take into account the double projection: onto the modal basis of the complete structure on the one hand, and onto the bases of the substructures on the other hand.

The developments carried out have resulted in modifications of the operators DYNA_VIBRA [U4.53.03](doc:u4.53.03) and REST_GENE_PHYS [U4.63.31](doc:u4.63.31). Their syntax has been very little modified, so that their use is identical during an analysis by substructuring and a direct analysis by modal recombination.

## 7 Dynamic condensation by dynamic macro-elements in static substructuring

### 7.1 Introduction

The objective of the dynamic condensation of a model by static substructuring is to reduce the total number of degrees of freedom of a model constituted of several subdomains by condensing the solution of the subdomains of linear behaviour onto the degrees of freedom of their interfaces with subdomains of non-linear behaviour.

The idea is then to represent each of these subdomains by a macro-element of static type in order to be able to use it in a mixed model also comprising finite elements for the non-condensed parts of non-linear behaviour in order to proceed to non-linear analyses by means of the non-linear computation operators STAT_NON_LINE [U4.51.03](doc:u4.51.03) or DYNA_NON_LINE [U4.53.01](doc:u4.53.01).

In the condensation of each subdomain, one also wants to integrate the dynamic part of its behaviour obtained by modal analysis and for that, one will therefore use dynamic macro-elements as the static macro-elements necessary in static substructuring.

The principle of this conversion and their application is described in the documents [bib13].

### 7.2 Classical problem and principle of the method

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/1000000000000195000000FCEE7F995A5C82EAD4.jpg)

**Figure 7.2-a: Subdomains of the classical condensation problem.**

On figure 7.2-a above are schematised the possible subdomains which one can encounter within the framework of a classical problem of condensation by static substructuring:

- the subdomains of type $\mathit{E1}$ : subdomains of structure of constant linear behaviour condensed and modelled by a dynamic macro-element,
- the subdomains of type $\mathit{E2}$ : subdomains of structure of potentially non-linear behaviour not condensed and modelled by finite elements,
- the subdomains of type $\mathit{E3}$ : soil subdomain represented by a macro-element coming from the conversion of a complex soil impedance matrix.

The basic principle of the condensation by macro-element consists in partitioning, for each subdomain of type $\mathit{E1}$ , the degrees of freedom $j$ of their interfaces denoted $\Sigma$ with the subdomains of type $\mathit{E2}$ as well as their other degrees of freedom $i$ called "internal".

The stiffness matrices $K$ and mass matrices $M$ are thus decomposed on these degrees of freedom:

$K=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{K}_{ii}& {K}_{ij}\\ {K}_{ji}& {K}_{jj}\end{array}\right]$         $M=\left[\begin{array}{cc}{M}_{ii}& {M}_{ij}\\ {M}_{ji}& {M}_{jj}\end{array}\right]$     $\begin{array}{}i\text{ddl internes}\\ j\text{ddl d'interfaces}\end{array}$

The principle of static substructuring amounts, for the matrix $K$ for example, to condensing it only onto the degrees of freedom $j$ of the interfaces $\Sigma$ by correcting the initial term ${K}_{jj}$ by a complementary term called "Schur complement" according to the principle of the so-called Guyan condensation [bib14]:

$$
\bar{{K}_{jj}}={K}_{jj}-{K}_{ij}^{T}{({K}_{ii})}^{-1}{K}_{ij}={\Phi}^{T}K\Phi
$$

The computation of the new term $\bar{{K}_{jj}}$ amounts to a projection of the matrix $K$ onto a basis of static modes $\Psi$ of "constraint" type obtained by static response to unit displacements imposed at each degree of freedom $j$ of the interfaces $\Sigma$ :

$\Psi =\left[\begin{array}{}{\Psi}_{ij}\\ I\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}{}-{K}_{ii}^{-1}{K}_{ij}\\ I\end{array}\right]$

The condensation onto the degrees of freedom $j$ of the interfaces $\Sigma$ of the mass matrix $M$ and of the loads of the subdomains of type $\mathit{E1}$ will be done in an analogous manner by the matrix product of the matrix and of the loading vectors onto the basis of static modes $\Psi$ .

One then observes that the terms ${\Phi}^{T}K\Phi$ and ${\Psi}^{\mathrm{T.}}M.\Psi$ can be obtained directly for each subdomain of type $\mathit{E1}$ by means of the operator for the computation of a dynamic macro-element MACR_ELEM_DYNA [U4.65.01](doc:u4.65.01) and that the projected load terms are obtained ${\Psi}^{T}.F$ by means of the operator PROJ_VECT_BASE [U4.63.13](doc:u4.63.13).

### 7.3 Condensation methods by complete or reduced basis

According to the mode of computation of the modes of representation of the motions of the interfaces, two methods are currently used:

#### 7.3.1 The computation method by complete basis

The motion $\mathrm{U}$ of any point of a subdomain of type E1 is expressed from the decomposition onto the static modes $\Psi$ and onto a complement brought by the dynamic part of the motion expressed by eigenmodes $\Phi$ :

$$
U=\Psi .{q}_{\Sigma}+\Phi .q
$$

With the method by complete basis, on the interface $\Sigma$ , the eigenmodes $\Phi$ have a zero contribution and the static modes $\Psi$ have a unit value and therefore the displacement $U$ of a point of the interface is expressed:

$$
U={X}_{\Sigma}={\mathrm{Id}.q}_{\Sigma}
$$

In this case, the physical degrees of freedom ${X}_{\Sigma}$ of the interface $\Sigma$ merge with the generalised coordinates ${q}_{\Sigma}$ associated with the static modes $\Psi$ .

The generalised coordinates $q$ associated with the eigenmodes $\Phi$ being zero on the interface, their solution will be uncoupled from that of the generalised coordinates ${q}_{\Sigma}$ . In the assembly of the matrices of the condensed problem, the terms of projection onto the eigenmodes ${\Phi}^{T}.K.\Phi$ and ${\Phi}^{T}.M.\Phi$ will be juxtaposed. In order to be able to "mix" physical degrees of freedom with generalised coordinates, one uses the device, transparent to the user, of passing the latter off as additional physical degrees of freedom taken from among the degrees of freedom internal to a domain of type $\mathit{E1}$ .

This alias is active in the model where the domains of type $\mathit{E1}$ are condensed by macro-elements, but in order to have the true displacement of the degrees of freedom internal to these domains, it is obtained in post-processing after solution of the assembled global problem by means of the operator REST_COND_TRAN [U4.63.33](doc:u4.63.33) by applying the initial relation:

$$
\mathrm{U}=\Psi .{\mathrm{q}}_{\Sigma}+\Phi .\mathrm{q}
$$

At the level of the assembly of a subdomain of type $\mathit{E1}$ , the initial storage represented on figure 7.3.1-a comprises terms both on internal degrees of freedom and on the interface degrees of freedom with a non-complete fill-in profile.

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/100000000000021C0000019F35AB3B22F9B86841.jpg)

**Figure 7.3.1-a: Initial assembly of the subdomains** $\mathit{E1}$ **before condensation.**

After condensation by the complete method, most of the internal degrees of freedom disappear, but a very small part is devoted to representing the generalised coordinates associated with the eigenmodes and the terms of the static macro-element are added to the interface degrees of freedom with a complete fill-in profile this time. The resulting storage is represented on figure 7.3.1-b below.

![Figure](/figures/r4.06.02/10000000000002D50000019F960852E9B3AB90F7.jpg)

**Figure 7.3.1-b: Assembly of the subdomains** $\mathit{E1}$ **with the complete method.**

The assembly of the terms of the static macro-element is carried out by simple summation on the interface degrees of freedom and does not in general require an additional connection on the interface when one takes into account all the constrained static modes of the interface. It is not so in the particular case of the condensation onto a single interface node where there one must add a connection relation, rigid on the interface degrees of freedom.

In the case of a subdomain of type $\mathit{E3}$ , the post-processing by REST_COND_TRAN is useless because the displacement has no component on dynamic modes and the displacement at the interface is definitively obtained as soon as the global problem is solved.

#### 7.3.2 The computation method by modal reduction

With the method by modal reduction, the motion $U$ of any point of a subdomain of type $\mathit{E1}$ is still expressed from the decomposition onto the static modes $\Psi$ and onto a complement brought by the dynamic part of the motion expressed by eigenmodes $\Phi$ :

$$
\mathrm{U}=\Psi .{\mathrm{q}}_{\Sigma}+\Phi .\mathrm{q}
$$

But this time, on the interface $\Sigma$ , the eigenmodes $\Phi$ have a non-zero contribution because the static modes $\Psi$ have a non-unit value and are in number generally very much smaller than the number of physical degrees of freedom ${X}_{\Sigma}$ of the interface $\Sigma$ which, in this case, do not merge with the generalised coordinates ${q}_{\Sigma}$ associated with the static modes $\Psi$ . One must therefore distinguish in the assembly and the solution the terms associated with the physical degrees of freedom and those associated with the generalised coordinates. In the same way as for the generalised coordinates $q$ associated with the eigenmodes $\Phi$ , one uses the device, transparent to the user, of passing the generalised coordinates ${q}_{\Sigma}$ associated with the static modes $\Psi$ off as additional physical degrees of freedom also taken from among degrees of freedom internal to a domain of type $\mathit{E1}$ .

But this time, the solution of the physical degrees of freedom ${X}_{\Sigma}$ of the interface $\Sigma$ will not be uncoupled from that of the generalised coordinates ${\mathrm{q}}_{\Sigma}$ . In the assembly of the matrices of the condensed problem, the terms of projection onto the eigenmodes ${\Phi}^{T}.K.\Phi$ and ${\Phi}^{T}.M.\Phi$ will be juxtaposed. But one will then have to add a connection and therefore Lagrange degrees of freedom between the physical degrees of freedom ${X}_{\Sigma}$ of the interface $\Sigma$ and the generalised coordinates ${q}_{\Sigma}$ . This connection is expressed by the relation:

$$
{X}_{\Sigma}=\Psi .{q}_{\Sigma}
$$

This relation is expressed by the repeatable keyword LIAISON_INTERF of the operator AFFE_CHAR_MECA [U4.44.01](doc:u4.44.01), which makes it possible to link each subdomain of type $\mathit{E1}$ to a subdomain of type $\mathit{E2}$ (cf. figure 7.2-a) by generating a particular case of LIAISON_DDL with the coefficients of the preceding linear relation.

Then, in order to have the true displacement of the degrees of freedom internal to the domains of type $\mathit{E1}$ , one proceeds in the same way in post-processing after solution of the assembled global problem by means of the operator REST_COND_TRAN [U4.63.33](doc:u4.63.33) by applying the initial relation:

$$
\mathrm{U}=\Psi .{\mathrm{q}}_{\Sigma}+\Phi .\mathrm{q}
$$

After condensation by the modal reduction method, most of the internal degrees of freedom disappear, but a very small part is devoted to representing the generalised coordinates associated with the eigenmodes, another small part is devoted to the generalised coordinates associated with the interface eigenmodes. This time, the terms of the static macro-element are not added to the interface degrees of freedom which then retain a non-complete fill-in profile. But one then obtains a quadrangular block of Lagrange degrees of freedom between the physical degrees of freedom ${X}_{\Sigma}$ of the interface $\Sigma$ and the generalised coordinates ${q}_{\Sigma}$ .

The resulting storage is represented on figure 7.3.2-a below.

One possible choice will be that used in soil-structure interaction [bib15] by considering a basis of eigenmodes of the subdomain of type $\mathit{E1}$ on a carpet of springs representative of the stiffness of the adjacent domains of type $\mathit{E2}$ or $\mathit{E3}$ .

## 8 Indications for the a posteriori control of the quality of the reduced model

One of the principles making it possible to validate the quality of a reduced model consists in estimating, a posteriori, the deviation from equilibrium which may exist after the analysis.

### 8.1 General case for an analysis on a reduced model

In order to characterise this deviation from equilibrium, one uses the principle of the force residual. This force residual ${F}_{r}$ is computed

- for the time responses:

$$
\left[{K}_{e}{T}_{r}\right]{q}_{r}(t)+\left[{D}_{v}{T}_{r}\right]\dot{{q}_{r}}(t)+\left[M{T}_{r}\right]\ddot{{q}_{r}}(t)-Bu(t)={F}_{r}(t)
$$

- for the harmonic responses:

$$
\left[({K}_{e}+j{K}_{h}+{K}_{v}(s))+s{D}_{v}+{s}^{2}M\right]{T}_{r}{q}_{r}(s)-Bu(s)={F}_{r}(s)
$$

where ${K}_{e}$ is the stiffness matrix of the structure, ${D}_{v}$ the matrix associated with the viscous dissipation, and $M$ the mass matrix. One furthermore denotes by ${q}_{r}$ the vector of the reduced degrees of freedom, and by ${T}_{r}$ the associated reduction basis. The external-force term is defined by the product of a matrix of localisation of the forces $B$ and of a vector $u$ specifying the time evolution of the excitation. This writing makes it possible to separate the spatial and time components of a force often denoted $f(t)$ . Concerning the harmonic response, ${K}_{h}$ is the matrix representing the hysteretic behaviour, ${K}_{v}$ the matrix representing the viscoelastic behaviour.

One can also adopt the same procedure for the computation of the complex eigenmodes, detailed in the documentation on the construction of the reduced models in dynamics [U2.06.04](doc:u2.06.04)

If the solution obtained is exact, then the residual ${F}_{r}$ is zero, by definition. Otherwise, one can build a measure of the deviation from equilibrium from the static response of the structure to this loading. One denotes by ${R}_{r}$ the displacement residual associated with each complex mode computed. By definition, one has

$$
{K}_{e}{R}_{r}={F}_{r}
$$

A measure of the deviation from equilibrium is given by the elastic potential energy of the structure, that is

$$
{E}_{r}=\frac{1}{2}{∥{R}_{r}^{T}{K}_{e}{R}_{r}∥}^{2}
$$

An example of use of this technique is given in the documentation [U2.06.04](doc:u2.06.04)

### 8.2 Particular case of the computation of the modes of a generalised model

The same principle can be implemented, but in a slightly more precise way, so as to bring out particular deviations from the various equilibria.

#### 8.2.1 Presentation

In the case of an analysis by substructuring with the use of interface modes, it is important to be able to determine more precisely the origin of an inaccurate analysis. The sources of inaccuracy are:

- the poor representation of the displacements at the interfaces, for each substructure,
- the dynamic behaviour of the internal parts, for each substructure,
- the differential displacements (separation) which may appear at the interfaces between the sub
- structures.

The command CALC_CORR_SSD [U4.52.16](doc:u4.52.16) makes it possible, on the basis of the computations of the works associated with these various forces, to build correction terms making it possible to improve separately the interface behaviours, and the internal dynamic behaviours of each of the substructures.

**NB:**

One will moreover have to take care, in the enrichment process, to orthogonalise separately the vectors with fixed interfaces, and the vectors with free interface, as is carried out in the test case SDLS122 [V3.02.122](doc:v3.02.122), before concatenating them and building the modal bases.

#### 8.2.2 Computation of the indicators and associated corrections

In order to detail the construction of the various indicators and corrective terms built in the command CALC_CORR_SSD, let us consider a simple example constituted of the assembly of 2 substructures along a single interface. This approach is easily generalised, but leads to cumbersome notations.

##### 8.2.2.1 Definition of the coupled problem

For each substructure, one introduces the mass and stiffness matrices. One denotes by $c$ the degrees of freedom associated with the internal parts ("complementary"), and by $i$ the degrees of freedom associated with the interfaces. One also introduces the associated reduction basis, composed both of modes with fixed interfaces ${\Phi}_{c}$ , and of interface modes ${\psi}_{i}$ , lifted statically onto the rest of the structure, so that the corresponding displacements on the complementary degrees of freedom are ${\psi}_{c}=-{K}_{\mathit{cc}}^{-1}{K}_{\mathit{ci}}{\psi}_{i}$

$$
{K}_{S}={\left[\begin{array}{cc}{K}_{ii}& {K}_{\mathit{ic}}\\ {K}_{\mathit{ci}}& {K}_{\mathit{cc}}\end{array}\right]}_{S}/{M}_{S}={\left[\begin{array}{cc}{M}_{ii}& {M}_{\mathit{ic}}\\ {M}_{\mathit{ci}}& {M}_{\mathit{cc}}\end{array}\right]}_{S}/{T}_{S}={\left[\begin{array}{cc}0& {\Psi}_{i}\\ {\Phi}_{c}& {\Psi}_{c}\end{array}\right]}_{S}
$$

The reduced mass and stiffness matrices are written

$$
{K}_{\mathit{rS}}={T}_{S}^{T}{K}_{S}{T}_{S}/{M}_{\mathit{rS}}={T}_{S}^{T}{M}_{S}{T}_{S}
$$

Because of the introduction of interface modes, the compatibility relation of the interface displacements between the substructures 1 and 2 can no longer be written ${q}_{i2}={q}_{i1}$ , but becomes, if the substructure 2 is slave:

$$
{\hat{T}}_{\mathit{iS}2}^{T}{T}_{\mathit{iS}1}{\eta}_{1}={T}_{\mathit{iS}2}^{T}{T}_{\mathit{iS}2}{\eta}_{2}
$$

One then defines a basis of the kernel of the constraints in order to eliminate the interface relations, on the basis of the elements present in section 2.5

$$
{Q}_{i}=\mathit{Ker}(\left[\begin{array}{cc}{\hat{T}}_{\mathit{iS}2}^{T}{T}_{\mathit{iS}1}& -{T}_{\mathit{iS}2}^{T}{T}_{\mathit{iS}2}\end{array}\right])\mathrm{\Rightarrow }
$$

The complete model is then assembled, then projected onto a basis of the kernel of the constraints:

$$
{K}_{g}={Q}^{T}\left[\begin{array}{cc}{K}_{\mathit{r1}}& 0\\ 0& {K}_{\mathit{r2}}\end{array}\right]Q/{M}_{g}={Q}^{T}\left[\begin{array}{cc}{M}_{\mathit{r1}}& 0\\ 0& {M}_{\mathit{r2}}\end{array}\right]Q
$$

One denotes by $\eta$ the generalised degrees of freedom associated with the problem thus obtained. One therefore has, if one denotes by $q$ the physical degrees of freedom, $q={T}_{r}{q}_{r}={T}_{r}Q\eta$ .

##### 8.2.2.2 Deviation from equilibrium

If one denotes by ${Z}_{0}={K}_{g}-{\omega}_{0}{M}_{g}$ the dynamic impedance associated with the eigenmode of angular frequency ${\omega}_{0}$ , and by ${\eta}_{0}$ the associated reduced eigenmode, one has

$$
{Q}^{T}{T}_{r}^{T}\left[{Z}_{0}\right]{T}_{r}Q{\eta}_{0}=0
$$

Under these conditions, the relation making it possible to quantify the deviation from equilibrium of this particular mode becomes:

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cccc}{Z}_{\mathrm{0cc}1}& {Z}_{\mathrm{0ci}1}& 0& 0\\ {Z}_{\mathrm{0ic}1}& {Z}_{\mathrm{0ii}1}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& {Z}_{\mathrm{0ii}2}& {Z}_{\mathrm{0ic}2}\\ 0& 0& {Z}_{\mathrm{0ci}2}& {Z}_{\mathrm{0cc}2}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{cccc}{\Phi}_{c1}& {\Psi}_{c1}& 0& 0\\ 0& {\Psi}_{i1}& 0& 0\\ 0& 0& {\Psi}_{i2}& 0\\ 0& 0& {\Psi}_{c2}& {\Phi}_{c2}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}{ccc}\mathit{Id}& 0& 0\\ 0& {Q}_{i1}& 0\\ 0& {Q}_{i2}& 0\\ 0& 0& \mathit{Id}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{\eta}_{\mathrm{0c}1}\\ \hat{{\eta}_{\mathrm{0i}}}\\ {\eta}_{\mathrm{0c}2}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{f}_{\mathrm{0c}1}\\ {f}_{\mathrm{0i}1}\\ {f}_{\mathrm{0i}2}\\ {f}_{\mathrm{0c}2}\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

If the projection basis is of good quality, then the deviation from equilibrium must be small, and, ideally, one must even verify

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{f}_{\mathrm{0c}1}\\ {f}_{\mathrm{0i}1}\\ {f}_{\mathrm{0i}2}\\ {f}_{\mathrm{0c}2}\end{array}\right\rbrace \approx \left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}0\\ 0\\ 0\\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

the solver having to ensure

$$
\left\lbrace \begin{array}{cccc}{\phi }_{\mathrm{0c}1}& {\phi }_{\mathrm{0i}1}& {\phi }_{\mathrm{0i}2}& {\phi }_{\mathrm{0c}2}\end{array}\right\rbrace \left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{f}_{\mathrm{0c}1}\\ {f}_{\mathrm{0i}1}\\ {f}_{\mathrm{0i}2}\\ {f}_{\mathrm{0c}2}\end{array}\right\rbrace \approx 0/{\phi }_{0}={T}_{r}Q{\eta}_{0}
$$

In order to be sure of having a model of good quality, one must therefore ensure the nullity of each of the terms, independently of one another. One must therefore verify the nullity of the works of the forces associated with the residual loads in each substructure, that is

$$
{\phi }_{\mathrm{0c}1}^{T}{f}_{\mathrm{0c}1}=0/{\phi }_{\mathrm{0c}2}^{T}{f}_{\mathrm{0c}2}=0
$$

One must also verify the nullity of the virtual works associated with the residual forces at the interface:

$$
{\phi }_{\mathrm{0i}1}^{T}{f}_{\mathrm{0i}1}+{\phi }_{\mathrm{0i}2}^{T}{f}_{\mathrm{0i}2}=0
$$

Finally, one must also verify the compatibility of the displacements, and avoid the differential displacements of interfaces (separations)

$$
{({\phi }_{\mathrm{0i}2}-C{\phi }_{\mathrm{0i}1})}^{T}{f}_{\mathrm{0i}2}=0
$$

$C$ is the operator of projection of the displacements of the master interface onto the slave interface. In the case of compatible interfaces, $C$ is the identity matrix.

In practice, in order to be more simply exploitable, the various works computed in CALC_CORR_SSD are divided by the natural angular frequency of the mode considered. This rescaling makes it possible to compare the error levels between the various modes.

##### 8.2.2.3 Corrections associated with the residual forces

Each of these terms makes it possible to define associated corrections. Thus, for each substructure $k$ , one builds a subspace dedicated to the enrichment based on the principle of the static correction (cf. [U2.06.04](doc:u2.06.04)):

$$
{T}_{\mathit{Enrich}}=\left[\begin{array}{cc}0& {T}_{iI}\\ {T}_{cC}& {T}_{cI}\end{array}\right]
$$

With,

$$
{K}_{\mathit{cc}k}{T}_{cC}={f}_{\mathrm{0c}k}
$$

And

$$
\left[\begin{array}{cc}{K}_{iik}& {K}_{\mathit{ic}k}\\ {K}_{\mathit{ci}k}& {K}_{\mathit{cc}k}\end{array}\right]\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{T}_{iI}\\ {T}_{cI}\end{array}\right\rbrace =\left\lbrace \begin{array}{c}{f}_{\mathrm{0i}}\\ 0\end{array}\right\rbrace
$$

In the case where the stiffness matrix would be singular for the substructure $k$ , one regularises the problem by building a mass-shifted matrix $K-{\alpha}^{2}M$ , where $\alpha$ is a value small compared with the natural angular frequencies of the first flexible modes of the structure.

##### 8.2.2.4 Corrections associated with the differential interface displacements

In the case where the coupled modes computed do not perfectly ensure the continuity of the interface displacements, it is possible to enrich the displacements on either side of the interface. One must however here consider two cases, according to whether, for the substructure $k$ , the interface considered is master or slave. If the interface is a slave interface, one then simply projects the displacement of the master interface onto the slave interface, and lifts this displacement statically onto the rest of the substructure. The complete reduction basis is then written

$$
{T}_{\mathit{Enrich}-\mathit{Slav}}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& {T}_{iI}& C{\phi }_{i\mathit{Mast}}\\ {T}_{cC}& {T}_{cI}& {T}_{c\mathit{Slav}}\end{array}\right]/{T}_{c\mathit{Slav}}=-{K}_{\mathit{cc}}^{-1}{K}_{\mathit{ci}}C{\phi }_{i\mathit{Mast}}
$$

In the case where the interface considered is master, one must then extrapolate the motions of the slave interface. The approach retained consists in building a basis of the modes of the master interface, and in seeking the best selection of them which makes it possible to reconstruct the displacements of the slave interface. This combination of interface modes is then lifted statically onto the complementary degrees of freedom of the substructure considered. If one denotes by $R$ the extrapolation operator thus defined, the complete basis becomes, for a master interface:

$$
{T}_{\mathit{Enrich}-\mathit{Mast}}=\left[\begin{array}{ccc}0& {T}_{iI}& R{\phi }_{i\mathit{Slav}}\\ {T}_{cC}& {T}_{cI}& {T}_{c\mathit{Mast}}\end{array}\right]/{T}_{c\mathit{Mast}}=-{K}_{\mathit{cc}}^{-1}{K}_{\mathit{ci}}R{\phi }_{i\mathit{Slav}}
$$

The construction of $R$ is carried out as follows:

- One computes the modes ${\Gamma}_{i\mathit{Mast}}$ of the interface of the master substructure
- One seeks the best linear combination of these modes which makes it possible to represent the displacements of the slave interface. One therefore seeks ${\beta}_{0}$ which realises

$$
{\beta}_{0}=\underset{\beta}{\mathit{ArgMin}}{∥C{\Gamma}_{i\mathit{Mast}}\beta -{\phi }_{i\mathit{Slav}}∥}^{2}
$$

If the norm of the error is greater than 1e-6, then one computes a greater number of modes ${\Gamma}_{i\mathit{Mast}}$ , and one iterates as long as the error norm is not satisfactory.

$R$ is then written formally

$$
R={\Gamma}_{i\mathit{Mast}}{(C{\Gamma}_{i\mathit{Mast}})}^{+}
$$

These corrections are computed for each interface of each substructure. One then separates each family of vectors into two families in order to be orthogonalised:

- vectors whose interface displacements are zero
- vectors whose interface displacements are non-zero

**NB:**

One will moreover have to take care, in the enrichment process, to orthogonalise separately the vectors with fixed interfaces, and the vectors with free interface, as is carried out in the test case SDLS122 [V3.02.122](doc:v3.02.122)
