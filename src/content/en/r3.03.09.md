# r3.03.09 Arlequin 3D–beam connection

**Summary:**

This document explains the Arlequin method developed in *Code_Aster*  in order to connect a 3D continuum modelling and a beam modelling.

In 3D, this connection takes the form of linear relations linking the displacements of the set of 3D nodes (3 degrees of freedom per node) tied to the set of beam nodes (6 degrees of freedom per node).

## 1 Presentation

The command used to treat connections of Arlequin type between beam elements and 3D elements is AFFE_CHAR_MECA[U4.25.01], keywords LIAISON_ELEM and OPTION = '3D_POU_ARLEQUIN'.

## 2 The Arlequin 3D–beam connection

### 2.1 Objectives and assumptions

Unlike the classical adaptive methods, the Arlequin framework makes it possible to superimpose adapted local models, rather than to correct the existing global models. The physical solution is then reconstituted in the superposition zone as a partition of the solution fields.

In what follows, the qualifications “fine” or “local” and “coarse” or “global” are used here in anticipation of the heterogeneities of the modellings used (1D beam and 3D solid).

The Arlequin method is a coupling method with overlap based on the combination of models of different finenesses and/or modellings. It allows the mixing as well as the “in-volume” connection of heterogeneous constitutive formulations, and this without imposing a priori constraints on the meshes to be joined 1
. It is founded on two principal ideas:

- The connection of the subdomains by means of a weak formulation: the introduction of Lagrange multipliers in the gluing zone guarantees the coupling of the models, the continuity of the kinematic quantities, as well as the control of the discrepancies in the stresses and in the strains between the coupled zones;
- The distribution of the energy between domains and models: in order not to count twice the energy of the global system in the overlap zone, the virtual works associated with the two models are distributed between the coupled subdomains through the gluing zone by means of weighting functions $(\gamma ,1-\gamma )$ which form a partition of unity (the sum of the two functions is equal to 1) over the whole of the domain of the study. One notes that for two subdomains ${\Omega}_{\mathrm{1D}}$ and ${\Omega}_{\mathrm{3D}}$ , the overlap zone is defined by their intersection.

![Figure](/figures/r3.03.09/100002000000020300000082FF9E97A775C17859.png)

Figure 2.1-a weighting functions in the Arlequin framework

This weighting makes it possible to avoid taking the energy into account several times in the same zone and thus allows the user a certain freedom in the choice of the predominant model. Indeed, it makes it possible to place the weight on the model which one wishes to have express itself.

In the Arlequin framework, as soon as heterogeneous modellings are coupled, it is recommended to discretise the Lagrange multipliers on the spatially coarse model. This makes it possible to avoid the phenomenon of numerical locking and, consequently, to allow more freedom to each of the models by allowing the fine model to express its richness. It follows that the finite-element space of these Lagrange multipliers is built on the coarse model (beam model for this case).

The Arlequin 3D–beam connection, as developed in Code_Aster, must satisfy a highly important requirement. Indeed, the 1D and 3D meshes must be “hierarchically compatible”, in the sense that all the 3D elements are included in the cylindrical space of the facing 1D element (no 3D element straddling two beam elements). The only constraint imposed on the user therefore consists in making an effort when elaborating the mesh, which is not a bad thing, because the final model will be better controlled.

![Figure](/figures/r3.03.09/10000200000001EC0000009A114696D91B2F4700.png)

Figure 2.1-b schematic representation of a connection of a mixed 1D–3D model of a cantilever beam with “hierarchical meshes”

The Arlequin method is used here to connect two models, 1D and 3D (a junction operation). Consequently, the coupling zone corresponds to the gluing zone. The management of the weightings in order to ensure the partition of unity in the free zone (of the Arlequin conditions) is managed by the user.

As this is a coupling of L2 type, it is sufficient to impose the weighting coefficients in the definition of the materials relating to the various free and gluing zones.

**Note:**

*A general procedure for managing incompatible meshes would be very heavy and costly to program; it would require a complicated management of the weighting functions in the gluing zone as well as an intersection or a subdivision into sub-elements of the 1D and 3D meshes in order to manage the integration of the coupling terms.*

### 2.2 Notation

One will denote:

- $\Omega$ the coupling zone
- ${\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{1D}}$ and ${\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{3D}}$ the displacement fields corresponding to the 1D beam and 3D models
- $\lambda$ the Lagrange multiplier associated with the 1D domain
- $\text{M}$ the classical beam stiffness matrix

### 2.3 Expression of the Arlequin connection condition

The L2 coupling equation in a continuum is written:

${\int}_{\Omega}\lambda :({\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{1D}}-{\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{3D}})\text{d}\Omega ={\int}_{\Omega}\lambda :{\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{1D}}\text{d}\Omega -{\int}_{\Omega}\lambda :{\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{3D}}\text{d}\Omega$                 éq 2.4-1

$\lambda$ and ${\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{1D}}$ have exactly the same type of degrees of freedom and the same type of shape functions, because they are defined in the same space of kinematically admissible fields. Indeed, to a 1D element of the Lagrange multiplier there corresponds a 1D beam element.

One assumes, for simplicity, that the axis of the beam is along X.

![Figure](/figures/r3.03.09/10000200000002F9000000DEFF1CD1C75E45FDBB.png)

Figure 2.3-a schematic representation of a beam element with 6 degrees of freedom

Then, the displacement of beam type is written classically as follows:

$\lbrace  \begin{array}{cccc}u=u(x)& +z{\theta}_{y}(x)& -y{\theta}_{z}(x)& +0\\ v=0& +0& +v(x)& -(z-{z}_{c}){\theta}_{x}(x)\\ w=0& +w(x)& +0& +(y-{y}_{c}){\theta}_{x}(x)\end{array}$

The Lagrange multiplier will then be written, according to a beam kinematics, as follows:

$\lbrace  \begin{array}{cccc}{\lambda}_{u}={\lambda}_{u}(x)& +z{\lambda}_{{\theta}_{y}}(x)& -y{\lambda}_{{\theta}_{z}}(x)& +0\\ {\lambda}_{v}=0& +0& +{\lambda}_{v}(x)& -(z-{z}_{c}){\lambda}_{{\theta}_{x}}(x)\\ {\lambda}_{w}=0& +{\lambda}_{w}(x)& +0& +(y-{y}_{c}){\lambda}_{{\theta}_{x}}(x)\end{array}$

On a 1D element denoted e1D, the first 1D–1D coupling term is written in the same 1D space:

${\int}_{{\text{e}}_{\mathrm{1D}}}\lambda :{\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{1D}}\text{d}\Omega ={\left\lbrace \lambda \right\rbrace }^{t}{\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{1D}}\left\lbrace {u}_{\mathrm{1D}}\right\rbrace ={\left\lbrace \lambda \right\rbrace }^{t}\text{M}\left\lbrace {u}_{\mathrm{1D}}\right\rbrace$     éq 2.4-2

where $\left\lbrace \lambda \right\rbrace$ is the vector of the 12 degrees of freedom of the Lagrange multiplier and $\left\lbrace {u}_{\mathrm{1D}}\right\rbrace$ is the vector of the 12 degrees of freedom of the beam elements:

$\left\lbrace {u}_{\mathrm{1D}}\right\rbrace =({u}^{1},{v}^{1},{w}^{1},{{\theta}_{x}}^{1},{{\theta}_{y}}^{1},{{\theta}_{z}}^{1},{u}^{2},{v}^{2},{w}^{2},{{\theta}_{x}}^{2},{{\theta}_{y}}^{2},{{\theta}_{z}}^{2})$

$\left\lbrace \lambda \right\rbrace =({\lambda}_{u}^{1},{\lambda}_{v}^{1},{\lambda}_{w}^{1},{\lambda}_{{\theta}_{x}}^{1},{\lambda}_{{\theta}_{y}}^{1},{\lambda}_{{\theta}_{z}}^{1},{\lambda}_{u}^{2},{\lambda}_{v}^{2},{\lambda}_{w}^{2},{\lambda}_{{\theta}_{x}}^{2},{\lambda}_{{\theta}_{y}}^{2},{\lambda}_{{\theta}_{z}}^{2})$

In the particular case of the L2 coupling, the matrix ${\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{3D}}$ is equal to the classical mass matrix of a beam element.

Still on a 1D element denoted e1D, the second 1D–3D coupling term is written in the same 1D space:

$-{\int}_{{\text{e}}_{\mathrm{1D}}}\lambda :{\mathrm{u}}_{\mathrm{3D}}\text{d}\Omega =-{\left\lbrace \lambda \right\rbrace }^{t}{\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{3D}}\left\lbrace {u}_{\mathrm{3D}}\right\rbrace$     éq 2.4-3

and $\left\lbrace {u}_{\mathrm{3D}}\right\rbrace$ is the vector of the (3 x ${N}_{\mathit{no3D}}$ ) degrees of freedom of the 3D elements (${N}_{\mathit{no3D}}$ being the number of nodes of the 3D element):

$\left\lbrace {u}_{\mathrm{3D}}\right\rbrace =({u}^{1},{v}^{1},{w}^{1},\text{...},{u}^{{N}_{\mathit{no3D}}},{v}^{{N}_{\mathit{no3D}}},{w}^{{N}_{\mathit{no3D}}})$

The procedure for building the matrix ${\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{3D}}$ on the fine 3D model is more delicate than that of the coarse beam model. The complexity of this coupling lies in the coexistence of two fields belonging to different spaces. It is obtained by considering all the 3D elements contained in the cylindrical space of the 1D element.

${\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{3D}}=\sum_{k=1}^{{N}_{\mathit{el3D}}}{\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{3D}}^{k}$

It is recalled that, by the very construction of the mesh, all the 3D elements must be included in this space. These elements are determined by a pairing procedure (parentage graph) which consists in projecting their barycentres into the geometric support of the associated 1D element (projection of a point onto a segment).

For each 3D element number k (k ranging from 1 to Nel3D), one computes the matrix ${\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{3D}}^{k}$ in the following manner:

${\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{3D}}^{k}=\sum_{g=1}^{\mathit{Nbgauss}}{\mathrm{W}}^{g}{\mathrm{B}}_{\mathrm{1D}}^{gt}({\xi}_{\mathrm{1D}}){\mathrm{G}}^{g}{\mathrm{B}}_{\mathrm{3D}}^{g}({\xi}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\eta}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\zeta}_{\mathrm{3D}})\det({\mathrm{J}}^{\mathrm{g}})$

with G the passage matrix from the reference element to the physical element, containing zeros and elements of the inverse of the Jacobian computed at the Gauss point g.

The matrix ${\mathrm{B}}_{\mathrm{1D}}^{g}$ is of dimension 3 x (3 x number of nodes of the 1D element). Its terms are computed at the Gauss points of the 1D elements.

${\mathrm{B}}_{\mathrm{1D}}^{g}=\left[\begin{array}{cccccccccccc}{N}_{1}^{p}& y{\xi}_{5}& z{\xi}_{5}& 0& z{\xi}_{6}& -y{\xi}_{6}& {N}_{2}^{p}& y{\xi}_{7}& z{\xi}_{7}& 0& z{\xi}_{8}& -y{\xi}_{8}\\ 0& {\xi}_{1}& 0& -z{N}_{1}^{p}& 0& -{\xi}_{2}& 0& {\xi}_{3}& 0& -z{N}_{2}^{p}& 0& -{\xi}_{4}\\ 0& 0& {\xi}_{1}& y{N}_{1}^{p}& {\xi}_{2}& 0& 0& 0& {\xi}_{3}& y{N}_{2}^{p}& {\xi}_{4}& 0\end{array}\right]$

where x, y and z are the local variables of the 1D element, ${N}_{1}^{p}$ and ${N}_{2}^{p}$ the shape functions for tension–compression–torsion, and ${\xi}_{\mathrm{1...8}}$ are the bending shape functions of the beam (cf. document [r3.08.01](doc:r3.08.01) of Code-Aster).

The matrix ${\mathrm{B}}_{\mathrm{3D}}^{g}$ is of dimension 3 x (3 x number of nodes of the 3D element). For example, the matrix ${\mathrm{B}}_{\mathrm{3D}}^{g}$ associated with the linear tetrahedral solid element (TETRA4) is written:

${\mathrm{B}}_{\mathrm{3D}}^{g}=\left[\begin{array}{cccccccccccc}{N}_{1}& 0& 0& {N}_{2}& 0& 0& {N}_{3}& 0& 0& {N}_{4}& 0& 0\\ 0& {N}_{1}& 0& 0& {N}_{2}& 0& 0& {N}_{3}& 0& 0& {N}_{4}& 0\\ 0& 0& {N}_{1}& 0& 0& {N}_{2}& 0& 0& {N}_{3}& 0& 0& {N}_{4}\end{array}\right]$

The variables x, y, z as well as the shape functions and their derivatives are computed at the Gauss point g of the 3D element. Thus, for example, one has:

$\begin{array}{c}x=\langle N\rangle \left\lbrace {x}_{n}\right\rbrace ,{u}_{x}=\langle N\rangle \left\lbrace {u}_{n}\right\rbrace \\ y=\langle N\rangle \left\lbrace {y}_{n}\right\rbrace ,{u}_{y}=\langle N\rangle \left\lbrace {v}_{n}\right\rbrace \\ z=\langle N\rangle \left\lbrace {z}_{n}\right\rbrace ,{u}_{z}=\langle N\rangle \left\lbrace {w}_{n}\right\rbrace \end{array}$

where:

- xn, yn and zn are the coordinates of the nodes of the 3D element,
- un, vn and wn are the degrees of freedom associated with the nodes of the 3D elements,
- $\langle N\rangle$ is the vector containing all the shape functions of the 3D element in the order of the 3D nodes (cf. document r3.01.01 of Code-Aster).

For each Gauss point of coordinates $({\xi}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\eta}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\zeta}_{\mathrm{3D}})$ in the 3D reference element, we introduce its physical representation in the form of the following interpolated coordinates $(\stackrel{ˉ}{X},\stackrel{ˉ}{Y},\stackrel{ˉ}{Z})$ :

$\stackrel{ˉ}{X}=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathit{el3D}}}{N}^{i}{x}^{i}({\xi}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\eta}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\zeta}_{\mathrm{3D}})$

$\stackrel{ˉ}{Y}=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathit{el3D}}}{N}^{i}{y}^{i}({\xi}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\eta}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\zeta}_{\mathrm{3D}})$

$\stackrel{ˉ}{Z}=\sum_{i=1}^{{N}_{\mathit{el3D}}}{N}^{i}{z}^{i}({\xi}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\eta}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\zeta}_{\mathrm{3D}})$

![Figure](/figures/r3.03.09/10000200000002C9000001A7116691059673AE5E.png)

Figure 2.3-b operation of locating the Gauss points of a 3D element in the single beam element with which it is paired

In order to simplify the illustration, we assume that the axis of the beam is along X. For each Gauss point of the 3D element, we introduce its projection onto the single real 1D element with which it is paired, as follows:

${\stackrel{ˉ}{\xi}}_{\mathrm{1D}}=\frac{\stackrel{ˉ}{X}-{X}_{inf}}{\text{Le}}$

The coordinates of the projection in the other two directions of the thickness of the beam are given by $y=\stackrel{ˉ}{Y}$ and $z=\stackrel{ˉ}{Z}$ .

This technique makes it possible to discretise the Lagrange multiplier on the beam elements and to compute the integrals in the expressions of the coupling matrices ${\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{3D}}^{k}$ . Thus, for each 3D element one computes the matrix ${\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{3D}}^{k}$ as follows:

${\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{3D}}^{k}=\sum_{g=1}^{\mathit{Nbgauss}}{\mathrm{W}}^{g}{\mathrm{B}}_{\mathrm{1D}}^{gt}({\stackrel{ˉ}{\xi}}_{\mathrm{1D}}){\mathrm{G}}^{g}{\mathrm{B}}_{\mathrm{3D}}^{g}({\xi}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\eta}_{\mathrm{3D}},{\zeta}_{\mathrm{3D}})\det({\mathrm{J}}^{\mathrm{g}})$

where the shape functions of beam type are interpolated at the parametric coordinates of the projected points obtained.

### 2.4 Implementation of the connection method

For each Arlequin connection, the user must define the following operands under the option 3D_POU_ARLEQUIN of the keyword LIAISON_ELEM:

| The group of 3D mesh cells of the gluing zone (keyword GROUP_MA_1) |
| --- |
| The group of 1D mesh cells of the gluing zone (keyword GROUP_MA_2) |
| The concept CHAM_MATER defining the materials (used to ensure the partition of unity) |
| The concept CARA_ELEM defining the elementary characteristics used for the computation of the coupling matrices ${\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{1D}}$ and ${\text{C}}_{\mathrm{1D}-\mathrm{3D}}$ |

For each beam element, Code_aster computes the linear relations arising from the writing of the assembled coupling matrices [éq 2.4-1]. These kinematic relations link:

- the 12 degrees of freedom of the two nodes of the beam element,
- with the degrees of freedom of the nodes of **all**  the elements paired with the beam element.

Finally, the resulting linear relations will be dualised, like all the linear relations arising for example from the keyword LIAISON_DDL of AFFE_CHAR_MECA.

In practice, the user of Code_Aster will be asked to provide hierarchical meshes.

## 3 What uses can be made of this modelling?

At present, it is possible to envisage three types of use of the Arlequin method:

- **The zoom**: This type of application aims at the detection and the study of a local phenomenon (contact, crack, ...) at the level of a precise zone belonging to a global domain. A fine patch is then superimposed on the existing coarse model, with the aim of refining the solution in the neighbourhoods of the zones of interest. In this case, the gluing zone is equal to one of the subdomains.
- **The junction**: The principal aim of this application consists in connecting two models which may be of different natures and/or finenesses. In this configuration, the gluing zone is strictly equal to the overlap zone of the two domains.
- **The substitution**: This operation consists in locally substituting an existing model by another, finer one. This technique proves very useful for introducing, with great flexibility, a defect into a sound model, such as cracking. Unlike the two previous cases, when substitution is applied, the gluing zone constitutes a subdomain of the overlap zone of the two models.
