# r7.01.25 Constitutive laws of dam joints: JOINT_MECA_RUPT, JOINT_MECA_FROT and JOINT_MECA_ENDO

**Summary:**

This document describes surface laws making it possible to model the fracture and the friction between the lips of a crack or of a joint. The law JOINT_MECA_RUPT represents only the damage phenomenon; it is based on a cohesive formulation of fracture. The law JOINT_MECA_FROT is an elastoplastic version of a Mohr-Coulomb friction law. The law JOINT_MECA_ENDO is a more complete law; it combines the two mechanisms: damage and plasticity are coupled through the free energy. This last law is written in the generalised standard formalism.

In mechanics these laws are based on the modellings of the standard joints XXX_JOINT. The laws are dedicated to the modelling of dams, more precisely of the concrete/rock joints or of the joints between the monoliths of a dam. Depending on the type of loading the use of one or the other law may be chosen for different parts of the structure.

In order to be able to simulate the behaviour of real dams certain specific features of construction have been introduced, in particular keying and sawing. Keying is an industrial procedure of injection of concrete under pressure between the monoliths of a dam. It is an intermediate stage of the construction of an arch dam; it serves to reinforce its watertightness after the construction phase of the vertical monoliths. Sawing is a similar procedure during which the dam is sawn in order to release the compressive stresses. Each procedure is defined via the keywords PRES_CLAVAGE and SCIAGE in DEFI_MATERIAU. Keying and sawing are not activated for the moment for the law JOINT_MECA_ENDO.

The effect of hydrostatic pressure due to the presence of fluid in the joint is taken into account via the Terzaghi stress. All the laws admit either the modelling without coupling (pressure imposed in the option PRES_FLUIDE), or a coupled hydromechanical modelling (XXX_JOINT_HYME) which activates the propagation of the uplift pressures at the dam-rock interface.

## 1 Introduction

The incidents which have occurred on concrete dams (Bouzey 1895, Gleno 1923, Malpasset 1959), as well as the results of monitoring, have shown that their stability, and consequently their safety, depends very largely on the hydromechanical behaviour of the weakest zones of the valley-dam assembly. Localised at the discontinuities in the structure and in the rock, these weak points are principally the faults of the abutment zones, the construction joints in the dam, the concrete-rock contact of the foundation and the joints between the monoliths of the dam. The mechanical behaviour of these zones at risk is strongly non-linear, but thanks to their surface character, industrial studies on large structures are complex but feasible. In addition to these difficulties, the mode of construction of dams, the keying/sawing techniques used and their multiple drainage points make them structures whose finite-element modelling is all the more complex in a conventional analysis code.

The three constitutive laws described in this document make it possible to take into account the principal non-linearities of the behaviour of the structures: the crack-opening phase (JOINT_MECA_RUPT/ENDO) and the sliding phase of its lips (JOINT_MECA_FROT/ENDO). The law JOINT_MECA_ENDO is the most complete law; it is capable of representing the evolution of the state of the joint by combining the two phenomena of fracture (thanks to the damage variable) and of friction (thanks to the plasticity variable).

### 1.1 The joints — weak links of the structure

As mentioned previously, the joints of dams have a varied origin (r7.01.25-fig-image-physique-joint). In a general way, one can imagine the joint as a rough discontinuity possibly reinforced by a filling material.

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/100002000000019F000000F4508D641D657A02A3.png)

*Physical image of a joint*

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/10000200000001C3000000FBFD11A9EF722508BD.png)

*Different types of joint of a dam*

Coulomb's friction law, which uses only a single parameter (friction coefficient), captures only a tiny part of the very complex mechanical behaviour of such a structure. Indeed, apart from friction, the joint exhibits the following important phenomena: the loss of tensile strength, the elastic behaviour at very small displacement, the progressive disappearance of the shear-stress peak for a cyclic loading. The relevance of these phenomena depends strongly on the physical state of the joint, in particular on its level of roughness, on the mean size of the asperities, on the mechanical properties of the filling materials and of the rock matrix. The standard parameters in mechanics such as Young's modulus, Poisson's ratio, or again the friction coefficient all influence the behaviour of the joint, but are not the only ones to be identified in order to capture the phenomena described above. The complete modelling of the joint therefore requires the introduction of a law depending on numerous parameters. The implementation of such a law in an implicit analysis code being an important technical challenge, we propose simpler laws, depending on few parameters but which nevertheless make it possible to capture the essential behaviour of the joint in most current conditions of use. These latter are named JOINT_MECA_RUPT, JOINT_MECA_FROT and JOINT_MECA_ENDO. They make it possible to model the behaviour of the joints which one finds between the monoliths of an arch dam and/or at the interface between the gravity dam and its foundation. We give a succinct description of them below, before detailing them in the parts which follow.

### 1.2 Mechanical constitutive laws (without hydraulic coupling)

#### 1.2.1 Fracture behaviour JOINT_MECA_RUPT

The law JOINT_MECA_RUPT is an elastic softening law, whose normal behaviour is founded on the cohesive formulation of fracture. It opens the possibility of modelling fracture in mode I (tension) and takes into account the coupling between the normal opening and the tangential stiffness. This law captures well the behaviour of real joints at small displacements, as long as the sliding regime is not reached.

In order to identify the regimes in which it is applicable one can take up again the physical image of the joint (r7.01.25-fig-image-physique-joint). These are two rough interfaces possibly containing a filling material between their lips; this may be either clay, or elements of the rock for the cracks between the dam and the foundation, or keying grout for the monolith joints. A loading of the crack first brings into play the properties of the filling material and the geometry of the asperities which define the behaviour of the structure at small displacements. As long as the filling material is not damaged or the asperities are not broken, the behaviour of the joint remains elastic both in opening and in shear. However the normal and tangential stiffness parameters, denoted ${K}_{n}$ and ${K}_{t}$ , are not equivalent, because they bring into play two distinct physical phenomena. The first is linked principally to the stiffness of the filling material, the second depends rather on the bending stiffness of the asperities. The joint presents a tensile strength, denoted ${\sigma}_{max}$ , which can be related to the filling materials, but also to the transverse frictions between the asperities of the two lips of the crack. The tensile strength is often quite low, not exceeding that of concrete ${\sigma}_{max}\ll 1\mathit{MPa}$ . As a first approximation the normal stiffness can be estimated from the Young's modulus of the filling material $E$ and from the joint thickness $e$ , which gives ${K}_{n}=E/e\simeq 10\mathit{GPa}/1\mathit{cm}={10}^{12}\mathit{Pa}/m$ . For sufficiently thick joints the elastic behaviour is isotropic and the tangential stiffness can be taken close to the normal stiffness ${K}_{t}\simeq {K}_{n}$ .

The fracture of the joint takes place in a progressive manner. Indeed, the joint is first damaged by partially decreasing its stiffness before breaking completely. In order to quantify this phenomenon we introduce a dimensionless penalisation parameter in fracture ${P}_{\mathit{rupt}}$ , which represents a relative opening in softening with respect to the elastic opening. For low values of ${P}_{\mathrm{rupt}}$ , the fracture is sudden. For large values ${P}_{\mathit{rupt}}\gg 1$ the transition is more progressive, but this significantly increases the initial dissipation energy. The details of this behaviour will be presented in the following parts. In practice the value is taken close to ${P}_{\mathit{rupt}}\simeq 1$ , which introduces a non-negligible softening phase without departing too far from the sudden-fracture behaviour.

In order to model the different types of profiles of the monolith joints of dams (represented in the figures r7.01.25-fig-joint-lisse, r7.01.25-fig-joint-rougeux), we introduce into the user interface an additional parameter $\alpha \in [0,2]$ . The latter links the normal opening of the joint with the decrease of its tangential stiffness. Physically it reflects the depth of the asperities and varies continuously between $0$ and $2$ . The value $\alpha =0$ corresponds to the smooth interface without asperities (the tangential stiffness drops to zero as soon as the joint opens normally, see r7.01.25-fig-joint-lisse). The value $\alpha =2$ represents another extreme case, where the interface is very rough with an infinite depth of the asperities, that is the equivalent of the profile of a crenellated monolith joint (r7.01.25-fig-joint-rougeux). In this case the tangential stiffness is not affected by the normal opening. The default value is fixed at $1$ , which represents an intermediate situation.

#### 1.2.2 Sliding behaviour JOINT_MECA_FROT

If one loads the joint further in pure shear, it will end up sliding with a certain friction coefficient. Before passing into this plastic regime, one observes experimentally a peak of the friction force. This phenomenon is linked to the fact that in order to be able to slide, the asperities must leave their embedded position (dilatancy). During this phase the frictional contacts are not necessarily parallel to the surface of the lips, which increases the effective friction coefficient. This “exit” procedure is moreover accompanied by the increase of the normal displacement of the joint r7.01.25-fig-contrainte-cisaillement. If this cycle is repeated several times, the stress peak attenuates and disappears completely. One notes that the deeper the asperities, the more important the friction peak. In the limit case even at a non-zero normal opening the joint can reveal the elastic behaviour in tangential slidings (this case is treated by JOINT_MECA_RUPT with $\alpha =2$ ).

The law JOINT_MECA_FROT does not take into account all the complexity of sliding and in particular the peak; it represents only the phenomenon of ultimate sliding, characterised solely by the friction coefficient $\mu$ and the adhesion $c$ . This latter is linked to the tensile strength ${R}_{t}=c/\mu$ . The loss of tensile strength is not modelled by this law. As the yield surface is assumed fixed, once one reaches in tension the apex of the cone one continues the evolution in perfect plasticity with the normal stress locked at the value of ${R}_{t}$ . This behaviour is clearly not physical for the brittle filling material and can be considered only as an approximation of the real behaviour. The law JOINT_MECA_FROT is written in an elastoplastic formalism with the elastic phase identical to that of the law JOINT_MECA_RUPT.

Coupling behaviour of fracture and friction JOINT_MECA_ENDO

The first two laws JOINT_MECA_RUPT/FROT are very basic and they reproduce only a single phenomenon at a time, either fracture or sliding. Each of the laws can be used in various parts of the structure according to the local loading defined upstream of the study. The advantage of such an approach lies in the simplicity of the laws used, because they depend only on a reduced number of parameters. However in many situations the local change cannot be defined upstream of the study, see the cases where certain zones of the structure are loaded both in fracture and in sliding.

The law JOINT_MECA_ENDO has been introduced in order to treat this type of situation; it combines the phenomena of fracture and of sliding. As for the law JOINT_MECA_FROT it is an elastoplastic law with a conical yield surface, but kinematic hardening is introduced in addition, whose damaging character makes it possible to represent the softening both in tension and in shear. This is easily illustrated by the representation of the evolution of the yield surface in the stress domain r7.01.25-fig-chargement-normal. The motion generated by the activation of the kinematic hardening of the yield surface spreads the elastic zone: the stress increases at first following the loading direction. However thanks to the damaging character of the coupling, the amplitude of the hardening decreases and the surface returns to its initial position for the ultimate loadings (completely damaged). This makes it possible to represent the peaks of tensile strength as well as that of sliding.

### 1.3 Hydromechanical coupling

In order to assess the stability of dams it is important to be able to model the propagation of the uplift pressures between the rock and the foundation of the dam. Two possibilities are offered to the user. First, an uplift-pressure profile can be imposed, as an input parameter of the loading. This simple possibility makes it possible to study the stability of the dam for a conservative hydraulic loading (the least favourable). On the other hand, the speed of the computation presents a non-negligible advantage. One can thus test an extreme hydraulic profile without spending more time than in the pure mechanical computation. Moreover, this feature makes it possible to carry out a hydromechanical chaining computation. The latter consists in starting with a mechanical computation with an initial pressure state in the joints. According to the damage of the latter, the pressure profile (whose form is given *a priori* ) is updated. Once the pressure is modified, the mechanical state of the joints evolves again, which can generate the fracture of some of them. The fluid then propagates more easily and the pressure profile again undergoes an evolution. This process is chained by means of a fixed-point loop in the command file in order to obtain converged mechanical and hydraulic states.

*Schematic representation of flow through the joint*

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/100000000000016C000001050D8B4F5B496F5296.png)

*Taking the hydrostatic pressure into account. Shift of the normal constitutive relation for the law JOINT_MECA_RUPT*

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/10000000000001710000010D9033CDE95B5AC08D.png)

*Taking the hydrostatic pressure into account. Shift of the normal constitutive relation for the law JOINT_MECA_FROT*

From the theoretical point of view, the introduction of the fluid into the joint modifies the normal mechanical stress ${\sigma}_{n}\to {\sigma}_{n}-p$ (Terzaghi's law). In practice, the constitutive law in question is shifted downwards according to the value of the pressure $p$ at each integration point.

These laws also accept a **coupled** hydromechanical modelling, named xxx_JOINT_HYME. The difference from the model with hydromechanical **chaining** (presented above) bears on the more precise taking into account of the action of the mechanics on the hydraulics. Indeed, in the chained model the profile is given *a priori* . In the coupled model, the opening of the joint modifies the flow law of the fluid; the pressure profile is an unknown of the problem. During the hydromechanical coupling, in order to model the flow, the mechanical law is enriched by taking into account the cubic Poiseuille flow, which is regularised for very small crack openings. Thus, the pressure profile is no longer imposed, but computed during the simulation. In addition to the standard mechanical equation one simultaneously solves the following flow equation:

$$
div\overrightarrow{w}=0;\overrightarrow{w}=\frac{\rho}{12\bar{\mu}}{\delta}_{n}^{3}\overrightarrow{\nabla}p
$$

where $\overrightarrow{w}$ corresponds to the hydraulic flux, ${\delta}_{n}$ is the normal opening of the joint, and finally $\rho$ and $\bar{\mu}$ denote respectively the density and the dynamic viscosity of the fluid.

**Note**

The hydromechanical modelling is available for all the constitutive laws JOINT_MECA_RUPT/FROT/ENDO.

### 1.4 Keying procedure

Keying is a key stage during the construction of an arch dam. It consists in an injection of concrete grout under pressure between the monoliths of the dam. It is therefore important to be able to model this process correctly. In practice the pouring of the concrete is decomposed into several stages where the concrete is injected at different places and at varied pressures. From the mechanical point of view keying is interpreted as a placing in compression of the lips of the keyed joint up to ${\sigma}_{n}=-{\sigma}_{\mathit{nc}}$ (pressure of the injected concrete, keyword PRES_CLAVAGE).

This option is included directly in the parameters of the constitutive law. Physically keying is accompanied by a solidification process of the injected grout; the procedure is modelled by the modification of the thickness of the joints concerned. If the joint is in strong compression initially, keying does not influence it. If on the other hand the joint is open or not sufficiently compressed (${\sigma}_{n}>-{\sigma}_{\mathit{nc}}$ ), keying will result in the change of the joint-thickness parameter denoted ${\delta}_{\mathit{nf}}$ . And, consequently, in a translation of the normal stress.

The normal behaviour of the joint after keying can be modified. Thus, the tensile strength can be restored (Figure ) either partially or completely according to the damage of the joints before the keying procedure. The procedure implemented consists in not restoring it; it keeps its current value.

**Note**

The modelling of keying is available for the moment only for the basic laws JOINT_MECA_RUPT/FROT. In view of the current numerical implementation the phenomena of sawing and of keying cannot be chained on the same joint.

### 1.5 Sawing procedure

It has been shown by *in situ*  tests that the concrete of certain hydraulic structures in France has suffered damage due to the alkali-aggregate chemical reaction unknown at the time of the massive construction of dams before the war. The most notable example is the Chambon dam situated on the Romanche, which after 80 years of existence was the victim of the phenomenon of concrete disease (AAR); consequently every fifteen years it must undergo a particular treatment. Microsawing operations on the structure make it possible to prolong its service life. As its name indicates, this procedure consists in sawing the dam in order to release the compressive stresses, which have developed over time. It is therefore important to be able to model this process correctly. From the numerical point of view, sawing is very close to the keying procedure: the joint thickness evolves in the course of the operation. Nevertheless, apart from the fact that this time the joint thickness decreases, its evolution is also driven by the displacement which is to be compared with the pressure driving during the keying procedure. The sawing phase is characterised by the saw thickness entered under a keyword SCIAGE.

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/10000000000002850000015907B26CED87E5A2AE.png)

*Illustration of the sawing procedure*

This option is included directly in the parameters of the constitutive law. In practice, in order

to make the phenomenon close to physical reality, the sawn thickness is decreased by the initial opening of the joint before the operation. If the joint is in strong initial opening, sawing will not influence it. If on the other hand the joint is compressed, it will undergo complete sawing. Numerically it will result in the change of the joint-thickness parameter denoted ${\delta}_{\mathrm{nf}}$ , and, consequently, in a translation of the normal stress, such that:

$$
{\delta}_{\mathit{nf}}=max(0,{\delta}_{n}^{-})-{\delta}_{\mathit{scie}}
$$

One also considers that the joint in its final state is completely damaged by the sawing. The latter, even if it is subsequently placed in tension, will therefore develop no tensile strength. The procedure is explicit; combined with the elastic unloading for the global structure which it generates, it results in the rapid numerical convergence of the modelling. At most one puts two Newton iterations, of which the first serves to update the thickness of the joints and the second is a linear unloading phase.

**Note**

The modelling of sawing is available for the moment only for the basic laws JOINT_MECA_RUPT/FROT. In view of the current numerical implementation the phenomena of sawing and of keying cannot be chained on the same joint.

### 1.6 Choice of parameters and the limits of application

The constitutive laws JOINT_MECA_RUPT/FROT are simple, robust, depending on few parameters. These laws do not make it possible to model the behaviour in the fracture-friction transition phase. For the friction law, which presents a non-zero tensile strength, the fracture of the joint is not implemented. Unlike these simplified behaviours, the law JOINT_MECA_ENDO is a more complete law; it is capable of representing the evolution of the state of the joint by combining the phenomena of fracture and of friction.

The laws introduced have the non-negligible advantage of being based on a theoretical formalism proven in the scientific literature. Certain parameters are specific to the given law, others (such as hydraulic coupling) have a universal character.

The apparent adhesion $C$ representing the value of the shear peak is given by (eq. 2.43 of the thesis):

$$
C=0.54\cdot \sqrt{2{D}_{1}({\mu}^{2}{B}_{n}+{B}_{t})}

$$
T=0.54\cdot \sqrt{2{D}_{1}{B}_{n}}

**First strategy based on the physical measurements**

In order to have a non-empty set of possible pairs of values constrained by  , one must respect the following condition:

$$
\mu T\le C\le \sqrt{2}\mu T
$$

This last equation imposes the bounds of the choice for the apparent value of adhesion for the fixed tensile strength. At first the values of the physical parameters $T\text{et}C$ (values in units of stress) are chosen in order to fit as well as possible the yield surface (obtained experimentally for example). Equations  and  make it possible to find the pair $\overline{{B}_{n}}={B}_{n}{D}_{1}$ and $\overline{{B}_{t}}={B}_{t}{D}_{1}$ and to fix the ratio ${B}_{n}/{B}_{t}$ independently of the choice of ${D}_{1}$ . One then proceeds to fix the values of the amplitudes of the kinematic coupling ${B}_{n},{B}_{t}$ from the inequalities  by choosing ${D}_{1}$ which respects these inequalities. For very large values of ${D}_{1}$ the inequalities  are automatically respected, because $\underset{{D}_{1}\to \infty }{\lim}{B}_{n,t}=0$ . There therefore exists a minimum value of ${D}_{1}$ which makes it possible to respect the condition of absence of snap-back. The closer one is to this value the more the robustness will be affected.

$$
{D}_{1}\ge {D}_{min}=\max\left(\frac{{T}^{2}}{2{K}_{n}};\frac{{C}^{2}}{2{K}_{t}}\right)\cdot 0.76/{0.54}^{2}
$$

The values of ${D}_{1}$ are then arbitrary, belonging to the interval $(0;\infty )$ .

## 2 Theoretical formulation of JOINT_MECA_RUPT

The law JOINT_MECA_RUPT accepts a coupled hydromechanical modelling, but these two phenomena can be treated separately. At first one will describe the mechanical part of the law, which encompasses fracture, contact, the keying procedure and the imposed pressure. It is based on the modellings XXX_JOINT (R3.06.09).

**Note**

The next two sections present the general theoretical concept. On a first reading one can omit them and pass directly to r7.01.25-vecteur-contrainte  , which provides enough elements to understand the numerical implementation of the law.

### 2.1 Cohesive law in mechanics

The theoretical framework chosen to model the fracture of the joints of concrete dams is based on the cohesive models ([r7.02.11](doc:r7.02.11), , ), because in brittle fracture, in order to avoid the problem of infinite stresses at the crack tip, one can introduce cohesive forces which impose a stress initiation criterion. The forces of vanishing character are then exerted between the particles on either side of the separation plane of the crack during its opening (see r7.01.25-fig-fissure-cohesive).

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/100002000000015A000000F2A889A2E9E5EE0BA5.png)

*Diagram of a cohesive crack*

From the physical point of view one considers that the opening of the crack costs an energy proportional to its length in 2D and to its surface in 3D. One calls it surface energy, which one expresses by means of the energy density $\Psi ={\int}_{\Gamma}\psi (\vec{\delta})d\Gamma$  1. The displacement field at equilibrium $\mathrm{u}$ is obtained by minimising the sum of the elastic energy $\Phi$ , of the surface energy, and of the work of the external forces ${W}^{\mathrm{ext.}}$ . The solution is obtained by using a variational approach to fracture. The state which realises the minimum of the total energy corresponds to a state of mechanical equilibrium:

$$
\underset{\mathrm{u}}{\min}\left(\Phi (u)+\Psi (\vec{\delta}(u))+{W}^{\mathit{ext}}\right)
$$

The discontinuity surface is discretised in 2D or 3D by joint finite elements (see the documentation [r3.06.09](doc:r3.06.09)). The displacement jump in the element $\vec{\delta}=({\delta}_{n},{\delta}_{\mathit{t1}},{\delta}_{\mathit{t2}})$ is a linear function of the nodal displacements. The cohesive force 2 which is exerted on the lips of the crack is denoted $\vec{\sigma}$ ; it is defined by the derivative of the surface-energy density with respect to the displacement jump. One calls cohesive law a relation between $\vec{\sigma}$ and the displacement jump $\vec{\delta}$ . The most relevant material parameters, which describe the joint of a dam, are:

- the two stiffnesses in normal loading ${K}_{n}$ and tangential loading ${K}_{t}$ , which characterise the surface and the filling materials of the crack;
- and the critical stress at fracture ${\sigma}_{max}$

One introduces, moreover, three dimensionless numerical parameters: ${P}_{\mathit{rupt}}$ , ${P}_{\mathit{cont}}$ and $\alpha$ . The first drives the regularisation of the softening slope in fracture, the second the penalisation of the contact and the third ensures a progressive recovery of tangential forces as a function of the normal opening. This last can be associated with the relative size of the asperities of the surfaces in contact: $\alpha \in [0,2]$ .

In the standard cohesive laws (R7.02.11) the surface energy depends on the displacement vector 3 and the stresses are defined as the first derivatives of the energy:

$$
{\sigma}_{n}=\frac{\partial {\psi}_{n}({\delta}_{n})}{\partial {\delta}_{n}}
\textrm{et}
\vec{{\sigma}_{t}}=\frac{\partial {\psi}_{t}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}})}{\partial \vec{{\delta}_{t}}}
$$

With the energy:

$$
\Psi (\vec{\delta})\equiv {\int}_{\Gamma}\psi ({\delta}_{n},\vec{{\delta}_{t}})d\Gamma
$$

Unlike these standard cohesive laws, in this model only the normal part of the law is derived from the surface energy, whereas the tangential component of the law is given in an explicit manner 4.

In both cases, the irreversibility of cracking is taken into account via a condition of increase of the maximum normal opening of the joint.

### 2.2 Surface energy for the normal behaviour

The surface-energy density $\psi$ , at a given point of the crack, depends explicitly on the normal displacement jump between the lips of the crack ${\delta}_{n}$ . It also varies as a function of the state of the joint, which is taken into account via an internal threshold variable $\kappa ⩾0$ , which manages the irreversibility of cracking. This latter stores the largest norm of the jump reached in the course of the opening. Its evolution law between two successive loading increments $-$ and $+$ is written:

$$
{\kappa}^{+}=\max({\kappa}^{-},{\delta}_{n}^{+})
$$

According to the value of the opening of the joint, one may find oneself in one of these three situations:

1. The compressed joint is in the contact regime;
2. For a positive normal opening, if the latter exceeds the threshold one speaks of the dissipative regime (dissipation of energy in the course of cracking);
3. In the intermediate case the joint is in a linear regime (linear unloading or reloading without dissipation of energy).

The normal part of the energy is written:

$$
{\psi}_{n}({\delta}_{n},\kappa )=\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}{\psi}_{n}^{\mathit{con}}({\delta}_{n},\kappa )\hfill & \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}<0\\ {\psi}_{n}^{\mathit{lin}}({\delta}_{n},\kappa )\hfill & \text{si}& 0⩽{\delta}_{n}<\kappa \\ {\psi}_{n}^{\mathit{dis}}({\delta}_{n})\hfill & \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}⩾\kappa \end{array}\right.
$$

And the tangential part:

$$
{\psi}_{t}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}})=\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}{\psi}_{t}^{\mathit{fer}}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}})& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}<0\\ {\psi}_{t}^{\mathit{ouv}}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}})& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}⩾0\end{array}\right.
$$

In a synthetic manner the surface energy is written in the following way:

$$
\psi ({\delta}_{n},\kappa )=H({\delta}_{n}-\kappa ){\psi}_{n}^{\mathit{dis}}({\delta}_{n})+H({\delta}_{n})\cdot H(\kappa -{\delta}_{n}){\psi}_{n}^{\mathit{lin}}({\delta}_{n},\kappa )+H(-{\delta}_{n})\cdot {\psi}_{n}^{\mathit{con}}({\delta}_{n})
$$

Where $H(x)$ is the Heaviside function ($H(x)=0\text{si}x<0,H(x)=1\text{si}x⩾0$ ).

One of the particularities of the law JOINT_MECA_RUPT is that, whatever the regime, the behaviour is always linear at the stress level. At the energy level, we obtain quadratic functions, which are given in what follows up to an additive constant, which itself depends on the threshold.

The normal behaviour of the joint is separated into three regimes: contact, linear and dissipative. The function ${\psi}_{n}^{\mathit{con}}({\delta}_{n})=\frac{{P}_{\mathit{con}}{K}_{n}{\delta}_{n}^{2}}{2}$ ensures the contact condition (non-interpenetration of the lips of the crack); it is regularised in order to obtain a better numerical convergence. By varying the parameter ${P}_{\mathrm{con}}$ , one can change the normal stiffness for the closed joint.

In the linear regime, in the case where an existing crack evolves without dissipating energy, the corresponding energy density depends only on the normal-stiffness parameter: ${\psi}_{n}^{\mathit{lin}}({\delta}_{n},\kappa )=\frac{{K}_{a}(\kappa ){\delta}_{n}^{2}}{2}$ , where ${K}_{a}(\kappa )=({P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1}+1){\sigma}_{max}/\kappa -{K}_{n}{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1}$ is a decreasing function of the fracture threshold $\kappa$ .

In the dissipative regime, in order to obtain a linear softening, the corresponding energy density has a quadratic form as a function of the opening:

$$
{\psi}_{n}^{\mathit{dis}}({\delta}_{n})=\left\{  \begin{array}{ccc}{\sigma}_{max}(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1}){\delta}_{n}-{K}_{n}{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1}{\delta}_{n}^{2}/2& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}<{\sigma}_{max}(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}})/{K}_{n}\\ {\sigma}_{max}^{2}{(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}})}^{2}/({\mathrm{2P}}_{\mathit{rupt}}{K}_{n})& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}⩾{\sigma}_{max}(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}})/{K}_{n}\end{array}\right.
$$

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/10000000000002110000019F4105B9964ECC69A5.png)

*Surface-energy density as a function of the displacement jump for different values of the damage threshold $\kappa$*

The additive constant translates the elastic energies ${\psi}_{n}^{\mathit{lin}}$ and ${\psi}_{n}^{\mathit{con}}$ defined previously so that, whatever the damage state of the joint, one always obtains the same energy-release rate (Griffith constant ${G}_{f}$ , see figure r7.01.25-fig-densite-energie and  . It depends only on the threshold and does not affect the expression of the stresses.

${P}_{\mathrm{rupt}}$ is introduced in such a way that the more it increases the more the critical opening at fracture is important and consequently the more this increases the dissipative Griffith energy ${G}_{f}$ .

**In summary** : *the normal behaviour of the law* JOINT_MECA_RUPT *is driven by the evolution of the surface energy density; the latter takes the form of a potential well. It comprises three principal regimes: contact, linear elastic tension, damage/softening, whose corresponding profiles are approximated by quadratic functions of the opening. The joint begins to be damaged when the normal stress reaches the critical value* ${\sigma}_{n}={\sigma}_{max}$ *. The more the joint is damaged the more the energy well flattens, see (Figure* r7.01.25-fig-contrainte-cohesive *). The damage parameter (the threshold) itself evolves only in this last regime, starting from its initial value for the sound joint* ${\kappa}_{0}={\sigma}_{max}/{K}_{n}$ *up to the ultimate value for the completely damaged joint* ${\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}={\sigma}_{max}(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}})/{K}_{n}$ *.* ${P}_{\mathit{con}}$ *is a constant defined by the user which changes the level of penalisation in contact (see Figure* r7.01.25-fig-contrainte-cohesive *).* ${P}_{\mathrm{rupt}}$ *changes the energy dissipated per unit surface* ${G}_{f}={\sigma}_{\max}^{2}(1+{P}_{\mathrm{rupt}})/({\mathrm{2K}}_{n})$ *. Finally, one obtains the following expression for the normal energy:*

$$
{\psi}_{n}(\textcolor{#0000ff}{{\delta}_{n}},\kappa )=\left\{  \begin{array}{ccc}{\sigma}_{max}(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1})(\kappa -{\kappa}_{0})/2+{P}_{\mathit{con}}{K}_{n}\textcolor{#0000ff}{{\delta}_{n}^{2}}/2& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}<0\\ {\sigma}_{max}(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1})(\kappa -{\kappa}_{0})/2+\left[{\sigma}_{max}(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1})/\kappa -{K}_{n}{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1}\right]\textcolor{#0000ff}{{\delta}_{n}^{2}}/2& \text{si}& 0⩽{\delta}_{n}<\kappa \\ {\sigma}_{max}(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1})(\textcolor{#0000ff}{{\delta}_{n}}-{\kappa}_{0}/2)-{K}_{n}{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1}\textcolor{#0000ff}{{\delta}_{n}^{2}}/2& \text{si}& \kappa ⩽{\delta}_{n}<{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}\\ {G}_{f}& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}⩾{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}\end{array}\right.
$$

This function is continuous and differentiable, which ensures the continuity of the stresses.

### 2.3 Stress vector

The stress vector in the element is denoted $\vec{\sigma}=({\sigma}_{n},\vec{{\sigma}_{t}})$  5; it can be separated into several regimes. For the normal part the stress vector is equal to the sum of the derivatives of the surface-energy density and of the contact-penalisation energy density with respect to the jump.

$$
{\sigma}_{n}=H({\delta}_{n}-\kappa ){\sigma}_{n}^{\mathit{dis}}+H(\kappa -{\delta}_{n})H({\delta}_{n}){\sigma}_{n}^{\mathit{lin}}+H(-{\delta}_{n}){\sigma}_{n}^{\mathit{con}}
$$

It therefore suffices to differentiate the expressions given in the preceding section r7.01.25-energie-surface in order to obtain the normal component of the stresses 6. The tangential part ${\overrightarrow{\sigma}}_{t}^{\mathrm{fer}}=f({K}_{t},\overrightarrow{{\delta}_{t}})$ is written:

$$
{\vec{\sigma}}_{t}=H({\delta}_{n}){\vec{\sigma}}_{t}^{\mathit{ouv}}+H(-{\delta}_{n}){\vec{\sigma}}_{t}^{\mathit{fer}}
$$

It is a function of the tangential opening; it brings into play the tangential stiffness for the closed joint. According to the profile of the interface the tangential behaviour for the open joint can be varied ${\vec{{\sigma}_{t}}}^{\mathit{ouv}}={\vec{{\sigma}_{t}}}^{\mathit{fer}}$ for crenellated surfaces (as for example in r7.01.25-fig-joint-lisse), or else ${\overrightarrow{{\sigma}_{t}}}^{\mathrm{ouv}}\equiv 0$ for very smooth surfaces (r7.01.25-fig-joint-rougeux).

#### 2.3.1 Normal stresses

Let us consider the expression of the normal stress:

$$
{\sigma}_{n}({\delta}_{n},\kappa )=\left\{  \begin{array}{ccc}{P}_{\mathit{con}}{K}_{n}{\delta}_{n}& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}<0\\ \left[{\sigma}_{max}(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1})/\kappa -{K}_{n}{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1}\right]{\delta}_{n}& \text{si}& 0⩽{\delta}_{n}<\kappa \\ {\sigma}_{max}(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1})-{K}_{n}{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1}{\delta}_{n}& \text{si}& \kappa ⩽{\delta}_{n}<{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}\\ 0& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}⩾{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}\end{array}\right.
$$

The evolution of this stress in the tension zone as a function of the jump is represented in r7.01.25-fig-dependance-contraintes. The arrows represent the possible direction of evolution of the stress according to whether the opening process is reversible (linear regime) or not (dissipative regime). At initiation, the joint first behaves in a linear elastic way, then as soon as the normal stress reaches the critical value ${\sigma}_{n}={\sigma}_{\max}$ , it has a softening behaviour: it progressively loses its stiffness, which gives the linear but not elastic regime; it is characterised by a fracture-softening slope $-{K}_{n}/{P}_{\mathrm{rupt}}$ . The elastic stiffness for the sound joint defines the initial value of the damage threshold ${\kappa}_{0}={\sigma}_{\max}/{K}_{n}$ . The fracture threshold is given by ${\kappa}_{\mathrm{rupt}}={\sigma}_{\max}(1+{P}_{\mathrm{rupt}})/{K}_{n}$ . The more important ${P}_{\mathit{rupt}}$ the more the dissipation energy increases ${G}_{f}={\sigma}_{\max}^{2}(1+{P}_{\mathrm{rupt}})/({\mathrm{2K}}_{n})$ .

#### 2.3.3 Tangential stress

For dam joints at small opening, one observes that independently of the normal loading regime, the tangential stress always varies linearly; the tangential stiffness is a function of the normal opening. In the extreme case of a perfectly smooth contact surface, the tangential stiffness drops suddenly to zero at a positive normal opening. Consequently, the surface energy of the law is no longer continuous, which in principle generates a peak in the normal stress (delta function $\textcolor{#ff0000}{\delta}(x)$ ) at opening. It is for this reason that we renounce, in the current version of the law, keeping the complete energetic formalism. The tangential law is then postulated in an empirical manner in linear form with the tangential stiffness depending on the normal opening:

$$
\vec{{\sigma}_{t}}({\delta}_{t},{\delta}_{n})=\left\{  \begin{array}{ccc}{K}_{t}\cdot (\vec{{\delta}_{t}}-{\vec{\delta}}_{\mathit{shift}})& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}<0\\ (1-{\delta}_{n}/{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}){K}_{t}\cdot (\vec{{\delta}_{t}}-{\vec{\delta}}_{\mathit{shift}})& \text{si}& 0⩽{\delta}_{n}<{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}\\ 0& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}⩾{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}\end{array}\right.
$$

The history variable $\vec{{\delta}_{\mathit{shift}}}$ represents the shift of the equilibrium point in tangential sliding with the joint open: $\vec{{\delta}_{\mathit{shift}}}$ is equal to the last value of $\vec{{\delta}_{t}}$ for which the joint has been open ${\delta}_{n}\ge {\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}$ . We introduce the tangential fracture threshold ${\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}={\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}\tan(\alpha \pi /4)$ , whose value can be modified by the user with $\alpha \in [0,2]$ and the keyword ALPHA. For a zero value the tangential slope changes suddenly at opening; for the value $\alpha =2$ the tangential stiffness does not evolve. For the sake of compatibility with the constitutive laws developed in the code Gefdyn, we do not correct the normal component of the stresses in the transition phase; the latter is always given by , which gives a non-symmetric tangent matrix and consequently the regime with uncontrolled dissipation. The evolution of the tangential stress is separated into three regimes: joint elastic in compression; joint elastic partially open with a reduced stiffness; joint completely broken (see equation  and figure r7.01.25-fig-illustration-couplage.)

### 2.4 Tangent operator

As the behaviour in each of the regimes is linear, the computation of the tangent matrix is easy:

$$
\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{n}({\delta}_{n})}{\partial {\delta}_{n}}=\left\{  \begin{array}{ccc}{P}_{\mathit{con}}{K}_{n}& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}<0\\ {\sigma}_{max}(1+{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1})/\kappa -{K}_{n}{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1}& \text{si}& 0⩽{\delta}_{n}<\kappa \\ -{K}_{n}{P}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{-1}& \text{si}& \kappa ⩽{\delta}_{n}<{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}\\ 0& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}⩾{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}\end{array}\right.
$$

$$
\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{n}({\delta}_{n})}{\partial {\delta}_{t}}=0
\frac{\partial \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}},{\delta}_{n})}{\partial \vec{{\delta}_{t}}}=\left\{  \begin{array}{ccc}{K}_{t}\mathit{Id}& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}<0\\ (1-{\delta}_{n}/{k}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}){K}_{t}\mathit{Id}& \text{si}& 0⩽{\delta}_{n}<{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}\\ 0& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}⩾{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}\end{array}\right.
$$

$$
\frac{\partial \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}},{\delta}_{n})}{\partial \vec{{\delta}_{t}}}=\left\{  \begin{array}{ccc}{K}_{t}\mathit{Id}& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}<0\\ (1-{\delta}_{n}/{k}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}){K}_{t}\mathit{Id}& \text{si}& 0⩽{\delta}_{n}<{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}\\ 0& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}⩾{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}\end{array}\right.
$$

$$
\frac{\partial \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}},{\delta}_{n})}{\partial {\delta}_{n}}=\left\{  \begin{array}{ccc}0& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}<0\\ -{K}_{t}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}}-{\vec{\delta}}_{\mathit{shift}})/{k}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}& \text{si}& 0⩽{\delta}_{n}<{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}\\ 0& \text{si}& {\delta}_{n}⩾{\kappa}_{\mathit{rupt}}^{\tan}\end{array}\right.
$$

Let us note that the tangent matrix is not symmetric. This results from the non-repercussion on the normal stresses of the regularisation of the evolution of the tangential stress at the opening of the joint (the singular terms are not taken into account).

### 2.5 Numerical realisation of keying

Keying corresponds physically to a procedure of injection of concrete grout under pressure between the monoliths of the dam; it is characterised by a single parameter, the local pressure of the injected grout ${\sigma}_{\mathit{nc}}⩾0$ . In order to set up keying the user must define a keying-pressure function, keyword PRES_CLAVAGE, which depends both on space (keying at different places at different pressure) and on time (several successive keyings). The places where the keying pressure is negative are not keyed.

The procedure is modelled by the modification of the thickness of the joints concerned. If the joint is in strong compression initially, keying does not influence it. If on the other hand the joint is open or not sufficiently compressed (that is if ${\sigma}_{n}>-{\sigma}_{\mathrm{nc}}$ ), keying will result in the change of the total-thickness parameter of the joint denoted ${\delta}_{\mathit{nf}}^{+}={\delta}_{\mathit{nf}}^{-}+{\delta}_{n}^{+}+{\sigma}_{\mathit{nc}}/({P}_{\mathit{con}}{K}_{n})$ .

Keying then preserves the normal opening of each joint while translating the constitutive law along the abscissa axis. The new local equilibrium position has a normal stress equal to the pressure of the injected concrete ${\sigma}_{n}=-{\sigma}_{\mathrm{nc}}$ . This procedure is applied locally. Although each joint subjected to keying finds itself in the linear regime in compression, the selection itself of the joints to be keyed is “non-linear”. The global procedure becomes, for this reason, also non-linear and it does not suffice to modify only the normal stresses of the keyed joints in order to obtain again the mechanical equilibrium after keying. Consequently a mechanical-equilibrium computation is carried out after this “local” keying in order to put the system back into its equilibrium state. If the clearance of the joint to be keyed is modified, the update of the thickness of the joints is carried out and so on as long as there exist points where ${\sigma}_{n}>-{\sigma}_{\mathrm{nc}}$ . The joints concerned close and place themselves progressively in compression while following the normal behaviour curve. This procedure is carried out only once during the numerical “construction” phase of the dam. An illustration is given in r7.01.25-fig-phase-clavage. The points “in” and “out” correspond respectively to the values of the stress before and after keying.

The normal behaviour of the joint after keying can be modified. Thus, the tensile strength can be restored either partially or completely (this is the case in r7.01.25-fig-phase-clavage) according to the damage of the joints before the keying procedure. *In the procedure as it has been developed we have made the choice not to restore the tensile strength; the latter keeps its current value.*

*The evolution of the normal stress during keying: partially damaged joint*

In order to identify the tangent matrix for keying it is useful to represent this procedure as a temporary modification of the initial constitutive law by an incremental law. For the tangential regime the keying procedure does not introduce any modification: the associated stress always depends linearly on the tangential displacement and the loading slope varies as a function of the joint opening. The normal coupling deserves to be analysed in more detail. Suppose that one is at a given loading state ${\delta}_{n+}$ and that one tries to recover the infinitesimally neighbouring stress state. Let us first look at the case where ${\delta}_{n}$ decreases. Since the joint thickness had already been updated at the previous Newton iteration, the joint enters the compression domain and the activation of keying plays no role at this stage. One therefore recovers the compression slope of the initial law. On the other hand, if one loads the joint in tension the keying procedure is activated and one proceeds to adapt the joint thickness so as to cap the normal stress by the keying pressure. De facto the behaviour becomes bilinear, with the compression slope of the initial law in compression and the slope in the tension zone, which vanishes completely (see r7.01.25-fig-phase-clavage).

## 3 Theoretical formulation of JOINT_MECA_FROT

We consider Coulomb's friction law, which depends on only a single parameter $\mu \in \left(0,\infty \right]$ . It enforces the non-interpenetration condition of the lips in contact (Signorini condition) by establishing a local link between the tangential and the normal stress in the sliding phase: $∣∣{\vec{\sigma}}_{t}∣∣=\mu {\sigma}_{n}$ . Several regularisations of this law are made in order to facilitate its numerical implementation. First the Signorini condition must be made differentiable, which is straightforward if one assumes that the behaviour of the surfaces in contact follows an elastic law. The same holds for the slope of the change of sliding direction in the tangential behaviour. Moreover, for the modelling of concrete dams one observes experimentally a non-negligible tensile strength between the joints. All these considerations bring us back to a Mohr-Coulomb law whose representation in the Mohr plane is given in figure r7.01.25-fig-loi-frottement-coulomb-2d; it describes the sliding phase of joints between the foundation and the dam or between the monoliths of a dam (in a simplified manner) while taking into account the most relevant effects.

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/10000200000000C400000084E9845AFABF191CFC.png)

The law JOINT_MECA_FROT is an elastoplastic variant of the Mohr-Coulomb law; it depends on four parameters: the normal stiffness ${K}_{n}$ , the tangential stiffness ${K}_{t}$ , the adhesion $c$ (which is linked to the maximum tensile strength ${R}_{t}=c/\mu$ ) and the friction coefficient of the joint $\mu$ . We also introduce an isotropic hardening parameter, which makes it possible to regularise the tangential slope in the sliding phase; it is denoted ${\rm K}$ . The elastoplastic model introduced concerns only the tangential part of the constitutive law. There is no plastic part of the displacement for the normal part: the latter is always elastic. The tangential displacement jump is decomposed into an elastic part ${\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}$ and a plastic part ${\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}$ ; $\lambda$ denotes the cumulated tangential displacement jump. The flow law is orthogonal to the cut-off plane of the sliding cone ${\sigma}_{n}=\text{const}$ (2D circle for a 3D cone). This gives, strictly speaking, a globally non-associated flow law. The rate mechanical formulation of such a law gives the following equations:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{c}{\vec{\delta}}_{t}={\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}+{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}\\ \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}={K}_{t}{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{el}}\equiv {K}_{t}({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}})\\ {\sigma}_{n}=min({K}_{n}{\delta}_{n},{R}_{t})\end{array}\right.
$$

With:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{c}f(\vec{\sigma},\lambda )=\parallel {\vec{\sigma}}_{t}\parallel +\mu {\sigma}_{n}-c-{\rm K}\lambda ⩽0\\ f\cdot \dot{\lambda}=0;\dot{\lambda}⩾0\\ {\dot{\vec{\delta}}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}=\dot{\lambda}\frac{{\vec{\sigma}}_{t}}{\parallel {\vec{\sigma}}_{t}\parallel }\end{array}\right.
$$

As long as one is in the elastic zone $f(\vec{\sigma},\lambda )<0$ the relations between the joint-opening jumps and the stresses are linear and the plastic tangential-jump parameter does not evolve ${\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}=\mathit{const}$ . As soon as one touches the edges of the sliding cone defined by $f(\vec{\sigma},\lambda )=0$ , the evolution of the plastic tangential jump is governed by the non-associated flow law. The regularisation of the flow-threshold function with the hardening term 1 ${\rm K}\lambda >0$ is necessary in order to make the tangent matrix of the law invertible and thus to avoid the problem of multiple solutions in the case of loading under imposed forces. The tensile strength of the joint varies in the interval $(0,{R}_{t})$ ; it is a function of the tangential stress, it is zero for a tangential stress greater than the adhesion parameter $c$ and takes its maximum value if the tangential stress is zero (see Fig. ). In the current version of the law, the maximum tensile strength is not affected by the sliding phenomenon; it does not evolve because of the hardening term (Figure ). One also assumes that once the value of the maximum tensile strength is reached, the normal stress no longer evolves. This last hypothesis remains valid as long as the number of joints broken by plastic shear and subsequently loaded in tension is negligible. In order to be able to take this phenomenon into account more rigorously, it is necessary to introduce a fracture-friction regularisation in the sliding phase in tension, which represents a fairly important theoretical difficulty.

### 3.1 Implicit discretisation of the friction law

The elastoplastic version of the friction law is formulated in rate form, which facilitates its numerical discretisation. The incremental version of the law remains strictly equivalent to the continuous version provided that the increments of change are infinitesimal. In order to limit the number of loading steps, we adapt the continuous version of the law to finite increments, in an implicit manner, that is to say that the sliding conditions are written in the final equilibrium state. The algorithm used is that of the radial return with elastic prediction. By convention we denote by a sign «-» the variables at the previous equilibrium state; the current state is denoted by the usual variables without an additional sign (see ).

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/100000000000014A000000E2C72A577426368CAF.png)

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/1000000000000145000000D771315BEFF76CA326.png)

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/100000000000019200000175DCA3B6CDBA743541.png)

*Coulomb friction law*

The continuous equations of the law are written in discretised form:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{c}\lambda ={\lambda}^{-}+\Delta \lambda \\ {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}={\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}+\Delta {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}\\ \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}={K}_{t}({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}})\\ {\sigma}_{n}=min({K}_{n}{\delta}_{n},{R}_{t})\end{array}\right.
$$

With the following law:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{c}f(\vec{\sigma},\lambda )=\parallel {\vec{\sigma}}_{t}\parallel +\mu {\sigma}_{n}-c-{\rm K}\lambda ⩽0\\ f(\vec{\sigma},\lambda )\cdot \Delta \lambda =0;\Delta \lambda ⩾0\\ {\Delta \vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}=\Delta \lambda \frac{{\vec{\sigma}}_{t}}{\parallel {\vec{\sigma}}_{t}\parallel }\end{array}\right.
$$

In a Newton algorithm the displacement jumps $\vec{\delta}=({\delta}_{n},{\vec{\delta}}_{t})$ as well as the internal variables at the previous instant ${\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}$ and ${\lambda}^{-}$ being known, in order to solve the law it is enough to obtain the stress values $\vec{\sigma}=({\sigma}_{n},{\vec{\sigma}}_{t})$ and all the internal variables at the current instant (in our case ${\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}$ and $\lambda$ ). The evolution equation of the law for the normal component is completely decoupled from the tangential motion, and can therefore be solved immediately:

$$
{\sigma}_{n}=min({K}_{n}{\delta}_{n},{R}_{t})
$$

We thus obtain a set of five equations and one inequality, in order to obtain five scalar unknowns:

$$
\vec{{\sigma}_{t}}={K}_{t}({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}-\Delta {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}})
$$

And:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{c}f(\vec{\sigma},\lambda )\equiv \parallel {\vec{\sigma}}_{t}\parallel +\mu {\sigma}_{n}-c-{\rm K}{\lambda}^{-}-{\rm K}\Delta \lambda ⩽0\\ f(\vec{\sigma},\lambda )\cdot \Delta \lambda =0;\Delta \lambda ⩾0\\ {\Delta \vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}=\Delta \lambda \frac{{\vec{\sigma}}_{t}}{\parallel {\vec{\sigma}}_{t}\parallel }\end{array}\right.
$$

In order to simplify this mathematical problem let us look in more detail at the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker condition $f(\vec{\sigma},\lambda )\cdot \Delta \lambda =0$ . There are two possibilities: either one slides $\Delta \lambda >0$ , or one is in the elastic domain $\Delta \lambda =0$ . If one is in the elastic domain, then $\Delta {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}=0$ and one obtains the elastic solution if and only if:

$$
{f}_{\mathit{el}}({\sigma}_{n},{\vec{\sigma}}_{\tau}^{-},{\lambda}^{-})\equiv {K}_{t}\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{\tau}-{\vec{\delta}}_{\tau}^{\mathit{pl}-}\parallel +\mu {\sigma}_{n}-c-{\rm K}{\lambda}^{-}⩽0
$$

In practice if  is satisfied then the elastic prediction is the solution of the problem:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{c}\lambda ={\lambda}^{-}\\ {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}={\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}\\ \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}={K}_{t}({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-})\\ {\sigma}_{n}=min({K}_{n}{\delta}_{n},{R}_{t})\end{array}\right.
$$

If the condition  is not satisfied, then $\Delta \lambda >0$ and one is in the sliding phase. One then obtains a system of three non-linear equations with three unknowns $\Delta {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}$ and $\Delta \lambda$ :

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{c}f(\vec{\sigma},\lambda )\equiv \parallel {K}_{t}({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}-\Delta {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}})\parallel +\mu {\sigma}_{n}-c-{\rm K}{\lambda}^{-}-{\rm K}\Delta \lambda =0\\ {\Delta \vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}=\Delta \lambda \frac{({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}-\Delta {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}})}{\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}-\Delta {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}\parallel }\end{array}\right.
$$

This equation can be solved by eliminating $\Delta {\overrightarrow{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathrm{pl}}$ , following the procedure commonly used for plasticity computations:

$$
{\Delta \vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}\lbrace \parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}-\Delta {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}\parallel +\Delta \lambda \rbrace =\Delta \lambda ({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-})
$$

By taking the norm of this last equation and noting that $\parallel \Delta {\overrightarrow{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathrm{pl}}\parallel =\Delta \lambda$ , one obtains the norm of the updated tangential-stress vector, which one inserts into equation  in order to obtain a scalar equation for $\Delta \lambda$ :

$$
\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}-\Delta {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}\parallel =\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}\parallel -\Delta \lambda
$$

And:

$$
\parallel {K}_{t}({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-})\parallel -{K}_{t}\Delta \lambda +\mu {\sigma}_{n}-c-{\rm K}{\lambda}^{-}-{\rm K}\Delta \lambda =0
$$

Once $\Delta \lambda$ is known, it is enough to note the collinearity of the following vectors $\Delta {\overrightarrow{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathrm{pl}}\uparrow \uparrow {\overrightarrow{\delta}}_{t}-{\overrightarrow{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathrm{pl}-}-\Delta {\overrightarrow{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathrm{pl}}$ , whence $\Delta {\overrightarrow{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathrm{pl}}\uparrow \uparrow {\overrightarrow{\delta}}_{t}-{\overrightarrow{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathrm{pl}-}$ . This makes it possible to rewrite equation  in a simplified form, which gives the value of the second unknown $\Delta {\overrightarrow{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathrm{pl}}$ :

$$
{\Delta \vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}=\Delta \lambda \frac{{\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}-\Delta {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}}{\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}-\Delta {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}\parallel }=\Delta \lambda \frac{{\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}}{\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}\parallel }
$$

This solution in fact corresponds to the slidings in the direction of the tangential stress under elastic prediction. This implies that the change of sliding direction will take place essentially in the elastic zone provided that the loading steps are small. The final solution in the case of sliding, obtained from equation , is written as:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{ccc}{\sigma}_{n}& =& min({K}_{n}{\delta}_{n},{R}_{t})\\ {f}_{\mathit{el}}({\sigma}_{n},{\vec{\sigma}}_{t}^{-},{\lambda}^{-})& =& {K}_{t}\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}\parallel +\mu {\sigma}_{n}-c-{\rm K}{\lambda}^{-}\\ \lambda & =& {\lambda}^{-}+\frac{{f}_{\mathit{el}}({\sigma}_{n},{\vec{\sigma}}_{t}^{-},{\lambda}^{-})}{{K}_{t}+{\rm K}}\\ {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}}& =& {\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}+\frac{{f}_{\mathit{el}}({\sigma}_{n},{\vec{\sigma}}_{t}^{-},{\lambda}^{-})}{{K}_{t}+{\rm K}}\cdot \frac{{\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}}{\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}\parallel }\\ \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}& =& {K}_{t}({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}})\end{array}\right.

### 3.2 Tangent matrix

For the law JOINT_MECA_FROT the tangent matrix is computed implicitly, which reinforces the robustness of the computations 2. As is shown in the reference , such a numerical scheme is unconditionally stable for laws with positive hardening $K\ge 0$ . In the case of the elastic regime (the inequality  is satisfied), the tangent matrix takes a simple form; it is diagonal:

$$
\left(\begin{array}{ccc}{K}_{n}& 0& 0\\ 0& {K}_{t}& 0\\ 0& 0& {K}_{t}\end{array}\right)
$$

In the case of sliding (the inequality  is not satisfied) the tangent matrix is obtained by differentiation of the equations . The derivatives with respect to the normal opening depend on the state of the joint. For the closed joint this gives:

$$
\text{si}{\delta}_{n}<\frac{c}{\mu {K}_{n}}\text{}\Rightarrow \text{}\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{n}}{\partial {\delta}_{n}}& =& {K}_{n}\\ \frac{\partial \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}}{\partial {\delta}_{n}}& =& -\mu \frac{{\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}}{\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}\parallel }\cdot \frac{{K}_{n}{K}_{t}}{{K}_{t}+{\rm K}}\end{array}\right.
$$

For the open joint all the corresponding derivatives are zero:

$$
\text{si}{\delta}_{n}⩾\frac{c}{\mu {K}_{n}}\text{}\Rightarrow \text{}\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{n}}{\partial {\delta}_{n}}& =& 0\\ \frac{\partial \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}}{\partial {\delta}_{n}}& =& 0\end{array}\right.
$$

The derivatives with respect to the tangential opening do not depend on the joint opening:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{n}}{\partial {\vec{\delta}}_{t}}& =& 0\\ \frac{\partial \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}}{\partial {\vec{\delta}}_{t}}& =& \frac{{\rm K}{K}_{t}}{{K}_{t}+{\rm K}}\mathrm{Id}+\frac{-\mu {\sigma}_{n}+c+{\rm K}{\lambda}^{-}}{{K}_{t}+{\rm K}}\cdot \frac{{K}_{t}}{\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}\parallel }\cdot \left(\mathrm{Id}-\frac{({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-})\otimes ({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-})}{{\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}\parallel }^{2}}\right)\end{array}\right.
$$

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{ccc}\frac{\partial {\sigma}_{n}}{\partial {\vec{\delta}}_{t}}& =& 0\\ \frac{\partial \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}}{\partial {\vec{\delta}}_{t}}& =& \frac{{\rm K}{K}_{t}}{{K}_{t}+{\rm K}}\mathrm{Id}+\frac{-\mu {\sigma}_{n}+c+{\rm K}{\lambda}^{-}}{{K}_{t}+{\rm K}}\cdot \frac{{K}_{t}}{\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}\parallel }\cdot \left(\mathrm{Id}-\frac{({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-})\otimes ({\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-})}{{\parallel {\vec{\delta}}_{t}-{\vec{\delta}}_{t}^{\mathit{pl}-}\parallel }^{2}}\right)\end{array}\right.
$$

## 4 Theoretical formulation of JOINT_MECA_ENDO

One of the key points in the precise description of the interfaces between rocks is the identification of their elastic domain (also called the yield surface or the criterion). Because of their softening nature, not only rocky contacts but also any kind of analogous interface involving similar materials such as concrete, clay or soil can generally be classified according to their strength under various mixed-mode loadings. This approach is closely linked to the basic safety rules in industrial applications, where it is accepted as a first approximation that geomaterials could present an unstable failure once the critical load is reached.

Throughout the last century, specific testing machines and the corresponding measurement protocols were set up in order to harmonise and standardise the experimental characterisation of the brittle interfaces cited above. To mention only a few, the simple tension test, or again shear at a fixed compression, are both well-documented experiments which are carried out in practice in order to catalogue the strength of materials by locating only a few points of their multidimensional elastic surface. This reduced “single-point” view of the strength of materials has been increasingly scrutinised in recent times, and multiple developments have been adopted. For example, the constant improvements of finite-element software allow new types of modelling, with loadings going beyond the elastic domain in order to explore a more subtle post-peak behaviour. While the complexity of the post-peak description could be reached through various theoretical formalisms, most of them still rest on the initial definition of the yield surface, and the question of this shape of the elastic domain remains the cornerstone of any identification of a non-linear model. Even though considerable progress has been made over the last decades, this precise identification of the yield surface remains today a rather difficult task.

A common characteristic of geomaterials is their high strength under compressive loads. In current industrial applications it is precisely this property of higher compressive strength that is exploited in order to increase the overall robustness of the structure. As the interfaces considered are composed of geomaterials it is natural to assume a similarity between the corresponding yield surfaces. For geomaterials, according to their physical origin, a wide variety of criteria defining the elastic domain are employed in order to model their mechanical behaviour. The simplest surface, admitting an infinite strength in hydrostatic compression, is the one in the shape of a linear cone. It was introduced for the first time more than a century ago as the combination of Coulomb's friction hypothesis with the Galileo-Rankine tension cut-off principle. The initial description in principal stress, commonly called the Mohr-Coulomb form, was then generalised in the work of Drucker and Prager to a smoother deviator-trace relation. The direct relation between the shear and compression loadings, which is the principal signature of these linear criteria, made possible the development of various constitutive relations based on the same simplified dependence. We retain at this stage that the linear conical surface is commonly used to this day for the modelling of geomaterials.

With many large infrastructure projects under way, more complex criteria emerged as new experimental data became available. For wider ranges of loadings, the dependence of shear strength on compression seemed to depart from the linear curve. In 1980 Evert Hoek and Edwin T. Brown propose a new particular form of criterion, of quadratic shape. It reproduces the Mohr-Coulomb type singularity for a low tensile loading while simultaneously taking into account the reduction of the shear strength under high compression. This purely empirical criterion has been widely used in the design of underground excavations. More recent work on rock joints seems to show the same modification of the yield surface.

The law JOINT_MECA_ENDO attempts to capture this phenomenon partially: starting from the linear elastic yield surface, the latter evolves thanks to kinematic hardening, and the tensile strength is captured by the damaging coupling of the hardening amplitude. The apparent shape of the yield surface is no longer linear and, as a result, it approaches the yield surfaces of real joints. We also have the advantage of being able to represent the cohesive-zone model and the elastoplastic model within one and the same formalism, which we describe briefly below.

Like the previous laws, the law JOINT_MECA_ENDO also accepts a coupled hydromechanical modelling; the HM coupling in it is identical and these two phenomena can be treated separately. We describe here the mechanical part of the law, which unifies the two mechanical phenomena: fracture and friction. It is based on the modellings XXX_JOINT (R3.06.09).

### 4.1 Generalised standard model for the joints

The theoretical framework chosen to model the coupling between fracture and friction of the joints of concrete dams is based on the generalised standard model ,  , . This model makes it possible to guarantee good thermodynamic properties by operating exclusively with energetic notions. Initially developed to represent three-dimensional phenomena, we adapt it to the joint model.

In interface mechanics, two physical quantities are typically introduced. The first is the vector of the Cauchy stress density, which characterises the state of the local forces, while the second is the displacement-jump vector, which makes it possible to measure the relative position of the lips on either side of the interface, up to a certain reference configuration.

If the reference configuration is known, the two quantities are objective and experimentally measurable. Consequently one of the aims of interface mechanics is to establish a link between these two quantities, which is called the material constitutive relation or the constitutive law.

Following the original ideas of Cauchy and in the simplest manner, we can postulate that the stress is a bijective function of the displacement jump. Not all bijective stress-jump relations are however admissible from a thermodynamic point of view. For example, the work of the internal forces for constitutive relations with a non-symmetric tangent operator depends on the loading path, which is physically inadmissible for a fully reversible elastic process. Hyperelasticity restricts this stress-jump relation by admitting the existence of a scalar strain-energy density function from which the stress is derived. The mechanical loading of a hyperelastic material is regarded as a reversible process with zero dissipation, the only state variable being the displacement jump. In this sense, the hyperelastic formalism is conservative and compatible with a general thermodynamic description. The ideas are similar for extending the notion of thermodynamically admissible laws to behaviours in the presence of variables which record the loading history.

In order to take proper account of the process of energy dissipation, which is very much present in the case of friction of the lips, it is necessary to introduce at least one additional state variable. This is generally done via the sliding vector, called the plastic displacement jump. The latter is intended to decompose the total displacement jump into elastic contributions and irreversible plastic contributions. Although this plastic variable has a clear physical meaning of residual strain for the fully unloaded system, it is generally not directly observable, which is why it is also called an internal (hidden) state variable. In order to capture not only the irreversible slidings, but also to take into account the attenuation of the roughness of the interface in the sliding phase, another internal variable must be introduced. We choose here to introduce a scalar damage variable, whose particular feature is that it is contained within the bounds [0,1]. Zero corresponds to the sound state and one to the completely damaged state.

The thermodynamic description of interface mechanics rests either on the definition of the Helmholtz free-energy density, or on the Gibbs free energy , .

As was explained above, for the isothermal evolution in the presence of plasticity and of damage, this function depends on three state variables, two of which are hidden. We choose a Helmholtz description with the total displacement jump. The projection onto the interface of the Cauchy stress tensor is then the dual conjugate of the total displacement jump $\vec{\sigma}=\partial \psi /\partial \vec{\delta}$ , while the energetic response of the system to the evolution of plasticity and of damage is captured by the corresponding generalised forces $\vec{X}=-\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial \vec{p}};Y=-\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial \alpha }$ .

Most elastoplastic models admit an additive separation of the elastic and plastic strains. This leads to the simplest quadratic form of the free energy describing the residual plastic state. We adapt the same hypothesis for the joints:

$$
\psi (\vec{\delta},\vec{p})=K\frac{{(\vec{\delta}-\vec{p})}^{2}}{2}
$$

where $K$ is the tensorial elasticity modulus, assumed constant. Note that we adopt abbreviated notations in order to simplify the description. In particular, in this linear elastic case, the generalised plastic force and the Cauchy stress are equal: $\vec{\sigma}=\vec{X}$ .

By adding the quadratic plastic term in the free energy one introduces de facto a kinematic hardening, because

$$
\psi (\vec{\delta},\vec{p},\alpha )=K\frac{{(\vec{\delta}-\vec{p})}^{2}}{2}+H(\alpha )\frac{{\vec{p}}^{2}}{2}
\textrm{et}
\vec{X}=\vec{\sigma}-H(\alpha )\vec{p}
$$

$H$ plays the role of kinematic-hardening amplitude. In order to reproduce the softening phenomenon we assume that the hardening amplitude decreases with the development of plasticity. The reversible elastic domain, characterised by the absence of evolution of plasticity and of damage, is defined in the space of the observable stresses:

$\vec{p}=\text{const et}\alpha =\text{const}\to \vec{\sigma}\in \text{domaine élastique}$ .

Thermodynamically, one must ensure that the rate of energy dissipation is positive:

$$
\text{D}=\vec{\sigma}\cdot \dot{\vec{\delta}}-\dot{\psi}\ge 0
$$

where $\dot{}$ corresponds to the time derivative: $\dot{\psi}=\partial \psi /\partial t$ . Considering first the elastic unloading, we obtain the relation $\vec{\sigma}=\frac{\partial \psi }{\partial \vec{\delta}}$ as a consequence of the process with zero dissipation $\text{D}\left(\vec{p}=\text{const};\alpha =\text{const}\right)=0$ , thus reducing the second thermodynamic principle eq. to the following direct inequality: $\text{D}=\vec{X}\cdot \dot{\vec{p}}+Y\cdot \dot{\alpha}\ge 0$ .

In order to obtain a complete description of the material, one must introduce the evolution laws of the hidden variables: $\dot{\vec{p}}\text{et}\dot{\alpha}$ . This is done either via a dissipation potential, or via a plastic potential, or directly via an explicit flow rule. For an arbitrary flow, the proof of the positivity of the dissipation rate eq. is a significantly difficult task. This is why additional restrictive hypotheses are commonly introduced. For example, if the evolution of the hidden variables depends exclusively on the generalised forces $(\dot{\vec{p}};\dot{\alpha})\iff (\vec{X};Y)$ , the dissipation rate of the material becomes an exclusive function of these forces $\text{D}=\text{D}(\vec{X};Y)$ . The principal advantage of such a description is that the positivity of the dissipation is independent of the loading path: it depends exclusively on the choice of the functions which drive the flow. Thus the whole of the model can be analysed in the space of the generalised forces.

We draw on the recent work of  by introducing the Helmholtz free energy for the interfaces in the following form:

$$
\psi (\vec{\delta},\vec{p},\alpha )={K}_{n}\frac{{({\delta}_{n}-{p}_{n})}^{2}}{2}+{K}_{t}\frac{{\Vert \vec{{\delta}_{t}}-\vec{{p}_{t}}\Vert }^{2}}{2}+{A}_{n}(\alpha )\frac{{{p}_{n}}^{2}}{2}+{A}_{t}(\alpha )\frac{{\vec{{p}_{t}}}^{2}}{2}
$$

with ${A}_{n}(\alpha )={B}_{n}\cdot {(1-\alpha )}^{3}/\sqrt{\alpha}$ and ${A}_{t}(\alpha )={B}_{t}\cdot {(1-\alpha )}^{3}/\sqrt{\alpha}$ . We use the decomposition of the displacement jump into a normal part and a tangential part $\vec{\delta}=({\delta}_{n},{\delta}_{t1},{\delta}_{t2})=({\delta}_{n},{\vec{\delta}}_{t})$ . The two stiffnesses under normal loading ${K}_{n}$ and tangential loading ${K}_{t}$ characterise the surface and the filling materials of the crack (the same meaning as for the laws JOINT_MECA_RUPT and JOINT_MECA_FROT). The two amplitudes of kinematic coupling (normal ${B}_{n}$ and tangential ${B}_{t}$ ) are likewise material parameters.

The expressions of the stresses and of the generalised forces give the following set of equations:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{n}=\frac{\partial \psi (\vec{\delta},\vec{p},\alpha )}{\partial {\delta}_{n}}={K}_{n}({\delta}_{n}-{p}_{n})\\ {X}_{n}=\frac{\partial \psi (\vec{\delta},\vec{p},\alpha )}{\partial {p}_{n}}={K}_{n}({\delta}_{n}-{p}_{n})-{A}_{n}\cdot {p}_{n}\end{array}
\textrm{et}
\begin{array}{c}\vec{{\sigma}_{t}}=\frac{\partial \psi (\vec{\delta},\vec{p},\alpha )}{\partial {\delta}_{t}}={K}_{t}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}}-\vec{{p}_{t}})\\ \vec{{X}_{t}}=\frac{\partial \psi (\vec{\delta},\vec{p},\alpha )}{\partial \vec{{p}_{t}}}={K}_{t}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}}-\vec{{p}_{t}})-{A}_{t}\cdot \vec{{p}_{t}}\end{array}
Y=-{A}_{n}'{p}_{n}^{2}/2-{A}_{t}'{\Vert \vec{{p}_{t}}\Vert }^{2}/2
$$

Where ${A}_{n}'=\frac{d{A}_{n}(\alpha )}{d\alpha }$ . By choosing the elastic domain as the intersection of two domains defined in the spaces of the generalised plastic stress (cone of revolution about ${X}_{n}$ ) and of the generalised force dual to the damage variable, we obtain the following flow law:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{f}_{X}(\vec{X})=\Vert \vec{{X}_{t}}\Vert +\mu {X}_{n}-\overline{c}\le 0\\ \dot{\vec{p}}=\dot{\lambda}\frac{\partial {f}_{X}}{\partial \vec{X}}\\ {f}_{X}(\vec{X})\dot{\lambda}=0;\dot{\lambda}\ge 0\end{array}
\textrm{et}
\begin{array}{c}Y=-{A}_{n}^{I}{p}_{n}^{2}/2-{A}_{t}'{\Vert \vec{{p}_{t}}\Vert }^{2}/2\le {D}_{1}\\ \dot{\alpha}\ge 0\\ (Y-{D}_{1})\cdot \dot{\alpha}=0\end{array}
$$

${D}_{1}$ is a material parameter which plays the role of penalisation in fracture, like ${P}_{\mathit{rupt}}$ for the law JOINT_MECA_RUPT. It can be shown that, with our choice of the softening function, the evolution of the damage variable and of plasticity is simultaneous (see the thesis of Fontana, ). This simplifies the description for the discretised version of the law:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{\sigma}_{n}={K}_{n}({\delta}_{n}-{p}_{n}^{-}-\Delta {p}_{n})\\ \vec{{\sigma}_{t}}={K}_{t}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}}-{\vec{p}}_{t}^{-}-\Delta \vec{{p}_{t}})\\ \vec{{X}_{t}}={K}_{t}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}}-{\vec{p}}_{t}^{-}-\Delta \vec{{p}_{t}})-{A}_{t}({\alpha}^{-}+\Delta \alpha )\cdot ({\vec{p}}_{t}^{-}+\Delta \vec{{p}_{t}})\\ {X}_{n}={K}_{n}({\delta}_{n}-{p}_{n}^{-}-\Delta {p}_{n})-{A}_{n}({\alpha}^{-}+\Delta \alpha )\cdot ({p}_{n}^{-}+\Delta {p}_{n})\\ Y=-{B}_{n}\cdot S'({\alpha}^{-}+\Delta \alpha )\cdot {({p}_{n}^{-}+\Delta {p}_{n})}^{2}/2-{B}_{t}\cdot S'({\alpha}^{-}+\Delta \alpha )\cdot {\Vert {\vec{p}}_{t}^{-}+\Delta \vec{{p}_{t}}\Vert }^{2}/2\end{array}
$$

where we have introduced the scalar function $S(\alpha )={(1-\alpha )}^{3}/\sqrt{\alpha}$ . The thresholds are easily expressed in implicit discretised form:

$$
\begin{array}{c}{f}_{X}(\vec{X})=\Vert \vec{{X}_{t}}\Vert +\mu {X}_{n}-\overline{c}\le 0\\ \Delta {p}_{n}=\mu \Delta \lambda \\ \Delta \vec{{p}_{t}}=\Delta \lambda \frac{\vec{{X}_{t}}}{\Vert \vec{X}\Vert }\\ {f}_{X}(\vec{X})\Delta \lambda =0;\Delta \lambda \ge 0\\ Y=-{B}_{n}\cdot S'({\alpha}^{-}+\Delta \alpha )\cdot {({p}_{n}^{-}+\Delta {p}_{n})}^{2}/2-{B}_{t}\cdot S'({\alpha}^{-}+\Delta \alpha )\cdot {\Vert {\vec{p}}_{t}^{-}+\Delta \vec{{p}_{t}}\Vert }^{2}/2\le {D}_{1}\\ (Y-{D}_{1})\cdot \Delta \alpha =0\end{array}
$$

The resolution of this system of equations is carried out by the standard procedure of elastic prediction. In the damaging plastic phase, we obtain a system of implicit equations with the following four unknowns $\Delta {p}_{n};\Delta \vec{{p}_{t}};\Delta \lambda ;\Delta \alpha$ . Using the fact that $\Delta \vec{{p}_{t}}\uparrow \uparrow {K}_{t}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}}-{\vec{p}}_{t}^{-})-{A}_{t}({\alpha}^{+})\cdot {\vec{p}}_{t}^{-}$ the system can be simplified and is reduced to an equation for the single scalar unknown ${\alpha}^{+}={\alpha}^{-}+\Delta \alpha$ . For this purpose the unknown $\Delta \lambda$ is expressed from the two threshold functions. From the plastic threshold we obtain:

$$
\Delta \lambda =\frac{\Vert {K}_{t}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}}-{\vec{p}}_{t}^{-})-{A}_{t}({\alpha}^{+})\cdot {\vec{p}}_{t}^{-}\Vert +\mu {K}_{n}({\delta}_{n}-{p}_{n}^{-})-\mu {A}_{n}({\alpha}^{+})\cdot {p}_{n}^{-}-\overline{c}}{{\mu}^{2}{K}_{n}+{K}_{t}+{\mu}^{2}{A}_{n}({\alpha}^{+})+{A}_{t}({\alpha}^{+})}
$$

In parallel, from the damage threshold we have:

$$
\begin{array}{c}F({\alpha}^{+})=-({\mu}^{2}{B}_{n}+{B}_{t})\Delta {\lambda}^{2}-2\left(\mu {B}_{n}+{B}_{t}{\vec{p}}_{t}^{-}\frac{{K}_{t}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}}-{\vec{p}}_{t}^{-})-{A}_{t}({\alpha}^{+})\cdot {\vec{p}}_{t}^{-}}{\Vert {K}_{t}(\vec{{\delta}_{t}}-{\vec{p}}_{t}^{-})-{A}_{t}({\alpha}^{+})\cdot {\vec{p}}_{t}^{-}\Vert }\right)\Delta \lambda -\\ -{B}_{n}{p}_{n}^{-2}-{B}_{t}{\vec{p}}_{t}^{-2}-\frac{2{D}_{1}}{S'({\alpha}^{+})}=0\end{array}
$$

By substituting $\Delta \lambda$ from eq. into eq. , we obtain a non-linear scalar equation for ${\alpha}^{+}$ . This non-linear equation is solved by the alternating dessication method with the chord method.

The same set of equations is solved at the apex of the cone by adopting the flow rule along the outward normals:

$$
G({\alpha}^{+})=-{B}_{n}{\left(\frac{{K}_{n}{\delta}_{n}-\overline{c}/\mu }{{K}_{n}+{A}_{n}({\alpha}^{+})}\right)}^{2}-{B}_{t}{\left(\frac{{K}_{t}\vec{{\delta}_{t}}}{{K}_{t}+{A}_{t}({\alpha}^{+})}\right)}^{2}-\frac{2{D}_{1}}{S'({\alpha}^{+})}=0
$$

## 5 Taking hydrostatic pressure into account without coupling

![Figure](/figures/r7.01.25/100000000000021A00000179365359D68827496D.png)

*Illustration of a possible stability computation of a dam with the imposed pressure profile*

Although the modelling XXX_JOINT does not couple mechanics and hydraulics, one can nevertheless introduce explicitly the influence of a fluid on the mechanics via an imposed pressure. The presence of the fluid in the joint modifies the normal mechanical stress ${\sigma}_{n}\to {\sigma}_{n}-p$ . By imposing a large pressure one is able to break the joint by a simple hydraulic effect. In order to take the hydrostatic effects into account the mechanical law is shifted downwards (Figure ) as a function of the value of the pressure $p$ at each integration point.

The numerical implementation is straightforward when the mechanical laws are written completely in explicit non-incremental form as a function of the displacements and of the internal variables (one must exclude the dependence of the stresses at the later instant on the stresses at the previous instant). In this case the sole modification of the normal curve is sufficient to introduce the coupling:

$$
{\sigma}_{n}={\sigma}_{n}^{\mathit{meca}}({\delta}_{n},{\delta}_{t})-p
$$

By restricting oneself to this type of physical phenomenon, it is possible to carry out studies in which the pressure profile is imposed by the user, for example a dam-stability study under a conservative hypothesis (Figure ), that is to say in the presence of uplift pressure, whose shape is highly penalising. In order to run a computation with an imposed pressure the user must define a function, by means of the keyword PRES_FLUIDE, which depends both on space (non-homogeneous pressure profile) and on time (evolution of the pressure profile).

## 6 Theoretical formulation of the hydromechanical coupling

The laws introduced can be based on a coupled hydromechanical modelling, by means of the elements XXX_JOINT_HYME. In this part we shall speak of the hydraulic part of the law, as well as of the coupling itself; all the details of the mechanical part of the law have been described previously.

### 6.1 Hydraulic modelling

The fluid flows from the zones of high pressure towards those of low pressure. A theoretical way of taking steady flow into account is to associate with the given hydraulic state an energy 1 $H(p(x))$ depending on the pressure distribution. The first hypothesis consists in assuming that the energy depends explicitly on the pressure variation and not on the pressure itself $H=H(\nabla p(x))$ . By taking the simplest possible convex form of this dependence on the gradient, one thus obtains the energy $H=C{(\overrightarrow{\nabla}p)}^{2}/2$ where $C$ is a parameter of the law, which does not depend on the pressure.

By computing the generalised forces corresponding to the pressure-gradient field one obtains Fick's first law. The hydraulic flux is proportional to the pressure gradient:

$$
\overrightarrow{w}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial \overrightarrow{\nabla}p}=C\overrightarrow{\nabla}p
$$

In this energetic formalism one seeks the pressure field at equilibrium by minimisation of the hydraulic energy $\underset{p(\overrightarrow{x})}{\min}\underset{\Omega}{\int}H(\overrightarrow{\nabla}p(\overrightarrow{x}))d\Omega$ . This gives an equilibrium equation resembling that of mechanics $div\overrightarrow{w}=0$ . Within the framework of this model the solution of the hydraulic equilibrium equation is equivalent to the solution of a mechanical problem in quasi-statics, where the hydraulic flux is equivalent to the stress $\overrightarrow{w}\iff \sigma$ , the pressure field corresponds to the displacement field $p(\vec{x})\iff u(\vec{x})$ and finally the pressure gradient is akin to the strain field $\overrightarrow{\nabla}p\mathrm{\iff }\varepsilon$ .

### 6.2 Influence of the hydraulics on the mechanics

The presence of the fluid in the joint adds a hydrostatic stress and thereby modifies the normal mechanical stress ${\sigma}_{n}\to {\sigma}_{n}-p$ . By imposing a large pressure one is able to break the joint by a simple hydraulic effect. One can shift the mechanical law downwards as a function of the value of the pressure $p$ at each point in order to take the effects of the pressure into account; see figures r7.01.25-fig-joint-rup and r7.01.25-fig-joint-frot.

### 6.3 Influence of the mechanics on the hydraulics

In the case of fluid flow through a crack the hydraulic flux must increase with the opening (${\delta}_{n}$ ) of the latter ($\overrightarrow{w}~O({\delta}_{n})\overrightarrow{\nabla}p$ ). In Poiseuille's law, which was found empirically for the laminar flow of a viscous and incompressible fluid, the dependence of the flux on the opening is cubic (the law is often called the cubic law). The hydraulic part of the law uses this type of coupling. The equations to be solved are written in the following manner:

$$
div\overrightarrow{w}=0;\overrightarrow{w}=\frac{\rho}{12\bar{\mu}}{\delta}_{n}^{3}\overrightarrow{\nabla}p
$$

In the case of a fluid flow through the junctions of a dam, one observes non-negligible fluxes even for the closed joints. It is then necessary to define a minimum thickness ${\epsilon}_{\min}$ , keyword OUV_MIN, below which the flux reaches its minimum value. We regularise the flow equations in the following manner:

$$
\vec{w}=\frac{\rho}{12\bar{\mu}}\max{({ϵ}_{min},{ϵ}_{min}+{\delta}_{n})}^{3}\vec{\nabla}p
$$

For a non-zero pressure gradient the flux never reaches the zero value, $\min\overrightarrow{w}~{\epsilon}_{\min}^{3}\overrightarrow{\nabla}p$ , which corresponds to the flow through the permeable walls of the closed joint.

### 6.4 Hydromechanical coupling

The hydromechanical coupling brings into play the two mechanisms described previously: on the one hand the fluid acts by pressure on the lips of the joint, on the other hand the more open the crack the easier the fluid flow. In the absence of external forces the hydromechanical computation is presented schematically in this form:

$$
\left\{  \begin{array}{cc}\vec{w}=\vec{w}(\vec{\delta}(u),\vec{\nabla}p);& div\vec{w}=0\\ \vec{\sigma}=\vec{\sigma}(\vec{\delta}(u),p);& div\vec{\sigma}=0\end{array}\text{}\Rightarrow \text{}\vec{Y}=\vec{Y}(\vec{X});div\vec{Y}=0\right.
$$

The solution of the hydromechanical equilibrium equations is equivalent to the solution of the mechanical problem in quasi-statics, where one introduces the generalised stresses $\overrightarrow{Y}=(\overrightarrow{w},\overrightarrow{\sigma})$ , and the vector field of the unknowns $\overrightarrow{X}=(p,u)$ .

Since the generalised forces depend on $u$ only through $\overrightarrow{\delta}(u)$ , in order to compute the tangent matrix of the hydromechanical coupling, it is necessary to know only the following four terms:

$$
\frac{\partial \overrightarrow{\sigma}}{\partial \overrightarrow{\delta}}
\textrm{,}
\frac{\partial \overrightarrow{\sigma}}{\partial p}` ,
$$

$\frac{\partial \overrightarrow{w}}{\partial \overrightarrow{\nabla}p}$ et :math:`\frac{\partial \overrightarrow{w}}{\partial \overrightarrow{\delta}}


The first term is the same as in pure mechanics; it is given in equation . The second term is trivial, because the only non-zero component is equal to $\partial {\sigma}_{n}/\partial p=-1$ . The diagonal hydraulic term takes a simple form because the hydraulic flux depends only on the pressure gradient:

$$
\frac{\partial \vec{w}}{\partial \vec{\nabla}p}=\frac{\rho}{12\bar{\mu}}\max{({ϵ}_{min},{ϵ}_{min}+{\delta}_{n})}^{3}
$$

In the last term only the derivative with respect to the normal opening is non-zero:
