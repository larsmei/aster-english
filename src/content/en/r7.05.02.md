# r7.05.02 Static stability analysis of embankment slopes

**Summary:**

The command CALC_STAB_PENTE makes it possible to analyse the static stability of embankment slopes modelled in 2D in hydraulic works such as dams, slopes and dykes around reservoirs, taking into account the pore pressure in the porous medium and possibly other types of mechanical loading.

In general, in order to analyse the static stability of slopes according to the various professional regulations in the world, one uses the method of progressive reduction of the material properties (**SRM method** – *strengh reduction method*), or else the method based on the limit-equilibrium theory (**LEM method**). Both are available in CALC_STAB_PENTE, so that the command adapts to all circumstances of analysis under static conditions.

In this document one presents in detail the algorithms implemented in [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47).

## 1 Introduction

### 1.1 Definition of the stability of embankment slopes

In a general way, the regulations on the design and the verification of embankment works require that the factor of safety (FS) associated with the static stability of the work does not exceed an imposed threshold. This factor of safety, whose definition may vary according to the category of the calculation methods used, measures in all cases the safety margin of the slope with respect to the failure state. Its value often depends on the geometry of the work, on the mechanical loadings applied and on the properties of the material. In practice, the factor of safety is verified once the static loadings to which the work is subjected have been taken into account.

Failure of the slope occurs when the plasticised zone of the material forms a continuous surface linking two ends on the profile of the slope. One thus defines this zone as being the failure surface. Along the failure surface, the shear strength of the soil becomes insufficient to resist the tangential stresses[bib1]_. Several processes can lead to the failure of slopes[bib2]_:

- **Reduction of the shear resistance force**, for example:

  - Increase of the pore pressure because of the infiltration of water into
the work.
  - Appearance of cracks around the crest of the work.
  - Loss of strength of the clayey fill.
- **Increase of the shear stresses**, for example:

  - Loading on the crest of the work.
  - Excavation at the toe of the slope.
  - Rapid drop of the upstream water level.

The shape of the failure surface depends on the nature of the slope.
For slopes made up of homogeneous and isotropic materials, the failure surface is
probably circular, which leads to rotational slip.
On the other hand, if the materials constituting the slope are heterogeneous or anisotropic,
the surface will be non-circular, thus leading to stepped slip.

Given the complexity of the failure mechanism and of the influencing factors, the practice in geotechnics is to employ mathematical tools (analytical or numerical) for the computation of the factor of safety. As a result, a great multitude of calculation methods developed during the past century. In the command CALC_STAB_PENTE two categories of the most popular methods are implemented, offering an effective tool for computing the factor of safety of slopes and visualising the failure surface.

### 1.2 Reminder of the functionalities of CALC_STAB_PENTE

The command CALC_STAB_PENTE makes it possible to analyse the static stability of embankment slopes modelled in 2D by two categories of method:

- **SRM method**: the method of progressive reduction of the parameters linked to the
shear strength of the material (named the SRM method).
This method was proposed by Griffiths et al. in 1999.
- **LEM method**: a series of methods based on the limit-equilibrium theory
(named the LEM method), including the simplified Bishop, Fellenius, Spencer and
Morgenstern-Price procedures.

The first (*SRM*) is based on a non-linear finite-element analysis,
and proposes to approach the factor of safety by modelling the failure of the slope. It
therefore requires more care when configuring the analysis.

Nevertheless, by dividing the slope into rigid slices, the second method (*LEM*)
is often numerically more efficient and is usually recommended by
the design regulations for hydraulic works
(despite the assumptions required on the interaction between the different slices).

The functionalities of CALC_STAB_PENTE are explained in more detail
in the document [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47).

## 2 SRM method

### 2.1 General remarks on the SRM method

The SRM method takes advantage of the accuracy of modelling
by the finite-element method in order to analyse the stability
of slopes without needing to assume the slip circles in advance.
Indeed, by using a failure criterion of Mohr-Coulomb or Drucker-Prager type,
the SRM method defines the factor of safety as being **the ratio between
the initial material parameters and the reduced parameters leading to the
divergence of the non-linear analysis under the static loadings applied**:

$$
\mathit{FS}
=
\frac{\cohesion}{{\cohesion}_{i}}
=
\frac{\tan(\anglFric )}{\tan({\anglFric }_{i})}
=
\frac{\tan(\anglDila )}{\tan({\anglDila }_{i})}
$$

where

- $\anglDila$ is the dilatancy angle of the material
- $\cohesion$ and $\anglFric$ represent respectively the cohesion and the friction
angle of the material constituting the embankment.

Griffiths et al. show that the expression
has the same physical meaning as the traditional definition by the ratio
between the resistance force and the tangential force along the failure surface.

As fig1-srm illustrates, the software treatment in the
command [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47) of the SRM method is carried out in three stages:

- **Stage 1**: Treatment of the SRM zone. One identifies the materials existing
in the SRM zone indicated, and one creates the mesh-cell groups in the SRM zone
in order to reassign the field of the degraded materials.
- **Stage 2**: Creation of the field of the degraded materials. One then tests
the convergence of the non-linear analysis and one refines the increment of the factor of safety
until the analysis diverges and the desired accuracy is reached.
- **Stage 3**: Post-processing. If requested, one obtains as output the cumulative plastic
strain field (V1 of the field VARI_NOEU) in order to visualise the failure surface.

### 2.3 Reduction of the strength properties of the materials

The Mohr-Coulomb and Drucker-Prager failure criteria are authorised in [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47). At the same time as the generation of the field of the degraded materials, one checks that there exists exactly one constitutive law among the two assigned to the SRM zone by the factor keyword COMPORTEMENT.

According to the expression, the degraded properties are computed by:

$$
\tan({\anglFric}^{\prime}) = \frac{\tan(\anglFric)}{\mathit{FS}}
\text{ et }
{\cohesion^{\prime}} = \frac{\cohesion}{\mathit{FS}}
$$

One makes the assumption of the associated plastic flow law of the Mohr-Coulomb behaviour, that is ${\anglFric}^{\prime}={\anglDila}^{\prime}$. In the contrary case, the dilatancy angle will automatically take the value of the friction angle during the execution of the SRM algorithm.

Apart from the Mohr-Coulomb criterion, it is also possible to employ the Drucker-Prager
criterion, which improves the singularity problem caused by the vertices of the cone
of the Mohr-Coulomb yield surface in the $\pi$ plane.
Instead of directly reducing the Drucker-Prager parameters (${\alpha}$
and $\yieldStress$), the command favours the *phi-c* strategy in order to avoid the
conservatism of the result:

$$
\tan {\anglFric}^{\prime}
=
\frac{3{\alpha}}{2\mathit{FS}\sqrt{(2{\alpha}+1)(1-{\alpha})}}
\text{ et }
{\cohesion}^{\prime}
=
\frac{\yieldStress}{2\mathit{FS}\sqrt{(2{\alpha}+1)(1-{\alpha})}}
\text{ et }
\tan{ {\anglDila }^{\prime}}
=
\frac{\tan{\anglDila }}{\mathit{FS}}
$$

### 2.4 Control of the convergence of the FS

In order to accelerate the search for the factor of safety (FS), it is possible
to control the iterative solution by configuring the parameters linked to the refinement
of the increment ${dP}$ (fig1-srm) according to the method indicated.
The increment decreases each time the non-linear analysis diverges until the
maximum residual is reached.

Two laws of variation of the increment ${dP}$ are available:

1. **'EXPONENTIELLE'**: Exponential variation

The increment of the factor of safety approaches the maximum residual in an exponential manner.
At the n-th refinement, the increment is computed by:

$$
{p}_{n}
=
{p}_{0} \, {2}^{-n}
$$

until ${p}_{N}<{p}_{\mathit{fin}}$, where

- ${p}_{n}$ is the current increment
- ${p}_{0}$ is the initial increment of the factor of safety.

1. **'LINEAIRE'**: Linear variation

The increment of the factor of safety approaches the maximum residual in a linear manner.
In that case an additional operand ITER_RAFF_LINE is necessary.
At the n^{th} refinement, the increment is computed by:

$$
{p}_{n}
=
{p}_{0}-n \, \frac{{p}_{0}-{p}_{\mathit{fin}}}{N}
$$

until ${p}_{N}={p}_{\mathit{fin}}$.

The advantage of the exponential method is that the analysis converges more rapidly,
whereas the linear method controls the accuracy of the result better.
The exponential variation of the increment is suited above all to an analysis with a large INCR_INIT.

The following method would simplify the choice between the two laws of variation of the increment.
Let $N$ be the desired number of refinements; one chooses the linear law if:

$$
\frac{{p}_{0}-{p}_{\mathit{fin}}}{{{log}}_{2}({p}_{0})-{{log}}_{2}({p}_{\mathit{fin}})}<N
$$

In this case, it is the exponential law which is chosen.

## 3 LEM method

### 3.1 General remarks on the LEM method

All methods of LEM type are based on the definition of the factor of safety as
being **the ratio between the shear resistance force and the tangential force**
along the failure surface.

By division of the slope into slices, which are considered as non-deformable solids,
one solves the balance of the forces and of the moments of each slice in order
to deduce from it the factor of safety. Respecting this calculation principle, the various LEM
methods are distinguished by the number of balances satisfied and the assumptions on the interaction force
between the slices.

A general overview of the solution methods by limit equilibrium is given in fig4-lem2.
4 LEM methods are available in [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47), suited to the analysis of slopes with a failure surface
which is either circular or non-circular:

- **Simplified Bishop method** and **Fellenius method**: these are the most
classical methods, which assume that the failure surface is **circular**.
  Thanks to the rotational slip, one reduces the number of moment equilibrium equations,
which is equal to the number of slices, to one equation describing the moment equilibrium
of the whole sliding part.
Thus, one computes the factor of safety in a simple manner by an explicit expression (Fellenius)
or by an implicit scalar equation (Bishop).

The assumptions of the model being too simplified, especially for the Fellenius method,
the result may be far from the physical observations.

- **Spencer method** and **Morgenstern-Price**: these are the most
classical methods assuming that the failure surface is **non-circular**.
  One solves all of the equilibrium equations, whose number is equal to 3 times
the number of slices, in order to obtain the most accurate possible result for the factor of safety.
The solution procedure becomes more sophisticated, which makes it possible to obtain a more
correct result if the failure surface is visually non-circular.

However, it should be noted that the result will not differ
significantly from that obtained by the simplified Bishop method if the material field is homogeneous and isotropic.

Furthermore, the LEM methods not relying on a finite-element analysis, the optimisation algorithms implemented in [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47) are indispensable in order to locate the failure surface which globally minimises the factor of safety:

- **Exhaustion method for the circular surface**: one runs through all the combinations of the end points of the potential failure surfaces. This is the method implemented in most commercial geotechnical software packages such as SLOPE/W of GeoStudio.
- **Enhanced fireworks algorithm (EFWA) for the non-circular surface**:
this is an intelligent-swarm algorithm which is inspired by the process of the generation of
sparks during the explosions of fireworks. It proves more efficient than other
optimisation algorithms for the solution of the slope-stability problem with a
non-circular failure surface.

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.02/1000020100000794000004EE67B81B2776ED4BCB.png)

*Overview of the LEM methods*

One of the particularities of the implementation of the LEM methods is that one computes the weight of the slices
with the aid of the same mesh used for the finite-element analysis. Although this imposes
mesh optimisations, developed in the command (so as to avoid as far as possible slowing down
the solution of the factor of safety), the advantage is expressed by the fact that one will no longer need
to switch between the solvers/models in order to carry out the different types of analysis of an embankment work.

For example it is possible to chain directly the computation of the pore-pressure field due to
the infiltration of water (see the THM model [u2.04.05](doc:u2.04.05)) and the slope-stability analysis. The latter can be followed by the computation of the irreversible displacement provided with a result of a dynamic transient analysis (see the command [POST_NEWMARK](doc:u4.84.05)).

### 3.2 Computation of the forces and of the geometry of the slices

In order to solve the balance of the forces and of the moments of the slices, one must first compute the geometrical parameters defining the sliding part of the soil, integrate the external mechanical loadings applied on the slices, compute their self-weight and possibly the pore pressure at the base of the slices. pre-proc shows the global workflow which evaluates the metrics necessary for the subsequent computation of the factor of safety.

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.02/pre_proc.png)

*Workflow of the recovery of the information on the slices in order to compute FS*
#### 3.2.1 Computation of the centre of the slip circle

In the case of a circular failure surface, in order to create the mesh-cell group
of the sliding part, one recovers the coordinates of the centre of the slip circle in two ways:

1. **With as inputs the end points and the radius:**
  By inserting the coordinates of the end points ($({x}_{1},{y}_{1})$
and $({x}_{2},{y}_{2})$) into the equation of the circle, one obtains the equations expressing
the relation between the coordinates of the centre $({x}_{0},{y}_{0})$
and a second-order equation in ${y}_{0}$:

$$
\left\lbrace
\begin{array}{l}
x_{0} = c_{1} - c_{2} \, y_{0} \\
(c_{2}^{2} + 1) y_{0}^{2} + (2 \, x_{1} c_{2} - 2 \, c_{1} c_{2} - 2 \, y_{1}) y_{0} + (x_{1} - c_{1})^{2} + y_{1}^{2} - R^{2} = 0
\end{array}
\right.
$$

  where the constants ${c}_{1}$ and ${c}_{2}$ are written:

$$
\begin{array}{c}
    {c}_{1} = \frac{{x}_{2}^{2}-{x}_{1}^{2}+{y}_{2}^{2}-{y}_{1}^{2}}{2({x}_{2}-{x}_{1})}\\
    {c}_{2} = \frac{{y}_{2}-{y}_{1}}{{x}_{2}-{x}_{1}}
\end{array}
$$

  In order to solve the equation, one expresses the coefficients in the second equation as:

$$
\begin{array}{c}
    A = {c}_{2}^{2}+1\\
    B = 2 \, ({x}_{1}-{c}_{1}){c}_{2}-2{y}_{1}\\
    C = ({x}_{1}-{c}_{1}^{2}+{y}_{1}^{2}-{R}^{2})
\end{array}
$$

  One thus obtains the ordinate of the centre. The failure surface being always convex, one takes the largest solution:

$$
{y}_{0} = \frac{-B+\sqrt{{B}^{2}-4 {AC}}}{2A}
$$

2. **With as inputs the end points and a horizontal / vertical tangent straight line:**
  In the case where one manually controls the search for the critical radius by the operands Y_MINI or Y_MAXI of [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47), one deduces the centre of the circle from the ordinate ${y}_{b}$ of the horizontal tangent straight line. One must therefore replace $R={y}_{0}-{y}_{b}$ in the equation and take up the same solution procedure.
  Likewise, one replaces $R^2$ by $R^2 = (x_0 - x_b)^2$ in the equation when evaluating the lower limit of the radius, that is the radius of the circle tangent to the vertical straight line $x = x_b$ where $x_b$ is the abscissa of the active end.

#### 3.2.2 Creation of the slices and computation of the self-weights

One creates the mesh-cell group of the slices with the aid of the operator [DEFI_GROUP](doc:u4.22.01) by following the following stages:

1. **Stage 1**: Creation of the mesh-cell group of the sliding part with the mesh cells whose nodes are strictly included in the sliding mass. One creates a reduced mesh from this mesh-cell group with a view to saving time in the following stages.
2. **Stage 2**: Creation of the mesh-cell group of the slice $n$ by the option INTERSEC, which makes it possible to recover the mesh cells common to the abscissa band of the slice and the sliding part.
3. **Stage 3**: Correction of the slice $n$ by the option DIFFE, which makes it possible to exclude the mesh cells belonging to the previous slice. This stage ensures that the total mass of the slices is equal to the mass of the sliding part.

An example of mesh-cell groups is shown in fig6-grma.
The self-weights of the slices are then computed via the operator [POST_ELEM](doc:u4.81.22).

One computes at the same time the inclination angle $\alpha$ of the base of the slices such that:

$$
\tan \alpha
=
\frac{{y}_{n+1}-{y}_{n}}{{x}_{n+1}-{x}_{n}}
$$

#### 3.2.3 Integration of the external mechanical loadings

##### 3.2.3.1 Integration of the distributed pressure

The distributed pressure applied on the surface of the slope is introduced via the keyword FONC_PRES in the form of a function or a formula with as variables the coordinates X and/or Y. As calc-pres shows, a series of operations is carried out so as to evaluate the nodal force induced by the distributed pressure. One takes advantage of this nodal field in order to determine the resultant vector of the forces and of the moments, per slice, by integrating the pressure along the 1D segments:

$$
\vector{P}_i
=
\int_{l_{i-1}}^{l_i} { - \pres(l) \, \normal(l) \measLine }
$$

$$
\vector{M}_{Q, i}
=
\int_{l_{i-1}}^{l_i} { [\vector{r}_c - \vector{r}(l)] \times \pres(l) \, \normal(l) \measLine }
$$

where ${\normal}$ represents the unit normal vector of the 1D elements, $\vector{r}$ is the coordinate vector of the nodes on the slope, $\vector{r}_c$ are the coordinates of the point with respect to which the moment is defined. For the Bishop and Fellenius methods, $\vector{r}_c$ is the centre of the radius. For the Morgenstern and Spencer methods, it is the point at the middle of the base of the slices. One adopts trapezoidal integration in order to compute numerically  and .

##### 3.2.3.2 Integration of the seismic inertial force

It is possible to apply the volume inertial force due to the earthquake via the factor keyword ACCE. Knowing the self-weight of the slices, one determines the inertial force and moment per slice such that:

$$
\vector{F}_{i} = k_a W_i \frac{\vector{r}}{\Vert \vector{r} \Vert_2}
$$

$$
\vector{M}_{S, i} = (\vector{r}_{b, i} - \vector{r}_c) \times \vector{F}_{i}
$$

where $\vector{r}_{b, i}$ represents the coordinates of the centroid of the slices, $k_a$ is the acceleration coefficient, $W_i$ is the weight of the slices, $\vector{r}$ is the direction of seismic excitation.

#### 3.2.4 Material properties and pore pressure

##### 3.2.4.1 Recovery of the strength properties

The material field at the input of the command [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47) makes it possible to recover the cohesion and the friction angle along the failure surface. The parameters of the Drucker-Prager behaviour are converted by the same formula as in the SRM method with $\mathit{FS}=1$.

Without the operand PHI_C_EQUI, one assumes that the properties are homogeneous on the base of each slice and equal to those of the material assigned to the mesh cell which contains the centre of the base. This “centre” mesh cell is defined by the criterion of minimum distance between the centre of the mesh cell and the mid-point of the base of the slices.

However, for non-homogeneous or stratified structures, there probably exist slices whose base crosses several materials. In that case, by activating PHI_C_EQUI, the program computes the equivalent cohesion and friction angle under the assumption that the normal stress is distributed uniformly on the base. To this end, one first identifies the intersection points between the base of the slices and the interfaces of the materials. By a dichotomy approach, one divides the segment of the base into two recursively until the materials at the ends are identical or the length of the segment is less than the value defined in the operand TOLE_DIST. The equivalent properties are determined such that:

$$
\cohesion_{eq}
=
\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N_c} \cohesion_k \, l_k}{L}, \quad \tan \anglFric_{eq}
=
\frac{\sum_{k=1}^{N_c} \tan \anglFric_k \, l_k} {L}
$$

where $N_c$ is the number of materials on the base of the slices, $l_k$ is the base length in the material $k$, $L$ is the total length of the base.

The computation of the equivalent properties has the effect of eliminating the discontinuity of the FS/KC in the search zone, which accelerates the location of the global minimum and makes the result more relevant in certain specific cases. As kc-plot-comp shows, with PHI_C_EQUI one obtains a smoother curve of variation of the critical acceleration coefficient, without local oscillation, during a stability analysis by the Bishop method.

##### 3.2.4.2 Interpolation of the hydraulic pressure field

It is possible to define the hydraulic pressure field either by a simple pore-pressure coefficient $R_u$, or by the phreatic line, or by an evol_noli result containing the components PRE1 and PRE2. The first two approaches make it possible to reconstruct the pore-pressure field analytically [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47). Under the keyword CHAM_PRES, two interpolation methods are available at the user's choice, which serve to evaluate the hydraulic pressure at the centre of the base of the slices.

- **Interpolation weighted by the inverse of the distance (IDW)**
  Let $u_i$ be the capillary pressure or the air pressure at the nodes of the mesh cell which contains the centre of the base of the slices; the interpolation is carried out by:

$$
u
=
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{(\frac{1}{{d}_{i}})}^{2}{u}_{i}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}{(\frac{1}{{d}_{i}})}^{2}}
$$

  where ${d}_{i}$ denotes the distance between the nodes of the mesh cell and the centre of the base.
  This is a simple and efficient method. However, the weighting of the distant nodes decreasing rapidly as a function of the distance, only the nodes in the immediate neighbourhood influence the interpolation result. This method is therefore rather suited to a fine mesh.
- **Interpolation with the aid of the shape function**
  One uses the command [PROJ_CHAMP](doc:u4.72.05) in order to project the nodal field of the hydraulic pressures onto a mesh made up of discrete elements alone whose coordinates are the base centres of the slices. Compared with the IDW, this method is more reliable when the mesh is coarse.

### 3.3 Circular failure surface

#### 3.3.1 Fellenius method

The Fellenius method is also called the “Swedish method” or
“*ordinary method of slices*”. This method completely neglects
the interaction force between the slices. Thus the formula for the factor of safety $\mathit{FS}$ becomes explicit.
The diagram of the forces is shown in fig7-fellenius.

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.02/1000000000000540000002729A3E1B0C439DD2EE.jpg)

*Diagram of the forces of the Fellenius method*

Given the weight of the slices, the distributed pressure and the seismic inertial force, the normal force applied on the base of the slices is written:

$$
N = W\left[k_h \sin\alpha + (1 - k_v)\cos\alpha\right] + P \cos(\alpha - \beta)
$$

where

- $W$ denotes the weight,
- $k_h$ and $k_v$ denote the normalised acceleration coefficients in the horizontal and vertical directions respectively.
- $P$ is the total force of the distributed pressure applied on the surface of the slices.
- $\alpha$ and $\beta$ denote the inclination angle of the base of the slices and the angle of the pressure force with respect to the horizontal.

By neglecting the interaction between the slices, by solution of the balance of the forces in the plane of the base of the slices, one deduces from it the shear force undergone by the slices:

$$
S = -W \left[ k_h \cos\alpha - (1-k_v) \sin\alpha\right] + P \sin(\alpha - \beta)
$$

Finally, with the presence of the hydraulic pressure field, an additional term linked to the hydraulic effect is added to the expression of the strength:

$$
\Omega_{\textrm{hydr}} = \left\lbrace
\begin{array}{l}
    \presCapi \Delta l \cos^2 \alpha \tan {\anglFric}^{\prime} & \text{ si } \quad \presCapi < 0 \\
    -\presAir \Delta l \cos^2 \alpha \tan {\anglFric}^{\prime} + \presCapi \Delta l \tan \anglFric_{\textrm{hydr}} & \text{ sinon}
\end{array}
\right.
$$

where $\Delta l$ is the base length of the slices; one has $\anglFric_{\textrm{hydr}} = \anglFric_b$ if the values of the matric-suction angle are supplied, otherwise

$$
\tan \anglFric_{\textrm{hydr}} = S_{\textrm{we}}(\presCapi)\tan {\anglFric}^{\prime}
$$

with $S_{\textrm{we}}$ the effective saturation evaluated by the hydraulic behaviour of the soil.

By definition the explicit expression of the factor of safety is then written:

$$
\mathit{FS}
=
\frac{\sum\cohesion \, {\Delta}l + N \tan{\anglFric}^{\prime} + \Omega_{\textrm{hydr}}}{\lvert \sumS \rvert}
$$

Because of the very strong assumption, the Fellenius method does not check the balances of the forces and of the moments at the same time, but all the loadings line up in the vertical direction. One has arbitrarily chosen the formula which satisfies the balances of the forces. The other possibility is to express the shear force by $S = W \sin\alpha - \frac{M_n}{R}$ where $M_n$ denotes the moment of the mechanical loadings apart from gravity with respect to the centre of rotation and $R$ is the radius of rotation. Thus the expression satisfies the balances of the moments.

According to the tests, it turns out that the two formulae for $S$ lead to different values of $\mathit{FS}$, but the deviation with respect to the exact solution depends on the case studied. It is therefore difficult to judge which is more relevant. As a result, the Fellenius method remains a simple but not very accurate means of computation. It is better to pass to the simplified Bishop method for a better balance between efficiency and fidelity.

By setting $\mathit{FS} = 1$ in the equation and assuming a horizontal seismic acceleration, one deduces from it the expression of the seismic acceleration coefficient:

$$
k_c = \frac{
    \sum\limits_i \pm \left[ W_i \sin\alpha_i + P_i \sin(\alpha_i - \beta_i) \right]
    - \left[ {\cohesion}^{\prime} \Delta l + \left[ W_i \cos\alpha_i + P_i \cos(\alpha_i - \beta_i)\right] \tan {\anglFric_i}^{\prime}  + \Omega_{\textrm{hydr}, i} \right]
    }{
    \sum\limits_i W_i \left[\sin\alpha_i \tan {\anglFric_i}^{\prime} \pm \cos\alpha_i \right]
    }
$$

where the sign $\pm$ is positive for the upstream slopes and negative for the downstream slopes.

#### 3.3.2 Simplified Bishop method

The simplified Bishop method assumes that the interaction force between the slices is horizontal,
as fig8-bishop-simple shows.

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.02/illus_bishop.png)

*Diagram of the forces of the Bishop method*

The balance of the forces in the vertical direction is written:

$$
N\cos\alpha +S\sin\alpha -W(1 - k_v) - P \cos \alpha = 0
$$

The contribution of the hydraulic pressure field taking matric suction into account is written:

$$
\Omega_{\textrm{hydr}}
=
\Delta l \cos\alpha
\,
\left[ -\presAir \tan {\anglFric}^{\prime} + \presCapi \tan\anglFric_{\textrm{hydr}} \right]
$$

According to the Mohr-Coulomb criterion, the tangential resistance force is written:

$$
S
=
\frac{1}{\mathit{FS}}
\left[
    {\cohesion}^{\prime} \Delta l
    +
    N \tan {\anglFric}^{\prime}
    +
    \frac{\Omega_{\textrm{hydr}}}{\cos\alpha}
\right]
$$

One therefore deduces from it the implicit expression of the factor of safety:

$$
\mathit{FS}
=
\frac{
    \sum\limits_i
        \frac{\deri{\cohesion} \Delta l \cos\alpha_i + \left[W_i(1 - k_v) + P_i \cos\beta_i\right] \deri{\anglFric} + \Omega_{\textrm{hydr}, i}}
            {\cos\alpha_i \pm \frac { \sin\alpha_i \tan\deri{\anglFric} }{\mathit{FS}}}
    }{
        \lvert \sum\limits_i W_i \sin\alpha_i - \frac{ M_{n,i}}{ R} \rvert
    }
$$

where $M_{n,i} = M_{S,i} + M_{Q,i}$ represents the moment with respect to the centre of rotation of the external mechanical loadings (ref.  and ).

One solves the equation by a fixed-point method.

Similar to the Fellenius method, one determines the critical acceleration coefficient explicitly such that:

$$
k_c =
\frac{
    \sum\limits_i W_i \sin\alpha_i - \frac{M_{n,i}}{R}
    -
    \sum\limits_i \frac{\deri{\cohesion} \Delta l \cos\alpha_i + \left[W_i(1 - k_v) + P_i \cos\beta_i\right] \deri{\anglFric} + \Omega_{\textrm{hydr}, i}}
            {\cos\alpha_i \pm (\sin\alpha_i \tan\deri{\anglFric})}
    }{
        \sum\limits_i W_i \frac{y_c - y_{b,i}}{R}
    }
$$

where $y_c$ and $y_b$ are the ordinates of the centre of rotation and of the centroid of the slices respectively.

The simplified Bishop method satisfies at the same time the moment equilibrium equations for the sliding mass and the vertical force equations for each soil slice, that is $n+1$ equations in total.

#### 3.3.3 Location of the critical surface

In order to locate the critical surface, one runs through all the possible combinations of the end points of the potential failure surfaces.
For each combination, one determines the trial bands of the radius by the two following approaches:

- **Option 1**: One runs through all the radii which form a kinematically admissible failure surface. The lower limit is the minimum value among:

  - The radius of the circle tangent to the vertical straight line $x=x_b$ where $x_b$ is the abscissa of the active end (located at the crest of the slope) of the failure surface.
  - The radius of the circle tangent to the base of the mesh.
  For the upper limit of the radius, one first examines whether all the convex discontinuity points lie outside the segment linking the entry and exit points. If the condition is satisfied, the upper limit is equal to infinity, which means that one runs through the circular surfaces even at very small curvature if the discretisation parameter NB_RAYON is high enough.
  Otherwise, one determines the upper limit of the radius by an adaptive algorithm which chooses the maximum radius as a function of the mesh supplied. By mesh analysis, one recovers the global minimum size of the 2D elements, denoted $s_{min}$, and the vertical sizes of the 2D elements containing the nodes of the mesh-cell group representing the slope profile defined by the keyword GROUP_MA, denoted $s_p$, which is therefore a function of the coordinates $X$. Starting from the maximum radius of the circles passing through the convex discontinuity points, denoted $R_{max}$, one corrects $R_{max}$ iteratively until it satisfies the two following conditions:

  1. The distance between the circle and the slope profile is greater than $s_{min}$ in the vertical direction.
  2. All the points constituting the base of the slices move away from the slope profile by a vertical distance greater than $s_p$ at the same abscissa.
- **Option 2**: One manually defines the trial band by defining the horizontal straight lines tangent to the failure surfaces. This search option accelerates the analysis if the experienced user can anticipate the reduced zone in which the failure surface is probably located. One also takes advantage of this option in order to examine the surfaces in the neighbourhood of that given by the result. It should be noted that one can use Y_MINI or Y_MAXI alone in order to impose the lower or upper limit of the radius; the rest is determined by the procedure described in option 1.

In order to generate the potential failure surfaces distributed uniformly in the search space, one defines the control variable $d$ which measures the distance between the segment linking the ends, denoted X1-X2, and the tangent straight line of the circle parallel to X1-X2, as illus-circ-gene illustrates. The limits of the radius being converted into $d_{min}$ and $d_{max}$, one generates the circular surfaces with a constant increment:

$$
\Delta d = \left\lbrace
    \begin{array}{l}
        \frac{d_{max}}{N_r} & \text{ si } \quad d_{min} = +\infty \\
        \frac{d_{max} - d_{min}}{N_r - 1} & \text{ sinon }
    \end{array}
    \right.
$$

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.02/illus_circ_gene.png)

*Generation of the circles in the Bishop and Fellenius methods.*

Modifying the discretisation parameter NB_RAYON is liable to be insufficient in order to locate the critical surface at the end of the global search.
Faced with this problem, after the computation of the factor of safety corresponding to all the radii tested
for a combination of the end points, if a new minimum appears,
one records it by carrying out a local verification around the critical radius.
This verification is done by iteratively refining $\Delta d$ in an
exponential manner, and by testing the radii around the radius which minimises the factor of safety.

At iteration $n$, the refined increment is written:

$$
\Delta d_{n} = {2}^{-n} \, \Delta d_{0}
$$

One then tests the two potential failure surfaces corresponding to:

$$
{d}_{n}={d}_{\mathit{minFS},n-1} \pm \Delta d_{n}
$$

During the iterations, one always records the radius minimising
the factor of safety and one continues to test its neighbourhood with the refined increment, until the stabilisation of the radius.

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.02/visu_surf_circ.png)

*Visualisation of the circular surfaces run through in the test case ssnp008 (cf. [V6.03.008](doc:v6.03.008)).*

According to the tests, this optimisation algorithm designed for the circular surfaces
proves effective even for inhomogeneous slopes. visu-surf-circ
shows that, with the slope modelled in the test case ssnp008 [V6.03.008](doc:v6.03.008),
the surfaces tested and coloured as a function of the FS are distributed uniformly in the search space.
The concentration of the surfaces around the optimum point reflects the effect of the local optimisation making it possible to locate the critical surface correctly.
### 3.4 Non-circular failure surface

#### 3.4.1 Computation of FS by the Spencer or Morgenstern-Price methods

The Spencer and Morgenstern-Price methods are designed to analyse
the stability of slopes whose failure surface is of a more complex shape because of
the inhomogeneity of the materials, such as the stratification of the foundation and the presence
of a weak layer (test case ssnp007 [V6.03.007](doc:v6.03.007)).

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.02/1000000000000198000001868CD60ADC881EC7AC.jpg)

*Diagram of the forces of the Spencer and Morgenstern-Price methods*

fig11-spencer-MP shows the diagram of the forces undergone by
the slices when one chooses the Spencer or Morgentern-Price methods. ${F}_{v}$ denotes the vertical component of the mechanical loadings, which is expressed by  and ${F}_{h}$ denotes their horizontal component.

These methods satisfy all the equilibrium equations, that is $n$
force equilibrium equations in the vertical direction, $n$
in the horizontal direction and $n$ moment equilibrium equations, where $n$
is the number of slices. The two methods are distinguished by the assumption on the direction of the
interaction forces between the slices ${Z}_{i}$.
One represents this direction by the angle ${\theta}_{i}$ with respect to the horizontal direction:

$$
\tan {\theta}_{i}
=
{f}_{0}({x}_{i}) + \lambda {f}_{1}({x}_{i})
$$

where ${f}_{0}$ and ${f}_{1}$ are the functions defining the variation of
the inclination angle of the interaction forces between the slices, and $\lambda$
is a multiplier coefficient which is one of the unknowns of the equilibrium equations.
The functions ${f}_{0}$ and ${f}_{1}$ depend on the method chosen:

- **Spencer method**: ${f}_{0}=0,{f}_{1}=1$.
- **Morgenstern-Price method** (proposed by Chen and Morgenstern in 1983):

  - ${f}_{0}(x) = \tan{\beta}_{0}+\dfrac{\tan{\beta}_{1}-\tan{\beta}_{0}}{L}x$,
where ${\beta}_{0}$ and ${\beta}_{1}$ are the inclination angles of the slope profile at the two
ends of the failure surface.
  - ${f}_{1}(x)=\sin \left( \dfrac{\pi}{L}x \right )$

One adopts the procedure for the solution of the factor of safety proposed by Zhu et al.
The advantage of this procedure, compared with the traditional procedures, is that it deduces from it the
fixed-point iteration scheme for computing the factor of safety and proposes a weakly
coupled approach for solving FS and $\lambda$. According to the authors, this solution
procedure is at the same time more efficient and more effective than the traditional procedures.

- The force equilibrium balance in the *normal* direction of the slice $i$ is written:

$$
N_i =
    {W}_i (1 - k_v) \cos\alpha_i
    + k_hW_i\sin\alpha_i
    + Z_i \sin(\theta_i-\alpha_i)
    - Z_{i-1}\sin(\theta_{i-1}-\alpha_i)
    + P_i \cos(\beta_i - \alpha_i)
$$

  where

  - $N$: the normal force on the base of the slices.
  - $Z$: the interaction force between the slices.
- The force equilibrium balance in the *tangential* direction of the slice $i$ is written:

$$
\begin{array}{l}
S_i &= \frac{N_i \tan {\anglFric}^{\prime}_i + \Omega_{\textrm{hydr},i} + {\cohesion}^{\prime}_i\Delta l}{F_S} \\
    &= W_i(1 - k_v)\sin\alpha_i
    - P_i\sin(\beta_i - \alpha_i)
    - k_h W_i\cos\alpha_i
    + Z_i \cos(\theta_i-\alpha_i)
    - Z_{i-1}\cos(\theta_{i-1}-\alpha_i)
\end{array}
$$

  where $S$ denotes the shear force, $\Omega_{\textrm{hydr}}$ gathers the terms linked to the hydro-mechanical coupling effect:

$$
\Omega_{\textrm{hydr}} = \left\lbrace
\begin{array}{l}
    \presCapi\Delta l \tan {\anglFric}^{\prime} & \text{ si } \quad \presCapi < 0 \\
    -\presAir \Delta l \tan {\anglFric}^{\prime} + \presCapi \Delta l \tan \anglFric_{\textrm{hydr}} & \text{ sinon }
\end{array}
\right.
$$

By eliminating the normal force in the equations  and , one has the simplified force balance:

$$
{Z}_{i+1}{\Phi}_{i+1}={\Psi}_{i}{Z}_{i}{\Phi}_{i} \pm {R}_{i+1}-{F}_{s}{T}_{i+1}
$$

where

$$
\begin{array}{l}
    {\Phi}_{i}={F}_{s}\cos({\alpha}_{i}-{\theta}_{i}) \pm \sin({\alpha}_{i}-{\theta}_{i})\tan{\anglFric }_{i}\hfill \\
    {\Psi}_{i}=[{F}_{s}\cos({\alpha}_{i+1}-{\theta}_{i}) \pm \sin({\alpha}_{i+1}-{\theta}_{i})\tan{\anglFric }_{i+1}]/{\Phi}_{i}\\
    {R}_{i}=\left[{W}_{i}(1 - k_v)\cos{\alpha}_{i} + k_hW_i\sin\alpha_i + P_i\cos(\beta_i - \alpha_i)\right]\tan{\anglFric }_{i} + \Omega_{\textrm{hydr},i}+{c}_{i}\Delta l\hfill \\
    {T}_{i}={W}_{i}(1 - k_v)\sin{\alpha}_{i} - P_i\sin(\beta_i - \alpha_i) - k_hW_i\cos\alpha_i\hfill
\end{array}
$$

The signs $\pm$ are positive for the upstream slopes and negative for the downstream slopes.

In fact, ${R}_{i}$ represents the shear resistance force generated
by all the forces applied on the slice except the interaction forces between the slices.
${T}_{i}$ represents the driving force leading to the destabilisation of the slice. Thus the equation satisfies the complete force balances in all directions. If one adds the assumption of zero interaction forces, one will recover the explicit expression of FS of the Fellenius method.

Thus, with the conditions ${Z}_{0}={Z}_{N}=0$ (the interaction forces on the two sides of the sliding part
are zero), one deduces from it the fixed-point scheme for computing FS:

$$
\mathit{FS}
=
\frac{ \sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \left( \lvert{R}_{i}\rvert\prod_{j=i}^{N-1}{\Psi}_{j}+\lvert{R}_{N}\rvert \right) }
{\sum_{i=1}^{N-1} \left( {T}_{i} \prod_{j=i}^{N-1}{{\Psi}}_{j}+{T}_{N} \right) }
$$

Finally, the moment equilibrium balance with respect to the centre of the base of the slice $i$ is written:

$$
{E}_i \left( {z}_{i+1}+\frac{b}{2}\tan{\alpha}_i \right)
=
{E}_{i-1} \left( {z}_{i-1}-\frac{b}{2}\tan{\alpha}_i \right)
+
\frac{b}{2} \left( \tan{\theta}_{i}{E}_{i} + \tan{\theta}_{i-1}{E}_{i-1} \right)
-
M_{Q, i}
-
M_{S, i}
$$

where

- ${E}_{i}$ is the horizontal component of the interaction force, which one can compute with the aid of
the equation,
- $b$ is the horizontal width of the slice,
- ${z}_{i}$ denotes the vertical distance between the point where the
interaction force is applied and the centre of the base of the slices.
- $M_{Q, i} = - P_i \sin\beta_i \left( y_{s,i} - y_{c, i} \right)$ is the moment of the distributed pressure with respect to the centre of the base of the slices.
- $M_{S, i} = - k_hW_i \left( y_{b, i} - y_{c, i} \right)$ is the moment of the seismic inertial force with respect to the centre of the base of the slices.

Let the moment of the horizontal component with respect to the centre of the
base be ${M}_{i}={E}_{i}{z}_{i}$; one rewrites the equation as:

$$
{M}_{i+1}
=
{M}_{i}-\frac{b}{2} \tan{\alpha}_{i+1}({E}_{i}+{E}_{i+1})
+
\frac{b}{2} \left(\tan{\theta}_{i}{E}_{i}+\tan{\theta}_{i+1}{E}_{i+1}\right)
-
M_{Q, i+1}
-
M_{S, i+1}
$$

Taking into account the condition ${M}_{0}={M}_{N}=0$, one deduces from it the expression of $\lambda$ in the equation:

$$
\lambda =\frac{
    \sum\limits_{i=1}^\limits{N} {
        \left[ \tan{\alpha}_{i} \left( {E}_{i}+{E}_{i+1} \right) - \left( {f}_{0,i}{E}_{i}+{f}_{0,i-1}{E}_{i-1} \right) \right] }
     +
        \frac{2}{b}\sum\limits_{i=1}\limits^{N}\left[M_{Q, i} + M_{S, i}\right]
    }
    {
    \sum_{i=1}^{N} \left( {f}_{1,i}{E}_{i}+{f}_{1,i-1}{E}_{i-1} \right)
    }
$$

One solves FS and $\lambda$ by the alternate evaluation of their values with
the aid of the equations  and  until the convergence of the two unknowns,
as fig12-FZ-spencer-MP illustrates.

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.02/10000000000002F3000004E9FE456BACD6AA77AB.jpg)

*Diagram of the solution of the factor of safety of the Spencer and Mogenstern-Price methods*

According to the result of the tests, this procedure for the solution of FS converges in two iterations,
as fig13-convergence-lambda shows.

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.02/100000000000038F00000285B1001E22B81E7ABC.jpg)

*Convergence of the FS and of lambda*

In order to find the critical acceleration coefficient, which is equal to the absolute value of $k_h$ when $F_S = 1.0$, it is sufficient to replace in the equation the mobilising tangential force $T$ by the destabilising force due to the earthquake $A$:

$$
A_i
=
- W_i \left( \cos\alpha_i \pm \sin\alpha_i \tan {\anglFric}^{\prime}_i \right)
$$

and the resistance force $R$ by the tangential force stabilising the slope $S$:

$$
S_i
=
P_i \sin (\beta_i - \alpha_i)
-
W_i \sin\alpha_i
\pm :
\left[
    {\cohesion}^{\prime} \Delta l
    +
    \left(
        W_i \cos\alpha_i + P_i \cos( \beta_i - \alpha_i) \right) \tan {\anglFric}^{\prime}_i + \Omega_{\textrm{hydr},i}
    \right]
$$

#### 3.4.2 EFWA optimisation algorithm

##### 3.4.2.1 Optimisation procedure

Because of the large number of state variables, the problem of locating the non-circular
critical surface proves too difficult to be solved by the trial-and-error method.
Recently, thanks to the emergence of intelligent-swarm algorithms, numerous researchers
use them in order to solve the problem of locating the non-circular failure surface.
For example, the particle-swarm algorithm (PSO), the genetic algorithm and the
ant-colony algorithm have shown their ability to find the optimum at an acceptable time cost.

Zheng et al. (2013) proposed the enhanced fireworks algorithm (EFWA).
Xiao et al. (2019) applied the EFWA for the first time to
slope-stability analysis, and according to their results, the EFWA reaches the optimum with fewer
iterations than other swarm algorithms.

**In [:ref:`CALC_STAB_PENTE <u4.84.47>`] one therefore adopts the EFWA as the optimisation algorithm.**

The EFWA simulates the explosion process of fireworks.
In the space of the state variables depicted as the night sky, one explodes the fireworks at different places.
Each explosion locally generates sparks in order to search for the optimum in the neighbouring space.
Among these sparks, certain ones which present a high selective value (inversely proportional to the FS)
are kept for the next explosion.

The EFWA proceeds in three stages:

1. **Random generation of the N initial fireworks**
  The vector of the state variables comprises $N+1$ components,
which are the abscissae of the end points of the failure surface, and $N-1$
which are the ordinates of the intermediate points, where $N$ is the number of slices:

$$
X=\lbrace {x}_{0},{y}_{1}, \cdots ,{y}_{N-1},{x}_{N}\rbrace
$$

  If one does not specify the initial state via the operand ETAT_INIT of [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47),
one randomly generates ${N}_{\mathit{FA}}$ initial positions of the fireworks
(uniform distribution law):

$$
{X}_{i}
=
\rand \left( {x}_{\min,i},{x}_{\max,i} \right )
$$

  One notes that $\rand([i,j])$ is a random-number generator in the interval $[i,j]$ and where the limits of the degrees of freedom are determined by the algorithm described in
section  3.4.2.2.
  If one supplies to ETAT_INIT the table output by [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47),
one adds it into the initial generation of the fireworks.
2. **Generation of the ordinary and Gaussian sparks**
  During the generation of the ordinary sparks from the explosion position $i$,
the amplitude of the explosion is computed by:

$$
{A}_{i}
=
A \,
\frac{\mathit{FS} \left({X}_{i} \right)-{\mathit{FS}}_{\min}+\varepsilon}
     {\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathit{FS}({X}_{i})-{\mathit{FS}}_{\min})+\varepsilon}
$$

  where $A$ is the value of the keyword A of [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47) denoting
the total amplitude of the ordinary sparks,
and $\varepsilon=1.0 \times 10^-8$ is a constant close to zero in order to avoid division by zero.
By the equation one searches in a wider interval if the central position
is of insufficient quality, and one concentrates on the space close to the minimum value of FS.
  According to the equation, the explosion amplitude will be very small around the surface minimising the FS in the current iteration, which restricts the local search capability. Therefore, one corrects the explosion amplitude by a lower limit which decreases during the iterations:

$$
A_{\textrm{min}}(N)
=
A_{\textrm{init}}
-
\frac{A_{\textrm{init}} - A_{\textrm{fina}}}{N_{\textrm{max}}}\sqrt{(2N_{\textrm{max}} - N)N}
$$

  where $N$ is the number of the current iteration and $N_{\textrm{max}}$ is the maximum number of EFWA iterations defined in the keyword ITER_MAXI. By default the parameters $A_{\textrm{init}}$ and $A_{\textrm{fina}}$ are fixed at 100% and 10% of the width of the slices. It is also possible to define them manually via the keywords A_INIT and A_FINAL.
  The number of ordinary sparks of an explosion is computed by:

$$
{S}_{i}
=
M
\,
\frac{{\mathit{FS}}_{\max}-\mathit{FS}({X}_{i})+\varepsilon}
      {\sum_{i=1}^{N}({\mathit{FS}}_{\max}-\mathit{FS}({X}_{i}))+\varepsilon}
$$

  where $M$ is the value of the keyword M of [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47) denoting
the total number of ordinary sparks. By the equation one generates more sparks
if the central position is of good quality. In order to avoid the appearance of absurd numbers, one corrects:

$$
\widehat{S}_{i}
=
\left\lbrace
\begin{array}{l}
    \round(aM) & \text{si } S_{i} < aM \\
    \round(bM) & \text{si } S_{i} > bM \\
    \round(S_{i}) & \text{sinon }
\end{array}
\right.
$$

  One generates the ordinary sparks by arbitrarily modifying the degrees of freedom of the positions
of the current generation:

$$
\widehat{X}_{i}^{k}
=
{X}_{i}^{k}
+
{\Delta}{X}^{k} \text{ avec } \Delta}{X}^{k}={A}_{i}\rand{-1,1})
$$

  In order always to keep the diversity of the search for the optimum,
one also generates the Gaussian sparks of quantity ${M}_{g}$.
One selects in an arbitrary manner the firework positions, and one arbitrarily modifies
the degrees of freedom:

$$
\widehat{X}_{i}^{k}
=
{X}_{i}^{k}
+
\left( {X}_{B}^{k}-{X}_{i}^{k} \right) e  \text{ avec } e=N(\left{0,1\right})
$$

  where ${X}_{B}^{k}$ is the position corresponding to the minimum FS of the current generation.
  If certain degrees of freedom are outside the limits determined by
the algorithm described in section  3.4.2.2,
one projects them into the admissible band:

$$
\widehat{X}_{i}^{k}
=
{X}_{\min,i}^{k}
+
\rand(\left{0,1 \right}) \, \left( {X}_{\max,i}^{k}-{X}_{\min,i}^{k} \right)
$$

  Because the modification of a degree of freedom influences the admissible band of the intermediate points
to its right, one also adjusts them so that the failure surface generated is kinematically admissible.
  Finally, in the case where the failure surface generated is so close to the slope profile that it is
impossible to create the mesh-cell group of the slices, one evaluates the admissible band of the degrees of freedom by taking into account the size of the 2D elements around the slope profile or the imposed MARGE_PENTE, and one adjusts the parameters of the new surfaces by a recursive algorithm (ref. 3.4.2.2) by rechecking the admissible bands of the degrees of freedom which follow.
3. **Selection of the sparks for the next generation**
  The new generation of fireworks occurs through the roulette algorithm.
The probability that an ordinary or Gaussian spark is kept in the next generation is:

$$
{p}_{i}
=
\frac{{\mathit{FS}}_{\max}-{\mathit{FS}}_{i}}
     {{\mathit{FS}}_{\max}-{\mathit{FS}}_{\min}}
$$

The diagram of the EFWA algorithm is represented in fig14-diag-EFWA.

*Diagram of the EFWA algorithm*

##### 3.4.2.2 Dynamic constraints on the state variables

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.02/100000000000036C000002CCA2CCD2EDFC7E2C5B.jpg)

*Illustration of the constraints on the state variables*

In order to ensure that the failure surface is always convex and kinematically admissible,
one adopts the procedure proposed by Cheng et al. in order to determine the dynamic
constraints on the state variables.

In this procedure, the limits of the ordinates of the intermediate points depend
on one or two preceding points (to the left). Let us take the example in fig15-cont-VarEtat:

- **Lower bound**:

  - The failure surface making an angle of less than 45 degrees with
the slope profile at the end point A, which determines the lower bound of the point C.
  - One extends the straight line AC up to the abscissa of the point D in order to determine the lower bound of the point D.
- **Upper bound**:

  - One links CB and checks whether CB intersects the slope
profile. If that is the case, one finds the intersection point of minimum ordinate
J and links CJ in order to determine the upper bound D2 of the point D.
Otherwise, D1 will be the upper bound of the point D.

In order to ensure that the failure surface remains in the domain,
one adjusts the lower bound to the bottom of the domain if it lies below the latter.

1. In [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47) one adopts a stochastic strategy which chooses a random number of surfaces at each EFWA iteration and generates / modifies the intermediate points in the reverse direction, that is from the active end (at the top) to the passive end (at the bottom). This makes it possible to increase the topological diversity of the potential surfaces, and therefore to accelerate the convergence of the EFWA. It also increases the probability of falling upon the exact solution of the critical surface thanks to the enlarged search space.
2. In a manner similar to the generation of the circular surfaces, a recursive algorithm applies to the verification of the consistency of the surfaces and to the automatic correction in the explosion phase of the EFWA. In the absence of the parameter MARGE_PENTE explicitly defined by the user, one recovers the size of the 2D elements around the slope profile by analysing the mesh and one ensures that the surface moves away from the slope by a distance greater than the vertical size of these elements at the edges.

### 3.5 Mesh adaptation

![Figure](/figures/r7.05.02/10000000000005C0000003778A881B0A4EEB1343.jpg)

*Diagram of the stability analysis with mesh refinement*

The stability analysis via [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47) being based on the mesh,
its fineness significantly influences the result of the factor of safety.
As a result, [CALC_STAB_PENTE](doc:u4.84.47) automates the mesh refinement.
However, the computation time increases all the more as the mesh is fine.
As a result, as fig16-sensibilite-maillage shows, one isolates the
mesh refinement at the moment of the search for the critical surface,
given that the fineness of the mesh has a negligible influence on
the location of the failure surface.

In order to accelerate the creation of the slice groups, with the aid of the command MACR_ADAP_MAIL,
one refines the mesh only on the contour of the slices until
the maximum number of refinements is reached or until the stabilisation of FS.
This process is driven by a proximity-indicator field expressed in
the form of a Gaussian function of zero mean and of standard deviation $\sigma$:

$$
\mathit{val}
=
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi }\sigma }\exp\left(\frac{-{d}_{\min}^{2}}{2{\sigma}^{2}} \right)
$$

where ${d}_{\min}$ is the minimum distance between a node of the mesh
and the contour of the slices. One refines the mesh cells according to a criterion given by the value of $\sigma$,
that is 68% of the mesh cells closest to the contour, and one unrefines the mesh cells according to the criterion of value
$2 \,\sigma$, that is 5% of the mesh cells furthest from the contour.

One decreases the standard deviation in an exponential manner in order to control dynamically
the refinement zone.
