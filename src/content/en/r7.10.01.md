# r7.10.01 Processing of random responses

**Summary:**

The introduction of a “stochastic approach to the seismic analysis” in order to solve a problem of vibratory mechanics under random excitation requires a particular post-processing.

The command POST_DYNA_ALEA [U4.76.02](doc:u4.76.02) makes it possible, from the power spectral density of a response cross-spectrum, to evaluate its standard deviation, its apparent frequency and the distribution of its peaks. It also makes it possible, as a first approach, to compute the Vanmarcke function, which is useful in the case of a seismic analysis.

**NB:**

*This command also makes it possible to carry out the statistical estimates for any type of response cross-spectrum to a random excitation which is not necessarily seismic (for example: the effect of swell or of a turbulent flow).*

> **Contents**

## 1 Introduction

For a structure subjected to a random excitation of swell, turbulent-flow or earthquake type..., the loading is not known in a deterministic manner, but is most often described by probabilistic or spectral information such as the power spectral density. For linear structures it is possible to use a stochastic computation method which makes it possible to determine the power spectral densities of the response to these random excitations.

The operator POST_DYNA_ALEA has the function of carrying out the statistical analyses of the power spectral density of the response. It therefore supplies the probabilistic information on the response of the structure. The computations of statistical parameters are carried out on the basis of the computation of the spectral moments of the power spectral density considered.

These statistical parameters are: the standard deviation, the apparent frequency, the peak factor according to the definition of Vanmarcke, the mean maximum and the irregularity factor.

**Note:**

*The operator POST_DYNA_ALEA, designed initially for the seismic approach, after an analysis with the operator* *DYNA_ALEA_MODAL*  [U4.56.06](doc:u4.56.06)([bib1], [bib2]) *, can also carry out the post-processings of the operator* *DYNA_SPEC_MODAL developed by the TTA department within the framework of the phasing-out of FLUSTRU. This operator carries out the computation of the response of a steam-generator tube structure uniformly excited by a transverse flow.*

## 2 Spectrum - Cross-spectrum - Cross-spectral matrix

### 2.1 Signal processing - Conventions adopted

#### 2.1.1 Introduction

A signal can have two representations: a temporal representation of the form $x=f(t)$ or a frequency representation of the form $X=F(f)$ . These two representations are linked to one another by the **Fourier transform.**

There exist, in the numerical domain and in the experimental domain, different ways of computing the spectral quantities relating to a temporal signal $x(t)$ (dimensional representation or not, factor 1/2 or not for the Fourier transform).

Now, if the various definitions of the PSD (cf. [§2.2.2] and [Appendix1]) from the Fourier transform of the signal change nothing in the computation carried out by CALC_INTE_SPEC [U4.56.03](doc:u4.56.03), it is on the other hand important, in the computations carried out by the post-processing operator POST_DYNA_ALEA, that the data be consistent so that the results produced by this operator have the physical dimension of the original signal.

It is also necessary to know, for a quantitative comparison between computation and experiment, what conventions are adopted for the computation of the spectral quantities. All of these conventions are recalled in [Appendix1] for each type of signal. Only the general formulae are given again here.

#### 2.1.2 Fourier transform

For the **Fourier transform** **in frequency** $(f)$ **of a signal** (of unit u) *,*  expressed in u/Hz, the following definition is adopted: $X(f)=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}x(t){e}^{-2i\pi \text{ft}}\text{dt}$

The inverse transform is then expressed by: $x(t)=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}X(f){e}^{+2i\pi \text{ft}}\text{df}$

One can also express the **Fourier transform in circular frequency** $(\omega =2\pi f)$ , by the following definition:

${X}^{p}(\omega )=\frac{1}{2\pi }\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}x(t){e}^{-i\omega t}\text{dt}$

The inverse transform is expressed by: $x(t)=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{X}^{p}(\omega ){e}^{+i\omega t}d\omega$

Which leads to the equivalence: ${X}^{p}(\omega )={X}^{p}(2\pi f)=\frac{1}{2\pi }X(f)$

### 2.2 Notion of power - Power spectral density

#### 2.2.1 Power of a signal - Power spectrum of a signal

Just as for the signal itself, the power of the signal can be expressed as a function of time or of frequency:

- the instantaneous temporal power is simply called power:

$p(t)=x(t).\text{x*}(t)$

where $\text{x*}(t)$ is the complex conjugate quantity of $x(t)$ .

- the frequency power is commonly called the power spectral density or spectrum:

$\underline{{S}_{xx}(f)=X(f).\text{X*}(f)={\mid X(f)\mid }^{2}}$

*This definition is possible only when the Fourier transform of the signal exists.*

One can then express the total energy of the signal by $E=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{S}_{xx}(f)\text{df}=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{\mid X(f)\mid }^{2}\text{df}$

The expression for this PSD for the various types of signal is given in [Appendix1]. One will see later [§3.3] another definition — equivalent according to the Wiener-Khinchin theorem — but more general, of the power spectral density based on the statistical approach.

#### 2.2.2 Interaction power - Interaction spectral density of two signals - Cross-spectrum

- One also defines the **instantaneous interaction power of two signals** $x(t)\text{et}y(t)$ :

$\begin{array}{}{p}_{xy}(t)=x(t).\text{y*}(t)\text{et}{p}_{yx}(t)=\text{x*}(t).y(t)\\ \text{reliées par}{p}_{xy}(t)={p}_{yx}\ast (t)\end{array}$

- If the two signals admit a Fourier transform $X(f)\text{et}Y(f)$ , one can express the **frequency interaction power**  or **cross-spectrum**  by ${S}_{\text{XY}}(f)=X(f).\text{Y*}(f)$
- If the **two signals are real**  then the interaction power ${p}_{xy}(t)={p}_{yx}(t)=x(t).y(t)$ is real. But there is no reason for ${S}_{\text{XY}}(f)$ also to be real; on the other hand ${S}_{\text{XY}}(f)$ is complex with Hermitian symmetry, namely:

even real part and odd imaginary part, or even modulus and odd phase

- If $X(f)=Y(f)$ , one then speaks of an **auto-spectrum.**

#### 2.2.3 Cross-spectral matrix

A cross-spectral matrix of order $N$ is a complex $N\times N$ matrix, each term of which depends on the frequency in the form of a function of $f$ *.*  The diagonal terms are the auto-spectra, the off-diagonal terms are the cross-spectra between the points considered (each row or column representing a point in the physical mesh or a mode in a modal analysis). The cross-spectra handled in practice being Hermitian, only the $\frac{N(N+1)}{2}$ terms of the upper (or lower) triangle are sufficient to define the cross-spectral matrix completely.

### 2.3 Implementation in Code_Aster

The cross-spectral matrices handled by the operator POST_DYNA_ALEA consist of complex functions of the frequency: ${S}_{\text{XY}}(f)$ .

These matrices are stored in tables of interspectre concept.

## 3 Reminders on the statistical laws [bib4]

### 3.1 Definitions

$t$ discrete parameter ${({t}_{n})}_{n=\text{1,N}}$ or continuous (time or a space variable).

$X(t)$ random process.

With each instant ${t}_{n}$ is associated a random variable ${X}_{n}$ , a random variable with realisation ${x}_{n}$ .

Then $x(t)={({x}_{n}=x({t}_{n}))}_{n=1\text{,N}}$ is a realisation of the process $X(t)$ , a process constituted of $N$ a priori independent random variables.

Each variable ${X}_{n}$ is characterised by its **cumulative distribution function** ${F}_{n}({\text{x,t}}_{n})=\text{Prob}({X}_{n}\le x)$ or by its **probability density** $p({\text{x,t}}_{n})=\frac{\partial {F}_{n}}{\partial x}({\text{x,t}}_{n})$ .

The random process is also characterised by its **moment functions** ; the first two moments are of particular importance. These are the **mathematical expectation**  or **mean** $m(t)$ also denoted $E\left[X(t)\right]$ and, for every pair $({t}_{1},{t}_{2})$, the **autocorrelation function** $R({t}_{1},{t}_{2})$ or ${R}_{\text{XX}}({t}_{1},{t}_{2})$ also denoted $E\left[X({t}_{1})X({t}_{2})\right]$ .

$\begin{array}{}m(t)=E\left[X(t)\right]=\int\mathrm{xp}(x,t)\text{dx}\\ {R}_{\text{XX}}({t}_{1},{t}_{2})=E\left[X({t}_{1})X({t}_{2})\right]=\int\int{x}_{1}{x}_{2}p({x}_{1},{t}_{1};{x}_{2},{t}_{2}){\text{dx}}_{1}{\text{dx}}_{2}\end{array}$

One also defines a **cross-correlation function**  for two processes $X(t)\text{et}Y(t)$ .

${R}_{\text{XY}}({t}_{1},{t}_{2})=E\left[X({t}_{1})Y({t}_{2})\right]=\int\int{x}_{1}{y}_{2}p({x}_{1},{t}_{1};{y}_{2},{t}_{2}){\text{dx}}_{1}{\text{dy}}_{2}$

The “spread” of the process is characterised by the **variance**:

${\sigma}^{2}(t)=E\left[{(X(t)-\mu (t))}^{2}\right]$

For a zero-mean process ($\mu$  = 0.), the **variance**, which then characterises the “intensity of the phenomenon” (square of the standard deviation, or mean-square value), is equal to the autocorrelation function at time $t={t}_{1}={t}_{2}$ :

${\sigma}^{2}(t)=E\left[X(t)X(t)\right]={R}_{\text{XX}}(\text{t,t})=\int{x}^{2}p(x,t)\text{dx}$

### 3.2 Assumptions in random dynamics

Very classically, several assumptions are made within the framework of random dynamics. It is thus accepted that the processes studied are **stationary, zero-mean and ergodic** .

#### 3.2.1 Stationary zero-mean processes - variance

A process is said to be **stationary**  if the whole of its “probabilistic characteristics” is invariant under a translation ${t}_{0}$ of the parameter $t$ . Which implies:

$\begin{array}{}\mu (t)=\text{Cte}\\ {R}_{\text{XX}}({t}_{1},{t}_{2})={R}_{\text{XX}}({t}_{2}-{t}_{1})={R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau )={R}_{\text{XX}}(-\tau )\end{array}$

For a zero-mean process ${\sigma}^{2}={R}_{\text{XX}}(0)$ .

#### 3.2.2 Ergodicity

This notion comes from a reasoning of Gibbs (1839-1903) for which the observation time of a physical phenomenon can be considered as infinite compared with the time scale at the molecular level. The system then passes through all the possible states, remaining as long as possible, or passing most often, in the states which are the most probable, so that the **temporal mean**  becomes **equal to the statistical mean over the states** , that is, the mathematical expectation. This extends to the correlation and cross-correlation functions.

$\begin{array}{}\mu =\underset{T\to +\infty }{\lim}\frac{1}{T}\underset{-T/2}{\overset{+T/2}{\int}}x(t)\text{dt}\\ {R}_{\text{XX}}(t)=\underset{T\to +\infty }{\lim}\frac{1}{T}\underset{-T/2}{\overset{+T/2}{\int}}x(t-\tau )x(t)\text{dt}\end{array}$

**Note:**

*For the remainder of the document it will be assumed that the random process is stationary and zero-mean. All of the developments carried out in Code_Aster satisfy these assumptions.*

### 3.3 Power spectral density

Within the framework of this statistical approach, one can give a very general definition of the **power spectral density**  or **PSD.** The following definitions, expressed in frequency or in circular frequency, will be retained for *Code_Aster* :

$\begin{array}{}{S}_{\text{XX}}(f)=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau ){e}^{-\mathrm{2i}\pi f\tau }d\tau ;{G}_{\text{XX}}(f)=\underset{0.}{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau ){e}^{-\mathrm{2i}\pi f\tau }d\tau \\ {S}^{{p}_{\text{XX}}}(\omega )=\frac{1}{2\pi }\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau ){e}^{-i\omega \tau }d\tau ;{G}^{{p}_{\text{XX}}}(\omega )=\frac{1}{2\pi }\underset{0.}{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau ){e}^{-i\omega \tau }d\tau \end{array}$

which lead to the following relations: $\begin{array}{}{G}^{{p}_{\text{XX}}}(\omega )=\frac{1}{2\pi }{G}_{\text{XX}}(f)\\ {S}_{\text{XX}}(f)=2{G}_{\text{XX}}(f){S}_{\text{XX}}^{p}(\omega )=2{G}^{{p}_{\text{XX}}}(\omega )\end{array}$

One can show that ${G}_{\text{XX}}(f)$ , which is equal to the Fourier transform of ${R}_{\text{XX}}(t)$ , is real and positive. One will refer to [Appendix1], which contains all the conventions adopted in order to ensure the consistency of the results.

### 3.4 Spectral moments

The following quantities are called spectral moments (which have been defined in circular frequency):

${\lambda}_{i}=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{\mid \omega \mid }^{i}{S}^{{p}_{\text{XX}}}(\omega )d\omega =\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{\mid \omega \mid }^{i}{S}_{\text{XX}}(f)\text{df}$

One has in particular: ${\lambda}_{0}={\sigma}^{2}{\lambda}_{2}={\sigma}_{\dot{X}\dot{X}}^{2}{\lambda}_{4}={\sigma}_{\ddot{X}\ddot{X}}^{2}$ which are the standard deviations of $X$ and of its first derivatives.

These moments are systematically computed up to order 4; by means of the keyword MOMENT it is possible to request the computation of the higher modes. In *Code_Aster* , the computation is carried out for a PSD expressed as a function of the frequency $f$ .

## 4 Threshold-exceedance measures and reliability

The classical methods make it possible to access only the maximum of the displacement (or of the acceleration) by an “adapted” summation of the maxima on each mode. The essential interest of the stochastic approach to random vibratory analysis lies in the statistical knowledge of the response of the structure, which can therefore be converted into statistical information on the reliability. In this respect, two modes of failure can be taken into consideration:

- failure by threshold exceedance: this type of failure occurs when the response of the system exceeds a limit value. This amounts to seeking the probability that the values of the process remain below a threshold value during the observation duration T. The peak factor ( **peak factor)**  makes it possible to estimate, in an approximate manner, the mean maximum, over an observation duration of the signal.
- failure by fatigue or accumulation of damage.

This second approach can also be treated from the first statistical elements computed in POST_DYNA_ALEA. It is carried out in the command POST_FATI_ALEA [U4.67.05](doc:u4.67.05) [R7.04.02](doc:r7.04.02).

Within the framework of studies under seismic excitations, we are essentially interested in the problem of threshold exceedance. Hence, as a first step, the computation of a certain number of statistical parameters which make it possible to characterise the signal to be studied (spectral moments and Rice formulae [§4.1]); equipped with these characteristics one will then be able to estimate the threshold-exceedance probabilities by means of classical probability models [§4.2], as well as a reliability criterion (Vanmarcke law [§4.3]).

### 4.1 Spectral moments and characteristic parameters

The spectral moments are defined by: ${\lambda}_{i}=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{\mid \omega \mid }^{i}{S}_{\text{XX}}(f)\text{df}$

The infinite set of these spectral moments characterises the cross-spectrum perfectly and thus makes it possible to establish a certain number of numerical results. In the particular case of a 1-dof oscillator or of a signal with a single peak, the first three spectral moments are sufficient to recover the auto-spectrum ${S}_{\text{XX}}$ . This is the case which is retained in *Code_Aster* , since it is assumed that the values are distributed according to a GAUSS law.

#### 4.1.1 Rice formulae

For a random signal such as defined previously: stationary with **zero mean (centred)**  and ergodic, it is furthermore assumed that the measured values are distributed according to a **normal law, Gauss-type profile**  (cf. [§4.2.1]).

The analysis of a stationary Gaussian random loading has the advantage of leading to simple analytical expressions — known under the name of Rice formulae — and of representing numerous real phenomena.

The following statistical parameters are obtained from the various spectral moments linked to the various derivatives of $X$ (cf. [§3.4]):

- Standard deviation: ${\sigma}_{X}=\sqrt{{\lambda}_{0}}$

**Note:**

*If only the positive part of the spectrum is supplied, Code_Aster multiplies the 1st spectral moment by 2* ${\sigma}_{X}=\sqrt{{\mathrm{2\lambda }}_{0}}$ *.*

An extremum (maximum or minimum) of amplitude $X$ is defined by the probability of having a zero derivative $\dot{X}=0$ associated with an arbitrary second derivative $\ddot{X}$ .

- Mean number of extrema per second: ${N}_{e}=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{\sigma}_{\ddot{X}}}{{\sigma}_{\dot{X}}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{{\lambda}_{4}}{{\lambda}_{2}}}$

The exceedance of a level ${X}_{0}$ is defined by the probability of having $X={X}_{0}$ with an arbitrary slope $\dot{X}$ : one therefore counts the passages through this level with positive **and**  negative slopes. Taking into account the assumptions of Gaussian laws, the number of passages through ${X}_{0}$ per second is expressed by: ${N}_{{X}_{0}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{\sigma}_{\dot{X}}}{{\sigma}_{X}}{e}^{-\frac{{X}_{0}^{2}}{2{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}}$

Which leads to the following expressions:

- Number of exceedances of the level **with positive slope**  per second: ${N}_{{{X}_{0}}^{+}}=\frac{1}{2}{N}_{{X}_{0}}$
- Number of passages **through zero**  (${X}_{0}$  = 0) per second: ${N}_{0}=\frac{1}{\pi}\frac{{\sigma}_{\dot{X}}}{{\sigma}_{X}}=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{{\lambda}_{2}}{{\lambda}_{0}}}$
- Number of passages **through zero with positive slope**  per second:

${N}_{{0}^{+}}=\frac{1}{2}{N}_{0}=\frac{1}{2\pi }\sqrt{\frac{{\lambda}_{2}}{{\lambda}_{0}}}$

${N}_{{0}^{+}}$ represents a mean statistical frequency of passage through zero with positive slope.

In the case of a “simple” signal, that is, with **a single peak** , ${N}_{{0}^{+}}$ , the number of passages through zero, can be assimilated to an **apparent frequency**  also denoted ${f}_{e}$ . In the much more general case of an arbitrary signal, the physical interpretation of the value ${N}_{{0}^{+}}$ is more open to caution!

The irregularity factor reflects the frequency appearance of the signal. Lying between 0 and 1, it tends towards 1 when the process is narrow-band; on the other hand it tends towards 0 for a broad-band process. Its expression is:

$I=\frac{{N}_{0}}{{N}_{e}}=\frac{{\sigma}_{\dot{X}}^{2}}{{\sigma}_{X}{\sigma}_{\ddot{X}}}=\sqrt{\frac{{\lambda}_{2}^{2}}{{\lambda}_{0}{\lambda}_{4}}}$

The three parameters — ${N}_{0},{N}_{e},I$ — characterise the signal completely. One can, in particular, estimate the mean number of positive peaks per second: ${N}_{{\text{pic}}^{+}}=1/4(1+I){N}_{e}$ *.*

All of these parameters are computed and stored in a "printable" table on the RESULTAT file by means of the command IMPR_TABLE.

### 4.2 Distributions of the positive peaks

One of the main items of knowledge of interest to the designers of structures, from its response estimated for a random excitation, is the determination of the threshold exceedances and in particular **the probabilities of exceedance** of certain critical thresholds.

The Rice formulae (previous paragraph) make it possible to know the mean rate of the crossings of certain levels. The following approach makes it possible to give a probability law for the presence of such and such a peak. One is therefore interested in the positive maxima of the response.

A maximum occurs when $\dot{x}(t)=0\text{avec}\ddot{x}(t)<0$ . One is therefore interested in the joint probability density $p(x,\dot{x}=0,\ddot{x},t)\text{de}X(t),\dot{X}(t),\ddot{X}(t)$ . (The process must therefore be twice differentiable, which is acquired when a Gaussian distribution of the signal is accepted.)

This probability density of the **positive peaks**  makes it possible for example to compute the proportion of peaks lying between a and b (or the probability that the next peak lies between a and b), which is:

${\int}_{a}^{b}p(x,0,\ddot{x},t)\text{dx}$

The signal being stationary Gaussian, centred with respect to its mean value (zero in seismic analysis), the distribution of the peaks is symmetric with respect to this mean. One is therefore interested in the **distribution of the positive peaks** . In the general case, the distribution of the peaks of positive amplitude $X$ is written in the form [bib5]:

$\begin{array}{}{p}_{\text{pic}}^{+}(X)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi }{\sigma}_{X}(1+I)}\left[\sqrt{1-{I}^{2}}{e}^{\frac{{X}^{2}}{2{\sigma}_{X}^{2}(1-{I}^{2})}}+\frac{IX}{{\sigma}_{X}}{e}^{\frac{{X}^{2}}{2{\sigma}_{X}^{2}}}{\int}_{-\infty }^{\alpha}{e}^{\frac{-{t}^{2}}{2}}\mathrm{dt}\right]\\ \text{Si}X<0\text{alors}{p}_{\text{pic}}^{+}(X)=0\text{avec}\lbrace \begin{array}{}I=\frac{{\sigma}_{\dot{X}}^{2}}{{\sigma}_{X}{\sigma}_{\ddot{X}}}\\ \alpha =\frac{X}{{\sigma}_{X}}\frac{I}{\sqrt{1-{I}^{2}}}\end{array}\end{array}$

This is the formula also known under the name of LONGUET-HIGGINS [bib6]. The graphical representation of this formula for 4 values of $I$ is presented below.

![Figure](/figures/r7.10.01/100035620000297200001ECDC88C6E798C9E5C60.svg)

**Figure 4.2-a: Distribution of peaks of positive amplitude** **normalised with respect to the standard deviation of the signal**

This distribution of the positive peaks is simplified in the case of signals for which the irregularity factor is $I=0\text{ou}I=1$ .

#### 4.2.1 $I=0$ Broad-band signal: Gauss law or normal law

In the case of a broad-band signal, the positive peaks are distributed according to a GAUSS law:

${p}_{\text{pic}}^{+}(X)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi {\sigma}_{{X}^{2}}}}{e}^{-\frac{{X}^{2}}{2{\sigma}_{{X}^{2}}}}$

#### 4.2.2 $I=1$ Narrow-band signal: Rayleigh law

In the case of a narrow-band signal, the positive peaks are distributed according to a RAYLEIGH law:

${p}_{\text{pic}}^{+}(X)=\frac{X}{{\sigma}_{{X}^{2}}}{e}^{-\frac{{X}^{2}}{2{\sigma}_{{X}^{2}}}}$

#### 4.2.3 Computation of the values in Code_Aster

The values of these two laws are computed in *Code_Aster* under the factor keywords RAYLEIGH or GAUSS.

From the standard deviation ${\sigma}_{X}=\sqrt{{\lambda}_{0}}$ computed previously, one computes the probability values of the peaks ${p}_{\text{pic}}^{+}(X)\text{pour}X\in \left[0,6{\sigma}_{X}\right]$ with a default step of $\frac{6{\sigma}_{X}}{200}$ .

If the user wishes to refine the analysis, he or she can supply the values VALMIN and VALMAX of the domain of variation of $X$ . The user can also supply the value of the computation step; otherwise it will be taken as one 200th of the band retained.

Figure [Figure 4.2-a] shows that the domain chosen by default, up to $6{\sigma}_{X}$ , does indeed cover the whole of the values of $X$ with non-zero probabilities.

### 4.3 Seismic response: Vanmarcke law

In the case of the response to an earthquake of a primary structure (that is, excited at its base by the ground) having a **predominant mode** , i.e. which responds (taking the exciting frequencies into account) on a single mode, one uses the reliability law of VANMARCKE [bib10], which makes it possible to estimate, over an **operating duration** $T$, the **probability that the process exceeds the failure threshold** .

*The notion of predominant mode is very important here; if the structure responds on several modes the formula in its present expression is no longer suitable.*

Let $X(t)$ be the response to a Gaussian white noise of a lightly damped linear oscillator. One defines the probability $W(T)$ that the process remains in the safety domain. $W(T)$ represents the fraction of samples which have not crossed the failure threshold after a duration $T$ ; it is a measure of reliability.

It can be written in the form $W(T)=\text{Prob}\left\lbrace \mid X(t)<{X}_{0};0\le t<T\mid \right\rbrace :{p}_{1}(T)\text{=-}\frac{\text{dW}(T)}{\mathrm{dT}}$ is the probability density of crossing of the threshold.

For high values of $T$ one will take: ${p}_{1}(T)=A\alpha {e}^{-\alpha T}$ where $A$ depends on the initial conditions and $\alpha$ is the limiting decay rate.

#### 4.3.1 Assumption of independent crossings

With the assumption that the threshold exceedances with a positive slope are **independent events** , the number of crossings over $[0,T[$ constitutes a Poisson process of arrival rate ${N}_{{X}_{0}}=2{N}_{{X}_{0}}^{+}$ (number of exceedances of ${X}_{0}$ defined in [§4.1.1]). The probability that $n$ passages occur over the duration $T$ is written by application of the Poisson law (see [§4] of [bib8]):

$P\left\lbrace n\text{passages sur}[0,T[\right\rbrace ={e}^{-{N}_{{X}_{0}}T\frac{{({N}_{{X}_{0}}T)}^{n}}{n!}}$

The structure is “reliable” if the threshold is not exceeded during the duration $T$ . The reliability $W(T)$ therefore corresponds to $n=0$ passage, whence $W(T)={e}^{-{N}_{{X}_{0}}T}$ .

The limiting decay rate is therefore here $\alpha ={N}_{{X}_{0}}=2{N}_{{X}_{0}}^{+}$ .

#### 4.3.2 Vanmarcke law

For a stationary Gaussian process, the probability of exceeding the value $X$ is [§4.1.1]: ${N}_{X}={N}_{0}{e}^{-\frac{{X}^{2}}{2{\sigma}_{{X}^{2}}}}$ ; one deduces from it that the probability that the initial value of the envelope is lower than the threshold $X$ is: $1-\frac{{N}_{X}}{{N}_{0}}=1-{e}^{-\frac{{X}^{2}}{2{\sigma}^{2}}}$ .

One then combines this expression with the limiting decay law obtained with the assumption of independent crossings, which leads to **the expression for the reliability**:

$W(T)={\mathrm{Ae}}^{-\alpha T}=(1-{e}^{-{s}^{2}/2}){e}^{-{N}_{0}T\frac{(1-{e}^{-\text{hs}})}{{e}^{\frac{{s}^{2}}{2}}-1}}$

with ${N}_{0}=\frac{1}{\pi}\sqrt{\frac{{\lambda}_{2}}{{\lambda}_{0}}}$ the rate of passage through 0 and $T$ the observation duration

$\text{où}s=\frac{X}{\sqrt{{\lambda}_{0}}}\text{}h={d}^{1.2}\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\text{}\delta =\sqrt{1-\frac{{\lambda}_{1}^{2}}{{\lambda}_{0}{\lambda}_{2}}}$

$\delta$ is an estimator of the bandwidth of the PSD of $X$ .

This relation has the immense advantage of supplying an explicit estimator of the reliability as a function of the reduced value of the threshold *s* , of the number of equivalent half-cycles ${N}_{0}$ , and of the bandwidth parameter $\delta$ .

**NB:**

*“The agreement between the estimator and the simulations can be improved if one replaces* $\delta$ *by* ${\delta}^{1.2}$ ” [bib6] “correction” introduced into the formula written above with respect to the expression for the limiting decay rate given in the previous paragraph.

The following graphical representation is carried out in the case of a process of apparent frequency $15\mathrm{Hz}$ , that is ${N}_{0}=30\mathrm{Hz}$ , for an observation duration of $T=\mathrm{1s}$ . The bandwidth estimator $\delta$ is taken equal to 0.30. As in the previous illustrations, the amplitude is normalised with respect to the standard deviation.

### 4.4 Peak factor implemented in Code_Aster

The operator POST_DYNA_ALEA makes it possible to compute the Vanmarcke peak factor (FACT_PIC) and the mean maximum d (MAX_MOY). The latter is obtained as the product of the peak factor with the standard deviation: $d=\sigma {\eta}_{{T}_{\mathit{SM}},p}$

More generally, the peak factor makes it possible to estimate the $p$ -fractiles of the distribution of the maxima of a Gaussian process from the standard deviation. The peak factor, due to Vanmarcke [bib10], is written:

${\eta}_{{T}_{\mathit{SM}},p}^{2}=2\ln({\mathrm{2N}}_{\eta}[1-\exp(-{\delta}^{1.2}\sqrt{\pi \ln({\mathrm{2N}}_{\eta})})])$ .

In this expression, $\delta$ is the bandwidth of the process (cf §4.3.2) and ${N}_{\eta}$ is determined from the central frequency ${\nu}_{0}^{+}={\mathrm{0.5N}}_{0}$ as ${N}_{\eta}={\mathrm{0.5N}}_{0}T{(-\lnp)}^{-1}$     .

It is therefore, for MAX_MOY, strictly speaking the median maximum; this being so, the two statistics (mean and median) coincide for the case of a Gaussian distribution.

**Conventions for the power spectral densities**

**A1.1** **Introduction**

In order to retain the consistency necessary for the whole of the computations and the comparisons with experiment (cf [§2.3] and [§3.3]), the two sets of definitions consistent with the random-response computations and the post-processing, as they have been retained for *Aster*, are developed below:

- the first from spectral data expressed as a function of the frequency. It is this set which is consistent with the computation carried out in the operator CALC_INTE_SPEC [U4.56.03](doc:u4.56.03).
- the second from spectral data expressed as a function of the circular frequency.

These two sets make valid the post-processing as it is expressed in POST_DYNA_ALEA.

The unit in which the various quantities handled are expressed will be specified each time, as a function of the unit u of the reference signal. The explanations given are brief. For more details one may refer to reference [bib10].

**A1.2** **Types of signal and definition of the power**

Four types of signal are considered:

- signals of finite energy,
- periodic signals,
- signals of finite power and deterministic signals,
- random signals satisfying the ergodicity assumption and stationary.

In random dynamic analysis the signals are random. For the interpretation of experimental results, the signals are either periodic or of finite power (deterministic).

For each type of signal a energy quantity is defined, which is either an energy or a power and which will be designated in the following paragraphs under the single term of power:

- The **signals of finite energy**  are defined by their energy $E$ expressed in ${u}^{2}s$ :

$E=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}x{(t)}^{2}\text{dt}\text{<+}\infty$                             eq An1.2-1

- The **periodic signals**  are defined by the power $P$ of the signal expressed in u2:

$P=\frac{1}{T}\underset{\left[T\right]}{\int}{\mid x(t)\mid }^{2}\text{dt}$                                 eq An1.2-2

$T$ denotes the period of the signal. $\left[T\right]$ is an interval of length $T$ .

- The **signals of finite power**  are defined by the mean power $P$ of the signal expressed in ${u}^{2}$ :

$P=\underset{T\to +\infty }{\lim}(\frac{1}{T}\underset{-T/2}{\overset{+T/2}{\int}}{\mid x(t)\mid }^{2}\text{dt})\text{<+}\infty$                     eq An1.2-3

- The **random signals**  are defined by the mean power $P$ of the signal expressed in ${u}^{2}$ :

$P=E\left[{\mid X(t)\mid }^{2}\right]=\underset{T\to +\infty }{\lim}(\frac{1}{T}\underset{-T/2}{\overset{+T/2}{\int}}{\mid x(t)\mid }^{2}\text{dt})\text{<+}\infty$                     eq An1.2-4

Use is made here of the ergodicity assumption, which implies that the statistical and temporal means carried out on a realisation of a process are identical.

**A1.3** **Autocorrelations**

Taking into account the statistical reminders carried out in the body of the text, one has, for each type of signal previously defined:

- Autocorrelation of the **signals of finite energy** , expressed in ${u}^{2}/\mathrm{Hz}$ :

${R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau )=\int\overline{x(t)}x(t+\tau )\text{dt}$                     eq An1.3-1

- Autocorrelation of the **periodic signals** , expressed in ${u}^{2}$ :

${R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau )=\frac{1}{T}\underset{\left[T\right]}{\int}\overline{x(t)}x(t+\tau )\text{dt}$                     eq An1.3-2

- Autocorrelation of the **signals of finite power** , expressed in ${u}^{\mathrm{2 }}$ :

${R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau )=\underset{T\to +\infty }{\lim}\frac{1}{T}\underset{-T/2}{\overset{+T/2}{\int}}\overline{x(t)}x(t+\tau )\text{dt}$                 eq An1.3-3

- Autocorrelation of the **random signals** , expressed in ${u}^{2}$ :

${R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau )=E\left[\overline{X(t)}X(t+\tau )\right]=\underset{T\to +\infty }{\lim}\frac{1}{T}\underset{-T/2}{\overset{+T/2}{\int}}\overline{x(t)}x(t+\tau )\text{dt}$     eq An1.3-4

**A1.4** **Definition of the power spectral density**

**A1.4.1** **Expression in frequency**

The power spectral density is defined by:

${S}_{\text{XX}}(f)=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau ){e}^{-2i\pi f\tau }d\tau \text{ou}{G}_{\text{XX}}(f)=\underset{0}{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau ){e}^{-2i\pi f\tau }d\tau$     eq An1.4.1-1

The mechanical engineer being interested only in the positive values of the frequency and of time, the function ${G}_{\text{XX}}$ is more often used.

One can show, in the case where the Fourier transforms of the signals exist, that this definition is equivalent (Wiener-Khinchin theorem) to the following definitions of the power spectral density.

- For the signals of finite energy:

${G}_{\text{XX}}(f)={\mid X(f)\mid }^{2}$ expressed in ${u}^{2}/{\mathrm{Hz}}^{2}$                 eq An1.4.1-2

- For the periodic signals:

If $X(f)=\sum_{n\text{=-}\infty }^{n\text{=+}\infty }{C}_{n}\delta (f-{\mathrm{nf}}_{0})$ then ${G}_{\text{XX}}(f)=\sum_{n\text{=-}\infty }^{n\text{=+}\infty }{C}_{n}^{2}\delta (f-{\mathrm{nf}}_{0})$     eq An1.4.1-3

${G}_{\text{XX}}(f)$ is expressed in ${u}^{2}/\mathrm{Hz}$ .

${f}_{0}$ is the inverse of the period of the signal.

${C}_{n}$ coefficient of the Dirac functions.

- For the signals of finite power:

${G}_{\text{XX}}(f)=\underset{T\to +\infty }{\lim}(\frac{1}{T}{\mid {X}_{\left[T\right]}(f)\mid }^{2})\text{en}{u}^{2}/\mathrm{Hz}$                 eq An1.4.1-4

where ${X}_{\left[T\right]}$ denotes the restriction of $x(t)\text{à}\left[-\text{T/}2;\text{T/}2\right]$ .

- For the random signals:

${G}_{\text{XX}}(f)=\underset{T\to +\infty }{\lim}E\left[\frac{1}{T}{\mid {X}_{\left[T\right]}(f)\mid }^{2}\right]\text{en}{u}^{2}/\mathrm{Hz}$                 eq An1.4.1-5

where ${X}_{\left[T\right]}$ denotes the restriction of $x(t)$ to $\left[-T/2;T/2\right]$ .

**Link between the PSD and the power.**

With the definitions given above for the power spectral densities, one has, for all the signals, the relation:

$P=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{G}_{\text{XX}}(f)\text{df}$                             eq An1.4.1-6

This relation is established by using Parseval's theorem.

**A1.4.2** **Expression in circular frequency**

In circular frequency, the power spectral density is defined by:

${G}^{{'}_{\text{XX}}}(\omega )=\frac{1}{2\pi }\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{R}_{\text{XX}}(\tau ){e}^{-i\omega \tau }d\tau$                     eq An1.4.2-1

In the same way as for the expression in frequency, one can show, in the case where the Fourier transforms of the signals exist, that this definition is equivalent (Wiener-Khinchin theorem) to the following definitions of the power spectral density

- For the signals of finite energy:

${G}_{\text{XX}}^{'}(\omega )=2\pi {\mid {X}^{'}(\omega )\mid }^{2}$ expressed in ${u}^{2}/{\mathrm{Hz}}^{2}$                 eq An1.4.2-2

- For the periodic signals:

$\text{Si}{X}^{'}(\omega )=\sum_{n\text{=-}\infty }^{n\text{=+}\infty }{C}_{n}\delta (\omega -n{\omega}_{0})\text{}\text{alors}{G}_{\text{XX}}^{'}(\omega )=\sum_{n\text{=-}\infty }^{n\text{=+}\infty }{C}_{n}^{2}\delta (\omega -n{\omega}_{0})$     eq An1.4.2-3

${G}_{\text{XX}}^{'}(\omega )$ is expressed in ${u}^{2}/\mathrm{Hz}$ , and ${\omega}_{0}=\frac{2\pi }{T}$ where $T$ is the period of the signal.

${C}_{n}$ coefficient of the Dirac functions.

- For the signals of finite power:

${G}_{\text{XX}}^{'}(\omega )=\underset{T\to +\infty }{\lim}(\frac{2\pi }{T}{\mid {X}_{\left[T\right]}^{'}(\omega )\mid }^{2})\text{en}{u}^{2}/\mathrm{Hz}$                 eq An1.4.2-4

${X}_{\left[T\right]}^{'}$ denotes the restriction of $x(t)$ to $\left[-\text{T/}2;\text{T/}2\right]$ .

- For the random signals:

${G}_{\text{XX}}^{'}(\omega )=\underset{T\to +\infty }{\lim}E\left[\frac{2\pi }{T}{\mid {X}_{\left[T\right]}^{'}(\omega )\mid }^{2}\right]$     in ${u}^{2}/\mathrm{Hz}$             eq An1.4.2-5

${X}_{\left[T\right]}^{'}$ denotes the restriction of $x(t)$ to $\left[-T/2;T/2\right]$ .

**Link between the PSD and the power.**

Likewise, one has for all the signals the relation — which follows from Parseval's theorem —:

$P=\underset{-\infty }{\overset{+\infty }{\int}}{G}_{\text{XX}}^{'}(\omega )d\omega$                             eq An1.4.2-6

**Hilbert transform**

| Let $X(t)$ | be a real signal with Fourier transform $X(\omega )$ . |
| --- | --- |
|  |  |
| Let $H(\omega )$ | be the transfer function: $H(\omega )=j\text{sign}(\omega )=\lbrace \begin{array}{cc}j& \omega >0\\ -j& \omega <0\\ 0& \omega =0\end{array}$ |

$H(\omega )$ transforms $X(t)$ into its Hilbert transform denoted $\stackrel{ˆ}{X}(t)$ . The system with transfer function $H(\omega )$ produces a phase shift of $+90°$ for the positive frequencies and of $–90°$ for the negative frequencies. It follows from the convolution theorem that $\stackrel{ˆ}{X}(t)$ can also be defined as the convolution of $X(t)$ by the corresponding impulse response, that is $h(t)=1/\pi t$ .

$\stackrel{ˆ}{X}(t)$ is likewise real; a second application of the Hilbert transform restores the initial signal, with a change of sign and stripped of its possible continuous component.

**Example**  : $x(t)=A\cos\omega t\to \stackrel{ˆ}{x}(t)\text{=-}A\sin\omega t$

This property is the basis of the use of the Hilbert transform in order to define the envelope of a narrow-band process.
